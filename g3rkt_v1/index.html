Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

An enduring topic of scientific interest since the beginning of cognitive psychological research is visual attention. This phenomenon describes the process by which certain visual percepts are prioritized for deeper processing in the brain, while others receive reduced processing and thereby exert less influence on perception and behavior. In our daily lives, visual attention is critical to performance across countless situations-from navigating busy traffic to maintaining focus on a friend in a crowded room. Therefore, understanding the neural mechanisms that dictate how we select relevant visual information and filter out distractions is central to understanding one of the brain's most fundamental cognitive abilities. Because these attentional processes unravel rapidly in time and are reflected in dynamic patterns of cortical activity, encephalographic measures (i.e. electro-and magneto-encephalography) have been popular tools for their study. In 2025, attention researchers from across Europe gathered in Amsterdam for two days of meetings, discussion, and debate on current issues and future avenues in the encephalographic investigation of attention. This perspective piece distills the ideas and topics that emerged from that meeting into a field guide to contemporary encephalographic research on visual attention. It provides a snapshot of several active research areas in visual attention, highlights where they are heading, and which questions are likely to shape their future. We hope that this piece will be especially useful for early career researchers looking to enter the field but unsure about what their next research question should be.

Introduction

How does the brain cope with the overwhelming amount of information it encounters at every moment? Since Broadbent's Filter Theory was proposed (Broadbent, 1958), this has remained a central question in attention research. Alongside major experimental and theoretical advances (Posner, 1980; Treisman & Gelade, 1980), a landmark advance came from Steven Hillyard and colleagues, who demonstrated that scalp-recorded electrophysiological signals-particularly event-related potentials (ERPs)-could reveal the neural mechanisms underlying attentional selection in both auditory (Hillyard et al., 1973) and visual (Van Voorhis & Hillyard, 1977) domains. In the ensuing years, electroencephalography (EEG) has become one of the principal methods for investigating the neural mechanisms of human attention.

Methodological advances have substantially broadened the range of neural signals that can be measured, allowing researchers to pursue increasingly intricate questions about the mechanisms and nature of human attention. ERPs and oscillatory analyses continue to provide precise, well-validated markers of attention, and they are now complemented by multivariate decoding techniques, as well as the emergence of rapid invisible frequency tagging (RIFT), which open new avenues for studying attentional operations that were previously only studied via behavior. Collectively, this research has significantly advanced the understanding of how humans effectively direct attention to a subset of meaningful percepts while filtering those deemed irrelevant or distracting.

In the summer of 2025, attention researchers from across Europe gathered in Amsterdam for an international symposium titled "Advances in the Encephalographic Study of Attention." During this two-day forum, researchers presented and debated their latest findings and emerging ideas. This meeting offered a snapshot of the questions and directions shaping contemporary attention research. While not a comprehensive overview of the entire field of visual attention research, here we synthesize several of the emerging themes from this meeting in hopes of offering a useful tool for contemporary researchers to understand some of the topics currently driving attention research, and why these questions warrant continued interest.

The Univariate Fingerprints of Attention -Interpreting the Prototypical ERPs of Visual Search

Contributors: Brandi Lee Drisdelle, Tobias Feldmann-Wüstefeld, Clayton Hickey, Dirk Kerzel, Heinrich Liesefeld, Juan Lupianez, George R. Mangun, Matt Oxner, Heleen Slagter, Dirk van Moorselaar, Alon Zivony The seminal work of Steven Hillyard and colleagues in the 1970s and 80s identified prototypical changes to evoked electrical responses measured on the scalp of participants when performing visual attention tasks, laying the foundations for a fiftyplus-year research program investigating how ERPs track visual attentional deployment (Van Voorhis & Hillyard, 1977). The striking persistence of ERP-based research can largely be attributed to the temporal precision of these signals, which can track attentional processes that unravel rapidly in time, thus making them a natural complement to behavioral chronometric methods (Posner, 1978) and visual search paradigms (Treisman & Gelade, 1980) common to cognitive psychology. Early work on cued (G. Mangun et al., 1987; G. R. Mangun & Hillyard, 1991) and reflexive attention (Hopfinger & Mangun, 1998) laid the foundation for ERP research on attentional processing. This soon gave way to the use of more complex bilateral stimulus arrays, introduced to investigate attention mechanisms during visual search (Heinze et al., 1990; Luck & Hillyard, 1990). By leveraging the spatial organization of the visual system (where stimuli presented in the left and right visual hemifields are initially processed in contralateral, or opposite, cerebral hemispheres) Hillyard and colleagues were able to isolate the electrical correlates of neural processing of items in attended and unattended hemifields; thereby allowing the study of stimulus configurations that more closely approximated natural scenes. This lateralized approach gave rise to the discovery of two electroencephalographic components in particular that are now considered paradigmatic in the study of visual attention -the target-related N2pc and distractor-evoked P D.

First came the N2pc, a lateralized component calculated by subtracting electrical activity in ipsilateral from contralateral electrodes (usually PO7/8) relative to the location of an attended item (Luck & Hillyard, 1994a , 1994b). This negative component reliably appeared when targets were presented among non-targets, but also sometimes in response to distractors, and has since shaped numerous research programs utilizing EEG in the study of attention. This ERP component was complemented in 2009 by the discovery of the P D -a distractor-related ERP component occurring when distractors were presented lateralized and targets were presented on the vertical midline (where their electrical activity was equally present in both hemifields; Hickey et al., 2009). Using the same electrodes as were implicated in N2pc analyses, Hickey and colleagues showed that an electroencephalographic signature of distractor suppression presented as a positivity over the contralateral hemisphere. With the growth of research on distractor processing in recent years, the P D has come to mirror the popularity of the N2pc as a central component of many ongoing research programs (Gaspelin et al., 2023).

Today, the study of lateralized ERPs that result from attentional processingspecifically the target and distractor N2pc and the distractor P D -remains a cornerstone of EEG attention research. These simple-to-register encephalographic components have been extensively utilized to study topics as far reaching as the divergent neural markers of schizophrenia or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder in children (Sawaki et al., 2017; E. Wang et al., 2016) to studies of trait anxiety or trust in AI (Gaspar & McDonald, 2018; Wiese & Feldmann-Wüstefeld, 2026). In the paragraphs below, we highlight some of the newest advances in ERP methodology, as well as the open theoretical questions driving research using the N2pc and P D today.

Interpreting modulations of the N2pc

In the original work by Luck and colleagues identifying the N2pc, the authors proposed that this negative component was the result of spatial filtering, and was thus largely reflecting a distractor-related neural process rather than pure target processing (Luck et al., 1997). Luck and colleagues reached this conclusion based on the observation that no reliable N2pc was observed when the displays consisted solely of targets, suggesting that the component depends on some degree of spatial competition between perceptual representations (Luck & Hillyard, 1994b). This conclusion was later challenged by Eimer (1996), who demonstrated that the N2pc was measurable when targets were presented alone in a hemifield, with a distractor presented in the opposite hemifield. Since this demonstration, the N2pc has been widely used as a paradigmatic index related to attentional selection, showing notable modulations in latency and amplitude based on the ease of target detection (Töllner et al., 2011). Despite this, there remains some uncertainty about the interpretation of modulations in the N2pc, and how these are linked to attentional activity in the attended hemifield.

For the N2pc, much like many other ERPs, it is common that a specified time window is selected and lateralized activity averaged in this window with its amplitude then compared between conditions. Typically this is the window between 200 and 300 ms post display onset, but bespoke windows are common in the literature. However, using preset windows presumes knowledge about the size and timing of the to-bemeasured component which is justified when replicating existing work or using a paradigm with well-established ERP timing, but risks missing the ERP altogether when a novel manipulation is tested. In a recent example of the well-known danger of predetermined window-based analyses, Constant et al. (2025) in a pre-registered ManyLabs replication of Eimer (1996) found that shape targets elicited a late N2pc almost completely out of their expected window across most datasets. The timing of the N2pc is further known to vary in line with response times in tasks where the time needed for target detection is variable trial-to-trial. Numerous studies have compared the N2pc in efficient, parallel search tasks to inefficient, serial search tasks and noted significant differences in its shape and temporal profile (Drisdelle & Jolicoeur, 2020; Kandemir et al., 2026; Oxner, Mazza, et al., 2024) with some only registering an N2pc in inefficient search when trials were separated between fast and slow responses (Dowdall et al., 2012). Liesefeld et al. (2017) similarly found that the N2pc can be significantly delayed on trials in which a competing distractor is on the screen, observing a distractor-evoked N2pc earlier in time that then needed to be resolved before a delayed, second N2pc could be measured towards a competing target. In these examples, the use of a set N2pc window will fail to register temporal variability between conditions, and simply register an attenuated N2pc in certain conditions. However, the alternative approach of simply selecting a window based on visual inspection following data collection faces issues of post-hoc data sorting (i.e. the risk of manufacturing effects from inherently noisy data; Luck & Gaspelin, 2017). Rather, datadriven alternatives to the predetermined window approach such as the latency estimate technique (Liesefeld, 2018) or residue iteration decomposition (RIDE; Ouyang et al., 2011), offer compelling alternatives to this approach which may better capture a temporally variable N2pc while remaining methodologically sound. Overall, these new data-driven approaches present an improvement over traditional predetermined windows with little-to-no drawbacks and thus the project to standardize these techniques remains timely.

It additionally remains a clear and worthwhile topic to explore what precise cognitive process the N2pc actually indexes, and what this says about the putative mechanism of attention itself. Notwithstanding debates about the meaning of "attention" (Hommel et al., 2019; W. Wu, 2024; Zivony & Eimer, 2022; Woodman, 2026), it is clear that the N2pc indexes a cognitive process closely related to the allocation of attention in space. However, a growing consensus emerged that the N2pc reflects a process that occurs further downstream, and may not even emerge under conditions where attentional shifting is observed (Woodman et al., 2009; Giattino et al., 2018; Rashal et al., 2022; Talcott et al., 2025; Zivony et al., 2018). This begs the question: what precise cognitive process does the N2pc follow from? It is uncontroversial to point out that the N2pc does not reflect salience processing per se, as evident by its relatively late occurrence (arising usually 200-300 ms following stimulus onset). Instead, a body of work has now implicated this component as relating to object individuation, perhaps representing a stage of attentional processing where objects are separated from their backgrounds (D. E. Anderson et al., 2013; Foster et al., 2020). Evidence for this comes from observations that the N2pc is reliably modulated by the number of salient targets in a hemifield, revealing a role for object individuation and subitizing for the N2pc (Mazza, 2017; Mazza et al., 2013; Mazza & Caramazza, 2015). Building on this, recent work using concurrently recorded EEG and fMRI noted that N2pc modulations robustly predicted the emergence of object category information in the fMRI signal, indicating that the N2pc marked some passage of object-related information from the early visual system to higher object processing areas (Acunzo et al., 2025). Furthermore the observation that a distractor-driven N2pc reliably occurs when distractors share potential target features (Luck & Hillyard, 1994a , 1994b) suggests that the N2pc is driven in part by target-template matching and indicates that it may be useful in elucidating the flexibility of attentional templates based on current task demands (i.e. Yu et al., 2023). The discourse on what component of attentional processing the N2pc precisely indexes highlights that visual attention consists of multiple stages, and that these stages remain poorly characterized. Already existing work has shown that the N2pc is influenced differentially by early (distractor salience) and late (distractor-target similarity) processing demands of a task (Aubin & Jolicoeur, 2016; Drisdelle & Jolicoeur, 2018). Pinning down precisely what attentional process the N2pc indexes may thus make it an essential tool in future research at characterizing the processing steps that make up visual attention, and thereby may aid in the larger challenge of arriving at a precise definition of attention itself.

Several decades of N2pc focused research has clearly demonstrated the utility of this easily detected lateralized component for measuring attentional processing in space. Both methodological improvements in the measure of the N2pc, as well as further work testing the boundary conditions in which an N2pc is measurable are worthwhile research endeavors. Additionally, the combination of the N2pc with other EEG measures (e.g. multivariate spatial or feature decoding) can offer even greater insights into the cognitive processes that drive this paradigmatic index of attentional processing.

The P D as EEG marker of distractor handling

Similar debate surrounds the interpretation of positive polarity components that often precede, follow, or replace a distractor-driven N2pc. Modulations of the P D seem clearly related to distractor handling, as a reduced P D has reliably been associated with better suppression due to spatial cueing (van Zoest et al., 2021), learning (Hanne et al., 2025; van Moorselaar et al., 2020), target-distractor distance (Feldmann-Wüstefeld et al., 2021; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2022) or simply due to less distracting distractors (Chen et al., 2023; Feldmann-Wüstefeld & Vogel, 2019; van Moorselaar & Slagter, 2019). Furthermore, combining ERP approaches with inverted encoding models reveals the P D coincides with below-baseline processing spatially localized at the distractor location, directly linking the component to spatial suppression (Feldmann-Wüstefeld et al., 2021). Crucially, concerns that the P D merely indexes an N2pc to the opposite hemifield are partially assuaged by studies finding a P D in conditions where no nontarget appears contralateral to the distractor (e.g. Drisdelle & Eimer, 2021; Hickey et al., 2009; van Zoest et al., 2021; Weaver et al., 2017; Wu & Fu, 2017; but this doesn't mean that the P D is never an N2pc to contralateral nontargets, i.e. Kerzel & Burra, 2020; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2023; Tam et al., 2022) However, interpretations of the P D are complicated by the possibility of a cooccurring distractor-N2pc which overlaps roughly in time with the P D in many experimental paradigms and presents in an opposite polarity (see Gaspelin et al., 2023 for a review). As such, modulations in the P D amplitude could well be the result of an overlapping negative-polarity N2pc component present at the same time and electrodes, causing destructive averaging of both signals (Forschack et al., 2022a; Oxner et al., 2025). Often, the amplitude of the P D and distractor-evoked N2pc covary, with a distractor-evoked N2pc preceding and delaying the P D (Feldmann-Wüstefeld, 2021; Feldmann-Wüstefeld & Schubö, 2013). This suggests that greater attentional capture elicits correspondingly greater reactive suppression and supports a serial processing characterization of the visual system (Liesefeld et al., 2017). Importantly, if capture by a salient distractor only occurs on half of all trials, this may produce an overlapping distractor-N2pc with the P D in the trial aggregate, presenting a risk of destructive averaging. One potentially useful method for tying ERP modulations to behavior is linear mixed-effects models, which provide single trial outputs that do not require averaging across the dataset, and so can potentially remedy issues related to trial averaging (Henare et al., 2024; Hickey et al., 2025; Oxner, Mazza, et al., 2024). Furthermore, recent research has proposed that multivariate approaches can be used to effectively isolate univariate neural signals in noisy data, allowing researchers to disentangle and isolate canonical ERPs from overlapping neural signals (Weindel et al., 2024). Cross decoding using multivariate decoders trained on one trial type and tested on another further offers another compelling method of detecting cognitive processes otherwise obscured by noisy data (Kandemir et al., 2026; Z. Zhang & Lewis-Peacock, 2024). It remains to be seen what new novel insights related to the distractor suppression and the P D will arise in the coming years by leveraging these new analysis approaches to unveil previously undetectable cognitive phenomena.

While the P D is widely viewed as a marker of suppression, some studies observe a much earlier distractor-driven positivity, which some research groups refer to as the early-P D and interpret as a marker of proactive suppression (Sawaki & Luck, 2010; Drisdelle & Eimer, 2021 , 2023; Gaspelin & Luck, 2018a; Redding & Fiebelkorn, 2024; Stilwell et al., 2022; B. Wang et al., 2019; Weaver et al., 2017). However, a number of research groups have pointed out that this early positivity exactly overlaps with the Posterior Positivity Contralateral component (Ppc) which has traditionally been thought to index raw salience processing (Burra & Kerzel, 2014; Corriveau et al., 2012). As such, it is an open question whether the early-P D truly represents a unique neural process to the Ppc, with unique neural populations and functions roughly overlapping in space and timing, or whether this early positivity is simply a well-known salience signal that also often appears with a salient distractor (Sawaki & Luck, 2010). Recent work has supported the latter assertion: showing that the early distractor positivity is present in cases where suppression is absent and therefore evoked by salient targets (McDonald et al., 2026; Oxner, Mazza, et al., 2024; Oxner et al., 2025). Furthermore, this early component has been found to be sensitive to target regularities (van Moorselaar et al., 2023) and research using learned suppression paradigms have failed to find modulations in this early component to match that which has reliably been found in its late-occurring counterpart (Hanne et al., 2025; van Moorselaar et al., 2020). Additionally, concerns about an overlapping N2pc nearby in timing are even larger for the early positivity which is reliably lower in amplitude than the later P D -meaning even an infrequent adjacent N2pc may be sufficient to obliterate this positivity from the signal after trial averaging.

What is clear is that much remains to be learned about the lateralized positivity that often follows the presentation of a salient distractor over the contralateral hemisphere. It remains to be seen how clever experimentation combined with methodological advances will allow researchers to better disentangle the underlying cognitive operations driving the P D . Clearly this is just the beginning of this research program and work in the coming years will allow us to better understand how a positive deflection following the presentation of a lateral distractor indexes the cognitive mechanisms of distractor handling.

Future Directions

The already-long history of ERP research on visual attention attests to the technique's durable appeal. The relatively low barrier to entry for ERP research, which requires only minimal experimental setups and is therefore feasible even for labs with limited resources, together with its ability to reveal patterns of cognitive processing that are not evident in overt behavior, makes it an especially appealing tool in the attention researcher's arsenal. Building on over three decades of work, ERP research is at an exciting and optimistic place, as new methodological advances allow researchers to ask ever more probing questions in the pursuit of a better understanding of human visual attention. Open questions about the precise cognitive mechanisms indexed by these ERPs further represent compelling topics of interest that will continue to drive research programs in the years to come.foot_0

Rapid Invisible Frequency Tagging: A New Frontier in Tracking Covert Attention

Contributors: Kabir Arora, Samson Chota, Oscar Ferrante, Surya Gayet, Eelke Spaak, Stefan Van der Stigchel For many decades, frequency tagging has offered a powerful method of tracking attentional allocation with unambiguous origins relative to the measurement of endogenous oscillations in the brain (for debates on the latter, see : Antonov et al., 2020; Bonnefond & Jensen, 2024; Jensen & Bonnefond, 2026; Schneider et al., 2022). By flickering stimuli at set frequencies, neural responses as recorded in M/EEG will show distinct peaks in the power spectrum at these stimulated frequencies. These steady state visual evoked potentials (SSVEPs) are further known to be modulated by attention such that if attention is directed to an item 'tagged' at a certain frequency, then the spectral peak will be enhanced at this frequency (M. M. Müller, Picton, et al., 1998; M. M. Müller, Teder-Sälejärvi, et al., 1998; M. M. Müller et al., 2003).

Frequency tagging has thus proven to be a useful tool for measuring visual attention. However, the use of slow, overt SSVEPs creates significant visual disturbances on the stimulus screen. This is often undesirable as it can disrupt or significantly influence ongoing cognitive activity (Alais et al., 2016; Cass et al., 2011), fundamentally altering the bottom-up characteristics of a display with opaque consequences. The inherent visibility of traditional, slow SSVEPs has precluded its use in more naturalistic stimuli, or in studies seeking to measure the role of attention in multisensory environments (where significant visual disturbances may unnaturally bias attention towards visual modalities). Furthermore, this inherent visibility has precluded the use of slow SSVEPs in the study of 'object-free' spatial attention.

Recent advances in display technology and improved M/EEG signal clarity have led to the development of Rapid Invisible Frequency Tagging (RIFT; Drijvers et al., 2021; Seijdel et al., 2023; Zhigalov et al., 2019). By pushing frequency tagging into the highfrequency range (typically between 55 and 75 Hz), stimuli flicker so fast that the tag becomes imperceptible yet still evokes a measurable oscillatory response in the early visual cortex (Spaak et al., 2024). Crucially, the resulting oscillatory neural signals have been shown to reliably modulate with attention such that attended items display higher power/coherence values than unattended items (Arora et al., 2025; Zhigalov et al., 2019; Zhigalov & Jensen, 2020), thereby opening the door for RIFT as a subtle method of detecting neural modulations as a result of covert attention.

Using RIFT to invisibly probe the visual system

A fundamental, longstanding question in attention research is: when does spatial attention proactively boost early visual processing at the attended location (i.e., enhance cortical excitability in early visual cortex), and when does it instead only reactively enhance the response to a stimulus once it appears at the attended location (i.e., upon evoking an object representation, and likely further down the processing pathway)? Distinguishing between proactive spatial (object-free) and reactive stimulus driven (object-based) attention requires probing of attentional enhancement at specific locations invisibly, in the absence of any discernable object at those locations. Arora et al. (2025) successfully dissociated these two types of attentional processing by utilizing RIFT to probe spatial excitability invisibly, demonstrating a new way to probe cortical excitability without any object presentation at all. Arora et al. (2025) found that cuing a region in space (where a memory target would soon be presented) resulted in elevated RIFT signal at the cued location, indexing enhanced attentional processing. Notably, this enhanced RIFT response was not observed in a slightly different design where the spatial cue indicated which of two previously presented memory targets is relevant for subsequent recall. This finding showcases a sharp divergence with alpha responses -a classical, endogenous oscillatory marker of attention -which showed an enhanced lateralized response following the spatial cue in both situations. Arora et al. (2025) is one example of a key feature of RIFT that opens new lines of inquiry that were previously closed: by allowing researchers to invisibly probe the visual system, the neural consequences of (covert) spatial attention can now be probed separately from higher order processing. Moreover, RIFT is easily split into multiple tagging signals, allowing researchers to tile the visual field with dissociable RIFT probes to measure how attentional enhancement waxes, wanes and shifts across the visual field in time. For instance, Wang et al. (2026) used RIFT across multiple locations to probe how top-down and bottom-up attention dynamically compete in an experiment using the additional singleton task (Theeuwes, 1992). They observed higher RIFT evoked signals for both targets and distractors relative to neutral nontargets, reflecting the influence of top-down goals and bottom-up salience on early visual processing. Importantly, the presence of a distractor attenuated the initial RIFT response to the target, indicating competition during the earliest stages of visual processing. Over time, however, RIFT responses to the distractor decreased below responses to both targets and nontargets, consistent with reactive suppression of task-irrelevant stimuli.

RIFT can be an important technique for answering a central question in the study of selection history: whether learned suppression is proactive or reactive in nature (for a more in depth discussion on this topic, see the below section on the role of learning in shaping attentional deployment). Because RIFT can track placeholder locations without altering their salience, it provides an ideal tool to probe preparatory neural responses before the onset of the search display. Ferrante et al. (2023) tested the hypothesis that statistical learning of distractor locations relies on proactive spatial suppression. By tagging placeholder stimuli before a search array appeared, they observed weaker RIFT responses in the pre-stimulus window for locations with a high probability of containing a distractor. These insights provide some of the first direct neural evidence that suppression can indeed operate proactively.

RIFT's ability to invisibly map shifts in cortical sensitivity across the visual field further allows for measuring other features of attentional processing -such as observing how attention moves through space, and whether it expands or contracts given current task demands. Furthermore, continuing advances in presentation and analysis techniques allow to simultaneously tag an increasing number of spatial locations or (static and even moving) stimuli. The option to measure RIFT responses within visible placeholders versus on a uniform background additionally offers researchers the unique opportunity to observe the role of objects in attention measurement, capitalizing on the visual system's inherent design to aid in object processing. Perhaps the greatest untapped application of RIFT is in naturalistic stimuli, where spatially specific attentional measurements are now possible without disturbing the visual scene itself, allowing for the neural tracking of attention in natural environments in a neutral manner with a level of flexibility which was previously impossible.

Understanding the limitations of RIFT responses and its cortical origins

Given it remains a relatively new technique, caution remains called for when interpreting modulations in RIFT signals given how much remains unknown about its precise cortical origins. Work using MEG has localized the RIFT response to the early visual system (Ferrante et al., 2023; Zhigalov et al., 2019; Zhigalov & Jensen, 2020), suggesting it is a tool for measuring early cortical processing shifts as a function of spatial attention. Concurrent EEG/fMRI studies have similarly localized slower SSVEPs in V1, but also notably find origins from later processing areas such as V2/3 (Di Russo et al., 2007; Mohr et al., 2024) and hMT (Boylan et al., 2023), suggesting modulations in lower frequency flicker may capture distinct cognitive processes from its high frequency counterpart.

It remains further unclear how/whether the RIFT signal captures different cognitive processes than traditional, slower SSVEPs. While the spatial sensitivity of RIFT is well established, it remains unclear whether RIFT tracks feature based attention similar to slow SSVEPs. It was recently demonstrated that RIFT can indeed capture feature-based attention in a guided visual search task, thereby demonstrating its utility in top-down search (Bouwkamp et al., 2024; Duecker et al., 2025). However, both these studies used a T among L's task, in which attention was directed at discrete items at specific locations in space. This differs from the more distributed attention typically measured in feature-based attention tasks such as random dot kinematograms (S. Andersen & Müller, 2010; M. Müller et al., 2006). It therefore remains unclear whether RIFT signals produce comparable results to those often observed in traditional random dot kinematogram studies. Furthermore, while some effects observed using slow SSVEPs have been replicated using RIFT (e.g.: Forschack et al., 2022; D. Wang et al., 2026), other effects have not (e.g.: Duncan et al., 2025; Ferrante et al., 2023), suggesting that the neural processes indexed by RIFT and traditional, slower SSVEPs may originate from different neuronal populations; highlighting the importance of understanding this divergence as RIFT gains popularity.

For these reasons, more work is clearly needed to better understand the locus of RIFT modulations, as well as what precise cognitive mechanisms it responds to. Source localization using MEG is a known and powerful tool for isolating cortical loci, and recent advances in optically pumped magnetometers (OPM) have the potential for improved source localization over traditional cryogenic MEG (Boto et al., 2016; Liang et al., 2025; Nugent et al., 2022), perhaps offering a better method of tracking the locus of the RIFT signal. Additionally, comparing fMRI modulations and M/EEG RIFT coherence could unlock further insights into the spatial origins of RIFT signals recorded in M/EEG, thereby improving our interpretation of RIFT modulations and their functional significance. The possibility of deploying both slow SSVEPs and RIFT at the same time within an experiment trial further may offer crucial insights into how these signals diverge in their sensitivities.

Future Directions

RIFT offers the chance to answer compelling questions about the human attention system that have previously been impossible or convoluted to probe. Most critically, the externally evoked nature of the RIFT signal (originating on an external computer screen rather than an opaque neural process) gives a straightforward and elegant measure of attentional processing which previously demanded the accompanying visual disturbances of SSVEP techniques. While still a young technique, RIFT has already revolutionized the study of attention in reading, where its invisible measurement allows researchers to capture the movement of attention undisturbed while participants parse complex symbols (Pan et al., 2021 (Pan et al., , 2024)). Future research avenues include the application of RIFT to studies with natural or dynamic scene stimuli, where locations or objects can be invisibly tagged and attention tracked unobtrusively. RIFT additionally has clear applicability in brain computer interfaces, where the ability to track attentional deployment without disturbing the visual experience may offer an ideal method of hands-free control (Brickwedde et al., 2022).

While the early work on RIFT used MEG and presented stimuli on high precision projectors at high refresh rates (1440 Hz), recent work has shown that RIFT is also measurable using EEG (Arora et al., 2024 (Arora et al., , 2025) ) and can be entrained even using commercially available high-performance gaming monitors with lower refresh rates than a Propixx (Çelik et al., 2025; Dimigen et al., 2025). A recently published comprehensive "How to RIFT" manual paper (Arora et al., 2026) further increases accessibility of RIFT to a broad range of researchers. Beyond researchers interested in studying attention per se, any group with a functioning M/EEG lab can now implement RIFT in their existing computer-based task, allowing to track (and control) for differences in attention over space/stimuli and time. These advances have demonstrated that RIFT is a technique with a significantly lower bar of entry than was initially assumed and thus may soon come to be a major mode of attentional measurement used in encephalography in labs across the world.

Integrating Ocular Metrics and Neural Signals to Understand Goal-Directed Attention

Contributors: Sebastiaan Mathôt, Freek van Ede

For decades, ocular behavior has been treated primarily as a nuisance in encephalographic studies of the brain. Because participants naturally tend to move their eyes toward the object of current attention, eye movements present a major confound when recording brain activity: any evoked response might simply reflect the muscular activity necessary to perform a saccade or the retinal displacement of an image following one. Consequently, removing eye artifacts has been an important step in data collection and preprocessing, involving stringent participant instruction to maintain fixation, careful trimming of eye-tracking data around saccades, or regressing out ocular signals via independent component analyses.

However, a major current topic driving modern encephalographic research is the dramatic reversal of this trend. Instead of eliminating ocular behavior to study 'pure' covert attention, researchers are now actively interrogating the crucial interplay between the oculomotor system and covert neural signals. This shift is motivated by action-oriented accounts, such as the premotor theory of attention (Rizzolatti et al., 1987), which conceptualize attention not merely as a sensory filter, but as a mechanism deeply tied to covert motor planning and goal-directed action selection (Koevoet et al., 2023 (Koevoet et al., , 2024)). The connection between attention and planned action is a strong one, with neuroimaging work demonstrating that activation patterns within the frontoparietal network during attention tasks vary depending on the type of movement that can be prepared (e.g., saccades vs. grasping), even in the absence of overt movement or motor imagery (Craighero et al., 1999). Within visual attention, eye movements are an essential component of the spatial attention network as often covert spatial attention precedes the movement of the eye to foveate on the current target of attention (Deubel & Schneider, 1996; Kowler et al., 1995; Rolfs & Carrasco, 2012). Leading some to wonder what rich attentional information can be recorded by looking at ocular behavior, and to what degree these simple measurements can complement/replace encephalographic measures.

Two measures in particular have recently gained traction as useful supplementary measures of neural activity -microsaccades and pupil size. Both in turn face challenges as the ongoing research attempts to elucidate the degree to which these measures supplement neural activity or alternatively drive the signals picked up in neuroimaging. Early indications show promise that these overt markers of attentional processing may not directly interfere with EEG signals, and may thus come to be a useful analogue to the effects measured from the brain.

Are classical EEG markers actually driven by the eyes?

When studying the neural basis of covert voluntary attention, a vital prerequisite is ensuring that visual stimulation-as registered by the retina-remains identical across conditions. Yet, humans actively sample their environment. Even during attempted fixation, humans continuously make microsaccades and experience pupil fluctuations (Martinez-Conde et al., 2004; Rolfs, 2009; Ruuskanen & Mathot, 2026). Because these micro-scale eye behaviors alter how visual inputs impinge on the retina, a fundamental question is currently driving the field: are classical neural markers of covert attention actually driven by 'bottom-up' sensory changes caused by these subtle eye behaviors? For instance, voluntary shifts of spatial attention spatially bias microsaccades (Engbert & Kliegl, 2003; Hafed & Clark, 2002), even when attention is directed inward to representations held in visual working memory (Liu & van Ede, 2025; Van Ede et al., 2019). Eye movements have also come under scrutiny when trying to decode the feature content of working memory from EEG signals, as studies show that features like orientation can be decoded from eye data alone (Mostert et al., 2018). Importantly, recent work has separately shown for both the N2pc and alpha lateralization (two paradigmatic measures of covert attentional shifts) that while highly correlated to microsaccades, these well-known markers of covert attention cannot be wholly explained by these small eye movements (Liu et al., 2022 (Liu et al., , 2025;; Meyberg et al., 2017; Popov, 2026), and thus are more likely a shared downstream output resulting from shifts of attention. Microsaccades have further been shown to largely index shifts of attention, but fail to capture sustained attention towards a spatial location (van Harmelen & van Ede, 2025), and while attention alters the direction of microsaccades, attentional shifts do not themselves cause microsaccades (Liu et al., 2024). Instead, attentional shifts simply bias the direction of microsaccades that would have occurred anyway, even if a non-attentional task was performed -a pattern of results consistent with the idea that microsaccades are a downstream process sharing the same upstream origins as common EEG markers of attention. As such, the future of microsaccadic research should treat these minute eye movements as a complementary measure to those recorded using EEG rather than solely as an alternative explanation to the patterns observed in the EEG signal (van Ede, 2026).

How do pupil dilation and covert attention differ in visual processing?

Just as microsaccades offer a window into spatial attention, pupillometry offers insights into the intensity of visual processing (Mathôt, 2018; Strauch et al., 2022). The expansion and contraction of the pupil changes the content of visual information passing through the visual system, thus altering the eventual representation of this visual input in the brain. It has already been noted that covert attentional demands can influence pupil dilation (Vilotijević & Mathôt, 2023), improve sensitivity in nearthreshold detection (Eberhardt et al., 2022; Mathôt & Ivanov, 2019; Ruuskanen et al., 2025) and increase SSVEP amplitudes recorded from the scalp (Suzuki et al., 2019). It is thus essential to ascertain the degree to which changes to the pupil cause or alter brain dynamics recorded via encephalography.

In a recent study, Mathôt et al. (2023) manipulated covert visual attention and pupil size orthogonally in a single task to observe their interactions. Mathôt et al. found that while attentional manipulations had clear effects on the alpha and theta frequencies, pupil size primarily modulated beta frequencies. Furthermore, a study by Ruuskanen et al. (2025) using a visual detection task found that while pupil size reliably predicted detection performance, alpha and beta power did not, suggesting that, in this paradigm at least, the improvement in detection performance was driven by an optical effect and not the result of changes in endogenous brain states (Ruuskanen & Mathot, 2026).

Importantly, while it is appealing to attribute improvements in performance to increased visual sensitivity derived from larger pupils, pupil size is also associated with arousal (Bradley et al., 2008; Hess & Polt, 1960; Kahneman & Beatty, 1966). Since arousal in turn affects performance as well as EEG markers, this is another route through which correlations between pupil size, behavior, and EEG markers may arise (reviewed in Ruuskanen & Mathôt¸ in press). Further work disentangling the relationship between pupil size, performance, and arousal thus remains needed.

Future Directions

While both pupil size and microsaccades are now known to modulate as a consequence of ongoing attentional activity -thereby providing a rich, complementary measure of visual attention -it is also important to note that they do not offer a complete record of attentional allocation (Smith & Schenk, 2012). Covert attention can be directed to a location without an overt eye movement, as demonstrated by Klink et al. (2023). In their study, macaques made saccades directly to a target without ever fixating a salient distractor; yet, neural recordings in V4 revealed a brief, initial attentional enhancement at the distractor location. This highlights why using the absence of (micro)saccades to infer an absence of attention is problematic: eye movements can fail to reveal transient covert shifts.

Nevertheless, this limitation underscores the value of concurrent eye tracking, particularly alongside EEG. Though eye-tracking data is already available in many EEG studies, it is frequently underutilized-often relegated solely to artifact control and trial exclusion. This practice overlooks a valuable, essentially "for free" dataset. Because covert attention often serves to prepare overt eye movements, integrating these measures allows ocular behavior to constrain the interpretation of neural activity, while neural measures expose the covert dynamics that behavior misses. Ultimately, a complete account of visual selection requires both; as concurrent recordings become standard practice, the field may well look back on eye-movement integration as a major methodological turning point in the electrophysiological study of attention.

The Role of Learning in Shaping Attentional Suppression

Contributors: Dock H. Duncan, Tobias Feldmann-Wüstefeld, Clayton Hickey, Anna Schubö, Jan Theeuwes, Dirk van Moorselaar Traditionally, visual attention was best characterized by a dichotomy of two mechanisms: a top-down volitional mechanism defined by the participant's current goals, and a bottom-up automatic system defined by the intrinsic salience of the visual environment (Desimone & Duncan, 1995; Koch & Ullman, 1987; Theeuwes, 2010). Together, these modules were thought to capture how attention is guided in a scene. However, this dichotomy left a critical gap in our understanding of attentional control. It failed to account for how specific, recent, and incidental experiences dynamically shape ongoing behavior. Early demonstrations of priming of pop-out showed that recent selection episodes can strongly influence subsequent attentional selection (Maljkovic & Nakayama, 1994 , 1996). These findings contributed to a broader realization that attentional control cannot be fully understood in terms of the traditional dichotomy between top-down goals and bottom-up salience.

In an influential paper, Awh et al. (2012) explicitly challenged this prevailing view and argued that, in many instances, attentional selection is shaped by prior selection experiences. They termed this influence selection history. According to this view, selection is often not guided solely by the observer's current goals, nor solely by the physical salience of objects in the environment. Instead, previous encounters with particular features, objects, or locations can bias what is selected on subsequent trials. Importantly, Awh et al. (2012) argued that effects previously interpreted as evidence for top-down control may, in some cases, be better understood as consequences of selection history. This proposal established selection history as a third major component of attentional control, alongside goal-driven and stimulus-driven influences (see also Failing & Theeuwes, 2018; Theeuwes, 2018 Theeuwes, , 2019)), and forms the basis of the recently proposed tripartite model of attention (Theeuwes, 2025).

Since then, the study of selection history has become a central topic in attention research, including the encephalographic study of visual attention. Considerable ERP work has demonstrated the influence of selection history on the target N2pc and distractor P D -from work on intertrial priming (Eimer et al., 2010; Feldmann-Wüstefeld & Schubö, 2016; Olivers & Hickey, 2010), to associative learning (Do Carmo Blanco & Allen, 2018; Feldmann-Wüstefeld et al., 2015; Qu et al., 2017) and reward learning (Feldmann-Wüstefeld et al., 2016; Hickey et al., 2010 Hickey et al., , 2023;; Kiss et al., 2009; Oemisch et al., 2017; Taylor & Feldmann-Wüstefeld, 2024), abundant work has demonstrated that learning effects are effectively tracked by modulations in amplitude or latency of wellknown attention-related ERPs.

A key question cutting across these mechanisms is how experience-driven changes influence attentional priority: do they proactively bias processing before the search display appears, or do they operate reactively after a stimulus has been encountered? Addressing this question requires methods that can reveal the neural mechanisms and temporal dynamics through which recent experience -whether driven by repetition, statistical structure, or learned value -shapes attentional selection.

Is learned distractor suppression proactive or reactive?

Central to this new study of learning was the observation that through experience, distracting stimuli can become greatly attenuated. While the role of top-down control in suppressing distractors is heavily debated (e.g. Addleman & Störmer, 2022; Arita et al., 2012), there is broad agreement that statistical learning offers a robust and efficient mechanism for reducing interference from distracting input. A critical question currently driving M/EEG research is whether this learned suppression operates proactively or reactively (Liesefeld et al., 2024; Luck et al., 2021) -if a region of space receives reduced attentional priority due to learning, is the initial feed-forward sweep of visual information attenuated before it even arrives (proactive)? Or is the information initially selected, only to be rapidly disengaged and suppressed by the attention system (reactive)?

For spatial learning, encephalographic techniques have provided conflicting answers. Experiments utilizing RIFT, machine learning classification, and novel 'pinging' techniques have revealed shifting spatial attentional priority maps prior to search onset, providing strong neural evidence for a proactive mechanism (D. H. Duncan et al., 2025; Ferrante et al., 2023 Ferrante et al., , 2026)). However, other encephalographic studies examining evoked responses to stimuli suggest that even highly suppressed locations may undergo initial selection followed by speeded disengagement (D. H. Duncan et al., 2025; C. Huang et al., 2025). Resolving how proactive baseline shifts interact with reactive disengagement mechanisms remains a central debate in the field.

The above section has been primarily concerned with spatial learning of distractor location probabilities. Feature based suppression is addressed in the following section (feature-based attention). It is notable, however, that while spatial and feature based learned attention have been extensively and independently studied, it is also known that these two forms of suppression can interact, resulting in feature specific spatial suppression (D. H. Duncan et al., 2024; Failing et al., 2019). Despite the fact that objects in real life often have both feature and spatial regularities (Biederman, 1972), it remains an underexplored question how the interaction of spatial and feature learning bears out in the brain, and whether this combined form of learned suppression reflects both proactive and reactive mechanisms.

What is the neural locus of selection history?

Beyond mapping the temporal dynamics of suppression, determining where and how the brain instantiates these learned changes remains a central, related and unanswered question. On the one hand, selection history may primarily affect the initial feed-forward sweep of visual processing. One such feed-forward theory is the synaptic theory of learned attention (D. H. Duncan et al., 2023; Ferrante et al., 2018; van Moorselaar & Slagter, 2020), which proposes that learned spatial suppression arises via alterations in the latent synaptic state of the early visual system -selectively up-and down-weighting spatial locations in the early visual system such that information is passed up the visual processing hierarchy better at expected target location, and worse at expected distractor locations (D. H. Duncan et al., 2026).

Alternatively, rather than operating in the early visual cortex, suppression may be expressed subcortically in the hippocampus or basal ganglia -structures more commonly associated with statistical learning and habitual action (Ashby et al., 2010; Schapiro et al., 2014). Such deep structures have been implicated in non-attentional statistical learning tasks, such as sequence learning, navigation or automatic motor learning (Schapiro et al., 2013 (Schapiro et al., , 2017;; Stachenfeld et al., 2017; Yewbrey & Kornysheva, 2024). It is thus highly plausible that learned attention bias is coordinated by subcortical regions and fed back to visual areas.

Because standard EEG struggles to definitively localize deep subcortical sources, researchers must push the boundaries of multimodal imaging. Combining the high temporal resolution of EEG with the spatial precision of fMRI (Mayhew et al., 2013) offers the most promising route to resolving this debate. Future research must determine whether learned suppression is mediated by shifting local connectivity that redirects the feed-forward sweep of salience, or if it relies on feedback mechanisms from subcortical structures evaluating the incoming visual volley.

Future Directions

Unlike top-down and bottom-up attention, which are well-described by the biased competition theory (D. M. Beck & Kastner, 2009; Desimone & Duncan, 1995; Reynolds & Heeger, 2009), there is currently no unified neural theory for selection history. This presents a clear knowledge gap to be filled in the literature: understanding the neural characterization of selection history to match that of top-down and bottom-up attention. This section has highlighted two topics of special interest in the field currently; identifying learned attentional bias as proactive versus reactive in nature, and whether this bias originates in structural changes in subcortical learning systems or in the visual cortex itself -thus reflecting feedforward or feedback mechanisms in the brain. Importantly, these two questions are highly interrelated as a feed-forward mechanism is in principle more compatible with a proactive mechanism than a feedback mechanism. And conversely, a feed-back mechanism originating from subcortical regions (such as the thalamus or pulvinar) and exerting control back onto the early visual system is conceptually aligned with a reactive disengagement system. Thus, understanding how experience changes the neural priority map is essential for determining whether learning biases attention proactively, before the search display appears, or reactively, after the display has been encountered. More broadly, this question echoes the argument made by Awh et al. (2012), who showed that the traditional dichotomy between top-down and bottom-up control is insufficient to explain the full range of attentional behavior. In much the same way, current neural models of attention remain incomplete if they do not adequately incorporate selection history. Further research is therefore needed to bring neural accounts of attention in line with what is now widely accepted at the behavioral level: that prior selection experiences play a central role in shaping attentional priority.

Feature-Based Attention: A Question of Capacity and Suppression

Contributors: Norman Forschack, Chris Olivers, Jan Theeuwes

For decades, research has consistently implicated non-spatial feature space-such as color or motion direction-as a critical guide for attention (Anllo-Vento & Hillyard, 1996; Hillyard & Anllo-Vento, 1998; Hillyard & Münte, 1984; M. Müller et al., 2006; Treue & Trujillo, 1999). Based on seminal animal studies, Treue and colleagues suggested the feature similarity gain model (Treue, 2001; Treue & Trujillo, 1999), proposing that attentional selection of a feature facilitates all stimuli sharing the same feature throughout the visual field (global feature upweighting). In the last two decades, noninvasive imaging confirmed global feature-based attention in humans both with electroencephalographic recordings using frequency-tagged stimuli that evoke SSVEPs (S. K. Andersen et al., 2008 Andersen et al., , 2011) ) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (Boylan et al., 2023; Saenz et al., 2002; Serences & Boynton, 2007). A key paradigm is to present spatially superimposed Random Dot Kinematograms (RDKs), each having a different color and flickering at different frequencies. Human observers were instructed to shift attention to one of the RDKs and to respond to target events in the to-be-attended while ignoring such events in the to-be-ignored color. The typical finding is that attending a feature led to greater SSVEP power throughout the display than when not attending it (M. Müller et al., 2006). Work using this technique uncovered that global feature upweighting is obligatory (Gundlach et al., 2023), persists even when it conflicts with the task at hand (S. K. Andersen et al., 2013), and operates simultaneously throughout the visual field (Gundlach et al., 2023; but see S. K. Andersen & Hillyard, 2024). Importantly, the capacity to down-weight or suppress an unattended feature seems to be a local phenomenon and requires spatial competition in these designs (Forschack et al., 2017; Gundlach et al., 2022; M. M. Müller et al., 2018).

While the basic mechanisms of feature-based attention are now wellestablished, their generalization to visual search is a central focus of the field today. Particularly, there are twofoot_1 open questions in regard to the efficiency of search based on feature subsets (known as subset selective search whereby humans efficiently select certain sets of colors to restrict their search; Egeth et al., 1984; Kaptein et al., 1995): whether multiple features can be simultaneously upweighted, leading to multiple template search, and whether global facilitation can be inverted to suppress certain color features, especially when these features come in the form of salient singletons. Both of these questions involve the concept of an attentional template -a putative memory representation consisting of target related features that guides attention. Both of these questions seek to elucidate features of these putative templates; asking in turn whether they can be multiplied into multiple-template search, or inverted into negative templates.

How many features can we attend to simultaneously?

Directing attention towards a certain feature requires activation of an internal representation of that feature in visual working memory, often referred to as an attentional template (Carlisle et al., 2011; J. Duncan & Humphreys, 1992; Eimer, 2014; Olivers et al., 2011; Soto et al., 2008). Influential early accounts proposed that attentional guidance is restricted to a single active template at a time (Dombrowe et al., 2011; Houtkamp & Roelfsema, 2009; Olivers et al., 2011). Given that visual working memory can typically maintain three to four representations (Cowan, 2001), this would mean that the attention-guiding capacity is functionally dissociated from maintenance capacity. However, others have provided evidence that participants can guide search toward multiple features at once (S. K. Andersen et al., 2008; Barrett & Zobay, 2014; V. M. Beck et al., 2012; Berggren & Eimer, 2020; Christie et al., 2015; Grubert et al., 2016; Grubert & Eimer, 2016; Irons et al., 2012; Kerzel & Grubert, 2022; Kristjánsson & Kristjánsson, 2018; Moore & Weissman, 2010; Ort et al., 2019). Consequently, research has shifted toward understanding two fundamental questions: (i) at which stage observable capacity limitations emerge, and (ii) what determines the effective capacity of multiple-template search.

The first unresolved question concerns the stage at which capacity limitations arise. Frequency-tagging studies point to a high capacity for parallel processing, showing simultaneous enhancement for multiple attended colors (S. K. Andersen et al., 2013; Martinovic et al., 2018) and additive modulation across features (S. K. Andersen et al., 2008), suggesting no selection bottleneck before search begins. Other electrophysiological studies observe N2pc reductions and delays when multiple search templates are maintained, suggesting mutual inhibition between templates rather than serial processing (Grubert et al., 2016 (Grubert et al., , 2025;; Grubert & Eimer, 2015 , 2016; Jimenez et al., 2024; Z. Wang et al., 2025). Rapid Serial Probe Presentation (RSPP) paradigms confirm this by showing that N2pc responses to target-color probes reflecting preparatory template activation, were virtually identical during single-and two-color search (Grubert & Eimer, 2020 , 2023), demonstrating that costs do not originate from template preparation (Dodwell et al., 2026; Grubert & Eimer, 2018). However, behavioral costs do emerge after the search display appears, during target selection itself. Evidence from multivariate decoding indicates that while observers can prepare for two colors, attention struggles to access both these colors simultaneously in a display; once a matching stimulus triggers one feature set, it automatically inhibits the other, imposing a serial bottleneck during actual selection of the stimulus (Ort et al., 2019; c.f. L. Huang & Pashler, 2007).

If multiple templates can activate concurrently, the next question is what determines their effective capacity. Firstly, it's important to note that measuring the upper limits of template activation is complicated by methodological issues like trial averaging (Ort & Olivers, 2020) which can make it seem like multiple templates are maintained even if on a trial-by-trial basis only one item actually is. Second, different tasks may invoke different strategies, and observers may not always activate multiple templates even if they can. For example when observers can freely choose between two targets, observers exhibit signs of proactive control as indicated by (pre-trial betaband suppression in the EEG) with little performance costs, whereas when different targets are externally imposed, switches costs arise along with signatures of reactive control (via enhanced delta/theta signals suggesting a wait-and-see approach; van Driel et al., 2019). Additionally, if there is a temporal order for which template will be used in the upcoming trial structure, participants will simply strategically activate only the immediately relevant template to reduce memory load (Grubert et al., 2025; Grubert & Eimer, 2020). Because active templates mutually inhibit one another, adding templates progressively reduces individual activation strength (Kerzel & Grubert, 2022; Ort et al., 2019). This causes parallel guidance to become increasingly inefficient as more templates are added, eventually making serial switching a more effective search strategy.

Taken together, current evidence suggests that the capacity to prepare multiple feature representations substantially exceeds the capacity to use them efficiently during target selection. Future research must dissociate preparatory feature activation from target selection to determine whether search limits reflect a fixed capacity constraint or emerge gradually from competitive, inhibitory interactions between active representations.

Can global feature upweighting be inverted, suppressing the evoked activity of certain features?

The influential signal suppression hypothesis is largely a feature-based account of suppression. At its core is the assumption that specific feature signals can be proactively downregulated, in much the same way that feature-based attention can selectively upweight relevant color signals (Gaspelin et al., 2025, see the section on the role of learning in suppression for additional discussion). Recent work has shown that the global upweighting of expected target features is a proactive process, spreading to unattended areas of the screen prior to search target onset (Menceloglu et al., 2026). Signal suppression therefore proposes a reversal of this global upweighting, an inversion of this mechanism to proactively suppress certain feature signals before any visual input has been processed, thereby preventing their intrusion on attentional processing (Gaspelin et al., 2015; Gaspelin & Luck, 2018b).

Importantly, unlike feature guided search which can be dynamically altered on a trial-by-trial basis (and thus implicates working memory as a core function), signal suppression cannot enact itself on a trial-by-trial basis (Addleman & Störmer, 2022; Cunningham & Egeth, 2016; Moher & Egeth, 2012), but instead requires some amount of experience before suppression can kick in (Gaspelin et al., 2019; Savelson et al., 2025; Y. Zhang & Gaspelin, 2026). This has led proponents of signal suppression to rebrand the mechanism as a form of selection history (Gaspelin et al., 2025), relying more on long term memory and casting doubt on the proposal that signal suppression is an exact inversion of the well-established global upweighting effect.

Alternatively, it has been proposed that signal suppression may not reflect suppression at all but instead is simply the enhancement of features known to belong to the search target but not the distractor (such as color). The enhancement of expected target color may then masquerade as suppression if care is not taken in the experimental design. Oxner and colleagues (Oxner et al., 2023; Oxner, Forschack, et al., 2024) demonstrated that much of the feature suppression effect can in fact be accounted for by enhancement of the known expected target color -as evident by the abolition of probe suppression effects when target colors varied randomly rather than being fixed as is commonly done in paradigms studying signal suppression.

Importantly, feature based suppression is often studied both in heterogeneous displays (feature search mode) as well as homogeneous displays (saliency detection mode) with significant differences observed between these search modes (Kandemir et al., 2026; Treisman & Gelade, 1980; J. M. Wolfe, 2021). Suppression of color features in singleton detection mode is now a well-established learning mechanism (Stilwell et al., 2019), with Hanne et al. (2025) recently demonstrating that this learning leads to a reduced P D towards salient singletons that are a high probability distractor color. In feature search mode, participants are often faster when a salient distractor is on the screen, with different groups alternatively interpreting this global speedup as suppression (Gaspelin et al., 2015) or a side effect of inefficient serial search (de Waard & Theeuwes, 2026; Liesefeld & Müller, 2020). Importantly, EEG evidence in favor of a proactive suppression mechanism across search modes has leaned heavily on the presence of an early distractor P D thought to index proactive suppression (Gaspelin & Luck, 2018a; Stilwell et al., 2022). However, recent work has challenged the interpretation of an early P D as anything other than a simple salience signal (McDonald et al., 2026; Oxner et al., 2025; see section 2 for extended discussion). Forschack et al. (2022b Forschack et al. ( , 2022a) ) alternatively demonstrated using SSVEPs that attention is often first captured by these salient distractors, and only later suppressed, suggesting that learned suppression in this feature search mode may not be proactive in nature at all, but instead may represent a form of speeded disengagement from familiar distractor features (Itti & Koch, 2001; Theeuwes, 2010). This sequential account, in which attentional capture precedes rather than is prevented by suppression, finds neural support from feature-cuing designs. Temporal neural dynamics of color directed attention shifts revealed that facilitation of the target color and suppression of the tobe-ignored color do not operate on the same timescale, but instead exhibit a bi-phasic process, in which fast facilitation is followed by a temporally delayed suppression (S. Andersen & Müller, 2010; Gundlach et al., 2022 Gundlach et al., , 2023)). It remains a topic of debate whether feature suppression truly represents suppression rather than enhancement of target guided features, and further whether this modulation can truly be characterized as proactive rather than reactive (Liesefeld et al., 2024).

Notably, the above research has heavily focused on the role of color-based suppression in the visual system. This is due to the ease in which color manipulations are implemented in experimental paradigms, as well as the color-centric nature of the human visual system (with color information already present in the earliest phases of visual processing in the retina). Despite this, some work has highlighted that suppression may occur also for other feature dimensions, including shape (Kim et al., 2023) and motion (Adams & Gaspelin, 2024). Notably, recent work comparing color to motion distractors has observed clearly distinct neural patterns relating to each format of distractor (van Moorselaar et al., 2025), raising crucial questions about the generality of neural responses measured in studies often focused on color-based distractors. This calls for future work to investigate whether suppression of divergent features is handled by a single mechanism applied differentially across feature maps, or whether perhaps completely separate cognitive mechanisms exist for different feature dimensions.

Future Directions

The study of nonspatial attention remains an active and vital area of research, particularly regarding how the brain weights feature subsets to direct attention toward task-relevant characteristics and away from distracting ones. A central challenge within this domain is determining whether feature-based suppression exists as an independent cognitive mechanism-operating via the proactive or reactive downregulation of unwanted signals-or whether it simply reflects a relative change in priority driven by the selective enhancement of all other competing features. This debate is closely tied to the architectural constraints of working memory. While it is well established that humans can hold multiple items in working memory simultaneously (Luck & Vogel, 1997), it remains a mystery why guiding attention via multiple target templates is so difficult to observe, and whether negative templates can actively suppress distracting features rather than merely upweighting competing ones. Resolving these questions and identifying the cognitive bottlenecks that limit the concurrent use of multiple templates remains critical for a complete account of nonspatial attentional selection.

Out of the lab and into the world: Searching for real world objects in natural scenes

Võ Historically, the encephalographic study of attention has relied heavily on simplified, static 2D displays utilizing artificial stimuli such as isolated singletons or arrays of basic shapes and colors (Eckstein, 2011; J. Wolfe, 2023). Usually, participants are required to remain still, maintain central fixation, and respond to briefly presented stimuli with a button press -constraints that are methodologically necessary because eye movements create artifacts in EEG data (Lins et al., 1993), but are ultimately conceptually restrictive. While such designs afford precise experimental control, they poorly approximate the structure of real-world environments where humans act as active samplers of their sensory environment (Zelinsky et al., 1997). Rather than being driven solely by incoming stimulation, observers continuously regulate what information is encountered in the first place. For example, eye movements can be directed away from distracting or irrelevant stimuli to attenuate their sensory consequences (Abeles & Yuval-Greenberg, 2021), and gaze trajectories themselves reflect strategic avoidance of known distractor locations (Van der Stigchel & Theeuwes, 2006). Moreover, visual selection in the real world is tightly coupled to overt action. Searching for an object in real life often involves coordinated eye, head, and body movements, as well as manual interactions such as reaching, grasping, or repositioning items. These actions do not merely select relevant information, they also actively exclude alternative sources of input by determining what is fixated, approached, or manipulated, and what is left uninspected. As a result, attentional selection in natural environments is inherently embodied and sequential, unfolding through cycles of sampling and action that are largely absent from traditional, fixation-based, "sit-in-front-of-a-computer" paradigms.

This creates an important gap between laboratory models of attentional selection and the active sampling that characterizes natural vision.

While classic paradigms have been undeniably instrumental in uncovering the core neural mechanisms of attention, a persistent question is to what degree findings in these highly controlled environments reflect attentional processing in the real world. It is possible that the reliance on artificial stimuli and setups leaves core attentional mechanisms opaque which would be apparent if attention was studied in more realworld environments using naturalistic stimuli. Already, research has shown that naturalistic stimuli are processed differently from artificial stimuli, both when examining behavior (Biederman, 1972; Li et al., 2002) and neural processing (S. V. David et al., 2004; Kisker et al., 2025; Marini et al., 2019). This serves as ever-present motivation for fundamental vision researchers to expand their research to use naturalistic stimuli to observe how neural responses differ from viewing synthetic stimuli and potentially unlock new layers of neural processing that would be otherwise obscured.

Luckily, the research program to study the neural correlates of natural vision has been buoyed by the many recent advances in techniques and technology that has allowed researchers to confidently interpret patterns of brain activity while participants make free eye movements and attend to real world objects (Dimigen, 2020; Fakche et al., 2024; Sulewski et al., 2026). While in principle, attention research using real-world stimuli can still occur in the experimental cubicle, perhaps the greatest driver of attentional research in real-world search is technical advances in mobile EEG (mEEG) allowing researchers to move away from the chinrest and observe attentional processing live in the real (or virtual) world.

Because the ultimate goal of attention research is to understand and predict when and where people attend in the real world, advances made possible by combining mEEG with eye tracking provide an important bridge between fundamental research on attention and its application to naturalistic environments. These novel frontiers also encourage new research questions that differ from their more traditional, laboratorybound counterparts.

Understanding how the grammar of scenes is stored in the brain and utilized in search

In controlled lab tasks, often stimuli are presented in a truly random configuration from trial to trial. This does not reflect the structure of the real world, where patterns govern the placement of things such that visual attention is often aided by a rich history of experience which helps to guide attention in an optimal pattern. This intrinsic statistical structure has been the subject of great research interest in the past decade in the form of selection history effects in synthetic displays (B. A. Anderson et al., 2021; Theeuwes et al., 2022). By embedding patterns in a search task, participants effectively and automatically encode these patterns to improve search performance.

But real-world regularities that guide search are far more complicated than the simple caricature presented in most visual statistical learning studies. Often these regularities are not so simple as a retinotopic high probability location that persists for several minutes, but instead are intricate patterns of embedded knowledge that guides search in a structure akin to the structure of language (Biederman, 1976). The "scene grammar" framework (Draschkow & Vo, 2017; Võ, 2021; Võ et al., 2019) captures how attention is guided in everyday life by patterns learned through a lifetime of experience. For instance, when searching for a toothbrush in a bathroom one may automatically retrieve the knowledge that toothbrushes are often found on top of the sink and automatically direct their search towards this visible "anchor". According to this framework, scenes are structured hierarchically in that a scene can be decomposed in meaningful, functionally optimized clusters of objects known as "phrases." At the heart of these phrases, anchors predict not only the identity but also the location of other local objects. Scene grammar therefore allows the human attention system to ignore vast swaths of a visual scene (such as a shower or toilet "phrase" when looking for a toothbrush) and limit the search space strictly to the relevant anchor and its phrase (Kaiser et al., 2019). This top-down conceptualization of attention moves away from the feed forward sweep of attention that has often been the subject of study in artificial stimuli to more complex multistep search which better characterizes attentional guidance in real life.

Naturalistic search is often said to comprise of selective and non-selective processing pathways, meant to capture how attention is guided by scene gist knowledge retrieved from episodic memory and unrelated to actual current features of the visual scene, that guide selective attention (J. M. Wolfe et al., 2011). Extant work has robustly established the N400 as a marker of violations of scene grammar rules (Draschkow et al., 2018; Nicholls et al., 2025; Võ & Wolfe, 2013; for a review, see Lauer & Võ, 2022), reliably capturing violations in this nonselective pathway. Furthermore, the P D ERP, a marker of suppression, effectively captures how gist guidance affects the very early guidance of visual search, constraining the search space before target presence can be processed (Hickey et al., 2019). However, the deeper question of how these rules are stored and accessed dynamically to guide visual search mere milliseconds after the first feedforward sweep of attention remains considerably underexplored. Thus, the use of scene grammar during visual search reflects an interplay between perception, memory, and attention that is still poorly understood. Utilizing mEEG in combination with eye tracking as well as multivariate analyses may provide a powerful way to examine how learned regularities are retrieved and implemented during naturalistic search, allowing researchers to track both where observers look and how the brain prepares, guides, and updates attentional priorities in real time.

How do search templates handle real-world variability and affordances?

It is commonly presumed that feature guided search involves the loading of a feature template into working memory, with subsequent search essentially being the serial loading and comparing of visual percepts against this putative template. This is often reflected by canonical search tasks where a certain synthetic stimulus is presented as the search target and participants know with 100% certainty how this item will look in the upcoming search trials (J. M. Wolfe, 1994; J. M. Wolfe et al., 2011). In contrast to this, objects in the real world differ in at least two major dimensions -firstly their presentation varies considerably such that a coffee mug can appear in any myriad of shapes depending on the angle from which it is viewed, and various retinal sizes depending on whether it is located nearby or far away. Despite this template variability, attentional guidance towards 'mugs' is quite efficient, showing resilience to perceptual variability and a seeming awareness of a three-dimensional structure underlying the two-dimensional percept (Yu et al., 2023). This reflects a form of categorical search (where the participant is given a vague search template rather than the exact visual appearance of the upcoming search target; Castelhano et al., 2008; Yang & Zelinsky, 2009) and broadly speaking, this approach better approximates the ambiguity inherent in real world object search. Secondly, real-world objects are often searched for with explicit goals for interaction. This means that the affordances of an object are particularly important during viewing, because visual information is not collected merely for identification, but also to guide subsequent motor interactions with the object. Recent behavioral and fMRI work suggests that search templates for real-world objects in naturalistic scenes are highly dynamic, and may simultaneously combine context-dependent features (e.g., adjusting expected size based on viewing distance) with context-invariant features that may generalize across searches, such as object orientation (Gayet et al., 2024; Gayet & Peelen, 2022; Lerebourg et al., 2026; Reeder & Peelen, 2013) or action plans (Trentin et al., 2024). A crucial open question for M/EEG research is how these multiple representational levels operate temporally. Future work aims to leverage M/EEG in combination with Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) to track, in real time, the evolving representational content of these search templates during preparation and detection, uncovering how the brain juggles abstraction and specificity on the fly.

Furthermore, an often-underappreciated feature of visual search in the real world is that the current target of search is often not actually present in sight, but the larger act of 'searching' often requires several steps to locate the current goal targets. For instance, searching for a spoon may guide attention to a spoon on a counter, or otherwise it may guide attention towards a drawer which needs to be pulled open and only then will reveal a set of silverware. These multi-step searches that integrate motor demands are ubiquitous in everyday life, and describes a format of search that is nearly never studied in experimental settings (E. David & Vo, 2022). Paradigms utilizing VR setups have proven especially promising in this regard, as attention is connected to behavior in more complex investigations into decision making (Duarte & Geng, 2024). Leveraging recent developments in mEEG along with multivariate approaches able to separate neural processes from noisy data promises to offer new avenues for studying the complex relationship between attention and decision making in such complex tasks. Such research programs promise to better capture the role attention plays in everyday life.

Future Directions

Basic research into the mechanisms underlying attentional selection has generated important theories that help explain search behavior. Much of this work has relied on simplified stimuli and two-dimensional displays, allowing researchers to isolate core mechanisms under tightly controlled conditions that do not permit active movement. It is now increasingly possible to test these theories in more naturalistic settings, for example by examining the neural correlates of attention while participants view, search, and act within virtual or augmented reality environments.

Pivoting toward more naturalistic stimuli inevitably requires researchers to confront the increased variability and noise that such settings introduce. Fortunately, methodological advances have made it increasingly possible to extract meaningful information from these richer but noisier data. Particularly important are recent developments in multivariate analysis techniques (the increased use of representational similarity analysis with EEG). These developing approaches allow researchers to move beyond traditional, often noisy, ERP components by decomposing the EEG response into increasingly refined underlying (theoretical) representations and further to relate these multivariate responses to the activations of artificial vision models (e.g., convolutional neural networks). In parallel, advances in linear mixedeffects modeling have improved the interpretability of complex data by better accounting for variability across participants, stimuli, trials, and contexts.

Conclusion

Although Hans Berger is widely regarded as the founding father of EEG recording following his first report of the human electroencephalogram in 1929 (İnce et al., 2021; Mushtaq et al., 2024), the systematic use of EEG to study visual attention began several decades later. Building on earlier work by Robert Eason and colleagues (1969), Steven Hillyard and colleagues demonstrated and popularized the method of measuring EEG to observe how attention modulates visual processing in the human brain (Luck & Hillyard, 1994a , 1994b; G. R. Mangun & Hillyard, 1991; Van Voorhis & Hillyard, 1977). Since then, the study of electrical brain activity in relation to visual attention has become a lively and highly productive field of research. Over the past 55 years, electroencephalographic methods have provided a unique window into the neural mechanisms of visual attention, revealing patterns of brain activity that would have been difficult, if not impossible, to observe without a temporally precise measure of neural processing.

The international symposium "Advances in the Encephalographic Study of Attention" held in the summer of 2025 provided a snapshot into some of the topics and techniques that may come to define the coming decades of attention research using electro-and magneto-encephalographic measurements. Exciting technical advances were paired with theoretical developments all highlighting persistent mysteries of the human attention system and how researchers hope to expand our knowledge of it in the future. Moving forward, the future of M/EEG research continues to be promising as the preferred technique for capturing the brain dynamics that underlie the rapid mechanisms of visual attention.
