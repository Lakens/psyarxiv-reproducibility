Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Understanding conditional inferences is fundamental to human reasoning, allowing us to evaluate possibilities and predict outcomes. For instance, the conditional, "If you finish your broccoli, you'll get a candy" can be interpreted literally, meaning eating broccoli is one way to get a candy, or pragmatically, implying it is the only way. Past studies find school-aged children (ages 7-12) struggle to arrive at literal meanings but, interestingly, compute adult-like, pragmatic interpretations at this age. An important limitation of past research is the lack of testing in contexts that support literal meanings. We conducted two studies to examine whether children can derive literal interpretations when supported by context. We found that preschoolers as young as 4 years old are adult-like in computing literal meanings when they are able to judge that the antecedent is not the only way to bring about the consequent, either because discourse introduces salient alternatives (Experiment 1) or because the stated antecedent is not causally related to the consequent (Experiment 2). These findings suggest that preschoolers have the logical ability and processing resources required to make conditional inferences when provided with contextual support.

[div-01]

Conditional reasoning is a fundamental aspect of human cognition. For children, conditional inference is important not only to the development of logical reasoning (Kuhn, 1977; Ward & Overton, 1990), but also supports learning about causal relations (Gopnik et al., 2001; Kushnir & Gopnik, 2007), understanding rules and conventions (Harris & Núñez, 1996), thinking about possibilities (Grigoroglou & Ganea, 2022), and understanding future hypotheticals and counterfactuals (Beck et al., 2006; Rafetseder & Perner, 2012; Riggs et al., 1998). For example, consider a child who hears their parents say, "If you finish your broccoli, you'll get a candy." The child might conclude that eating broccoli is the only path to the reward, and therefore interpret the conditional as "if and only if p, then q." Alternatively, the child might take the statement literally, concluding that although eating broccoli is one way to get candy, it might also be obtained in other ways, such as tidying their room, finishing homework, and so on.

While previous research establishes that adults can access these different interpretations, studies in children, using different methods, conclude that they often do not 1 . In the present study, we investigate how preschoolers interpret conditionals and specifically whether they are capable of accessing both of these interpretations.

Multiple previous studies have investigated the development of conditional reasoning in school-aged children (7-to 12-year-olds), and have found that they generally struggle to arrive at literal meanings of conditional statements (Barrouillet & Lecas, 2002; Barrouillet et al., 2008; De Neys & Everaerts, 2008; Di Bacco, 2018; Gauffroy & Barrouillet, 2009; Daniel & Klaczynski, 2006; Klaczynski et al., 2004; though see Rumain et al., 1983). Our use of literal and pragmatic instead follows the tradition in formal semantics and pragmatics. The literal interpretation corresponds to the logical, truth-conditional meaning of "if p then q", while the pragmatic interpretation involves a strengthened, biconditional reading "if and only if p then q," often arising from conversational reasoning about relevance and alternatives.

(2016) presented participants with conditionals such as, "If the circle is yellow, then the star is black," followed by a test statement like, "The circle is green, and the star is black." Although this statement is literally true, children often rejected it, suggesting a biconditional interpretation ("if and only if"). Importantly, children's performance in tasks like this differs according to a variety of factors, including the structure of the task (e.g., truth-table evaluation vs. conditional inference) and the type of conditional involved (e.g., abstract, category-based, causal; Gauffroy & Barrouillet, 2009; Janveau-Brennan & Markovits, 1999; Markovits, 2017). In general, however, there is a consensus that school-aged children struggle to compute the literal meanings of conditionals and are less proficient logical reasoners, relative to adults.

To explain these difficulties with conditional reasoning, some accounts point to limited logical competence or immature representational capacity, suggesting that young children may lack the abstract reasoning abilities required to engage with conditional structure (e.g., Byrnes & Overton, 1986; Inhelder & Piaget, 1958; Braine & O'Brien, 1998). A second class of accounts emphasizes working memory limitations and argue that children's difficulty stems from their inability to maintain both the conditional structure and its potential alternatives in mind, a capacity that is claimed to reach maturity only in adolescence (Barrouillet & Lecas, 1999; Gauffroy & Barrouillet, 2009; Johnson-Laird & Byrne, 1991). A third possibility is that children have both the logical and processing capacities needed to interpret conditional statements, but struggle to spontaneously generate and reason about hypothetical antecedents that might bring about the consequent (Markovits, 2000).

Consistent with this third hypothesis, several previous studies report that children as young as 7 years of age are more likely to interpret conditionals literally when they can easily generate alternative antecedents (e.g., Cummins et al., 1991; Cummins, 1995; Markovits et al., 2016; Markovits et al., 1996; 1998; Markovits, 2000; Rumain et al., 1983). For example, 8-year-olds can provide literal meanings with conditionals that link familiar categories and properties (e.g., "If it is a dog, then it has four legs"), likely because there are many alternative animals with four legs that come to mind easily (Markovits et al., 1996). Relatedly, children are more likely to provide literal meanings when alternative antecedents are more strongly associated with the consequent than the stated antecedent. For example, Quinn and Markovits (1998) found that 12-year-olds were more likely to interpret, "If a dog has skin disease, then it will scratch constantly" literally, than a statement like, "If a dog has fleas, then it will scratch constantly," which they were more likely to interpret biconditionally. On their account, in the first case the association between skin disease and scratching is weaker, making it easier to think of more strongly associated alternative antecedents like "If a dog has fleas," because most older children know that fleas are a common cause of scratching in dogs. However, in the second case, where the association between fleas and scratching is stronger, alternatives are more difficult to generate because children are less familiar with other causes of scratching in dogs, leading them to endorse the conclusion that the dog will not scratch constantly if it does not have fleas.

Crucially, while such findings rely on children's semantic knowledge of which things have four legs, or what causes dogs to scratch, other work finds that similar processes apply to contextually-specified alternatives. For example, Rumain et al. (1983) found that when 7-year-olds heard multiple conditionals with the same consequent (e.g., "If there is a dog in the box, then there is an orange"; "If there is a tiger in the box, then there is an orange"), they interpreted statements literally, since multiple antecedents could produce the same consequent.

Rumain et al. argued that children's ability to interpret conditional statements depends critically

on representing what they called the "logic of the conversation", i.e., the broader discourse structure that gives conditionals their meaning.

The idea that conditional inferences depend on reasoning about alternatives is compatible with a broader theoretical framework found in pragmatic accounts of adult language comprehension. Since Grice (1975), researchers have argued that conditional statements feature both a literal and a pragmatic meaning, and that selecting between these is sensitive to contextual availability of alternative utterances (e.g., Cummins et al., 1991; Cummins, 1995). Interestingly, while adults can access literal meanings of conditionals when required by context, like children they often prefer a stronger "if and only if" meaning, an inference sometimes referred to as "Conditional Perfection" (Cornulier, 1983; Evcen & Barner, 2025; Geis & Zwicky, 1971; Horn, 2000; Levinson, 2000; van der Auwera, 1997; von Fintel, 2001). On most accounts, these "perfected" interpretations do not reflect a deficit in reasoning, but are the product of a pragmatic inference based on what the speaker chose to say. For example, adults generally interpret a conditional like, "If you finish your broccoli, you'll get a candy," to mean that broccoli is the only way to get a candy, because they reason that if the speaker knew of other ways to get candy (e.g., if you tidy up your toys; if you are a good kid; if you finish your homework), then they would have said so (as per the Gricean Maxim of Quantity). Since the speaker didn't mention these alternatives, they must not believe that they are true (i.e., if they believed that it were possible to get candy by tidying up the toys, then they would have mentioned this; Cornulier, 1983; van der Auwera, 1997). Thus, they derive an inference that corresponds to the conclusion that, "You'll get candy only if you finish your broccoli." Recent studies suggest that adults adopt a perfected meaning of conditionals by default (Evcen & Barner, 2025; van Tiel & Schaeken, 2017), and that they only compute literal, non-perfected, meanings under very particular contextual conditions, such as when there are contextually salient alternative antecedents that might bring about a consequent (Farr, 2011; von Fintel, 2001).

The idea that children's conditional reasoning hinges on the ability to reason about alternative antecedents also raises the possibility that such inferences might be found in children much younger than 7 or 8. Children begin to produce conditional sentences in speech by at least 3 years of age (Bowerman, 1986; Scholnick & Wing, 1991), and exhibit the ability to reason about multiple, alternative, possibilities by at least age 4. For example, previous studies find that preschoolers make sophisticated causal inferences that require them to compare events, track covariation, and intervene to test competing hypotheses (e.g., Gopnik & Tenenbaum, 2007; Griffiths et al., 2011; Sobel & Munro, 2009). These findings suggest that preschoolers reason about possibilities by integrating prior knowledge with the available evidence (Xu & Kushnir, 2013; also see Denison & Xu, 2019, for early sensitivity during infancy). By age five, they engage in counterfactual reasoning, and consider how alternative antecedent events might have led to different possible outcomes (Harris et al., 1996; Nyhout & Ganea, 2019; Nyhout et al., 2019; Rafetseder et al., 2010; cf. Beck & Riggs, 2014). Preschoolers also engage in disjunctive reasoning, and draw inferences like "not A, therefore B" from statements such as "A or B" (Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2018; Gautam et al., 2021; Mody & Carey, 2016), which requires tracking and eliminating alternatives (see also Alderete & Xu, 2023; Leahy et al., 2022, for evidence from modal possibility reasoning). Finally, preschoolers reason about alternative utterances to interpret quantifiers (Skordos & Papafragou, 2016; Skordos et al., 2022), number words (Barner & Bachrach, 2010; Marušič et al., 2021), and disjunction (Chierchia et al., 2001; Hochstein et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2016; Skordos et al., 2020 Skordos et al., , 2022;; Tieu et al., 2017). For example, when told that "some of the toys are in the box," 5-year-olds infer that not all of them are, when the alternative statement "all of the toys are in the box" is contextually available (Skordos & Papafragou, 2016).

Such findings make it plausible that preschoolers, like older children and adults, can interpret conditionals literally when the context supports it. In the present study we asked whether their interpretations are sensitive to the meaning of the conditional itself, reporting two experiments that each test a case in which a literal reading is felicitous and expected. In Experiment 1, this was achieved by making alternative antecedents available in the context, akin to Rumain et al. (1983): children saw a scene in which both a boy and a girl went shopping for toys, and were given evidence that the girl had toys in her basket, but were told either (1) "If the boy buys toys, the cat will find toys in the box" or (2) "The cat will find toys in the box only if the boy buys toys." Unlike plain conditionals, which are semantically compatible with a literal interpretation but often pragmatically perfected, "only if" constructions grammatically encode necessity: "The cat will find toys in the box only if the boy buys toys" entails that the box will be empty if the boy does not buy toys, without any pragmatic inference required. We reasoned that if children treat these sentences equivalently, i.e., interpreting the consequent as true only when the antecedent is true, then they should judge that the cat will not find toys in either case, regardless of what the girl bought -i.e., the availability of contextual alternatives should have no impact in either case. If, however, they are able to access a literal meaning for the plain conditional, they should recognize that the girl's toys provide an alternative antecedent and respond accordingly, and should do so selectively for standard conditionals but not for "only if" conditionals.

In Experiment 2, we tested cases where perfection is generally not warrantedsometimes called Biscuit Conditionals (Austin, 1961; Biezma & Goebel, 2023; Franke, 2009). In such statements, the truth of the consequent does not obviously depend on the truth of the antecedent, and therefore remains true even if the antecedent is false. For example, in the statement, "If you are hungry, there are biscuits in the cupboard," the listener understands that there are biscuits in the cupboard regardless of whether they are hungry. Therefore, such sentences are not susceptible to perfection, since under normal circumstances it would be infelicitous to infer that there are biscuits in the cupboard only if the listener is hungry. The absence of a strong causal connection makes the antecedent less likely to be interpreted as the sole condition for the consequent, and more likely that almost any alternative antecedent could bring it about. Consequently, adults accept these statements even when the antecedent is false and the consequent is true. We asked whether preschoolers, like adults, would also assign literal meanings to these statements, or if instead they would persist in interpreting them biconditionally to mean, e.g., "If and only if you are hungry, there are biscuits in the cupboard." Again, we reasoned that if children interpret conditionals as biconditionals, they should reject the consequent when the antecedent is false. However, if they are able to adjust their interpretation based on the discourse context and the speaker's intent, they should access literal meanings, endorsing the consequent despite the false antecedent rather than perfecting the conditional.

Experiment 1

In Experiment 1, we investigated children's ability to derive literal meanings of conditionals when the context specified that multiple alternative antecedents might bring about the consequent, akin to Rumain et al. (1983), but using a simpler method that was friendly to preschool-aged children. We reasoned that if children interpret conditionals as biconditionals, the presence of a contextual alternative antecedent should not affect their judgments, and their judgments should be identical to those in an "only if" condition. By contrast, if children are able to access a literal meaning, and do not assign an "only if" meaning, then the contextual availability of an alternative antecedent should lead them to endorse the consequent even when the stated antecedent is false. To test this, we compared plain conditionals, presented with or without contextual alternatives, to "only if" conditionals presented with alternatives.

In all three conditions, children heard a story in which the characters went shopping for toys for their friend, a cat. In the No-Alternatives condition, only one character (e.g., the boy) went to the toy store. Then, children heard a conditional statement such as, "If the boy buys toys, the cat will find toys in the box." This was followed by the crucial information that the boy did not, in fact, buy any toys. Children then saw the cat searching for toys and were asked whether it would find toys in the box or not. Here we expected children to perfect the conditional, reasoning that since the antecedent is false, the consequent must be false as well. The Alternatives condition differed in one respect: both a boy and a girl went shopping, and children saw that the girl already had a toy in her shopping basket, suggesting that she had bought one. Children then heard the same plain conditional as in the No-Alternatives condition. Here, a literal interpretation predicts that the cat will find toys, since the girl's purchase offers an alternative way for toys to end up in the box.

Finally, in the Alternatives with Only condition, the same two-character scene was paired with an "only if" conditional (e.g., "The cat will find toys in the box only if the boy buys toys").

Because "only if" grammatically encodes necessity, we predicted that children would reject the consequent here even though the girl's basket contained toys, in contrast to the literal "yes" expected for plain conditionals in the Alternatives condition.

Methods

All methods and analyses were preregistered prior to data collection. The No-Alternatives and Alternatives conditions were preregistered and tested together (https://osf.io/ujdep/?view_only=40268f4858a443429ded6ad4d3115300). The Alternatives with Only condition was preregistered separately and tested afterward (https://osf.io/kpngh/?view_only=1e703c84a2414650b44ce1be9c98a189), with a new sample of children. The three conditions used the same procedure and visual materials and differed only in the linguistic details described below, so we report them together. All data and analysis code for both experiments are available at https://osf.io/e6a2h/?view_only=2145f397f33544dc86b7ab2fdcfe3543.

Participants

We recruited a total of 97 children between the ages of 4;0 and 5;11 (M = 4;11), recruited from preschools and a children's museum in the United States. All participants spoke English as their primary language. Across both preregistered studies, seven children were excluded for failing two or more of three attention checks, leaving a final sample of 90 children. Children were tested in one of three conditions: No-Alternatives (n = 30), Alternatives (n = 30), and Alternatives with Only (n = 30). We also tested 45 adult participants (n = 15 per condition) via the online research platform Prolific.co, who completed the same task as a control group for all three conditions.

Materials and Procedure

Children were tested in person using an animated slide deck, and adults were tested online using a narrated video of the same materials. Each participant completed one of three between-subjects conditions: No-Alternatives, Alternatives, or Alternatives with Only (see Fig. 1). In all versions of the task, an animal character was introduced alongside a box where items could be placed for the animal to find later. Each trial featured a different story, with different child characters, a different animal, and a different set of objects (e.g., a cat looking for toys, a rabbit looking for carrots), so that no two trials involved the same characters, box, or items.

Before analyzing the critical trials, we verified that participants understood the conditional relations. Discussion

CONDITIONAL REASONING IN PRESCHOOLERS

In Experiment 1, children gave more literal "yes" responses in the Alternatives condition than in the No-Alternatives or Alternatives with Only conditions. This suggests that children as young as four, like adults, are able to differentiate a grammatically obligatory "only if" meaning from the meaning of a plain conditional, and can access a literal meaning when supported by context.

Interestingly, although children overall interpreted "only if" conditionals as expected, some did not arrive at the expected interpretation. This suggests that some children in this condition -and likely also in the Alternatives condition -overlooked the semantics of the conditional statement, and instead based their judgments on the contextual availability of toys.

Even so, this appears to be true only for a subset of children, since in the Alternatives with Only condition they perfected conditionals more often than chance, and more often than in the Alternatives condition (see Supplementary Materials, Fig. S2.1). We found that children as young as four can interpret conditionals literally when alternative antecedents are made salient in context. To test whether they can also do so in the absence of such cues, we next turned to a different paradigm where literal interpretations are expected not simply because of salient alternatives, but because of the conditional's discourse function. This provided converging evidence from a structurally different paradigm involving non-causal, "biscuit" conditionals, where a literal reading is felicitous and natural in conversation.

Experiment 2

In order to strengthen the evidence for the analysis provided in Experiment 1, we sought converging evidence for children's ability to access literal meanings of conditionals in Experiment 2. Most prior work, including our study in Experiment 1, has tested children on conditionals where a non-literal pragmatic meaning is either plausible or even preferred by adults. Few studies, however, have tested children's ability to derive literal meanings in contexts where they are also preferred by adults. Consequently, previous findings that children fail to access literal meanings may reflect their predisposition to favor default adult-like meanings, particularly when tested with sparse contextual support, or abstract stimuli. To address this, we tested children's interpretation of conditionals for which adults generally endorse a literal meaning. In particular, we asked whether children could interpret conditionals literally when there was no clear causal link between the antecedent and the consequent. Previous studies find that adults are more likely to adopt literal interpretations when the antecedent introduces the relevance of the consequent but is not itself a cause, as in statements like, "If you're hungry, there are biscuits in the cupboard" (Evcen & Barner, 2025). Similar to Experiment 1, participants were told short stories featuring two characters and a box, followed by a conditional statement that was either Causal (e.g., If the girl bakes cookies, the boy will find cookies in the box) or Non-Causal (e.g., If the boy wants to read, he will find books in the box). In critical trials, the antecedent was explicitly denied (e.g., "The girl didn't bake cookies", or "The boy doesn't want to read"), and participants were asked whether the consequent still held (e.g., "Do you think the boy will find cookies/books in the box or not?"). These trials tested whether children would interpret the conditionals pragmatically, rejecting the consequent when the antecedent was false, or literally, endorsing the consequent regardless of the antecedent's truth. If children interpret all conditionals as biconditionals, they should reject the consequent in both conditions. However, if they are able to adjust their interpretation based on the discourse context and the speaker's intent, they should respond pragmatically in the causal condition and literally in the non-causal condition.

Methods

In each trial, participants were told a short story involving two characters and a box. In the Causal Conditional condition, participants heard a conditional statement (e.g., If the girl bakes cookies, the boy will find cookies in the box), followed by one of three context types. In Control-Yes trials, the antecedent was affirmed (e.g., The girl baked cookies), and participants were asked about the consequent (e.g., Do you think the boy will find cookies in the box?). In Control-No trials, the story denied the consequent (e.g., There are no cookies in the box), and participants were asked about the antecedent (e.g., Do you think the girl baked cookies or not?).

In Critical trials, the antecedent was explicitly denied (e.g., The girl didn't bake cookies), and participants were asked about the consequent (e.g., Do you think the boy will find cookies in the box or not?). These critical trials allowed for either a literal interpretation ("yes") or a pragmatic interpretation involving conditional perfection ("no") (see Fig. 3, Table S1.2).

In the Non-Causal Conditional condition, participants heard conditionals in which the antecedent was causally unrelated to the consequent (e.g., "If the boy wants to read, he will find books in the box"). The trial structure paralleled the Causal Conditionals condition, except that Control-No trials were omitted, as presenting an empty box would contradict the truth of the conditional statement. Thus, participants in this condition received Control-Yes trials, in which the antecedent was affirmed (e.g., "The boy wants to read") and participants were asked about the consequent (e.g., "Do you think the boy will find books in the box?"), and Critical trials, where the antecedent was explicitly denied (e.g., "The boy doesn't want to read"), and told that the girl wants to read. Then, they were asked about the consequent (e.g., "Do you think the girl will find books in the box or not?"). These critical trials tested whether participants would continue to endorse the consequent when the antecedent was false, consistent with a literal interpretation, or reject it, consistent with a pragmatic reading (see Fig. 3, Table S1.2). Discussion

In Experiment 2 we found that both children and adults were more likely to interpret Non-Causal Conditionals literally relative to Causal Conditionals, regardless of age. In the Non-Causal Conditionals condition, participants across all age groups consistently gave literal responses at rates above chance. By contrast, participants generally favored pragmatic, perfected meanings for Causal Conditionals, though in this case 4-and 5-year-olds were not fully adult-like, and were actually more likely to endorse literal meanings than adults (see Supplementary Materials, Figure S2.2, which shows that this difference reflects a small number of younger children who responded literally on every trial, rather than a systematic change with age). These findings suggest that children can flexibly interpret conditionals both literally and pragmatically, depending on the context.

General Discussion

We asked whether preschoolers can access both the literal and pragmatic meanings of conditional statements when context provides appropriate support. Across two experiments, we found converging evidence that they can. In Experiment 1, children as young as four interpreted conditionals literally when potential alternative antecedents were made salient in the discourse, and they distinguished "if" from "only if" statements, suggesting that they do not treat conditionals as biconditionals. In Experiment 2, children again responded literally when the antecedent and consequent were causally unrelated, i.e., in contexts where adults themselves favor a literal reading. These results suggest that even preschool-aged children can compute both the literal and pragmatic meanings of conditional statements. Rather than defaulting to one interpretation, they chose the meaning that was most natural in context. When a literal interpretation was felicitous, children interpreted conditionals literally, and when the discourse or sentence structure supported a pragmatic reading, they responded biconditionally.

As noted in the Introduction, some previous studies attribute older children's difficulties with conditionals to limitations in logical competence or processing capacity. Whereas our study was inspired by recent investigations of conversational reasoning, which emphasize the interpretation of utterances in conversational context, earlier studies often investigated conditional inference as a type of decontextualized logical ability, in which children are assessed using paper-and-pencil tasks, and are asked about relations between arbitrary stimuli. For example, in an effort to prevent children from relying on real-world causal knowledge (e.g., "if it rains, the streets will get wet"), some previous studies have probed judgments about arbitrary relations between abstract elements like shapes, colors, or letters, often without any supportive context (e.g., "if there is a triangle, then there is a circle"). Abstract stimuli such as these may be harder to represent in working memory, given the need to simultaneously track multiple properties and their combinations to support reasoning. Adding to this, some previous tasks have required formal meta-linguistic judgments about truth, relevance, or logical structure involving multi-step inferential chains, which contrast with the ways in which conditional statements are generally encountered by children in naturalistic conversation. Given these considerations, it is possible that domain-general factors like working memory and metacognitive reasoning are critical predictors of performance in these more abstract tests of conditional reasoning, even if they are not as critical to explaining the comprehension of conditional statements in richer conversational contexts (see Markovits & Vachon, 1990; Markovits, 2000; Markovits et al., 2016 for further discussion).

Although children in our study were able to compute literal meanings for conditionals, they nevertheless differ from adults in one important respect. Whereas adults often arrive at literal meanings by generating alternative antecedents from their own world knowledge (e.g., Cummins et al., 1991; Cummins, 1995), children appear to struggle in identifying which alternatives are relevant in a given context and require that alternatives be provided for them contextually (e.g., Bale & Barner, 2013; Barner et al., 2011; Chierchia et al., 2001; Foppolo et al., 2012; Skordos & Barner, 2019; Skordos et al., 2022; Tieu et al., 2017). On some views, interpreting a conditional requires identifying the speaker's intended Question Under Discussion (QUD): the implicit question that the speaker's utterance is meant to answer (Roberts, 1996 (Roberts, /2012)). A single utterance can be consistent with several QUDs, each making different alternatives salient. For example, when the QUD is "What happens if the boy buys toys?", the discourse highlights the antecedent and invites the listener to consider different possible outcomes: perhaps the cat will find toys in the box, perhaps it won't, or perhaps something else will happen instead. In contrast, when the QUD is "When will the cat find toys in the box?", focus shifts to the consequent and invites comparison across possible antecedents, for example, whether the boy or the girl bought toys (e.g., Evcen et al., 2026; Farr, 2011; von Fintel, 2001). In each case, the conditional is interpreted relative to the possibilities that answer the current question, making either the literal or the pragmatic interpretation felicitous. Which question is under discussion, however, is rarely stated, and listeners must infer it. As in our study, other work finds that children's success in making this inference often depends on contextual support.

For example, preschoolers' difficulties with universal quantifiers largely disappear when a warm-up trial signals that the question concerns how many girls are riding elephants (Skordos et al., 2022), and they compute scalar implicatures more often when preceding trials signal that the question concerns quantity (Skordos & Papafragou, 2016).

Identifying and reasoning about relevant linguistic alternatives that are not contextually specified is a special case of abductive inference, which is commonplace in both natural language and human reasoning. Abductive inference can be characterized as an "inference to the best explanation", in which an observer makes a best guess about the most likely cause of an event or state on the basis of partial evidence. For example, on seeing that the streets are wet, an observer might conclude that it has rained, but revise this conclusion on noticing a street-cleaning truck -an alternative explanation. Interpreting an utterance can be characterized as a form of abductive inference, since most of the time utterances in isolation cannot fully determine what a speaker intends to communicate or which question they have in mind (Hobbs et al., 1993). Given this framework, children's difficulty in accessing and reasoning about alternatives when computing conditional statements might be explained as flowing from a limitation in their world knowledge, and in understanding which antecedents could generate a consequent. Also possible, however, is that children are able to generate alternatives, but struggle to maintain them in working memory without rich contextual support. Adults may not struggle in this way because, unlike young children, they are better able to hold multiple competing representations of the world in mind at once and move flexibly between them, a capacity that is slow to develop in childhood (Doebel & Zelazo, 2015). Although previous work has investigated the role of world knowledge and working memory in older participants, future studies should explore these factors in preschool-aged children who are just beginning to use and comprehend conditional statements.

Although in isolation Experiments 1 and 2 are each subject to alternative interpretations, the combined pattern across conditions and experiments is most simply explained by a capacity to generate literal meanings. For example, in Experiment 1, one might worry that children simply ignored the conditional statements, and judged that the cat would find toys in the box whenever one of the two characters had purchased them. If so, their responses should have been identical in the Alternatives and Alternatives with Only conditions, since the visual stimuli in these conditions were identical. Instead, children made different judgments, and in the Alternatives with Only condition predicted that the cat would not find toys in the box even when the girl had toys. This provides strong evidence that these two forms of conditionals were assigned different meanings, and therefore that children do not treat conditional statements identically to biconditionals. In Experiment 2, rather than introducing explicit alternatives, we manipulated the causal relation between the antecedent and consequent. When the antecedent was causally unrelated to the consequent, children did not treat "if" to mean "if and only if" even without any contextually salient alternatives, showing that they were tracking what each conditional type communicates in context rather than defaulting to a single interpretation. Together, these two experiments provide complementary evidence: Experiment 1 shows that children can access literal readings when alternatives are contextually available, and Experiment 2 shows that they do so spontaneously when the conditional's discourse function makes a biconditional reading infelicitous, as in non-causal biscuit conditionals.

While our study was designed to assess children's understanding of conditional statements, it may also be relevant to understanding related phenomena, such as the development of counterfactual reasoning. Counterfactual reasoning tasks often involve reasoning about conditional statements, in which children are required to consider alternative, unattested, antecedent conditions. For example, in Rafetseder et al.'s (2013) "muddy boots" experiment (based on Harris et al., 1996), children were asked, "Would the floor be clean if Max had taken his shoes off?" and often answered "yes," even when another character, Susie, had also tracked in mud. Interestingly, the logical structure of this task is nearly identical to the one used in Experiment 1 of our study: whereas in Rafetseder et al. (2013) children see two potential causes of muddy footprints, and should be able to reason that they will be present regardless of what one character does, in our study there were two potential sources of toys, such that there should be toys in the box even if one character does not buy any. Crucially, however, in Rafetseder et al. (2013), the question directed attention only to the antecedent, Max, potentially leading children to ignore Susie as another cause, whereas in our study the children were asked a more open-ended question about the outcome (e.g., "Will the cat find toys in the box?"), encouraging them to consider all relevant contributors. By shifting focus to the consequent rather than singling out one antecedent, our design may have helped children access a broader set of alternatives. Supporting this view, Nyhout et al. (2019) found that 6-to 8-year-olds responded correctly in the "muddy boots" task when the two muddy events were clearly separated in time and motive, making each antecedent more salient and therefore easier to treat as distinct alternatives. Given this, earlier failures may then reflect difficulty overriding the more available interpretation without this kind of support, that is, difficulty identifying which alternatives are relevant and which question is being asked, rather than an inability to reason about unattested possibilities.

In conclusion, the results of two experiments show that children as young as 4 can make conditional inferences when supported by context. In Experiment 1, children were more likely to interpret conditionals literally when alternative antecedents were made contextually available. In Experiment 2, children successfully assigned literal meanings when antecedents bore no causal CONDITIONAL REASONING IN PRESCHOOLERS relation to the consequents of conditional statements. Together, these findings suggest that preschoolers access both literal and pragmatic interpretations of conditionals, and that, like other inferences about what speakers mean, their interpretations depend on what the surrounding context makes available. What develops with age may be the ability to generate relevant alternatives when the context does not supply them. Future research should build on these findings by exploring how children's understanding of conditionals interacts with their abilities in future hypothetical thinking and counterfactual reasoning (e.g., Nyhout & Ganea, 2019; Rafetseder et al., 2010), with reasoning about possibilities more broadly (e.g., Leahy & Carey, 2020; Turan-Küçük & Kibbe, 2024), and whether the conditional reasoning abilities observed here have prelinguistic precursors (e.g., Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2018).

Participants

We recruited 128 children between the ages of 4;0 and 6;11 from preschools in the United States and Canada, and a children's museum in the United States. All participants spoke English as a primary language. We excluded 4 participants who missed more than one attention check trial. Our final sample included 124 children with 41 4-year-olds, 40 5-year-olds and 43 6-year-olds. 5,6 We also tested 40 adult participants on the online research platform http://prolific.co.

Materials and Procedure

Similar to Experiment 1, materials were presented as an animated slide deck. The experimenter placed the slide deck in front of the participant and said, "I'm going to tell you some stories and ask you some questions after." Participants were randomly assigned to one of two between-subjects conditions: a Causal Conditionals condition (e.g., If the girl bakes cookies, the boy will find cookies in the box), where a pragmatic interpretation was supported, or a Non-Causal Conditionals condition (e.g., If the boy wants to read, he will find books in the box), where a literal interpretation was supported. 6 We preregistered a sample size of 120 participants but tested 128 to allow for potential exclusions. The final sample included 124 children who met inclusion criteria.
