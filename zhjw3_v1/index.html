Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Full text

Abstract

Moving objects frequently become occluded from view, yet we often experience a compelling sense of their continued motion and position during occlusion. Despite this subjective impression, recent behavioral and neuroimaging evidence suggests that representations of moving objects during occlusion may be limited. Here, across four experiments, we investigated 1) the accuracy of object localization during dynamic occlusion, 2) the motion information which may support successful position judgments, and 3) the relationship between gaze behavior and localization performance. In Experiment 1a, participants estimated the position of a moving object at unpredictable time points during occlusion. Position estimates were highly inaccurate, with errors increasing as objects travelled further behind the occluder. Increasing object speed exacerbated these errors in Experiment 1b, whereas decreasing speed improved performance. With decreased speed, there was also an increase in pre-occlusion motion information, which may aid object localization during dynamic occlusion. In Experiment 2, we found post-occlusion motion information could support accurate judgments of object reappearance. The necessity of post-occlusion information was further supported in Experiment 3, when removing postocclusion motion information while testing a predictable intercept location resulted in inaccurate estimations. Across experiments, gaze position predicted behavioral errors, with eye movement strategies varying according to task demands. Together, these findings suggest that the position of moving objects is poorly represented during dynamic occlusion, despite our compelling subjective experience of their occluded location. Instead, accurate judgments depend critically on information acquired after the object re-emerges, consistent with a postdictive reconstruction of occluded object position.

Introduction

Moving objects in everyday life often become occluded from sight for varying lengths of time. For example, vehicles travelling on a highway can often become fully obscured by other vehicles. Yet, drivers claim to have a strong sensation of the location of occluded vehicles. This claim has been supported by seminal work on motion perception during occlusion (e.g. Burke, 1952; Michotte, Thinès, & Crabbé, 1964 / Thinès, Costall, & Butterworth,1991; Scholl & Pylyshyn, 1999), although more recent findings have questioned the robustness of occluded motion representation (e.g. Makin & Poliakoff, 2011; Battaglini et al., 2013; Teichmann et al., 2022). Here, we tested the reliability of the perceived experience regarding an object's physical position during a period of dynamic occlusion events. We examine what elements of occluded motion support successful prediction of an occluded object's position and how gaze position during occlusion provides an account of strategy during each occlusion task. Support for maintained perception of an occluded object has come from tasks requiring estimation of a moving objects' reappearance time following occlusion. Rosenbaum (1975) employed a "prediction-of-motion" task to measure the time-to-contact of an occluded moving object with the end of an occluder. They found participants made errors ranging from 140-250ms after the true time-to-contact. However, these errors were assumed to be equivalent to a response latency, suggesting that participants were in fact accurate in determining the object's contact with the end of the occluder. Others demonstrated accurate time-to-contact estimation when occlusion duration is limited to one second (Lyon & Waag, 1995; Bennett et al., 2010). Further, Battaglini and colleagues (2013) demonstrated participants were successful in judging if a moving occluded item reappeared too early or too late after occlusion, commonly termed an interruption paradigm.

However, alongside literature supporting successful estimation of the position of a moving occluded object are studies which suggest poor estimation. Using the time-to-contact task, some have found estimates lag far behind the true object location (Peterken et al., 1991; Makin & Poliakoff, 2011; Battaglini et al., 2013). Further, using interruption paradigms others have demonstrated only ~60-70% of correct reappearance trials were judged accurately, suggesting a weaker representation of correct reappearance time than our subjective impression (DeLucia & Liddell, 1998; Makin et al., 2008; Makin & Poliakoff, 2011).

Similarly, the extent of the neural representation of an occluded moving object is also split. Seminal primate studies indicate the primate posterior cortex (Assad & Maunsell, 1995) and the frontal eye fields (Barborica & Ferrera, 2002) hold some representation of a dynamic occluded object. Yet, more recent human neuroimaging demonstrates the representation is restricted. Teichmann and colleagues (2022) demonstrated that position location was only maintained for 200ms after a dynamic object entered an occluder. This representation held no feature information regarding the occluded object. This result was further supported by Patel and colleagues (PsyArXiv preprint) where position location was maintained up to ~240ms after occlusion.

With a growing consensus that the representation of an occluded object is weak, how do we reconcile our successful navigation in a world with continually occluded moving items? Here we aim to understand what elements of occlusion bring about successful position estimates during occlusion. In Experiments 1a and 1b, we developed a paradigm which probes the inherent representation of object position during occlusion at unpredictable time-points. The approach to study dynamic position perception at unpredictable locations during occlusion has rarely been employed in previous research, yet it provides clear insight to object localization throughout occlusion without reliance on post-occlusion information provided in interruption paradigms or a predetermined test location found in the typical time-to-contact studies. In Experiment 1a we found position estimates throughout occlusion were inaccurate, with error increasing as a function of the distance travelled behind the occluder. In Experiment 1b, we tested whether reducing occlusion duration by increasing object speed would improve position estimates. Contrary to this prediction, increasing object speed exacerbated position error, whereas decreasing speed improved position estimates. Decreasing object speed also increased the duration for which the object was visible prior to occlusion, potentially providing additional information that could support position estimation during occlusion. Thus, Experiments 1a and 1b both demonstrated poor position estimation during occlusion, although errors were reduced at slower object speeds.

In Experiment 2, we examined whether position estimates improved when post-occlusion motion information was available. With access to post-occlusion information, participants estimated the object's reappearance with high accuracy. Finally, in Experiment 3, we tested whether post-occlusion information itself was necessary for accurate position estimation, or whether knowledge of a fixed interception location was sufficient. Participants were therefore asked to determine when the occluded target reached the end of the occluder, eliminating postocclusion motion information while providing a known interception location. Despite knowledge of this location, position estimates remained inaccurate.

Across all experiments, we additionally found that final gaze position predicted errors in the behavioral localization of the occluded object. Gaze position during occlusion was heavily influenced by the task, with participants employing different strategies depending on task goals.

Together, these experiments indicate that the position of a moving object is represented inaccurately throughout occlusion. Accurate estimates of its position during occlusion emerged only when post-occlusion motion information was available, suggesting that perceived object position during occlusion is reconstructed postdictively using information obtained after the object re-emerges.

General Methods

Stimuli & Procedure

In all experiments, we used PsychoPy3 for stimulus presentation and response collection (Peirce, 2007). Stimuli were presented on a 1920 x 1080 VIEWPixx/3D monitor (VPixx Technologies Inc., Saint-Bruno, Québec, Canada). Participants rested their heads in a fixed chinrest located 57 cm away from the monitor. Eye tracking data was recorded using an EyeLink 1000 Desktop Mount camera and EyeLink 1000 Plus software (version 5.15; SR Research Ltd., Ottawa, Ontario, Canada).

For all experiments, a stationary dark gray rectangular occluder (9° x 2.25°) was displayed in the center of the screen on a mid-gray background (Figure 1). During each trial, an orange circle (0.5° diameter), which will be referred to as the target, moved horizontally across the screen from either left-to-right or right-to-left at random. Both motion directions were presented equally often to introduce variability in the stimulus trajectories and minimize potential learning or anticipatory effects associated with the object's stopping position. As motion direction was not a factor of theoretical interest, data were collapsed across directions for all analyses. When the target reached the occluder it was dynamically occluded with gradual deletion of the target, suggesting that it moved behind the occluder rather than just disappearing. Eye-tracking data collection Before the experiment and at fixed intervals (~every 2.5 minutes), participants completed the Eyelink 5-point calibration. Rotating spirals were shown in the center, top left, top right, bottom left, bottom right of the screen until stable fixation was achieved. We then validated the measures, and if the recorded eye position deviated more than 1.0 degrees of visual angle from any of the 5 positions, we repeated the calibration procedure.

Eye-tracking Preprocessing

We preprocessed the eye-tracking data using a pipeline from previous work (Kret & Sjak-Shie, 2019; Allen et al., 2022; Hebart, Contier, Teichmann et al., 2022). First, we removed all recorded pupil samples that were outside of the edges of the screen. Second, to identify eyeblinks, we checked how much pupil dilation changed from one timepoint to the next and excluded samples where the pupil dilation change exceeded a threshold based on the participants' data. Specifically, the threshold was defined as the median absolute deviation from all dilation changes in the task multiplied by a constant of 16 (Kret & Sjak-Shie, 2019). When the pupil dilation change exceeded the threshold, we considered the sample as an eyeblink and expanded the gap for sample exclusion by 100 ms before and 150 ms after the eyeblink. Then we removed samples that were temporally isolated (at least 40 ms away from other valid measures) and that had only a few consecutive valid measurements around them (maximum 100 ms). We passed over this cleaning routine four times. Finally, we ran linear detrending on the x-, y-, and pupil-size measurements to account for slow drifts. Our data release contains the raw as well as the cleaned eye-tracking data.

Transparency and Openness

We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions, all manipulations, and all measures in this study, and we follow the Journal Article Reporting Standards. For all four experiments, the initial target sample size was set a priori at N = 20 participants. We evaluated the relative strength of evidence for and against our hypotheses using Bayes Factors (Morey, Romeijn, & Rouder, 2015). We committed to collecting data from additional participants if the primary hypotheses yielded insufficient evidence (defined as falling within our predetermined inconclusive window of 1/6< BF <6; see Jeffreys, 1961; Lee & Wagenmakers, 2013 for commonly used cutoffs).

All deidentified behavioral as well as eye-tracking data will be made publicly available upon acceptance of this paper. Data analysis code necessary to reproduce the reported findings are publicly available on GitHub: https://github.com/Section-on-Learning-and-Plasticity/Occlusion_behavioural. Data collection for this study took place between December 2021 and February 2023. The experiments were not formally preregistered.

Experiment 1a: Position estimation lags behind during occlusion

We first estimated the representation of a moving target throughout occlusion by testing unpredictable stopping locations during occlusion. Previous work tends to examine the representation of the location of an occluded moving object by presenting a visible boarder for the observer to report time-to-contact (Makin & Poliakoff, 2011; Battaglini et al., 2013). We also tested the time-to-contact paradigm ourselves in Experiment 3. Here, we avoided prior knowledge of the stopping location by pairing a moving target with a sound, so that when the sound stops, this indicated the target stopped behind the occluder. Importantly, without predictable stopping locations, the observer must extrapolate target position for consistent accuracy throughout occlusion. We also examined the relationship between behavioral position accuracy during occlusion and gaze position to determine if gaze reflects behavioral response.

Participants

We collected usable behavioral and eyetracking data from 20 participants (16 female, 4 male; mean age = 24.65, SD = 4.39 years, all right-handed). One participant had to be excluded at the analysis stage because of technical issues during recording. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and provided consent under the National institutes of Health (NIH) Institutional Review Board as part of the study protocol 93-M-0170 (NCT00001360).

Stimuli & procedure

In the first task, we tested whether there was evidence for accurate target motion trajectory representation during occlusion (Figure 1 & Figure 2a). In this task, the target moved at 10.04 degrees/second and was associated with a constant sound (middle C, 261.33 Hz). The target began 8.10 deg from the occluder and was visible for 800 ms prior to occlusion. After the target became occluded, the target stopped moving at one of seven positions, indicated by the stopping of the sound [97.87, 215.31, 332.76, 450.2, 567.65, 685.09, 802.54 ms, relative to start of occlusion]. Participants completed 167 trials (24 per stopping location), presented in a random order. The cursor appeared after the sound stopped to the right of the occluder.

Participants were asked to click on the position where they estimated the target to have stopped moving. No feedback was provided to the participants.

Behavioral Analyses

We collapsed across the movement directions and measured how far the estimated stopping position deviated from the true stopping position along the x-axis. At the group level, we calculated Bayes Factors (following the implementation of Teichmann et al., 2022) for every stopping position to quantify the amount of evidence for a deviation from the true stopping position of the target based on its speed prior to occlusion.

Eye-tracking Analyses

After epoching the preprocessed eye-tracking data relative to stimulus movement onset, we examined the x-axis gaze position over time for the different trial types. We also calculated the distance between the stopping position and the final gaze position (as defined by the end of the sound). To relate the behavioral performance to the eye-tracking data, we performed a repeated measures correlation on behavioral and final gaze deviations. To obtain a Bayes Factor across participants, we used each individual participant's correlation.

Results and discussion

On average, participants underestimated the distance travelled by the target during occlusion (Figure 2b). In general, the degree of underestimation increased with occlusion time such that at the first stopping position (97.87 ms after occlusion started) the estimated position was only 0.42° (BF = 10^4.82) behind the actual position but at the last stopping position (802.54 ms after occlusion started) the estimated position was 3.63° (BF = 10^6.88) behind the actual position. When fitting a linear regression to the difference between the true stopping positions and the estimated stopping positions, the slope indicated an increased error of 0.52° with each stopping position behind the occluder.

The position of the eyes also fell behind the actual position during occlusion, similar to the observed task performance (Figure 2c). Following movement onset, participants initially made a saccade to the stimulus and then followed the stimulus trajectory into the occluder. Examining single trials, the initial saccade occurred around 200 ms after the stimulus started moving before gaze position closely matched the stimulus moving into the occluder. Gaze followed the target's true position at the beginning of occlusion but fell behind the stimulus position over time. By the first stopping position (97.87 ms after occlusion started), the average gaze position was 0.25° behind (BF = 3.38). The lag behind the true stimulus position increased with time so that by the final stopping position (802.54 ms after occlusion started), the average gaze position was 4.2° behind the actual stimulus position (BF=10^7.77). We found a repeated measures correlation of r = 0.74 (BF = 10^7.48) between error in gaze position and error in behavior (Figure 2d), demonstrating a strong relationship between behavioral position estimation and gaze position.

In sum, Experiment 1a shows error in position estimation of the moving target behind the occluder which accumulated with increased occlusion duration, demonstrated in both behavioral performance and gaze position.

Experiment 1b: Slower speeds improve position estimation during occlusion

In Experiment 1b) we replicated Experiment 1a) with two additional speeds (Figure 1). We added a fast and a slow speed condition to determine if duration of occlusion influences position estimation. With the two additional speed conditions we manipulate two factors which could influence position estimation of a moving target during occlusion: 1) duration of occlusion and 2) duration of visibility prior to occlusion. The faster and slower speed conditions enable us to tease apart the influence of these two factors. If the faster speed condition improves position estimation, it indicates shorter durations of occlusion positively influences position estimation during occlusion. However, if the slower speed condition improves position estimation, the longer period of visibility prior to occlusion becomes the critical factor (Teichmann, Edwards, & Baker, 2021).

Participants

We recorded usable behavioral data from 21 participants (15 female, 6 male, mean age = 26.27 years, SD = 6.23 years, all right-handed) as well as usable eyetracking from a subgroup of 10 participants. 11 participants from the cohort of Experiments 1a & 2 overlapped with Experiment 1b. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and provided consent under the National institutes of Health (NIH) Institutional Review Board as part of the study protocol 93-M-0170 (NCT00001360).

Stimuli & procedure

All experiments used the same apparatus and software for stimulus presentation (see General Methods for details). The stimulus was the same as Experiment 1a: an orange circle that moves left to right or right to left and becomes dynamically occluded by a stationary dark gray rectangular occluder. In Experiment 1b we introduced three different motion speeds. The orange circle could move at a fast (23.43 deg/sec), medium (10.04 deg/sec, same as in Experiment 1a), or slow (5.02 deg/sec) speed (Figure 1 and Figure 2a). Participants completed 84 trials per speed condition and were required to click the position where they estimated the target to have stopped moving behind the occluder, as in Experiment 1a. The different speed conditions were randomly interleaved. No feedback was provided to the participants.

Behavioral Analyses

We collapsed across the movement directions and measured how far the estimated stopping position deviated from the true stopping position, as in Experiment 1a, separately for each speed condition. At the group level, we fitted a linear regression for each speed condition to capture how much the position estimation deviated from the true stopping position. We then calculated Bayes Factors comparing the slopes of these regression lines to quantify the amount of evidence we have for an interaction between speed and stopping positions.

Eye-tracking Analyses

We examined the horizontal gaze position over time for the seven stopping position and three speeds and calculated the distance between the stopping position and the final gaze position. We ran the same analyses as for the behavior and calculated Bayes Factors, this time considering the deviation between the final gaze position and the true stopping position. Again, we performed a repeated measures correlation on behavioral and final gaze deviations. We then performed Bayes Factors across the participants' correlations.

In Experiment 1b, participants completed the same task as in Experiment 1a. We replicated the findings of Experiment 1a showing that participants on average underestimated the distance travelled by the target during occlusion (Figure 3a). In general, the degree of position underestimation increased as the occluded target traveled further behind the occluder. The position of the eyes also fell behind the actual position during occlusion, similar to the observed task performance (Figure 3b). Consistent with Experiment 1a, the initial saccade occurred around 200 ms after the stimulus started moving before gaze position closely matched the stimulus moving into the occluder. Gaze followed the target's true position at the beginning of occlusion but fell behind the stimulus position as the stimulus travelled further behind the occluder (Figure 3b). There is a strong relationship between gaze position error and the behavioral error in all speed conditions (Figure 3c). timepoint. In contrast to Experiment 1a, the stimulus could move at one of three speeds in Experiment 1b. In the behavior, the rate of error increased by 0.75° for fast, 0.64° for medium, and 0.53° for slow at every stopping position, determined by the linear regression. Similarly, in the gaze position, error increased by 0.77° for fast, 0.58° for medium, and 0.51° for slow at every stopping position. The degree of position underestimation was most pronounced for the fastest speed for both behavioral position estimation (BF=10^5.7 when comparing fast and slow speeds; Figure 4a), as well as the gaze errors (BF=10^1.12 when comparing fast and slow speeds; Figure 4b). Experiment 2: Accurate position estimation with post-occlusion information

Experiments 1a) and 1b) indicated an increasingly inaccurate representation of target position during occlusion, with improved, yet still hampered performance when the period of visibility prior to occlusion is increased by a slower moving target. In Experiment 2) our goal was to determine if accurate experience of the occluded target position was improved with the addition of post-occlusion information (Teichmann, Edwards, & Baker, 2021). A paradigm for studying representation of occlusion with post-occluder information is commonly called an interruption paradigm (DeLucia & Liddell, 1998; Makin et al., 2008; Makin & Poliakoff, 2011; Battaglini et al., 2013; Battaglini & Ghiani, 2021). Similar to previous studies, we presented a moving target which reappeared on-time or across varying degrees of too early or too late after occlusion and examined observers' ability to detect reappearance intervals using the point of subjective equality. We hypothesized post-occlusion information improves position estimation.

Participants

The tasks from Experiments 1a) and 2) were performed within the same session by the same participants. Task presentation order was counterbalanced.

Stimuli & procedure

All experiments have the same apparatus and software for stimuli presentation (See experiment 1a and Figure 1 for details). An orange circle moves left to right or right to left and becomes dynamically occluded by a stationary dark gray rectangular occluder. We then tested whether there is evidence for position estimation under the occluder when a target's motion trajectory was visible before and after a period of occlusion. In the task, the target moved at 10.04 degrees/second when it was visible. Based on its initial speed, the target could re-appear too early, too late, or on-time. Figure 5a illustrates the nine unique reappearance times: four different early reappearance times visualized in warmer colors, one on-time in black, and four late in cooler colors. Participants were asked to consider whether the target had re-appeared too early or too late and completed 24 trials per condition. No feedback was provided to the participants.

Behavioral Analyses

We collapsed across the movement direction and used a psychometric curve to estimate the point of subjective equality (PSE) for each participant's dataset. The PSE represents the reappearance time in which participants are unsure whether the target re-appeared too early or too late, reflected by a 50% split between "too early" and "too late" responses. PSEs were averaged at the group level to determine which reappearance time was associated with spatiotemporal continuity.

Eye-tracking Analyses

We present both single subject and averaged group horizontal gaze position over time for the nine trial types (4 x early reappearance, 1 x on-time, 4 x late reappearance). During the occlusion period, we calculated the deviation of the gaze position from the true occluded target position at every millisecond. True target position during occlusion was determined as if the target moved at the same speed during occlusion as prior to occlusion. From the deviations, we calculated Bayes factors for each condition to determine when there was evidence for a difference between gaze position and the occluded target position.

Results and discussion

In Experiment 2, participants indicated whether the occluded target had reappeared from the occluder too early or too late. The estimated reappearance was reported with minimal error (Figure 5b), illustrated by the averaged psychometric curve across participants. The point of subjective equality (PSE) was 49.8%, which is 0.20% away from true subjective equality at 50% demonstrating evidence for a PSE equal to 50% with a BF=0.337. These data show that when the target reappeared on time, participants were equally likely to selected "too early" as "too late" in their response.

Following target onset, participants initially made a saccade to the target and then pursued the target into the occluder (single subject: Figure 5c and group data: Figure 5d). Prior to reappearance, participants gaze position followed the same average path across all nine conditions. If we assume the participant represents the moving target at the speed it entered the occluder, the initial gaze position was aligned with the occluded target until ~213 ms across all 9 conditions (range: 200ms to 228 ms), at which point there is evidence the averaged gaze position moved ahead of the occluded target (BF > 6). The target reappearance induced a large saccade towards the end of the occluder. For the three earliest reappearance conditions, the gaze was always on average ahead of the occluded target. However, for the other six conditions (1 x early reappearance, 1 x on-time, and 4 x late reappearance) the gaze started to lag behind the occluded target by approximately 735 ms of occlusion and then fixation was held near the end of the occluder until target reappearance.

The gaze pattern in Experiment 2 was notably different from Experiments 1a and 1b. As the datasets from Experiments 1a and 2 were collected on the same participants, we can directly compare gaze position. In Supplemental Figure 2, we plot an overlay of averaged gaze position from Experiment 1a and the gaze position of the averaged on-time reappearance trials from Experiment 2 (in red). When there is an expectation of the reappearance of the occluded target, participants gaze tends to be ahead of the occluded target (Experiment 2 in red). However, when the task involves maintaining a position estimation of the occluded target during occlusion the gaze tends to be behind the occluded target. These gaze pattern differences indicate large task effects impacting target representation during occlusion.

Experiment 3: Consistent position estimate error judging time to contact with occluder end

In Experiment 3 we examined if post-occlusion information (tested in Experiment 2) is necessary for an accurate prediction of location. Alternatively, is accurate position perception enabled by knowledge of the reappearance location alone? To examine this, we employed a "prediction-of-motion" task where participants estimate the time-to-contact (TTC) of an occluded moving object with the end of an occluder (e.g. Tresilian, 1990; Battaglini et al., 2013; Makin & Poliakoff, 2011). Accurate position perception of the occluded object would suggest prior knowledge of the position test point (here the end of the occluder) is the critical variable. However, inaccurate performance would suggest the post-occlusion information supports the perception of position maintenance during occlusion, demonstrated in Experiment 2.

Participants

We recorded usable behavioral and eyetracking data from 20 participants (9 female, 11 male, mean age = 25.28 years, SD = 3.51 years, 19 right-handed, 1 left-handed). No participants were excluded. 4 participants from the cohort of Experiment 1b participated in Experiment 3, and 1 participant from the cohort of Experiments 1a & 2 participated in Experiment 3. In total, 1 participant performed all aforementioned experiments. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and provided consent under the National institutes of Health (NIH) Institutional Review Board as part of the study protocol 93-M-0170 (NCT00001360).

Stimuli & procedure

All experiments have the same apparatus and software for stimuli presentation (See figure 1 and Experiment 1a for details). The stimulus was the same as Experiment 1a): an orange circle moves left to right or right to left and becomes dynamically occluded by a stationary dark gray rectangular occluder. In this task, the target became occluded but did not re-appear and participants were asked to press a button when they perceived the target reached the end of the occluder, typically called a "time-to-contact" (TTC) task (Battaglini & Ghiani, 2021). To ensure time-to-contact with the end of the occluder varied, the target moved at one of twelve unique speeds (Speeds deg/sec: 5.04, 6.72, 8.4, 10.08, 11.76, 13.44, 15.12, 16.81, 18.49, 20.17, 21.85, 23.53; Figure 6a). Participants completed 16 trials at each speed. After completion of these trials, participants were then asked to complete the same task with a transparent occluder, meaning the participant could see the target behind the occluder. The transparent version aimed to provide an estimate reaction time for the button press at each speed. No feedback on performance was provided to participants.

Behavioral Analyses

We present the behavioral data in three ways for both the occluded and transparent trials, we collapsed across movement directions, and 1) we report the participants' temporal estimate of time to contact. 2) We transformed the button press for estimated reappearance time into a spatial position and then we measured how far this estimated stopping position deviated from the endpoint of the occluder. 3) We converted each participants' response into subjective velocity (Makin & Poliakoff, 2011; Battaglini et al., 2013). Subjective velocity was calculated by actual velocity x (occlusion duration/occlusion duration-error), where error was the time of would-be reappearance minus response time (Makin & Poliakoff, 2011). We presented both group averages and single subject data for all three measures. We also estimated time-tocontact for each speed in the transparent version of the experiment for comparison. Finally, we calculated Bayes factors for the mean error in degrees per condition compared to the true endpoint of the occluder.

Eye-tracking Analyses

We examined the horizontal gaze position over time for the 12 speed conditions. In addition, we describe the gaze position for occlusion trials and transparent trials to examine whether there are any characteristics in gaze patterns that are unique to occlusion. Again, we performed a repeated measures correlation on behavioral and final gaze deviations and performed Bayes Factors across the participants' correlations for all speeds together, and for each individual speed.

Discussion

Our subjective impression that we can accurately estimate the position of a moving target during occlusion appears to depend on motion information available after the target re-emerges. When observers are instead probed to report the target's position while it remains occluded, position estimates are poor. Across four experiments, our findings suggest that the compelling impression of a continuously represented occluded trajectory may arise through postdiction. Motion information available after occlusion is integrated retrospectively to reconstruct the target's trajectory during the period in which it was hidden.

Our two experiments which tested dynamic object localization during occlusion demonstrated poor position estimates. The target was perceived to be moving slower than the reality, resulting in an underestimation of the distance the target travelled behind the occluder. This underestimation increased with distance travelled under the occluder. However, when probing target localization across speeds, we found errors increased with faster speeds, indicating shorter durations of occlusion did not improve the position estimate. Slower targets may be better represented during occlusion due to increased visibility of the dynamic stimulus prior to occlusion (Battaglini & Ghiani, 2021; Teichmann, Edwards, & Baker, 2021). The target in the slowest trials was visible for 1610 ms prior to occlusion compared to 360 ms in the fastest trials. In both these experiments, gaze fell behind the true target trajectory, reflecting the behavioral signature in the task. We noted that participants were accurate in position estimation at the first stopping position across all three speeds in Experiment 1b, indicating that target position estimation was close to the true trajectory for a short distance into the occluder. However, this same effect was not detected in Experiment 1a, indicating that successful position estimation at this stopping location was not reliable.

In our next experiment we found post-occlusion information supported accurate estimation of incorrect target reappearance times. Participants were accurate at detecting targets which reappeared extremely early or late, and they converged on chance when the target reappeared on time, indicating an equal likelihood of responding "early" or "late". Eye gaze was also modulated by the task. Participants followed the target trajectory for ~213 ms during occlusion and then moved ahead of the target. Therefore, target reappearance improved accuracy and altered the gaze strategy.

Finally, to pinpoint the post-occlusion information as critical for accurate occluded target position estimates, we tested position accuracy with a known probe location (the end of the occluder). Here participants could use the known intercept location to help estimate the time-to-contact with the end of the occluder, unlike the unpredictable test times studied in experiments 1a and 1b. However, we found poor estimation of intercept of the moving target with the end of the occluder, and this estimation was worse with faster speeds. Similar to our first experiments, the target was perceived to move slower than the actual speed. Finally, eye gaze strategy also supported the task. Gaze was maintained on the trajectory for ~200 ms, then moved ahead of the occluded stimulus, but then lingered just prior to the end of the occluder. Another critical element of our final study was the addition of the transparent condition, where participants were estimating the interception of the moving target with the end of the occluder, yet the occluder was transparent. This condition demonstrates that participants are extremely precise in estimating time-to-contact when the stimulus is visible. In fact, we can see participants are predicting the interception and factoring in their own reaction time to allow for the precise position estimate.

Some of our findings challenge prevailing interpretations of previous work on dynamic occlusion. In particular, our results question the assumption that observers maintain an accurate representation of an object's state as it moves behind an occluder. Instead, our results suggest that post-occlusion motion information may enable a retrospective reconstruction of the object's position during the period of occlusion. Phenomena such as object persistence has been interpreted across several areas of research as evidence that objects continue to be represented while occluded (Burke, 1952; Michotte, Thinès, & Crabbé, 1964 / Thinès, Costall, & Butterworth,1991; Carey & Xu, 2001; Scholl & Pylyshyn, 1999; Flombaum & Scholl, 2006; Flombaum et al., 2008; Liberman et al., 2016). Many of these studies demonstrate that observers tolerate substantial changes to an occluded object provided that a plausible spatiotemporal or kinematic relationship is maintained between the object entering and emerging from the occluder (Flombaum & Scholl, 2006). Our findings offer a different interpretation of this tolerance: such changes may be accepted precisely because the representation of the object during occlusion is relatively imprecise, allowing pre-and postocclusion information to interact in determining the perceived state of the object. Consistent with this account, when a dynamic Gabor changes orientation during occlusion, its post-occlusion orientation is perceived as biased toward its pre-occlusion orientation (Liberman et al., 2016). Rather than necessarily demonstrating the maintenance of a detailed representation throughout occlusion, such effects may therefore reflect the retrospective integration of information available before and after occlusion.

Another finding which is questioned in our data is the interpretation that object localization during occlusion is more accurate for shorter durations of occlusion (Lyon & Waag, 1995; Peterken et al., 1991; Bennett et al., 2010; Battaglini et al., 2013). Our two experiments which modulated speed demonstrated that shorter durations of occlusion caused by faster moving targets actually increase position error. Importantly, it was plausible to examine differing occlusion durations due to the different speeds of targets, we did not isolate occlusion duration alone. Therefore, while occlusion duration was modulated by speed, so was the extent of exposure prior to occlusion. Longer exposure duration prior to occlusion has been demonstrated to improve position estimates during occlusion (Battaglini & Ghiani, 2021). What is clear from our study is that prior exposure eclipses occlusion duration in modulating localization accuracy during occlusion within our experimental parameters.

Our result which demonstrated successful position estimation of a dynamic occluded target was found using an interruption paradigm. In trials where a moving target reappeared on time after occlusion, we found participants were equally likely to select "too early" or "too late" suggesting this was the point of subjective equality (PSE). This result replicates that of Battaglini et al., (2013) who also found the PSE at 50% across three occluder lengths for the on-time trials. However, studies using interruption paradigms with different judgment tasks have found that participants are relatively poor at explicitly identifying trials in which a target reappears at the correct time (DeLucia & Liddell, 1998; Makin et al., 2008; Makin & Poliakoff, 2011). Why, does performance deteriorate when observers are directly asked to judge whether a reappearance is correctly timed? Often these prior studies aim manipulate many factors simultaneously within their interruption paradigms including reappearance interval, target speed, and occluder size (DeLucia & Liddell, 1998; Makin et al., 2008; Makin & Poliakoff, 2011). The interaction of these factors could influence participants ability to decern a correct reappearance. Our task also takes a more orthogonal approach by measuring the sensitivity of incorrect target reappearance times.

Our study provides a detailed characterization of gaze patterns during occlusion across multiple dynamic object localization tasks. Importantly, participants were given no instructions regarding their eye-movements. They naturally performed the gaze patterns found within each of our four experiments. Across experiments we found participants gaze mirrored their behavior tightly. We also found that the task influenced the gaze patterns during occlusion. When the task focused on unpredictable localization during occlusion, gaze fell behind the occluded moving target. However, when the task focused on the end of the occluder in either the interruption paradigm or the time-to-contact paradigm, gaze followed the moving target for ~200 ms and then moved ahead of the target. If the task was regarding reappearance, gaze reached the end of the occluder, however when the task focused on the interception with the end of the occluder, gaze lingered just before the end of the occluder. The pursuit of the dynamic object into the occluder for ~200ms calls back to the neuroimaging work which has demonstrated position decoding up to ~200ms after occlusion onset (Teichmann et al., 2022; Patel et al., PsyArXiv). Perhaps the short period of pursuit into the occluder reflects the neural mechanism detected using MEG and EEG. This short period of pursuit is also reflected in previous literature on ocular pursuit during occlusion (Orban de Xivry et al., 2005; Bennett & Barnes, 2006). Others have also demonstrated an advantage in occluded target localization during free viewing in comparison to fixation tasks (Makin & Poliakoff, 2011).

Collectively, evidence across four experiments suggests that position estimates for dynamically occluded objects are poor. Accurate position estimates emerged only when post-occlusion information was available, suggesting that our apparent ability to localize moving objects during occlusion may rely largely on retrospective processing. One possibility is that the subjective impression of successful localization is itself supported by this retrospective reconstruction. In everyday environments, observers rarely need to estimate the position of an occluded moving object with high spatial or temporal precision. Instead, behavior typically incorporates substantial safety margins that may allow imprecise representations during occlusion to go unnoticed. For example, when driving, people generally maintain sufficient distance from other vehicles to avoid collisions, meaning that errors in estimating an occluded object's position or time-to-contact may have little consequence and therefore remain undetected.
