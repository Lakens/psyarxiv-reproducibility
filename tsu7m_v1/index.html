To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

A flag means look at this column. Where the data is already de-identified (an ip column reading Anonymized, a name column the authors emptied), no action is needed.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Despite extensive research on the role of social media use in adolescent mental health, fundamental questions remain about what current self-report measures capture. We systematically reviewed 96 adolescent self-report measures of social media use, comprising 1,526 unique items. Through content analysis, we examined what measures assess and the behavioural and psychological processes through which they are operationalised. We found substantial conceptual overlap between SOCIAL MEDIA MEASUREMENT measures, particularly those assessing problematic use and addiction, alongside discrepancies between item content and young people's reported experiences.

Importantly, current measures were found to frequently combine motivation, behaviour, experience, and mindset, despite these psychological behaviours and processes being theoretically distinguished. This raises questions about whether existing measures meaningfully test the hypothesises mechanisms and theoretical frameworks underpinning social media and mental health research. Our findings highlight the need to move beyond content-focused approaches to measurement, towards greater attention to how social media is operationalised and recognition of its potentially dynamic and interdependent nature.

1. Introduction

The reported increased prevalence of mental health difficulties among young people 1 coincided with the widespread uptake of smartphones and social media. This led to an ongoing debate about their effects on adolescent mental health and an explosion of research studying this topic 2 . Thousands of studies later, a fundamental question remains: what do we mean by social media use, and how should we measure it? Major umbrella reviews of studies conducted between 2014 and 2026 report null to small social media effects on adolescent mental health and wellbeing but with considerable inconsistency in findings [3] [4] [5] [6] . Problems in the conceptualisation and measurement of social media use have been identified as one of the key challenges driving this inconsistency 5, [7] [8] [9] . We cannot meaningfully progress the field without clarity about what we are measuring and how. The present study addresses this fundamental challenge.

Currently, most studies rely on retrospective self-reporting of time spent on social media 3, 10 . Despite its ease of use, especially in large cohort studies in which it is typically the primary measure of social media use, self-reported estimates have been found to be unreliable 11 . Even when this is assessed more accurately, duration alone provides only limited insight into how social media relates to adolescent outcomes 8, 10 , highlighting its restricted policy and practice reach 6 . Therefore, increasingly, researchers are calling for a shift towards a more nuanced exploration of underlying mechanisms to understand this relationship 7, 8, 12, 13 . This is consistent with the hypothesis that two young people may spend the same time on social media but have different experiences and outcomes 8 . To address this, recent years have seen an increased attention into the development of adolescent self-report measures that assess more nuanced aspects of social media engagement 9, 10 . These have generally focused on different behavioural and psychological processes, including why (motivation 14 ) and how (activity and behaviour 15, 16 ) young people use social media, how they experience their use (experience 17, 18 ), and, more recently, what they think of social media and their own use (mindsets and belief [19] [20] [21] ).

However, research typically examines these processes in isolation, providing a fragmented exploration of social media effects. This thus obscures how each of these behavioural and psychological processes may interact and together contribute to mental health. This approach also relies on the assumption, reflected in dominant theoretical frameworks, that processes such as mindsets, motivations, behaviours, and experiences can be distinguished into a linear sequence. Typically, these frameworks, including the Uses and Gratification theory 22 , self-determination theory 23 , and Dweck's 24 psychological needs framework, see motivations and cognitions as first shaping behaviour, which, in turn, influences experience. This broad linear sequencing is also reflected in frameworks developed specifically to understand media use and its effects [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] , which similarly distinguish between antecedent processes, media-use behaviours, and subsequent experiences or outcomes. However, in reality these distinctions are blurred in young people's accounts of their social media use, likely suggesting that many of these processes may be more difficult to disentangle and neatly align in a linear order 8 . This conceptual challenge may be further compounded by the proliferation of social media measures within often inconsistent or limited theoretical and conceptual groundwork 30 . This partly reflects the inherent trade-off between capturing rapidly evolving digital phenomena and allowing sufficient time for careful conceptualisation and validation 31 . However, it also reflects wider and persistent problems in measurement science, where clear and explicit theories are often neglected during early scale development 32 . Regardless of the reason, this is particularly concerning given that policy decisions about young people's social media use may hinge on findings obtained from measures with poor validity, due to unclear or poorly conceptualised constructs 33 . What is more concerning is that many of these measures may nevertheless appear to perform psychometrically well 34, 35 . This, of course, is not unique to the social media literature, but rather reflects another wider and persistent issue within psychological measurement 32, [36] [37] [38] , that is, an overreliance on psychometric evaluation at the expense of construct validity. As a result, statistical coherence may mask underlying conceptual ambiguity, raising important questions about what these measures are capturing.

Yet, how social media use is conceptualised within these measures, and the assumption that the psychological and behavioural processes they aim to capture are conceptually separable, remain poorly understood. Although some previous reviews have touched on how social media use is conceptualised and measured, they have either focused on broad conceptualisations 9,10 or on particular forms of use, especially problematic or addictive use 30, 39 . There has been no systematic attempt to examine item-and construct-level conceptualisation across the broader landscape of adolescent social media measures, and the extent to which their content overlaps.

Importantly, despite the increasing development of theories seeking to explain how different aspects of social media use may contribute to adolescent mental health, it remains unclear to what extent current measures can support the theoretical distinctions the field is trying to test.

The aim of the current study is, therefore, to systematically map how adolescent social media engagement is conceptualised and operationalised across existing self-report measures, examining both their content and the behavioural and psychological processes they capture. Through a systematic review and content analysis of measures, we examine the extent to which these conceptualisations overlap and consider what this means for current theoretical frameworks of social media use and adolescent mental health.

2. Methods

This systematic review was part of a larger project, #So.Me (https://osf.io/g7fkh/), which aimed to develop and validate a comprehensive measure of adolescent social media experience. The development of this systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines 71 . Before commencing the systematic search, the review protocol was published with PROSPERO (reference: CRD42020141428) and the protocol was updated periodically to reflect changes to the study, the search criteria, or the search strategy. While we also pre-registered an aim to assess the psychometric properties of these measures, this will not form part of the current work.

2.1. Searches

A systematic search of six databases (ACM Digital Library, ASSIA, Communication & Mass Media Complete, Embase, PsycINFO, and PubMed Central/MEDLINE) was conducted in April 2023, and was later updated in July 2025.

The reference lists of related systematic reviews and the papers within were handsearched to check for additional eligible studies, and Google Scholar was checked for further relevant research that may have cited these. Due to the anticipated large number of relevant results and our focus on published measure development and validation studies, searches were restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and did not include grey literature. No specific publication period was imposed; however, the searches were restricted to publications made in the English language.

The keywords focused on social media use ("social media" OR "social network*"

OR Facebook OR Instagram OR Twitter OR Snapchat OR TikTok OR YouTube OR WhatsApp), measurement (survey* OR questionnaire* OR checklist* OR check-list* OR measure* OR tool* OR instrument* OR scale* OR inventor* OR assess* OR framework*), and development and/or validation (develop* OR valid* OR factor* OR psychometric* OR construct* OR dimension* OR create*). The search terms were imposed on title and abstracts only.

This decision related to both the manageability of the volume of generated results (see Figure 6), and the fact that the review's focus meant that social media measurement and the methods used (development, validation) should feature in the abstract of any relevant paper. The results of the database searches were uploaded to the systematic review management programme Covidence, which automatically removed duplicate results.

2.2. Study selection

Studies were included within the review if they focused on the development and or validation of an adolescent self-report social media measure. Eligible measures were those that explored both generic use and specific social media platforms in relation to a behavioural, psychological or sociological aspect of use. Therefore, measures assessing topics such as consumerism, advertising, or marketing, were excluded. Studies with proxy-report measures (e.g. parent-report) or those that focused on self-reports of time spent online were also excluded. Given the review's focus on adolescence, only studies with a sample mean or age range of 10 to 24 years old were included 72 . Qualitative studies were excluded; however, mixed-method studies were considered for inclusion if the quantitative component, which pertained to measure development or validation, was completely separable from any qualitative lines of enquiry. Translations and validation studies of existing measures were not included, and the primary study describing the measure was included instead.

Screening at title/abstract stage was completed by EB, LB, and MP. At an early stage of title/abstract screening, a subset of 10% of the results was re-screened by another reviewer to check the usability of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. When this was completed, minimal discrepancies were discussed and resolved. Full text screening was completed by EB, JHD, LB, and MP, with regular discussions to ensure consistency in the criteria applied.

A total of 44,370 studies were identified, out of which 73 were included in the current review (see Figure 6). A further eight studies were identified through secondary searches, and ten more were identified through the repetition of searches, totalling 91 included studies.

2.3. Data extraction and coding

Characteristics that were relevant for the content analyses were extracted from each study by JD, SH, EB and MP: author name, publication year, sample characteristics (age, clinical or community sample), social media platform examined, the origin of development, whether youth consultation was employed, the measure description (what it aimed to assess), as well as its items and subdomains, where relevant (see Supplementary Information for the full dataset). While 91 studies were included, some of these contained multiple measures, resulting in total of 96 measures.

EB conducted the initial coding followed by a discussion with MP. MP then reviewed all items and performed a second round of coding, as well as refining the code names. JHD and LB were consulted throughout the coding. Measures with identical items were coded as one measure. Specifically, the Problematic TikTok Use Scale, Internet Addiction Test-Social Networking Sites Version, and Instagram Addiction Scale, were all drawn from Young's Internet Addiction Test. Therefore, while all measures were included in the review, items were only coded once under the name of "Internet Addiction Test Young". This was also the case for the Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale, Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale, Problematic QQ Use Scale and Problematic TikTok Use scale, which were all coded under the name of 'Bergen scales'. Finally, the Twitter Excessive Use Scale was identical to the Microblog excessive use scale. Once this overlap (which was all in measures assessing social media addiction) was taken into account, a total of 90 unique measures remained for content-related analyses. Therefore, number of measures used (90 vs. 96) depended on the aim of each analysis. Specifically, content-related analyses included the 90 unique measures to avoid duplication and inaccurate over-representation of content.

Analyses focusing on the measures themselves, used all 96 measures.

We followed a four-step coding process for each item, which we detail in Table 1 using an example item. First, following an inductive approach, we assigned an item code that best described each item's content. Second, we reviewed all item codes and grouped them into item code groups, based on content similarity. For example, item codes "maintain relationships" and "form new relationships" were both coded into the item code group of "social". Third, for each item we specified the behavioural and/or psychological process it captured (item process). The third step is described in more detail in the next section. Coders were blinded to the parent measure the items belonged to during coding steps 1-3. Finally, each measure was assigned to one of nine construct groups (measure construct), based on the authors' original definition of the measure. Each item was then assigned the measure construct of its parent measure.

2.3.1. Coding of Step 3 (item process).

The hybrid approach used in the third step of coding included a deductive approach based on the four broad psychological processes of behaviour, experience, mindset, and motivation identified thus far in the literature, alongside inductive coding to allow for the identification of any additional processes. However, no new processes were identified. Items capturing multiple processes were given a primary and secondary code, and were then coded as multiprocess items.

We followed the definitions and linguistic keywords provided in Table 2, which MP, EB, LB, and JHD discussed and trialled together and subsequently discussed with CB. However, we acknowledge two things: first, some of these behavioural and psychological processes have been debated for years and still lack an agreed definition 73 , therefore requiring a pragmatic approach to their conceptualisation and operationalisation within our coding. For example, given that beliefs (specific understandings) are nested within mindsets (broader interpretative frameworks), we considered both under the umbrella of mindset. Second, more specific items often refer to multiple processes, as they combine actions with their emotional or motivational context. For example, the item "I feel happy on social media" reflects a subjective internal state and therefore captures an experience. In contrast, the item "I feel happy when I watch funny videos on social media" also captures an experience, but additionally specifies the behavioural context ("watch funny videos") in which that experience occurs. However, reference to multiple processes did not necessarily mean that the item was coded as multi-process. Such items were coded according to their primary process, which we considered to reflect the central behavioural or psychological process captured by the item. Therefore, references to another process that provided the context or antecedent for the central process did not alone result in multi-process coding. In the example above, "when I watch funny videos on social media" provides the behavioural context for the experience being assessed, rather than behaviour being the central process. In contrast, "I enjoy using social media to pass time and/or to have fun" was coded as multi-process as two processes were the central focus: "I enjoy" indicating experience, and "to pass time…" indicating motivation for use.

2.4. Analysis and synthesis

Findings were synthesised descriptively based on the item content and visualised using ggplot2 74 . While Jaccard analysis was planned (see pre-registration) this was not carried out.

Jaccard is a useful metric to understand the level of content overlap between measures assessing the same construct (e.g. depression measures 75 ).

However, the included measures assessed varied and largely distinct constructs, making such pairwise comparisons inappropriate and difficult to meaningfully interpret. Following our content analysis, the item and construct content was explored and synthesised in the following three ways.

2.4.1. Synthesis 1: conceptualisation of measures.

First, we descriptively explored how adolescent social media was conceptualised across the included measures. The distribution of measure constructs was explored for all 96 measures, whereas item-level content analyses were based on the 90 unique measures to avoid duplication of identical item content. The content of the 1,526 items was examined at multiple levels: 1) what each item assessed (item codes); 2) the broader conceptual group to which each item code belonged (item group code); 3) the construct assessed by each measure (measure construct); and 4) the behavioural or psychological process reflected in each item (item process).

2.4.2. Synthesis 2: alignment between measure constructs and item

content. Second, we examined the alignment between item content and their parent measure constructs, based on the 90 unique measures. Specifically, we compared the item code groups with their parent measure constructs based on our coding process (steps 2 and 4; Table 1), to assess the extent to which constructs accurately reflected item content.

2.4.3. Synthesis 3: conceptual overlap between processes.

Third, we explored the extent to which the five behavioural or psychological processes (motivation, behaviour, experience, mindset, multi-process) appeared in the 90 measures. We explored this both within the item codes and measure constructs (steps 1,3 and 4; Table 1). Heterogeneity in these processes within measure constructs was further explored through Shannon entropy. This was a post-hoc analytical decision used to capture and quantify within-construct heterogeneity of behavioural and psychological processes. Standardised values of zero indicate all items within a given measure construct are concentrated in one process (e.g. all items assessing only motivation), and 1 indicates items are equally distributed across the five processes.

The within-construct heterogeneity, and limited alignment between construct and item content identified through the above syntheses prompted us to further explore these findings through two post-hoc syntheses.

First, we aimed to further understand the extent to which item content within current social media measures reflected young people's lived experience. We therefore mapped the key themes and subthemes of four existing qualitative reviews [41] [42] [43] [44] onto the 70 item codes identified through synthesis 1 of our content analysis. Generic qualitative reviews were chosen, synthesising young people's own perspectives or experiences of social media use, with samples corresponding to the age range of the current review.

3.1. Synthesis 1: conceptualisation of measures

Following the measure developers' definitions, the 96 measures were grouped into the following nine constructs: 1) addiction/disorder defined as social media addiction or disorder, including addictive tendencies, symptoms, or diagnostic features such as salience, tolerance, withdrawal, impaired control, relapse, and conflict (n = 20, 20.8%); 2) activity/use defined as the activities, behaviours, practices, or intensity of social media use, including what young people do and how they use or interact with social media (n = 16, 16.7%); 3) motivation defined as the reasons, motives, goals, or purposes underlying social media use (n = 14, 14.6%); 4) attitudes/perceptions reflecting the beliefs, attitudes, perceptions, evaluations, or metacognitions about social media, its use, or one's own relationship with it (n = 10, 10.4%); 5) problematic assessing problematic social media experiences including for instance excessive use, difficulty regulating, or negative consequences, without being explicitly conceptualised by the measure developers as an addiction or disorder (n = 9, 9.4%); 6) social defined as the interpersonal and relational aspects of social media use and experience, including relationships, social interaction, support, conflict, peer experiences, and relationship maintenance (n = 9, 9.4%); 7) internalising defined as the negative emotional and psychological experiences associated with social media, including anxiety, stress, distress, fatigue, and depressive feelings (n = 8, 8.3%); 8) engagement reflecting the multidimensional involvement with social media, encompassing combinations of cognitive, affective, behavioural, motivational, and/or experiential aspects of use, including different styles or forms of engagement (n = 7, 7.3%); and 9) gratifications/benefits assessing the positive outcomes, benefits, utilities, or gratifications perceived or obtained from social media use (n = 3, 3.1%).

3.3. Synthesis 3: Conceptual overlap between processes

Finally, our coding indicated that the 1,526 items mapped onto the four processes reflected in the literature: behaviour (n = 482; 31.6%), experience (n = 437; 28.6%), motivation (n = 276; 18.1%), and mindset (n = 204; 13.4%), or a combination of those, which we refer to as 'multi-process' items (n = 127; 8.3%). Double-barrelled items, i.e. those asking about two distinct behaviours or experiences (e.g. "I give and receive emotional support by sending a virtual gift, a kiss, hug, etc") were more likely to be coded as multi-process items. No new processes were identified. However, we found that some items captured the behaviour of other people. For example, items coded under relationship conflict included contextual information relating to the participants' social media use (e.g., "I argue with my parents about spending too much time connected to WhatsApp"). The importance of perspective became particularly evident when examining items with similar content but capturing either the participant's own actions or receipt of others' actions. For example, within the anti-social item code, items captured one's own behaviour (e.g. "I used to use offensive language on SNSs") but also the receipt of other people's behaviours (e.g. "I have received threats or abusive messages through social media"). Although the latter involves the behaviour of others, it captures the participant's experience of receiving that behaviour and was therefore coded as capturing experience.

Figure 3A summarises the distribution of behavioural and psychological processes across the 70 item codes. The majority of item codes were represented by multiple processes. For example, under the code alleviating boredom/killing time social media use could be framed both as a motivation (e.g. item "I use Instagram to escape from boredom") or mindset (e.g. item "Watching Facebook posts is good for overcoming boredom") depending on the wording of the item.

Importantly, this overlap was not limited to the item level but extended to the measure construct level (Figure 3B), with moderate to high entropy values (0.48-0.97).

Aside from measures assessing activity/use, which showed moderate heterogeneity, all measure constructs were operationalised to varying degrees through multiple behavioural and psychological processes, showing substantial within-construct heterogeneity. Approximately 30% of items drawn from measures originally defined as assessing motivation were coded as reflecting experience, mindset, or behaviour.

Similarly, 20% of items of activity/use measures intended to capture behaviour reflected additional processes. A similar pattern was observed for measures of perceptions or attitudes. Although ten measures were defined as capturing these constructs, approximately 53% of their items were coded as reflecting processes other than mindset, the process most closely aligned with attitudes and perceptions 40 . The great variation in content and processes observed within constructs raises questions about the measures' validity, prompting us to further explore these findings through two post-hoc syntheses, in an effort to offer tentative explanations: 1) the degree to which measure content is represented in young people's lived experience; and 2) how current measures were developed and where they originated.

3.4. Synthesis 4 (post-hoc): alignment between youth experience and item content

Further examination of the 70 item codes indicated generally good representation of young people's experiences identified in existing qualitative systematic reviews, although some disparities remained. Item codes received an asterisk (see Figure 1) when their definition conceptually corresponded to a theme, subtheme, or narrative representation of the findings in at least one of the four qualitative reviews. Discussion The current systematic review provides a comprehensive exploration of how adolescent social media use is conceptualised and operationalised across 96 existing self-report measures with 1,526 unique items. Across five complementary syntheses, our findings suggest substantial conceptual challenges in the measurement of adolescent social media use, raising important questions about what existing measures capture and whether current conceptualisations adequately reflect the Comparisons with existing qualitative syntheses of young people's social media use [41] [42] [43] [44] indicate that existing measures cover key areas of social media engagement, consistent with young people's lived experience. However, a substantial proportion of the areas captured by measures are not reflected in young people's accounts. While this does not necessarily indicate that these aspects are not experienced by young people, just over one third of the included measures reported any consultation with young people during development and/or validation, limiting confidence in this assumption. It is important to note that the current study did not seek to conduct a mixed-methods review and some of the social media experiences noted by young people's qualitative accounts may therefore have been missed. Still, the observed discrepancies noted here, alongside the limited involvement of young people in measure development, raise important questions about the validity of social media measurement. This is consistent with the wider psychological measurement literature that finds limited consultation with the population for whom the measures are intended, potentially resulting in measures that disproportionately reflect researcher perspectives rather than lived experience 32, 37, [45] [46] [47].

These concerns are particularly evident in the measures operationalising social media use as an addiction. In the current study, half of the addiction/dysregulation item codes were not captured in young people's qualitative accounts. This may not be surprising as approximately 60-84% of the studies included in qualitative reviews [41] [42] [43] [44] and 100% of the included social media addiction measures relied on non-clinical samples. This means that more severe or addiction-related experiences may not only be less prominent in the current literature, but also mis-or under-represented for young people who may be having those experiences.

Beyond concerns about lack of representation of lived-experience, our itemcontent coding also raised concerns about how addiction-related experiences are conceptualised in the included measures. Due to our adoption of blind coding, the resulting codes were not influenced by measure names, pre-existing frameworks, or diagnostic criteria, thus allowing for an important conceptual distinction to emerge:

Both the included social media addiction measures and qualitaitve syntheses appear, at times, to blur habitual use with compulsive or addiction-like experiences.

Qualitative syntheses specifically describe young people reflexively picking up their phones and repeatedly checking social media apps, but causally interpret this behaviour as resembling compulsive checking in obsessive-compulsive disorder 41, 43 . This is consistent with the wider behavioural addiction literature, where compulsion has been conceptualised in multiple and inconsistent ways, including as automatic or habitual behaviour 48 . In the current review, 42% of the items coded as 'habitual use' were part of social media addiction measures (e.g. "Going on social media is the first thing I do when I wake up in the morning"). Yet, habitual behaviour alone is not considered to be synonymous with compulsion or addiction, and indeed can characterise non-pathological behaviour 48, 49.

Concerns about potential overpathologisation of habitual social media behaviour are not new 35, 50 , but our review findings further accentuate them.

Importantly, this issue extends beyond individual items to how measures are more broadly conceptualised. For example, we find substantial conceptual overlap between measures assessing problematic social media use and addiction, even though their distinction may have implications for whether a young person is classed as requiring treatment. In practice both are operationalised using highly similar or identical content. This questions whether these constructs represent meaningful distinctions or simply re-labelled versions of the same underlying experiences. This has important implications for the interpretation of findings, potentially contributing to the wide reported variance in prevalence estimates of 'social media addiction', but also for distinguishing overuse from clinically relevant problematic use 30, 39, 51 . Inadequate or inconsistent conceptual groundwork therefore creates risks in both directions: measures may fail to accurately capture the experiences of young people who require support, while simultaneously pathologising affective or habitual experiences that do not represent addiction 35, 50 , contributing to both false positives and false negatives.

These findings therefore highlight how strongly the current measurement landscape appears weighted towards problematic or disorder-related engagement relative to the wider range of ways adolescents describe their social media use and experience [41] [42] [43] [44].

Another potential explanation behind these conceptual issues, beyond the lack of youth consultation, is the measures' development origin. Our origin mapping shows that, consistent with other work 14, 30, 39 , many social media measures in the current study were reportedly developed from existing scales or diagnostic frameworks, which themselves draw on conceptually discrete constructs, such as gambling or substance use. Of those, one third can be traced back to DSM Gambling criteria. This should give us pause. This practice can lead to the transfer of items and constructs that were not originally developed to capture the nuances of social media engagement, thereby contributing to serious misrepresentation of the construct, measurement overlap, and with potential methodological and practical implications 37, 52 . Ultimately, this practice artificially creates convergence between measures as a result of shared methodological heritage, rather than conceptual validity, fundamentally biasing measurement: Developing measures based on the assumption that social media use is identical or similar to established addictions risks reproducing these assumptions in subsequent measures, rather than accurately capturing social media use and experience 53.

4.2. Beyond item content: The overlooked role of behavioural and psychological processes

However, shared measurement heritage cannot fully explain the conceptualisation issues observed in the current review. Indeed, issues were also observed in measures developed independently of existing scales or frameworks, suggesting a broader issue that extends far beyond social media measurement. From conceptualisation to item development and psychometric validation, researchers are called to consider whether items adequately represent the construct they are intended to assess. However, our findings point to a second, relatively neglected consideration in content validity: the behavioural or psychological process through which that construct is operationalised. An item may appear to assess 'social media addiction', for example, but does it do so through a behaviour, motivation, experience, or mindset? For example, the Bergen Addiction scales 54, 55 include items capturing motivation ("Used Facebook to reduce restlessness"), behaviour ("Ignored your partner, family members, or friends because of Facebook"), and experience ("Become irritable if you have been prohibited from using Facebook"). Existing measurement and psychometric approaches have largely focused on the item content, with less attention to how the psychological process itself is constructed within individual items. This distinction matters because different behavioural and psychological processes, although theorised to serve different functions [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] , can contribute to the same broader construct without being conceptually equivalent. This complexity may not be problematic for certain constructs, like addiction and internalising, which inherently involve multiple processes 56, 57 . For example, anxiety can involve cognitive experiences such as worry ("During the day, I spend time thinking about how attractive I might look when people see pictures of me on social media"), as well as behavioural processes such as avoiding posting pictures ("I do not post any photo on my profile if I am not satisfied with my appearance"). However, this becomes problematic when measures are meant to be able to isolate a particular process. For example, 30% of items drawn from social media motivation measures reflect processes other than motivation. In practice, conceptually distinct processes are therefore conflated within a single total score of 'social media motivation', with unclear consequences for its validity. Still, even for constructs that are inherently multidimensional, theories often treat these processes as conceptually distinct. For example, the I-PACE model considers internet-use disorders to arise from interactions between key processes, distinguishing, for example, between motives, affective responses, and gratification 57 . Our findings suggest that such distinctions are not consistently preserved in how these processes are operationalised in existing measures.

This issue, although not discussed in the literature of measurement science, may not be surprising given that the behavioural or psychological process an item aims to assess is often much more implicit than the construct itself, and therefore likely more strongly influenced by the grammatical and linguistic structure of the item. This might make it more difficult to identify, more prone to overlap, and less frequently scrutinised during scale development. Indeed, guidance on item construction emphasises clarity, brevity, and minimising cognitive load 58,59 , but pays limited attention to how grammatical form constrains theoretical meaning. As a result, measures may combine items that are meant to reflect distinct processes likely introducing conceptual ambiguity and psychometric instability. This issue can be particularly misleading for measures that appear to perform well psychometrically.

For example, items such as "I use Instagram to escape from boredom" and "You can always find something interesting happening on Instagram" from the Instagram Motives Questionnaire 60 were found to load onto the same "escapism" factor in the original study. However, these items reflect different processes: the former captures motivation, whereas the latter reflects a belief or mindset about the platform. This suggests that, despite strong model fit, conceptually distinct processes may be conflated within a single factor. Although mindset is theoretically related to motivation 24 , treating such measures as capturing only one process risks obscuring this conceptual distinction, and makes it difficult to test theories [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] that depend on these processes being distinguished.

This issue is unlikely to be resolved through content-focused approaches alone, such as improving construct definitions or incorporating youth consultation. When process overlap was examined at the individual-measure level, among the 90 unique measures included in the content analyses, we found no notable differences in the process overlap (average Shannon scores) between measures developed with youth consultation (n = 34, M = 0.49, SD = 0.29) and those without (n = 56, M = 0.43, SD = 0.33; t(76.84) = -0.88, p > .05, d = -.19, 95% CI [-. 61, .23]). This should not be interpreted as reducing the importance of youth consultation. Young people can provide expertise on their own experiences, and how they understand the items, but cannot be expected to identify subtle linguistic distinctions in how psychological processes are operationalised within items. This requires different, interdisciplinary approaches, including insights from fields such as psycholinguistics. Such expertise may help identify how linguistic features of items, including agency, causality, temporality, and goal-directed language, shape the psychological process participants are asked to report 61.

4.3. Rethinking theoretical distinctions: From linear processes to dynamic systems

Importantly, while some of the observed overlap in behavioural and psychological processes may arise from methodological limitations in the earlier stages of measure development (e.g., limited psycholinguistic expertise), we argue that some may also be intrinsic to complex phenomena such as social media use, where adolescents' mindsets, goals, actions, and experiences likely co-occur in time and mutually shape one another. Despite this, current behavioural and media theories [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] , on which the development of some of these measures rely, assume a linear or near-linear sequence, where cognitions and goals precede action, and experience follows as an outcome (e.g. Mindset + Motivation  Behaviour  Experience). For example, a framework aimed at understanding young people's social media use in relation to their wellbeing distinguishes between three dimensions: activities, motives, and communication partners, proposing that future work should explore how a particular motive may lead to a specific activity or greater communication, in turn influencing young people's wellbeing 29 . Such frameworks have been useful in shifting our attention from rigid estimates of social media duration towards the more nuanced ways in which young people engage with social media. However, our findings suggest that the operationalisation of the measures needed to explore such a chain of events is less clear than these theoretical distinctions imply.

Some theoretical frameworks that distinguish psychological processes and retain some directionality highlight the potential interdependence between these processes. For example, Dweck's 24 psychological needs framework conceptualises basic needs as giving rise to goals, while goal pursuit activates stored representations of past experiences that shape subsequent goals, behaviours, and experiences. In Figure 5, we use items drawn from existing measures in the current review, to showcase this theory. This serves as a theoretical illustration, and was not tested in the current study. In this figure, for example, the motivation behind social media use "to feel important" could be theorised to arise from the need to be socially accepted.

To meet this need, goals are selected and pursued, activating stored representations of past beliefs, emotions, and action tendencies, that influence not only those goals, but also future actions and experiences. For example, someone with pre-existing beliefs that "being tagged on a photo shows you are important" may select specific actions, such as "checking photos that one has been tagged on", or have specific experiences, for example "feeling disappointed if there are no notifications". These behaviours and experiences may then influence the stored representations by updating them or creating new ones. Note. In this figure underlying needs give rise to need-driven goals (motivation), and as these goals are pursued, stored representations (beliefs, emotions, and action tendencies) are activated which guide future goals, behaviour and experiences. These behaviours and experiences feed back into and update stored representations over time.

This model, however, still suggests that motivation and goals serve as the starting point that activates this chain of dynamic events. Yet young people often report going on social media without a specific goal in mind 8 . There are also limits to what self-report measures can tell us about the processes involved in picking up one's phone or going on social media. Reported reasons for use may reflect conscious motives, but may also capture expected gratifications or retrospective explanations for behaviours initiated habitually or without a conscious goal, and may themselves be biased 62, 63 . Thus, for example, reporting that social media is used "to keep in touch with friends" does not necessarily mean that social connection was the proximal motivation for a particular instance of use. Young people also tend to switch between platforms, and frequently use social media while engaging in other activities 64, 65 , making the distinction between such behavioural and psychological processes even more difficult. While digital worlds are starting to be explored as distinct systems in adolescent development 66 , digital engagement co-occurs and overlaps with offline behaviour, making this an even more complex picture. These findings thus call for a shift from treating behavioural and psychological processes as independent or static constructs, to considering them as distinguishable but interacting components of a dynamic system. This reflects a wider challenge within psychological theory and measurement, where phenomena traditionally represented as distinct latent constructs may instead emerge from interactions among their components 67 . From this perspective, the overlap observed in the current study may not simply represent measurement error to be eliminated, but may partly reflect the complex and interconnected nature of the processes through which social media use unfolds. The critical issue may therefore not be the presence of overlap itself, but whether measures acknowledge and model this interdependence or collapse conceptually distinct processes into a single score.

4.4. Limitations

A few limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings reported herein. First, although our coding framework was informed by existing theoretical distinctions, the coding and classification of items into behavioural and psychological processes required interpretative judgements by the team, particularly where conceptual boundaries were blurred. Second, our comparison with existing qualitative syntheses was exploratory and may not have captured the full range of young people's lived social media experiences. Third, our analyses examined the conceptual content of measures rather than their psychometric properties, and therefore cannot establish whether the observed conceptual overlap translates into differences in measurement performance.

4.5. Implications for social media measurement and research

The current systematic review suggests that the field of social media measurement can benefit from several changes. First, measure development or revision must focus on clear conceptualisation grounded in young people's lived experiences, rather than uncritically relying on existing measures or diagnostic frameworks. Measure development should move beyond simply replacing terms such as 'gambling' with 'social media' in existing measures. This is particularly important for measures of problematic social media use, where greater involvement of young people experiencing such difficulties is needed. Second, greater attention should be paid not only to what constructs items are intended to assess, but also to the behavioural and psychological processes through which these constructs are operationalised. This requires greater interdisciplinary integration between measurement science and fields such as psycholinguistics, alongside existing approaches to item development and cognitive interviewing 68, 69 . However, the inherent complexity of adolescent social media use, but also adolescent development more broadly 70 cannot be ignored. Social media measure development must consider how to linguistically and meaningfully distinguish between the different behavioural and psychological processes involved. If these processes are difficult to disentangle within measurement, the implications of using such measures to test theories that assume linearity of separable processes must be considered. Finally, the findings of the current review point to implications beyond measurement. The field of social media research must consider whether the assumption underlying many current theories, that motivation, mindset, behaviour, and experience are cleanly separable processes, is realistic. This is important as it may in turn have consequences for interventions targeting one process alone, which may overlook how changes in behaviour, motivation, mindset, and experience influence one another. Therefore, other approaches, such as those allowing the modelling of their complex dynamic interrelationships, may be more appropriate. Research on whether social media influences adolescent mental health and wellbeing will continue, but unless we attend to such conceptualisation and measurement issues, we risk mistaking research motion for progress.
