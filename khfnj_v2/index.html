Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Reading and spelling are foundational skills for academic success in both firstlanguage (L1) (e.g., National Reading Panel, 2000) and second-language (L2foot_0 ) contexts (August & Shanahan, 2006), enabling children to access and participate fully in the wider curriculum. Research in English-majority contexts has identified both cognitive and linguistic factors that underlie these skills in children L2 learning. Phonological short-term memory (PSTM) and phonological awareness (PA), as well as grammar and vocabulary knowledge, are associated with L2 English reading (Chen et al., 2015; Grabe & Yamashita, 2022) and spelling (e.g., Keilty & Harrison, 2015; Zaretsky, 2020).

Far less is known about whether these findings hold in English as a foreign language (EFL) contexts, where children learn English mainly in the classroom and have limited language exposure. In such contexts, studies have reported inconsistent results regarding the influence of precursory skillssuch as phonological awarenesson reading (e.g., Huo & Wang, 2017) and spelling skills in EFL learners (Mlakar et al., 2025a (Mlakar et al., , 2025b;; Russak, 2020).

Moreover, few studies examine cognitive and linguistic predictors together, across reading and spelling, and for both real words and pseudowords, which would show whether different skills support lexical and non-lexical processing.

The German context also raises a question of transfer. German has a relatively shallow orthography with highly consistent grapheme-phoneme correspondences, whereas English is deep, characterized by ambiguous sound-spelling mappings and unique phonemes that do not exist in the German phonological inventory (Landerl, 2005; Schmalz et al., 2015). According to the Linguistic Coding Differences Hypothesis (Sparks & Ganschow, 1991) and accounts of cross-language transfer (Geva & Siegel, 2000; Geva & Yaghoub Zadeh, 2006), L1-indexed phonological and cognitive skills should nevertheless relate to L2 literacy. However, these differences between the two orthographies may limit how directly such skills transfer to English.

The present study therefore examines how nonverbal intelligence (NVI), PSTM, PA, working memory (WM), and L2 English grammar and vocabulary knowledge relate to L2 English reading (comprehension, oral reading rate and accuracy, pseudoword reading) and spelling (real words and pseudowords) among EFL elementary school learners in Germany.

The aim is to clarify the cognitive and linguistic foundations of L2 literacy in a low-exposure context and to inform both theoretical models of literacy acquisition and EFL instruction.

Theoretical Models Underpinning L2 Reading and Spelling

The present study draws on established theoretical models of reading and spelling to examine the predictors of literacy attainment in EFL learners. Specifically, the framework integrates perspectives on (a) reading comprehension, guided by the Simple View of Reading Turning first to reading comprehension (a), it is widely recognized as one of the most crucial foundations of academic achievement. Reading comprehension involves the integration of diverse cognitive and linguistic skills, making its development a complex process that has prompted the formulation of various theoretical models. A foundational perspective is offered by the SVR (Gough & Tunmer, 1986; Hoover & Gough, 1990), which conceptualizes reading comprehension as the joint product of decoding and language comprehension. SVR is a widely used and influential model in reading research, with substantial empirical support across languages, orthographies, and learner populationsincluding L2 and EFL contexts (Lee et al., 2022). The Direct and Indirect Effects Model of Reading (DIER; Kim, 2017) has expanded the SVR and highlights the contributions of foundational language skills (e.g., vocabulary and grammatical knowledge), upper-level language skills (e.g., word reading and listening comprehension), and cognitive abilities (e.g., WM) to reading comprehension outcomes. Core language resources support the construction of an initial textbase representation, enabling learners to understand word meanings and sentence structures. Building on this linguistic foundation, cognitive skills such as WM play a pivotal role in integrating and manipulating linguistic information during reading (e.g., Chen et al., 2015). The DIER model is especially useful in contexts such as EFL, where limited L2 exposure and weaker decoding skills can make vocabulary and grammar particularly salient predictors of reading comprehension. Empirical studies consistently indicate that, during the initial years of schooling, decoding skills explain most of the variance in reading comprehension outcomes (Catts, 2018). As learners advance, however, foundational language skillsparticularly L2 receptive vocabulary (r = .79) and L2 grammar knowledge (r = .85)become increasingly prominent predictors of L2 reading comprehension outcomes (Jeon & Yamashita, 2014). These findings support a theoretical framework in which cognitive resources (e.g., WM) and linguistic resources (e.g., vocabulary and grammar) are conceptualized as complementary foundations of reading comprehension, particularly in the context of L2 learning and among developing readers (Juffs & Harrington, 2011; Vettori et al., 2024).

Turning to (b) reading fluency: While models of reading comprehension emphasize the interaction of linguistic and cognitive processes in constructing meaning, complementary perspectives focus on the fluency with which these processes are executed. Within second language acquisition frameworks, automaticity theory (DeKeyser, 2001; Segalowitz, 2003) emphasizes the importance of efficient, accurate, and rapid word recognition as a foundation for fluent reading. Within reading research, fluency is typically defined as the ability to translate written text into oral language with both speed and accuracy. It is widely regarded as a key indicator of the extent to which reading sub-processessuch as phonological decoding, word recognition, and syntactic parsinghave become automatized and well-coordinated in the L2 (Grabe, 2010). Both oral and silent reading fluency are considered critical, as fluent reading reflects the successful integration of lower-level processes and provides the cognitive efficiency needed to support higher-level comprehension (Grabe & Yamashita, 2022). For EFL learners, these processes have to be (re-)automatized in English, regardless of L1 reading skill, because orthographic and phonological processing are language-specific (Koda, 2005).

In this framework, reading fluency functions not only as an outcome of the acquisitional progress in decoding and word recognition, but also as a bridge to successful comprehension. This dual role makes reading fluency a central component of L2 literacy development, which is particularly relevant in EFL contexts where learners must allocate substantial cognitive resources to both speed and accuracy of processing. Although much of the foundational fluency research has stemmed from English L1 contexts, a growing body of work in L2 and EFL settings affirms the central role that fluency plays for L2 learners. For example, research suggests that oral reading fluencymeasured in terms of rate and accuracy, as in the study at handis associated with both vocabulary development and reading comprehension among L2 readers (Grabe, 2010; Jeon, 2012; Taguchi & Gorsuch, 2012). Psycholinguistic models of L2 development emphasize that building fluency allows learners to process larger linguistic units more rapidly and with greater accuracy, thus supporting overall reading proficiency in a foreign language (Segalowitz, 2003).

Finally, with respect to (c) spelling and pseudoword reading, the present study's focus is informed by the Dual-Route Cascaded model (e.g., Barry, 1994; Coltheart et al., 2001). This model distinguishes between two primary pathways in reading and spelling: the lexical (direct) route and the sublexical (indirect) route. The lexical route provides direct access to stored word representations in the mental lexicon, supporting rapid recognition and accurate spelling of familiar words, including those with irregular or unpredictable graphemephoneme correspondences. By contrast, the sublexical route relies on the serial application of grapheme-phoneme conversion rules, enabling learners to decode or spell unfamiliar words and pseudowords by systematically mapping letters onto sounds. This route is essential for processing novel words or orthographic patterns not yet stored in the mental lexicon, making it particularly relevant for tasks involving pseudoword reading and spelling. Building on these theoretical frameworks, the following section reviews empirical evidence regarding cognitive and linguistic predictors of EFL reading.

Cognitive and Linguistic Predictors of EFL Reading

Cognitive abilities have received increasing attention as predictors of individual differences in L2 reading outcomes. Among the cognitive predictors, PA has received particular attention. PA refers to the ability to perceive, identify, and manipulate the sound structure of spoken language at different grain sizes, including syllables, onsets and rimes, and phonemes. While PA is widely recognized as a foundational skill for word reading in L1 English (e.g., Melby-Lervåg et al., 2012), its impact on EFL and English as a Second Language (ESL) word reading appears to be more moderate (Murphy Odo, 2021), and evidence for a direct contribution to EFL reading comprehension remains limited (Huo & Wang, 2017). By contrast, both studies provide strong evidence that PA is a key predictor of L2 pseudoword (or nonword) reading accuracy, as advanced PA skills facilitate the application of grapheme-phoneme correspondence rules to unfamiliar letter strings. However, transfer from L1 German PA to L2 English reading is not necessarily straightforward. Core aspects of PA, such as the ability to identify, segment, and manipulate phonological units, are considered language-general and may transfer across alphabetic languages (Melby-Lervåg & Lervåg, 2011). At the same time, German and English differ substantially in orthographic depth and phonological structure. German has relatively consistent grapheme-phoneme correspondences for reading, whereas English is less transparent and requires learners to deal with inconsistent mappings, larger grain-size units, and language-specific orthographic patterns such as vowel and consonant digraphs, or silent-letter patterns (Landerl, 2005; Seymour et al., 2003). In addition, English includes phonemes and contrasts that are absent or less salient in German, such as /θ/or /ð/. These differences may constrain how directly L1 phonological skills can be applied to English word and pseudoword reading. Thus, although PA is a robust predictor of pseudoword reading, its contribution may vary depending on the structural and phonological overlap between the L1 and L2. This assumption is consistent with the Linguistic Coding Differences Hypothesis (Sparks & Ganschow, 1991), which proposes that individual differences in foreign-language learning partly reflect learners' established L1 linguistic coding abilities. From this perspective, phonological abilities assessed in the L1 may provide resources for L2 reading.

PSTM is another cognitive factor implicated in L2 literacy. It supports the temporary maintenance of phonological information, which is relevant when learners decode unfamiliar words and when they hold spoken or written language in mind during comprehension (Farnia & Geva, 2013; Walter, 2008). PSTM has been linked to pseudoword reading accuracy (Geva & Siegel, 2000; Geva & Yaghoub Zadeh, 2006). The strength of this association may depend on how PSTM is measured. Nonword repetition draws on the sound structure of a specific language and is therefore more language-sensitive, whereas digit span relies on highly familiar items and is likely to index a more language-general capacity (Gathercole & Baddeley, 1990). In the present study, PSTM was assessed with a digit span task in L1 German.

WM and NVI are two further cognitive resources linked to L2 reading comprehension, although the evidence for WM is mixed. According to Kim's (2017) DIER model, WM allows readers to hold and manipulate linguistic information during sentence and discourse processing, which supports inference generation and the integration of information across clauses. A meta-analysis found moderate associations between WM and several reading components (Peng et al., 2018), and positive associations with L2 reading comprehension have been reported in children and adults (Farnia & Geva, 2013; Jeon & Yamashita, 2014; Juffs & Harrington, 2011; Vettori et al., 2024). Other findings are less consistent, however.

Meta-analyses show that the WM-L2 reading relationship varies with methodological factors such as the language of the WM task, measurement reliability, and task type (In'nami et al., 2022; Shin, 2020). NVI, often understood as fluid reasoning ability (e.g., Schneider & McGrew, 2018), may support comprehension through abstract thinking, pattern recognition, and the construction of situation models from text, particularly when vocabulary and background knowledge are limited, as is common in EFL learners (e.g., Motallebzadeh & Tabatabaee Yazdi, 2016). Importantly, the predictive value of both constructs may also reflect their shared cognitive basis. A large body of research suggests that the limited capacity of WM (i.e., working memory capacity) is strongly associated with measures of reasoning ability (Kyllonen & Christal, 1990; Oberauer et al., 2005), which is often measured using non-verbal scales like figural matrices (e.g., Raven, 1990). Thus, the predictive effects of both WM and NVI on L2 reading comprehension reported in the literature may reflect their shared underlying mechanism, rather than two distinct sources of cognitive support. Although not assessed in the present study, rapid automatized naming (RAN) is a well-established predictor of reading fluency, as it indexes the speed of retrieving phonological codes for familiar visual symbols, a process that underlies efficient word recognition (e.g., Geva & Yaghoub Zadeh, 2006).

Beyond cognitive factors, linguistic resources play a crucial role in ESL and EFL reading comprehension and fluency. L2 vocabulary knowledge is a well-established predictor of reading comprehension, as lexical knowledge provides the foundation for accessing and constructing meaning from text (Nation, 2009; Vettori et al., 2024; Yamashita, 2022; Zhang & Zhang, 2020). A robust vocabulary also facilitates the rapid and accurate recognition of words, thereby supporting greater reading fluency (Grabe, 2010; Jeon, 2012; Taguchi & Gorsuch, 2012). In addition to vocabulary, L2 grammatical knowledge has been shown to make an independent contribution to reading comprehension (Jeon & Yamashita, 2014; Vettori et al., 2024). Orthographic knowledge, that is, familiarity with the letter patterns and spelling conventions of a writing system, is a further key resource, particularly for reading fluency and spelling. According to the self-teaching hypothesis (Share, 1995), each successful decoding of an unfamiliar word helps readers build word-specific orthographic representations, which support fast and accurate word recognition (Ehri, 2014) and, in turn, spelling.

Cognitive and Linguistic Predictors of EFL Spelling

Research on the predictors of L2 spelling comes mainly from North American ESL contexts, and evidence on young learners in non-immersive EFL settings is scarce. Among cognitive skills, PA is the one most consistently linked to L2 spelling. Stronger PA has been associated with more accurate spelling in ESL learners, particularly in the early stages of learning, when children must master unfamiliar phoneme-grapheme correspondences (Harrison et al., 2016; Jongejan et al., 2007; Keilty & Harrison, 2015; Yeung et al., 2013).

PSTM may support the retention of novel phonological sequences and thus the spelling of unfamiliar words (Baddeley et al., 1998), and nonword repetition has been linked to spelling accuracy in young ESL learners (Zaretsky, 2020).

The contributions of WM and NVI are less clear. Models of writing assign WM a role in coordinating orthographic, phonological, and morphological information (Hayes, 1996; Kellogg et al., 2013), but its specific contribution to L2 spelling accuracy has received little attention (Kormos, 2023). In ESL learners, word-level skills have been linked to languageindependent processes such as WM and PA (Geva & Siegel, 2000; Lesaux & Siegel, 2003).

For NVI, direct L2 evidence is lacking, but L1 studies have linked it to early spelling in English-speaking (Caravolas et al., 2001) and German-speaking children (Schneider & Niklas, 2017; Zarić et al., 2021).

Among linguistic predictors, vocabulary is a key correlate of general EFL writing proficiency (Miralpeix & Muñoz, 2018). It may also support spelling, because mapping sounds onto letters is aided by knowledge of a word's meaning and by orthographic learning through repeated, meaningful exposure. Evidence for grammar is inconsistent, partly because "grammar" is defined and measured differently across studies: syntactic knowledge predicted spelling in Canadian ESL kindergarteners (Keilty & Harrison, 2015), but other studies found no association (Harrison et al., 2016; Jongejan et al., 2007). Earlier analyses of overlapping subsamples from the present project were only partly consistent: in a mixed-effects analysis, both vocabulary and grammar predicted spelling (Mlakar et al., 2025a), whereas in a multiple regression on a smaller sample, only grammar made a unique contribution (Mlakar et al., 2025b). Whether and how linguistic knowledge contributes to EFL spelling therefore remains open.

The Present Study

Little is known about how cognitive and linguistic skills predict specific literacy outcomes in young EFL learners in Germany, who begin formal English instruction in Grade 3 (age 8-9), often before their L1 literacy is fully established. The present study therefore examines to what extent cognitive skills (NVI, PSTM, PA, WM) and linguistic skills (L2 receptive grammar and vocabulary), assessed at the beginning of Grade 3, predict six L2 English literacy outcomes at the end of Grade 4: reading comprehension, pseudoword reading, oral reading rate and accuracy, and real-word and pseudoword spelling. More specifically, we addressed four main research questions. We provide all materials necessary to reproduce the analyses in an online repository: https://osf.io/wvdyt/overview?view_only=c6c340bd9eed4a78952eb8950533e5b4

Research Question 1

To what extent do cognitive abilities (NVI, PSTM, PA, WM) and linguistic skills (L2 grammar and vocabulary) at the beginning of Grade 3 predict L2 English reading comprehension at the end of Grade 4?

Based on the Simple View of Reading and its extensions (Gough & Tunmer, 1986; Kim, 2017), we expect that L2 vocabulary will be a strong predictor of reading comprehension (Nation, 2009; Vettori et al., 2024; Yamashita, 2022; Zhang & Zhang, 2020), with grammar knowledge possibly making an additional contribution (Jeon & Yamashita, 2014; Vettori et al., 2024). Evidence for WM is mixed: it is theoretically relevant to integrating information in text (Farnia & Geva, 2013; Kim, 2017), but meta-analyses show that the WM-L2 reading relationship varies considerably (In'nami et al., 2022; Shin, 2020). We therefore treat the role of WM as exploratory.

Research Question 2

Which cognitive and linguistic skills predict L2 pseudoword reading?

Reading pseudowords requires decoding without lexical support, that is, converting graphemes into phonemes and blending them (Coltheart et al., 2001). In line with metaanalyses, we therefore expect PA to predict pseudoword reading accuracy (Huo & Wang, 2017; Murphy Odo, 2021), given its role in grapheme-phoneme conversion (Geva & Siegel, 2000; Lesaux & Siegel, 2003; Ziegler & Goswami, 2005). We further anticipate that PSTM will support pseudoword reading, as it is involved in maintaining phonological information during decoding (Gathercole & Baddeley, 1990).

Research Question 3

Which cognitive and linguistic skills predict L2 English oral reading fluency (rate and accuracy)?

We hypothesize that L2 vocabulary knowledge will predict reading fluency outcomes (Grabe, 2010; Jeon, 2012; Taguchi & Gorsuch, 2012). A rich vocabulary enables rapid lexical access, reducing processing time for familiar words.

Research Question 4

Which cognitive and linguistic skills predict L2 English real-word and pseudoword spelling accuracy?

Based on dual-route accounts of spelling (e.g., Barry, 1994), PA is expected to predict both real-word and pseudoword spelling because both require the analysis of phonological forms and their mapping onto graphemes (e.g., Jongejan et al., 2007). We hypothesize that vocabulary knowledge contributes particularly to real-word spelling (Jongejan et al., 2007; Zaretsky, 2020), which draws on stored lexical and orthographic representations. Given the limited or inconsistent EFL evidence concerning PSTM, WM, NVI, and grammar, their contributions will be examined exploratorily.

Method

Participants and English learning context

The sample consisted of N = 107 young EFL learners from three elementary schools in Lower Saxony, Germany. Key demographic and background characteristics are presented in Table 1. Germanylearners received English lessons twice weekly (45 minutes per session). The teaching approach in all classes followed a communicative language teaching framework and targeted all four primary language skills: listening, speaking, reading, and writing. Parent questionnaires indicated that, before entering Grade 3, participants had very limited exposure to English. Most of learners' contact with English came through L2 teacher input, the course textbooks, and supplementary materials such as children's books or audio resources.

Instruction followed Lower Saxony's elementary school English curriculum (Years 3-4) and the CEFR (Council of Europe, 2020), targeting A1 proficiency in listening, speaking, and reading, with writing approaching A1 by Year 4.

Data Elicitation and Procedure

Our study employed a combination of reading, spelling, cognitive, and linguistic assessments for data collection. Participants were monitored over a two-year period, beginning with the administration of cognitive and linguistic assessments (PA, PSTM, WM, NVI, vocabulary, grammar) at the start of Grade 3 and concluding with the reading and spelling assessments at the end of Grade 4. Testing conditions were carefully controlled: PA, PSTM, WM, oral reading fluency, and pseudoword reading were assessed individually in quiet surroundings, while spelling, reading comprehension, vocabulary/grammar, and NVI measures were administered in group settings. All instructions were provided in German to ensure consistency. The data collection team comprised researchers and graduate students who had received formal training in the administration and scoring of the measures. In the present sample, Test B demonstrated good internal consistency (KR-20 = .88).

Measures

L2 English Pseudoword Reading.

To assess pseudoword reading ability, we created a set of 10 pseudowords derived from real words found in Pirate Pat (Mackinnon et al., 2010). For each pseudoword, one phoneme was altered from a target word in the reading passage, resulting in the following pseudowords: wint, sot, dook, nack, sig, rit, mang, chesp, nid, and nat. Responses were scored as correct only when all phonemes were produced in accordance with the target English pronunciation. German-accented realizations were therefore scored as incorrect. Each correct response was awarded one point, yielding a maximum score of 10.

Interrater agreement between two independent raters was 97%. Internal consistency of the task was acceptable (KR-20 = .71).

L2 English Oral Reading Rate and Accuracy.

Oral reading rate and accuracy were assessed with the children's book Pirate Pat (Mackinnon et al., 2010), presented as a plain 140-word text without illustrations or visual cues. Each participant read the text aloud individually, with readings audio-recorded for later analysis. No practice trial was provided. From the recordings, we calculated reading rate (words per minute, WPM) and reading accuracy (words correct per minute, WCPM). Interrater agreement between two independent raters was 95% across all recordings.

L2 English Real-Word Spelling.

This task assessed participants' ability to spell English words with phonological and orthographic features unfamiliar to L1 German speakers.

The 23 target words were grouped into four categories: (1) eight with non-German phonemes (/æ/, /θ/, /ð/, /ɒ/); (2) four with unique English orthographic patterns (e.g., silent <e> in like, time; <sh> in shirt, shop); (3) seven high-frequency words with both regular and irregular phoneme-grapheme correspondences; and (4) four with non-novel phonemes (/e/, initial /h/).

Words were played as British English audio, each presented first in isolation, then in a sentence, and again in isolation. Participants wrote each word. Each fully correct spelling received one point.

L2 English Pseudoword Spelling.

This task paralleled the real-word spelling test.

Pseudowords were created from categories 1, 2, and 4 of the real-word test (e.g., hab from had, trog from frog), while minimizing orthographic ambiguity. Sixteen pseudowords were presented via audio recordings and transcribed by participants. Responses were scored as correct or incorrect.

Predictor Measures Nonverbal Intelligence. General cognitive abilities in logical thinking and reasoning were assessed using the standardized matrices subtest of the Basisdiagnostik umschriebener Entwicklungsstörungen im Grundschulalter (BUEGA; Esser et al., 2008). In this task, learners completed incomplete matrices by selecting the correct element from five to eight response options. The maximum possible score was 41.

Phonological Short-Term Memory.

PSTM was assessed using the Digit Span Forward (DSF) subtest of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children -Fourth Edition (WISC-IV; Petermann & Petermann, 2011). Children repeated sequences of digits in the same order as presented. The task began with two-digit sequences and increased progressively in length.

Each correct response was awarded one point. Two trials were administered per sequence length, and testing was discontinued after failure on both trials of the same length. Raw scores reflected the total number of correct sequences.

L1 German Phonological Awareness.

PA at the phoneme level was assessed using the Test of Basic Competencies for Reading and Spelling Skills (BAKO 1-4; Stock et al., 2017).

Three subtests were administered: nonword segmentation, vowel substitution, and vowel length. The maximum possible score was 30.

Working Memory.

Children's WM was assessed using two subtests of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children -Fourth Edition (WISC-IV; Petermann & Petermann, 2011):

Digit Span Backwards (DSB) and Letter-Number Sequencing (LNS). In the DSB, children were asked to repeat sequences of digits in reverse order; in the LNS, participants recalled intermixed sequences of numbers and letters, placing numbers in ascending and letters in alphabetical order. Each correct response received one point. Two trials were given per sequence length, and testing was discontinued after failure on both trials of the same length.

Raw scores reflected the total number of correct responses.

L2 English Receptive Grammar Knowledge.

Participants' receptive grammar knowledge was assessed using the ELIAS Grammar Test II (Kersten et al., 2012), which includes 72 items covering 12 grammatical phenomena (e.g., possessive pronouns, negation, passive). The test is designed to assess both morphosyntactic and syntactic knowledge, targeting learners' ability to process grammatical morphemes (such as plural and possessive markers) as well as to comprehend a range of English sentence structures (e.g., word order, passive, and relative clauses). Learners listened to grammatical prompts and selected the matching picture from three options.

L2 English Receptive Vocabulary Knowledge.

The British Picture Vocabulary Scale III (Dunn et al., 2009) test was used to assess the participants' receptive (hearing) vocabulary.

The paper/pen test was administered in a group setting using a recorded format. Children listened to words from the recording and marked the correct image (from four options) that matched the spoken word. Sets A to J were used (120 items).

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025) with RStudio 2025.05.1+513 (Posit team, 2025). First, we used suitable descriptive statistics for each of the variables of interest. We calculated bivariate linear correlations between all variables in the data. The goal was to examine the longitudinal relationships between early cognitive-linguistic skills and subsequent literacy development in young German EFL learners. To investigate these associations, we fitted linear mixed-effects models with the package lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) to predict all six literacy outcomes at the end of Grade 4 (reading comprehension, pseudoword reading, reading rate, reading accuracy, real-word spelling, and pseudoword spelling) from cognitive (NVI, PSTM, PA, WM) and linguistic measures (grammar, vocabulary) assessed at the beginning of Grade 3. Sociodemographic variables (age, gender, and bi-/monolingual background) were included as covariates. The nested data structure (students within classes) was accounted for by specifying random intercepts for classes. To quantify the degree of clustering, we computed the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for each outcome. Degrees of freedom for the fixed effects were approximated using Satterthwaite's method (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). Because the random intercepts were estimated from only five classes, we conducted a robustness check in which all six models were re-estimated with class entered as a fixed effect (dummy-coded) and with cluster-robust standard errors clustered by class. Homoscedasticity and normality of residues were tested graphically. Multicollinearity was tested using the variance inflation factor (VIF).

Model fit was evaluated using conditional and marginal R² and a t-test for each predictor.

Because six outcomes were modelled with a common set of predictors, we additionally controlled the false discovery rate across the focal cognitive-linguistic predictor tests using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

Results

Descriptive statistics for all variables are presented in Table 2, and zero-order correlations among predictors and outcomes are provided in Table 3, offering an overview of the sample characteristics and the relationships between variables. All VIF values were well below standard thresholds (maximum VIF = 1.58), indicating no evidence of problematic collinearity. Thus, the lack of significant WM effects in the presence of NVI cannot be attributed to shared variance. All plots for homoscedasticity and normality of residues were found to be inconspicuous; Shapiro-Wilk tests on the residuals were non-significant for all six models (all W ≥ .98, all p ≥ .25). Effect sizes (R²) for the mixed-effects models are interpreted using field-specific benchmarks, with values of .20, .35, and .50 corresponding to small, medium, and large effects, respectively (Botes et al., 2025). The ICCs indicated that the proportion of variance located at the class level was negligible for oral reading rate, oral reading accuracy, and reading comprehension (ICC = .006-.029), but more substantial for pseudoword reading, real-word spelling, and pseudoword spelling (ICC = .168-.182). The inclusion of random intercepts for classes was therefore particularly warranted for the latter three outcomes. Regarding the predictive models, L2 English reading comprehension was significantly predicted by NVI, PA, and L2 English vocabulary. Here, a total of 44.4% of the variance were explained by the fixed effects as indicated by the marginal R² value. L2 English pseudoword reading was significantly predicted by PSTM, accounting for 12.4% of variance explained by the fixed effects (Table 4). Neither L2 English oral reading rate nor accuracy was significantly predicted by any of the variables included in the linear mixed model (all p > .05). This was confirmed by low values for the conditional and marginal R² scores for both models (Table 5). Real-word spelling was significantly predicted by L2 English grammar and vocabulary knowledge (marginal R² = 23.3%), while pseudoword spelling was predicted by NVI, with fixed effects explaining 13.5% of variance (see Table 6). Additionally, when the false discovery rate was controlled across the 36 focal predictor tests, the effects of NVI (padj = .036) and PA (padj = .047) on reading comprehension remained significant. The effects of L2 vocabulary on reading comprehension, of L2 grammar and vocabulary on real-word spelling, of NVI on pseudoword spelling, and of PSTM on pseudoword reading were no longer significant after correction (padj = .072-.123) and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This study aimed to deepen our understanding of the foundational knowledge bases underlying early literacy skills in young EFL learners in Germany. We examined the predictors of L2 English reading comprehension, pseudoword reading, oral reading fluency, and both real-word and pseudoword spelling.

Addressing research question 1 (predictors of L2 reading comprehension), NVI and PA emerged as robust cognitive predictors that held even after we accounted for the number of predictors tested. L2 English vocabulary knowledge was a further predictor in the primary models, though one that did not reach significance after correction and therefore warrants investigation. These findings suggest that both domain-general (NVI) and domain-specific (PA) skills make distinct contributions to early EFL reading comprehension, reflecting the demands of reading in a low-exposure context, where learners must coordinate developing that we assessed WM in German. This choice may have contributed to the weak association with L2 reading outcomes, as previous research suggests that the transfer of WM skills across languages can be limited (Alptekin & Erçetin, 2010).

Turning to research question 2 (predictors of L2 pseudoword reading), PSTM was the only significant predictor of English pseudoword reading in the mixed-effects model. The effect held when class was modelled as a fixed effect, but it weakened once the number of predictors tested was considered, so we treat it as preliminary. The finding is consistent with the view that PSTM supports sublexical decoding by holding phonological representations in place (Gathercole & Baddeley, 1990) while graphemes are sequentially converted and blended into a whole-word pronunciation (Melby-Lervåg et al., 2012), and with dual-route models in which unfamiliar items are read via the sublexical (phonological) route (Coltheart et al., 2001). PSTM was assessed with German digit sequences, whereas the outcome required English-accurate pronunciation. The association therefore suggests that a languageindependent phonological capacity contributes to L2 decoding, in line with the Linguistic Coding Differences Hypothesis (Sparks & Ganschow, 1991) and with accounts of crosslanguage transfer. Contrary to findings from previous meta-analyses with elementary school EFL learners (Huo & Wang, 2017) and both EFL and ESL learners (Murphy Odo, 2021), PA did not predict pseudoword reading, although the same PA measure predicted reading comprehension. Although its internal consistency was acceptable (KR-20 = .71) and scores covered nearly the full range (0-9; M = 5.34, SD = 2.02), the pseudoword task contained only ten items, limiting measurement precision and statistical power.

Addressing research question 3 (predictors of L2 oral reading rate and accuracy), we expected vocabulary knowledge to play a significant role (Grabe, 2010; Jeon, 2012; Nation, 2009; Taguchi & Gorsuch, 2012). This expectation received only weak support, and none of the cognitive or linguistic variables significantly predicted fluency. Vocabulary, grammar, and PA correlated with rate and/or accuracy at the bivariate level (rs = .19-.26), but these associations did not hold in the multivariable models. One possible explanation is that the T1 measures did not capture key determinants of reading fluency in the EFL context, including individual variation in L2 exposure and reading practice (Grabe & Yamashita, 2022; Taguchi & Gorsuch, 2012). Where print exposure is limited, orthographic learning is particularly relevant. According to the self-teaching hypothesis (Share, 1995), successful decoding of unfamiliar words allows readers to build word-specific orthographic representations, which, over repeated encounters, support fast and accurate word recognition. Because oral reading rate and accuracy depend on such representations, individual differences in orthographic knowledge may have accounted for fluency beyond vocabulary and phonological skills. We did not assess orthographic knowledge, so this account remains untested. The same applies to RAN, which has been linked to L2 reading rate and accuracy (e.g., Geva & Yaghoub Zadeh, 2006).

Turning to research question 4 (predictors of L2 real-word and pseudoword spelling), receptive grammar and vocabulary knowledge predicted real-word spelling, while NVI predicted pseudoword spelling. These effects held when class was modelled as a fixed effect, but they were no longer statistically significant once we accounted for the number of predictors tested; we therefore interpret them as preliminary. With this caveat, our results suggest that L2 real-word spelling might depend on language-specific knowledge. This aligns with models of spelling, which emphasize the integration of semantic, morphosyntactic, phonological, and orthographic knowledge (Apel et al., 2004; Treiman, 2017) and with Ehri's (2014) account of orthographic mapping, in which spellings are stored in memory as links between pronunciations, meanings, and letter sequences. We propose that L2 vocabulary knowledge contributes to this process in two ways. First, familiar words provide phonological and semantic representations that can support the retention of their spellings, whereas unknown words offer little to anchor them (Ehri, 2014; Share, 1995). Second, lexical familiarity may facilitate the identification and segmentation of spoken word forms during dictation, particularly when these contain phonemes absent from German (e.g., /θ/, /ð/). The relationship between L2 vocabulary and spelling has been documented in ESL contexts in North America (Jongejan et al., 2007; Zaretsky, 2020), but evidence from EFL contexts remains limited.

Receptive L2 grammar also predicted real-word spelling, consistent with some ESL research (e.g., Keilty & Harrison, 2015) but not with others (Harrison et al., 2016; Jongejan et al., 2007). Given that the spelling items were predominantly short and monomorphemic (e.g., like, time, shirt, shop), a direct contribution of morphosyntactic knowledge appears unlikely.

We suggest instead that the ELIAS grammar score may index general L2 proficiency, which may help learners apply English sound-spelling conventions, such as the representations of /æ/, /ɒ/, /θ/, /ð/, the digraph <sh>, and the silent-<e> pattern. Moreover, the grammar score combined morphological and syntactic items, so we cannot determine which aspects of grammatical knowledge drove the effect.

Contrary to our hypothesis, PA did not predict L2 spelling in the present study. PA was nevertheless related to both real-word and pseudoword outcomes at the bivariate level (r = .32 and r = .25), and it predicted L2 reading comprehension in the model, which is consistent with cross-linguistic transfer of PA from German to English. Transfer of PA is unlikely to be complete, because PA has both universal and language-specific components.

The ability to attend to and manipulate speech sounds is largely language-independent, but phoneme inventories and sound-to-letter mappings differ between orthographies. German has a shallow orthography with consistent grapheme-phoneme correspondences, whereas English is deep and contains phonemes absent from German, such as /θ/, /ð/, /æ/, and /ɒ/ (Landerl, 2005). Many of our real-word spelling items targeted exactly these sounds and Englishspecific patterns (e.g., silent <e>, <sh>), for which German PA offers little direct support.

English spelling may therefore depend more on language-specific knowledge, such as vocabulary and grammar, which is consistent with our results. Although PA is essential for spelling in L1 German (e.g., Pfost, 2015; Sigmund et al., 2024), its unique role in early L2 English spelling therefore remains open and should be tested with PA measured in both languages.

For pseudoword spelling, NVI was the only significant predictor. One possible explanation is that abstract reasoning helps learners infer and apply phoneme-grapheme correspondences when lexical support is unavailable, in line with dual-route accounts of spelling (Barry, 1994; Coltheart et al., 2001). Spelling pseudowords requires analyzing novel sound sequences, inferring likely correspondences between phonemes and graphemes, and applying previously acquired orthographic rules, which may draw on analytic and problemsolving skills.

Limitations

There were several limitations in our study that warrant consideration. The pseudoword reading task comprised only 10 monosyllabic items. Although its internal consistency was acceptable (KR-20 = .71), it is too brief to provide a representative sample of English grapheme-phoneme correspondences and phonemic diversity. Moreover, our findings regarding the significant predictive effects of L2 grammar on spelling in our regression analyses warrant further investigation. The ELIAS grammar test we administered covered both morphological elements (e.g., plural -s, 3rd person -s) and syntactic aspects (e.g., Subject-Verb-Object structures, negation, passive forms). This heterogeneity makes it difficult to draw explicit conclusions about the relative contributions of morphological versus syntactic knowledge to spelling. Another limitation concerns the language of assessment, as PA was measured only in German. To more fully understand the role of PA in EFL reading and spelling, future studies should include PA tasks in both the L1 and L2. WM was also assessed in German, which may have underestimated its potential impact on L2 reading comprehension (Alptekin & Erçetin, 2010). Furthermore, we relied on only two WM tasks (Digit Span Backwards and Letter-Number Sequencing), which may not capture all facets of WM relevant to L2 spelling, such as those assessed by listening span or updating tasks (Kormos, 2023; Peng et al., 2022). Most importantly, we did not assess L2 orthographic knowledge, a strong predictor of reading and spelling. This limits our interpretation, particularly for oral reading fluency, where limited English print exposure may mean that differences in orthographic learning matter (Share, 1995). Another limitation refers to the participant population, which was linguistically heterogeneous: 82 of the 107 learners spoke German at home, and 25 spoke one of 13 other languages. Due to the lack of a formalized measure of linguistic distance, it was not possible to control for the systematic advantages or challenges that specific L1 backgrounds might pose when acquiring EFL reading and spelling skills. Lastly, a further limitation concerns the statistical analyses. The models were based on only five classes, which is a small number of clusters for estimating between-class variance; although the fixed-effect conclusions were supported by using cluster-robust standard errors, the random-effect estimates should be interpreted with caution.

In addition, because the study was not preregistered and multiple predictors were examined across six outcomes, only the effects of NVI and PA on reading comprehension survived correction for multiple testing.

The remaining effects, while consistent with theoretical expectations and prior findings, should be regarded as preliminary and in need of replication.

Conclusion

This study examined which cognitive and linguistic skills at the beginning of Grade 3 predict English reading and spelling at the end of Grade 4 among EFL elementary school learners in Germany. The pattern differed by outcome. PA and NVI predicted reading comprehension, with a smaller contribution of L2 vocabulary. L2 vocabulary and grammar were associated with real-word spelling, whereas NVI was associated with pseudoword spelling, and PSTM with pseudoword reading. The longitudinal association between L1 German PA at the beginning of Grade and EFL reading comprehension at the end of Grade 4 is consistent with evidence that PA can support literacy development across alphabetic writing systems (Melby-Lervåg & Lervåg, 2011). As German and English both use alphabetic scripts, learners may draw on their sensitivity to phonological structures when acquiring English reading skills, despite differences in the consistency of grapheme-phoneme correspondences across German and English.
