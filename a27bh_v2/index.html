Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Illumination has occupied a central position in theories of creativity since Wallas identified it as one of the four phases of the creative process. Although subsequent models have continued to recognize illumination as the moment at which new ideas emerge into awareness, relatively little attention has been devoted to the cognitive processes underlying this transition. At the same time, a parallel literature on insight problem solving has generated extensive evidence describing the processes that precede and accompany the sudden emergence of new ideas, yet these findings have rarely been integrated into broader theories of creativity. This chapter brings together these traditions to reconsider illumination within the broader process of creative idea generation. Building on classical and contemporary theories of creativity, a Dual-Route Model of Creative Idea Generation is proposed. In the continuous route, intermediate states remain accessible to conscious awareness as an idea develops. By contrast, in the discontinuous route, an idea becomes abruptly available to conscious awareness, following processing that is not fully accessible to introspection. Behavioral, metacognitive, and neurocognitive evidence relevant to these different modes of idea emergence is reviewed, alongside their potential relationships with the quality and subsequent memory of the ideas generated. Finally, the broader implications of the framework are considered by examining insight as a possible form of model updating, outlining the main empirical predictions of the Dual-Route Model, and discussing its relevance to creative problem solving in engineering and design.

1 Introduction

How do creative ideas come to mind? This apparently simple question has occupied philosophers, scientists, artists, and psychologists for centuries. Creative work does not always unfold in the same way. Some ideas develop gradually through deliberate reasoning; others emerge suddenly, with little conscious awareness of how they were reached. An engineer might design a new device by progressively modifying its components, testing alternatives, and improving the design step-by-step. In other cases, a solution may arise from suddenly seeing the problem differently, for example, realizing that an assumed constraint is unnecessary or that a component could serve an entirely different function. Both outcomes can be creative, but the experience of reaching them differs. The first experience feels like building an idea progressively; the second, like discovering one. Kuhn (1962) argued that scientific revolutions are driven by discontinuous shifts that give rise to new paradigms, rather than by continuous, cumulative progress within an existing conceptual framework. New paradigms enable researchers to ask new theoretical questions of existing data, moving beyond the "puzzle-solving" of the former paradigm and changing the framework that directs subsequent research. While Kuhn explained scientific progress rather than individual cognition, his account offers a useful parallel for understanding how creative ideas emerge. Some creative ideas develop gradually, much like the cumulative progress of science within an established paradigm; others emerge when the existing conceptual framework shifts abruptly, opening up new questions, interpretations, and solutions to problems. Just as a scientific revolution changes the perspective from which existing knowledge is understood, an insight can suddenly reveal a new way of looking at a familiar problem. Research on insight problem solving offers an experimental literature for investigating how ideas emerge through these two routes and, in particular, the illumination phase described in theories of creative cognition. Wallas (1926) distinguished four stages of creativity: preparation, incubation, illumination, and verification. Preparation involves acquiring relevant knowledge and beginning work on the problem. Incubation refers to an interval during which deliberate work is suspended, allowing for the possibility of continued processing outside awareness. Illumination marks the appearance of the "happy idea," often described as a "flash" of understanding, and verification involves evaluating, refining, and developing the idea toward implementation. Long before modern cognitive psychology, several scholars tried to describe the illumination phase in similar terms. At the end of the nineteenth century, Hermann von Helmholtz, the physicist and physiologist, described how, after investigating a problem thoroughly, "happy ideas come unexpectedly without effort, like an inspiration" (Helmholtz, as quoted in Wallas, 1926, p. 80; Wallas cites Rignano, 1923, pp. 267-268). Others noted that such ideas often arise during a peaceful walk or periods of rest (Ochse, 1990; Wallas, 1926). Einstein and Infeld (1938, p. 4) illustrated the sudden emergence of an idea through Conan Doyle's detective stories: after gathering seemingly unrelated clues, an investigator turns away from the problem. In Einstein and Infeld's account, "[...] plays his violin, or lounges in his armchair enjoying a pipe, when suddenly, by Jove, he has it!" These descriptions capture the phenomenology of illumination remarkably well, but not all creative ideas are characterized by such sudden emergence.

Several subsequent theories elaborated and refined different aspects of Wallas's original framework. Earlier discussions encompassed diverse approaches, including those of Epstein (1990); Milgram (1990), alongside accounts of insight, invention, and creative transformation (Feldman, 1988; Langley and Jones, 1988; Ohlsson, 1984; Perkins, 1988; Simonton, 1988). Campbell (1960) proposed an evolutionary account of creative thought based on blind variation and selective retention. This framework distinguishes the production of alternatives, the selection of successful variants, and the retention of selected outcomes. Building on this approach, Simonton (1988) developed a chance-configuration account in which creativity arises through permutations of mental elements. These elements constitute the basic units available for combination and recombination during the creative process. Through such combinatorial processes, novel configurations may arise, leading to a creative idea. The appearance of such a configuration is not, however, the end of the creative process. The individual must engage in conscious work to transform it into a communication configuration: a symbolic form through which the insight can be expressed and made available to others. Simonton's account therefore distinguishes the emergence of an idea from the deliberate work required to articulate and develop it. Building on the broader distinction between the generation and subsequent selection or development of creative possibilities, Finke et al. (1992) advanced the Geneplore model, which provided a more explicit cognitive account of recurring generative and exploratory activity. Generative processes concern the construction of loosely formulated ideas called preinventive structures and include knowledge retrieval, association, synthesis, transformation, and analogical transfer. These structures are preliminary possibilities rather than fully specified creative products. Exploratory processes concern their examination, interpretation, elaboration, and testing, including the search for useful functions or implications. Exploration can reveal unexpected properties of an initially ambiguous structure and can lead back to further generation or modification. Amabile's componential model likewise describes a creative process involving (a) problem or task identification, (b) preparation through gathering and reactivating relevant information and resources, (c) response generation through seeking and producing possible responses, and (d) response validation and communication through testing those responses against relevant criteria (Amabile, 1988 (Amabile, , 1996)). The resulting outcome can lead to completion or to further work on the task. These activities operate within a broader framework of domain-relevant skills, creativity-relevant processes, task motivation, and the social environment. Amabile's model therefore connects the development of an idea with the knowledge, motivation, and contextual conditions that support its generation and validation. Despite their theoretical differences, these models contributed to a broader distinction between the generation of creative ideas and their subsequent evaluation and development. This distinction is reflected in two-stage and three-stage descriptions of creativity. However, these theories do not explicitly account for the different ways in which some creative ideas emerge into awareness.

Recent models offer a more detailed framework by emphasizing the interacting processes and systems involved in creative idea generation. The dual pathway to creativity model proposed by Nijstad, De Dreu, Rietzschel, and Baas distinguishes cognitive flexibility from cognitive persistence as contributors to creative ideation (Nijstad et al., 2010). Flexibility supports movement between categories and perspectives, whereas persistence supports sustained exploration within a smaller set of possibilities. Both can produce original and appropriate ideas. Beaty and colleagues have integrated cognitive and neuroscientific findings to explain creative thought in terms of dynamic interactions among large-scale brain networks (Beaty et al., 2015 (Beaty et al., , 2016)). In particular, their work highlights cooperation between the default mode network, associated with internally generated thought, and executive control networks, which support the regulation of cognitive activity in relation to task goals and constraints. Complementing this account, Benedek et al. (2016) distinguished the neural mechanisms associated with internally directed attention from those supporting selfgenerated thought. Together, these models highlight different dynamics of creative cognition, including cognitive flexibility and persistence, exploration and exploitation, and the interaction between spontaneous and controlled processes. Nonetheless, how these cognitive processes lead to the emergence of an idea into conscious awareness remains unclear.

Other frameworks have focused more directly on the cognitive processes underlying illumination. The explicit-implicit interaction (EII) theory of Hélie and Sun (2010), for example, explicitly describes "illumination" and proposes that interactions between explicit and implicit processing can explain how an idea becomes suddenly available to consciousness. Its implementation in the CLARION cognitive architecture provides a mechanistic framework of creative problem solving rather than simply another description of stages. Re-examining Wallas's original model, Sadler-Smith (2015) argued that the familiar four-stage model does not fully capture Wallas's description of the creative process and restored intimation as a distinct stage between incubation and illumination. Intimation refers to a state of fringe consciousness in which an idea may be sensed as approaching before it becomes fully available, and Sadler-Smith situated incubation, intimation, and illumination along increasing levels of proximity to consciousness. This account therefore provides an important precedent for distinguishing the processes preceding an idea from its eventual emergence into conscious awareness. These contributions establish that the relationship between cognitive processing and conscious awareness has already been recognized as central to illumination. What remains less systematically examined is the distinction between ideas that emerge suddenly into awareness, and those that develop gradually through a consciously accessible sequence of intermediate steps. A further question concerns whether the way an idea emerges influences its quality. Consider again the engineer's example introduced earlier. One solution may take shape through the continuous modification of a design, whereas another may emerge after a sudden change in how the problem is represented. Do these different ways of arriving at a solution produce equally good ideas? Is one more likely to yield solutions that are original, useful, or correct? And does the way an idea comes to mind influence how its creator evaluates it or makes subsequent decisions involving risk? These questions are particularly consequential in design and engineering, where generating an idea is rarely sufficient: the resulting solution must ultimately satisfy practical constraints and, in many cases, work.

In sum, these contributions show that the correspondence between illumination and insight is not new, and that mechanisms underlying the sudden emergence of ideas have already been proposed. Nevertheless, these theoretical connections have not been followed by a comparable integration of research on creative cognition and insight. Research on creative cognition has predominantly investigated the generation, evaluation, and development of creative ideas, whereas insight research has accumulated extensive evidence concerning the processes that precede, accompany, and follow their sudden emergence into awareness. The present chapter brings these two research traditions together to reconsider illumination within the broader process of creative idea generation. Drawing on contemporary insight research, it examines the distinction between the gradual development of creative ideas and their sudden emergence into conscious awareness, and whether these different trajectories have consequences for the quality, evaluation, and subsequent development of creative ideas.

2 From Parallel Traditions to an Integrated Framework

During the twentieth century, two major research traditions emerged to explain how novel ideas are generated. One tradition developed within creativity research and focused on creative ideation, typically operationalized through divergent thinking (DT) tasks. Originating from Guilford's theory (Guilford, 1967), this tradition emphasized the generation of multiple original ideas in response to open-ended problems, as operationalized, for example, in the Alternative Uses Task (AUT) and the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking (Torrance, 1966). This framework focused on the cognitive processes supporting the generation and evaluation of multiple ideas, where creativity was conceptualized primarily in terms of fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration. A second tradition developed within the problem-solving literature. Introduced by the Gestalt school of psychology, insight research sought to explain how solutions can emerge through a sudden restructuring of the initial problem representation rather than through incremental, stepby-step search (Köhler, 1925; Maier, 1930; for a review, see Vitello and Salvi, 2023). Importantly, insight was not originally defined in terms of convergent thinking (CT). Early research relied on classic insight problems, such as the nine-dot and candle problems (Duncker, 1945), because these problems were assumed to require a change in representation or the relaxation of implicit constraints. Over time, however, insight research progressively shifted toward short, repeatable tasks with a single correct solution, including the Remote Associates Test (RAT), Compound Remote Associates (CRA) problems, anagrams, and rebus puzzles (Bowden et al., 2005; Bowden and Jung-Beeman, 2007; Salvi et al., 2016b Salvi et al., , 2020b)). This shift was primarily methodological rather than theoretical: these tasks provided many trials within a single experimental session, making it possible to distinguish insight from step-by-step solutions through self-reported Aha! experiences and to combine behavioral measures with neurophysiological and neuroimaging methods. The methodological choices made within the two traditions gradually produced what came to appear as a theoretical dichotomy. Creativity researchers predominantly used open-ended DT tasks, whereas insight researchers predominantly used single-solution CT tasks. Consequently, task structure became conflated with cognitive process: creative ideation became associated with DT, whereas insight became associated with CT. Yet, neither association necessarily follows from the underlying cognitive processes. DT and CT describe properties of the task and its solution space, particularly whether multiple possible responses or a single target solution are permitted, whereas insight concerns the cognitive processes and subjective experience through which a solution is reached. The resulting DT-CT division therefore reinforced a methodological boundary between phenomena that may share important cognitive mechanisms. As a consequence, creativity and insight research developed along parallel but largely non-interacting theoretical trajectories (Salvi et al., submitted). This methodological separation may have exaggerated the differences between the two traditions while obscuring their shared cognitive foundations. In particular, it may have discouraged researchers from examining whether the mechanisms traditionally investigated in insight problem solving also contribute to open-ended creative idea generation.

Both creative ideation and insight involve navigation of a semantic search space, retrieval and recombination of information from long-term memory, overcoming dominant associations, and the construction of representations that extend beyond immediately available information (Bowden and Jung Beeman, 1998; Jung-Beeman et al., 2004; Kenett et al., 2014; Kenett and Faust, 2019; Luchini et al., 2023; Madore et al., 2019; Mednick, 1962). Indeed, several influential theorists anticipated this overlap decades ago. Guilford (1950) explicitly suggested that Gestalt notions of reorganization were relevant to creative thought, while Mednick's (1962) associative theory proposed a common mechanism whereby remote associations give rise to novel ideas, whether expressed in open-ended ideation or in problems with a single correct solution. Empirical evidence supports this convergence. Behavioral, neurophysiological, and neuroimaging studies demonstrate substantial overlap between creative ideation and insight across memory retrieval, attentional regulation, cognitive control, semantic network organization, and affective-reward mechanisms (Salvi et al., submitted).

Despite these theoretical and empirical similarities, the methodological separation between creativity and insight research left a seemingly obvious question largely unexplored: can insight occur during DT? Although there is no theoretical reason to restrict insight to problems with a single correct solution, experimental studies have begun to address this question only recently. Research using generative tasks, including the AUT, has shown that Aha! experiences occur during open-ended idea generation and that ideas accompanied by an Aha! tend to receive higher creativity ratings than those generated without one (Moroshkina et al., 2026; Smith et al., 2026; McGuinness et al., 2026). These findings demonstrate that sudden, phenomenologically marked idea emergence is not restricted to convergent problem solving. However, experiencing an Aha! during DT does not indicate that insight has occurred. Thus, before moving forward we need to distinguish several closely related concepts that have often been used interchangeably in the creativity and insight literatures. According to a recent componential definition jointly developed by Salvi, Bhattacharya, and Ormerod (submitted to the Creativity Definitions Consensus project; Salvi et al., 2027), insight is defined as an event characterized by the sudden emergence of an idea, concept, or problem solution that is experienced as correct, novel, and effortless. This is typically accompanied by a distinct shift in understanding and is often marked by an Aha! experience. The definition identifies three overlapping components: phenomenology, process, and product. Phenomenology concerns the subjective experience of suddenness, surprise, pleasure, and clarity (summarized by the Aha! experience). Process refers to the cognitive mechanisms underlying insight, characterized by their relative opacity to introspection and nonlinear dynamics (e.g., nonlinear restructuring). Product concerns the resulting idea or solution, particularly its correctness, novelty, and elegance. The intersections between these components capture additional characteristics: the experience of effortlessness, the feeling of correctness, and the pragmatic fit of the resulting solution. Their convergence characterizes true insight, whereas events involving only one or two components are classified as partial insights. This distinction is particularly relevant to experimental research, where insight is frequently operationalized through self-reported Aha! experiences. Although the Aha! experience captures important phenomenological characteristics of insight, it does not independently establish the cognitive processes underlying the solution.

Within this framework, illumination and insight are not synonymous. Illumination refers to the sudden emergence of a creative idea into conscious awareness (consistent with its original position within Wallas's creative process (Wallas, 1926)) whereas insight encompasses the phenomenological, processing, and product-related characteristics described above. Insights can indeed occur outside the pure creative process, while illumination does not satisfy all the components of insight.

Restructuring, in turn, refers to a change in an existing representation of a problem or situation, a concept originating in the Gestalt tradition and subsequently formalized in cognitive theories of insight (Duncker, 1945; Knoblich et al., 1999; Ohlsson, 1984 Ohlsson, , 1992)). More broadly, restructuring can be understood as a meaningful change in how existing information is reorganized or reinterpreted. While some scholars have argued that the restructuring and Aha! experiences do not always correspond (Cushen and Wiley, 2012; Wiley and Danek, 2024) the two remain often conflated in experimental designs. Many studies investigating insight in CT and DT tasks rely on self-reported Aha! experiences without independently measuring restructuring and vice versa. This distinction is particularly relevant to interpreting recent findings on Aha! experiences during DT.

3 A Dual-Route Model of Creative Idea Generation

Understanding the cognitive processes operating within each creative stage is an important theoretical challenge in creativity research. Pinkow (2023), for example, argued that the creative process continues to contain a "black box," emphasizing the need to move beyond broad stage descriptions toward a more explicit specification of the underlying mechanisms. To address this challenge, Figure 1: Dual-Route Model of Creative Idea Generation. Following preparation, creative ideas may emerge through two routes that differ in the conscious accessibility of intermediate processing. Idea generation is followed by downstream processes, including evaluation, verification, elaboration, and implementation, which may lead to the selection or rejection of an idea or prompt a return to idea generation for further exploration and refinement.

the present framework draws on different theoretical approaches of problem solving. Informationprocessing accounts describe problem solving as a search through intermediate states within a problem space (Newell and Simon, 1972), providing a foundation for understanding incremental, step-by-step solution processes. Research on insight has further examined the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying different forms of problem solving, particularly those in which solutions emerge suddenly, with limited conscious access to the processing that precedes them (Kounios and Beeman, 2014; Metcalfe and Wiebe, 1987; Ohlsson, 1992). The Dual-Route Model integrates these perspectives by specifying the cognitive organization of idea generation within the broader creative process, which includes preparation, idea generation, and the downstream processes through which ideas are evaluated, selected, refined, or rejected. Preparation involves the cognitive and attentional states that may influence subsequent idea generation, including those established before the problem is encountered. The model proposes that ideas can emerge through two routes. The continuous route involves step-by-step exploration in which intermediate processing remains relatively accessible to conscious awareness. The discontinuous route, which encompasses incubation and illumination, involves critical intermediate processing that remains comparatively inaccessible until an idea suddenly becomes consciously available. Ideas emerging through either route may subsequently undergo evaluation, verification, elaboration, and further development, potentially leading to implementation or rejection ( see Figure 1). These downstream processes may feed back into idea generation when a candidate is rejected or modified, or when it prompts further search. The model does not posit two separate idea-generating systems analogous to those proposed in classical dual-process accounts, nor does it map the two routes onto different classes of creativity tasks. Continuous and discontinuous processing instead describe alternative trajectories through which individual candidate ideas may develop and become available to awareness. Accordingly, CT and DT can recruit either route: a convergent problem may be solved through step-by-step analysis or insight, while individual ideas generated during DT may likewise develop through relatively continuous processing or emerge discontinuously.

The EII theory provides an important mechanistic precedent for this account through its implemen-tation within the CLARION cognitive architecture (Hélie and Sun, 2010). EII connects Wallas's stages with interacting explicit and implicit processes. Explicit knowledge is represented through identifiable concepts and rules, whereas implicit knowledge relies on distributed representations supporting associative processing. Both forms of processing can operate simultaneously, and their outputs can be integrated across successive rounds of processing. Within EII, preparation and verification rely more heavily on explicit processing, whereas incubation places greater weight on implicit processing. A candidate becomes reportable when an internal confidence measure crosses a threshold, and a large, sudden increase following integration can correspond to an intense Aha! experience (Hélie and Sun, 2010). The computational model reproduced selected findings concerning incubation, recall, and insight problem solving, providing a concrete example of how ongoing processing can culminate in a sudden conscious outcome without implying that this implementation is the only possible mechanism. The Dual-Route Model builds on this precedent but makes a different distinction. The explicit and implicit levels of CLARION should not be mapped one-to-one onto the continuous and discontinuous routes, because both routes may involve interactions between explicit and implicit processing. Instead, the present framework distinguishes routes according to how their products become available to awareness, separating this temporal distinction from the mechanisms that may produce them. In this respect, it also differs from the flexibility-persistence pathways proposed by Nijstad et al. (2010). Flexible exploration and persistent search describe different ways of navigating an idea space, either of which may contribute to continuous development or culminate in a discontinuous transition. The two routes are therefore not proposed as independent cognitive systems, but as alternative processing dynamics within idea generation.

3.0.1 The Continuous Step-by-Step Route

The continuous route is characterized by intermediate states that remain relatively accessible to awareness, allowing the individual to monitor and guide the development of an idea. Candidate ideas or solutions can be compared with the current representation, revised in light of new information, and further elaborated as search unfolds. Continuity here does not imply that a fully formed idea enters consciousness gradually. Rather, continuity refers to the accessibility of the generative trajectory. Early metacognitive studies provided some of the first empirical evidence for differences in the accessibility of intermediate solution states. Metcalfe (1986a) found that prospective feelings of knowing were poor predictors of subsequent solutions to insight problems, suggesting that people have limited awareness of their progress toward a solution. Subsequently, Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987) examined feelings of warmth, or the subjective sense of approaching a solution, during problem solving. For noninsight problems, warmth ratings increased progressively as participants approached a solution, whereas insight problems were characterized by relatively stable ratings followed by an abrupt increase near solution. These findings provided an early metacognitive basis for distinguishing continuous problem solving, in which progress can be monitored through intermediate states, from solutions that emerge with little anticipation. Further evidence concerning the cognitive processes supporting step-by-step solving comes from studies examining working memory and cognitive load. Fleck (2008) found that working-memory capacity is associated with performance on step-by-step problems, whereas verbal short-term-memory capacity is more closely associated with insight problem solving. These findings suggest that maintaining and manipulating information contributes to step-by-step solution development. More direct evidence comes from Stuyck et al. (2022), who manipulated cognitive load during problem solving and found that increasing load made step-by-step solutions slower and less frequent, whereas insight solutions were comparatively unaffected. This pattern suggests that step-by-step solving depends more strongly on capacity-limited cognitive resources, consistent with the maintenance and monitoring of intermediate solution states. Importantly, step-by-step solving should not be understood as a single, uniform strategy. Using verbal protocols, Fleck and Weisberg (2013) documented different approaches to problem solving, including the application of existing knowledge and heuristic search. Their findings highlight the diversity of processes through which solutions can be developed progressively. Within the present framework, these studies complement the metacognitive evidence by suggesting that the continuous route involves intermediate states that can be monitored and used to guide subsequent processing, supported by working-memory resources. However, the involvement of working memory or cognitive control does not establish that every underlying operation is always consciously accessible. Rather, the defining feature of the continuous route is the relative accessibility of intermediate states as an idea develops. People may deliberately explore possibilities, evaluate alternatives, and progressively develop an idea through conscious thought, while other processes potentially contribute outside awareness. The unconscious analytic thought account proposed by Macchi et al. (2019) suggests that systematic, goal-directed processing may occur without conscious access. More recently, Macchi et al. (2026) found sustained pupil dilation during incubation among participants who subsequently solved an insight problem, consistent with continued cognitive effort despite the suspension of deliberate problem solving. Although these findings concern insight rather than continuous idea generation, they raise the possibility that consciously accessible, step-by-step development may coexist with processing that remains outside awareness. People may not consciously select one route in advance; instead, idea generation may begin through accessible, step-by-step processing, while the relative contributions of accessible and inaccessible processing change throughout the generative episode. Thus, whether and how processing outside awareness contributes to the continuous route remains an important question for future research.

3.0.2 The Discontinuous Route: The Emergence of an Idea into Awareness

The discontinuous route is characterized by limited conscious access to intermediate states, such that an individual may move from having no consciously available candidate idea to its sudden emergence into awareness. Importantly, this discontinuity concerns conscious access rather than necessarily the underlying cognitive processes, which may unfold over time without being accessible as a progressive sequence. Illumination refers to this sudden emergence of a creative idea into awareness, which is regularly accompanied by an Aha! experience, especially when the emerging idea involves a meaningful change in the individual's existing mental representation. Early evidence consistent with this distinction comes from Metcalfe (1986b), whose investigation revealed that feelings of knowing did not reliably predict subsequent success on insight problems. Metcalfe interpreted this pattern as suggesting that insight involves a sudden illumination that cannot be anticipated in advance. Extending this work, Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987) observed an abrupt increase in feelings of warmth immediately before solutions to classic insight problems, contrasting with the gradual progression characteristic of noninsight problem solving. Complementing these subjective reports, Smith and Kounios (1996) investigated whether partial solution information becomes available before an insight using speed-accuracy decomposition. Across two experiments involving anagrams, they found little or no evidence of partial information preceding solution, supporting an all-or-none pattern in which the solution becomes available suddenly, without accessible intermediate results. These findings suggest that, although processing may unfold before an insight, the information generated during this process remains inaccessible until the complete solution emerges into conscious awareness. The suddenness of this transition was captured behaviorally in real time. Laukkonen et al. (2021) introduced a continuous, embodied measure of the Aha! experience using a dynamometer.

Participants rapidly increased their grip force during moments of insight, and the magnitude of these responses was associated with both Aha! intensity and solution accuracy. This method provided a temporally sensitive measure of insight discontinuous unfolding, complementing retrospective reports and allowing the dynamics of the subjective experience to be examined as they unfold. Together, these metacognitive findings support the view that, during discontinuous problem solving, solution-relevant information becomes consciously available in an all-or-none manner.

Incubation Incubation refers to an interval during which a previously encountered task is set aside before work resumes. Several studies have shown that incubation can facilitate subsequent problem solving and creative idea generation. For example, Baird et al. (2012) found that performance on previously encountered DT problems improved following an incubation period involving an undemanding task. Further, Gilhooly et al. (2012) found that both immediate and delayed incubation improved performance on a DT task, with a larger benefit following immediate incubation. The nature of the activity performed during incubation also appears to matter. Gilhooly et al. (2013) examined the effects of verbal and spatial interpolated activities on verbal and spatial creative tasks, finding greater incubation benefits when the interpolated activity differed from the target task. Specifically, spatial activities facilitated subsequent verbal DT, whereas verbal activities facilitated performance on a spatial creative task. These findings are consistent with a meta-analysis by Sio and Ormerod (2009), which shows an overall incubation benefit across DT and linguistic and visual insight problems, although its magnitude varied depending on the type of task, the preparation period, and the activity performed during incubation. An alternative explanation is provided by the forgetting-fixation hypothesis, which proposes that initial attempts to solve a problem can strengthen unproductive associations or interpretations, making alternative solutions more difficult to access. Temporarily setting the problem aside allows these dominant but unhelpful responses to become less accessible, reducing their interference with subsequent problem solving. When the individual returns to the task, previously overlooked alternatives may therefore become easier to retrieve or explore. From this perspective, the benefits of incubation arise from the gradual reduction of interference caused by earlier unsuccessful attempts (Smith and Blankenship, 1989, 1991). Mind wandering provides a specific context in which such processing occurs. Zedelius and Schooler (2015) found that mind wandering is associated with insight solving, whereas mindful attention was more strongly associated with step-by-step solving. Mind wandering during incubation has also been associated with successful insight problem solving (Tan et al., 2015), although different forms of mind wandering may have different consequences for creative performance (Yang and Wu, 2022). Incubation benefits may therefore arise through different mechanisms, depending on the problem, the extent of prior preparation, and the activity performed during the interruption. Disengagement may weaken an unproductive representation, allow task-relevant associations to develop, or create opportunities for external cues to trigger a new interpretation (Seifert and Patalano, 2001). These mechanisms may operate independently or in combination. Incubation is therefore best understood as a condition that can facilitate subsequent creative performance through different processes, rather than as evidence for a single form of unconscious problem solving. Importantly, putting a problem a side is different from an interruption of deliberate work, and the first may not be necessary for discontinuous idea emergence. During ongoing divergent ideation, Smith et al. (2026) observed longer pauses preceding responses accompanied by an Aha! experience while people were still working on the problem. These pauses suggest that sudden idea emergence may be preceded by an extended period of processing, even without an interruption of the task. However, their underlying cognitive dynamics remain unknown, and should not be equated with incubation as traditionally defined (i.e., putting a side a problem). Whether processing during such pauses shares mechanisms with experimentally induced incubation is an empirical question that future studies need to adress. Within the Dual-Route Model, incubation is therefore considered a possible context for the processes preceding illumination, rather than a necessary stage of the discontinuous route. Maintaining this distinction is important because equating incubation with any unobserved processing before an idea would make the concept difficult to operationalize and test. Experimentally defined incubation involves a period of disengagement from the target task, whereas the processing preceding sudden idea emergence may also occur during ongoing exploration. Whether these circumstances engage similar mechanisms, and whether they involve representational restructuring, requires further empirical investigation.

Illumination An idea may result from extended processing while its emergence into awareness is experienced as sudden. This distinction is particularly relevant to illumination, a term that retains something of its original metaphorical meaning: bringing into the "light" of awareness an idea whose preceding development was largely hidden from introspection. Understanding illumination therefore requires examining how information contributing to an idea may develop before the idea itself becomes consciously available. Research on insight problem solving provides relevant evidence for this distinction, showing that solution-related information can become activated before individuals are able to identify or report a solution. In the classic accumulation-of-clues paradigm, Bowers et al. (1990) found that participants progressively accumulated information relevant to a solution before they could explicitly identify it. Similarly, Yaniv and Meyer (1987) demonstrated that semantic information related to an unresolved problem could influence subsequent processing even when the solution remained unavailable for conscious report. A computational account of how such gradual processing might culminate in a sudden change in understanding has recently been proposed by Doulfoukar et al. (2026), who modeled a situation in which a problem solver gradually accumulates information while several possible interpretations remain in competition. Insight occurs when the accumulated evidence makes it possible to abandon less useful interpretations and reorganize the problem around a better one. Although processing develops over time, the resulting emergence into awareness can be abrupt and feel sudden. This model illustrates how gradual information accumulation can culminate in a discontinuous change in understanding, providing a possible mechanism for the sudden emergence of an idea.

Neurocognitive research on insight provides further evidence that the sudden emergence of a solution can be preceded by cognitive and neural processes that are not reflected in conscious reports of progress. Kounios et al. (2006) found that neural activity preceding the presentation of a problem differed depending on whether participants subsequently solved it through insight or step-by-step processing. These findings suggest that the cognitive state in which a problem is encountered may influence the subsequent trajectory of solution generation, even before processing of the particular problem begins. Complementing this evidence, Jung-Beeman et al. (2004) identified a burst of high-frequency gamma activity over the right anterior temporal cortex shortly before insight solutions were reported. This activity was specifically localized to the right anterior superior temporal gyrus (rSTG), a region implicated in integrating distantly related semantic information into a coherent interpretation. The same region has been associated with the comprehension of novel metaphors, humor, and implicit meanings, where understanding depends on establishing connections that are not immediately apparent (St George et al., 1999; Mashal et al., 2007; Goel and Dolan, 2001; Bartolo et al., 2006). Noninvasive brain stimulation provides further evidence for a causal contribution of right anterior temporal processing to insight problem solving. Salvi et al. (2020a) found that high-definition transcranial direct current stimulation (HD-tDCS) targeting the rSTG increased the proportion of CRA problems solved via insight relative to sham stimulation and an active left frontopolar control condition. Using a different stimulation method, Santarnecchi et al. (2019) found that gamma-frequency (40 Hz) transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) over the rSTG improved accuracy on CRA problems relative to sham stimulation. These findings suggest that the sudden emergence of a solution may involve integrating previously weakly associated information into a coherent representation. They also show that neural activity associated with insight can be observed both before a problem is presented and immediately before its solution is reported, providing correlational and causal evidence about distinct temporal phases of the processes leading to sudden solution emergence.

In addition, pupillometry provides a complementary approach to examining physiological changes surrounding moments in which a previously unavailable interpretation or solution becomes consciously accessible. A useful parallel comes from bistable perception. When viewing an ambiguous figure such as the Necker cube, the physical stimulus remains unchanged while its interpretation suddenly switches from one percept to another. Einhäuser et al. (2008) found a sharp increase in pupil diameter around these perceptual reversals, with the strongest change in pupil dilation occurring at the time of the reported switch. A remarkably similar pattern was observed during insight problem solving by Salvi et al. (2020c). Pupil diameter began to increase approximately 500 milliseconds before participants reported insight and peaked approximately 200 milliseconds before their response; no comparable increase occurred before step-by-step solutions. Thus, pupil dilation occurs around the perceptual switch of an ambiguous figure and immediately before the reported emergence of an insight solution. This parallel is relevant in light of Gestalt psychology, which proposed that both perceptual reversals and insightful solutions involve a sudden reorganization of existing elements into a new, coherent representation (Köhler, 1925; Vitello and Salvi, 2023). Further evidence for a relationship between perceptual and cognitive reorganization comes from Laukkonen and Tangen (2017), who found that exposure to a Necker cube that induced perceptual conflict increased subsequent insight problem solving and the frequency of self-reported insights. A result that evidences how shifting between alternative representations engages processes relevant to both. Because the pupillary change precedes the overt insight response, it cannot simply reflect a physiological reaction to the retrospective report of how the problem was solved. Drawing on evidence linking pupil dilation to the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine system, Salvi et al. (2020c) proposed that this pupillary response may provide a physiological marker of the switch into awareness, potentially reflecting the transition through which previously inaccessible information becomes consciously available. This interpretation is consistent with previous findings showing that pupil dilation accompanies transitions in conscious perception, including perceptual reversals and the recognition of previously ambiguous stimuli (Einhäuser et al., 2008; Suzuki et al., 2018). Although the precise temporal relationship between activation of the rSTG, pupil dilation, and subjective awareness remains to be established, this interpretation is particularly relevant to illumination, which is characterized by the sudden conscious emergence of an idea.

Taken together, these findings support a distinction between the processes leading to an idea and its eventual availability to conscious awareness. Evidence of solution-relevant activation before explicit identification (Yaniv and Meyer, 1987; Bowers et al., 1990), insight-related neural activity (Jung-Beeman et al., 2004; Kounios et al., 2006), and physiological changes around moments of sudden understanding (Einhäuser et al., 2008; Salvi et al., 2020c; Laukkonen et al., 2021) indicates that the transition into awareness is preceded by processing that is not fully reflected in conscious reports.

4 Which Route Produces Better Ideas?

The journey of an idea is as important as its destination. But are ideas that emerge suddenly any more creative than those developed through a gradual, step-by-step process? Historically, this question has been addressed almost exclusively within convergent problem solving, where quality can be evaluated against an objective criterion: a proposed solution is either correct or incorrect. Across this literature, a remarkably consistent pattern has emerged: insight is associated with successful problem solving, both when examined in terms of representational restructuring, which can facilitate reaching correct solutions (Knoblich et al., 1999; Kershaw and Ohlsson, 2004; Öllinger et al., 2008), and when assessed through measuring the Aha! experience, with solutions accompanied by this experience being more likely to be correct than those reached through step-by-step analysis. Early evidence for a relationship between insight and solution accuracy came from studies showing that solutions characterized by a sudden, rather than incremental, increase in subjective "warmth" were more likely to be correct (Metcalfe, 1986a; Metcalfe and Wiebe, 1987). This link became clearer when researchers began distinguishing insight and step-by-step solutions within the same problem-solving tasks, rather than classifying problems themselves as requiring one approach or the other (Bowden et al., 2005). Using this approach, Salvi et al. (2016a) directly compared the accuracy of self-reported insight and step-by-step solutions across four experiments involving substantially different problem types: CRAs, anagrams, Rebus puzzles, and degraded visual-object identification problems. Across all four experiments, solutions reported as insight were significantly more accurate than those reached step by step. Accuracy ranged from 78.4% to 97.6% for insight solutions, compared with 41.5% to 91.9% for step-by-step solutions. Subsequent studies have reported similar findings across laboratories and problem types, including magic tricks and verbal and visual problems. Both the occurrence and intensity of an Aha! experience have also been found to predict solution accuracy (Danek et al., 2014; Danek and Wiley, 2017; Hedne et al., 2016; Webb et al., 2016; Zedelius and Schooler, 2015; Threadgold et al., 2018; Laukkonen et al., 2021 Laukkonen et al., , 2023)).

A relevant addition to this accuracy advantage concerns the different error profiles associated with insight and step-by-step processing. Kounios et al. (2008) observed that individuals who predominantly solved problems via insight were relatively more likely to make errors of omission (that is, to reach the deadline without producing a response), whereas step-by-step solving was associated with relatively more errors of commission. Salvi et al. (2016a) subsequently demonstrated the same asymmetry at the level of trial solutions. During step-by-step processing, intermediate candidate solutions remain relatively accessible and can therefore be submitted when a deadline approaches, even if processing has not been completed. By contrast, because insight processing provides less access to such intermediate products, if the solution has not yet emerged, there is no candidate available to guess with, resulting in fewer commission errors (Smith and Kounios, 1996; Danek and Salvi, 2020). The insight-accuracy effect is conditional on a solution reaching awareness and being reported: insight solutions are disproportionately accurate when they occur, but discontinuous processing is also more likely to produce no response when a solution fails to emerge before the deadline. This finding is relevant to the distinction between the two routes proposed here. In the continuous route, intermediate products remain relatively accessible as the candidate idea develops and can be monitored, evaluated, and revised. In the discontinuous route the idea becomes available more abruptly, with comparatively limited access to the intermediate products preceding it (Metcalfe and Wiebe, 1987; Smith and Kounios, 1996; Kounios and Beeman, 2014; Salvi, 2023). Yet, when an idea reaches awareness in this way, it is on average more likely to be correct (in CT processing). This creates an interesting paradox for the present model: people have little or no access to the underlying steps that brought them to an idea, yet they often experience a powerful sense that it is accurate and, remarkably, that sense is frequently informative. How can a cognitive product whose derivation cannot be inspected nevertheless provide a reliable signal of its own value? The relationship cannot readily be reduced to confidence alone. Confidence is an important component of the Aha! experience and covaries with insight ratings, but the accuracy effect has also been observed when confidence was not included in the definition of insight (Hedne et al., 2016; Salvi et al., 2016a; Danek and Salvi, 2020). Moreover, differences between insight and step-by-step processing can be detected before the solution is reported, including preparatory neural activity and physiological markers (Kounios et al., 2006 (Kounios et al., , 2008;; Kounios and Beeman, 2014; Jung-Beeman et al., 2004; Salvi and Bowden, 2016; Salvi, 2023; Salvi et al., 2016a). These findings have motivated the proposal that the Aha! experience carries metacognitive information about the quality of an emerging solution. According to the Eureka Heuristic account, insight phenomenology may provide a signal that helps determine whether an idea generated through processes that are not fully accessible to awareness should be selected, trusted, and acted upon (Laukkonen et al., 2023). Because the solver cannot inspect the processes that produced an insight in the same way that a step-by-step chain of reasoning can be inspected, the characteristic certainty, affective salience, and fluency associated with the Aha! experience may provide information about the coherence of the newly available representation. From the perspective of the present model, this gives the Aha! experience a potentially important functional role: it may mark a product of inaccessible processing as worthy of attention and further pursuit. Importantly, this signal is fallible. False insights are less common but do occur (Danek and Wiley, 2017), and experimentally induced Aha! experiences can increase perceived truth even when the Aha! is unrelated to the proposition being evaluated (Laukkonen et al., 2020; Grimmer et al., 2022). The Aha! experience should therefore be understood as an informative metacognitive cue rather than a guarantee of correctness. Crucially, however, the evidence establishing the insight-accuracy relationship has been obtained almost entirely in convergent problem-solving contexts. As mentioned in the former section, this reflects the historical and methodological development of the field rather than a theoretical reason to restrict insight to convergent cognition. Consequently, for much of the history of insight research, asking whether insight produced a "better" idea effectively meant asking whether it produced a more accurate solution. Extending the study of insight to DT changes the relevant criterion of quality. In an AUT, for example, there is no solution key against which an idea can be classified as correct or incorrect. Instead, successful ideas are those that depart from common or readily accessible responses while remaining appropriate to the task, and they can be classified as more or less original or elaborated. If the relationship observed in convergent problem solving reflects something more general about the quality of cognitive products that emerge through insight, its behavioral expression should therefore change with the structure of the task. In CT, having an insight increases the probability of reaching the correct solution; in DT, the corresponding advantage may instead be expressed as increased creativity. Recent findings provide initial support for this possibility. Smith et al. (2026) examined Aha! experiences during generative cognition using taxonomic category generation, ad hoc category generation, and DT tasks. Aha! moments occurred across all three generative contexts, demonstrating that insight phenomenology is not restricted to problems with a single correct solution. More importantly, ideas accompanied by Aha! experiences were judged to be more novel and creative than ideas generated without an Aha!. Converging evidence comes from Moroshkina et al. (2026), who reported a similar result and found that idea elaboration statistically mediated the relationship between Aha! experiences and creativity. McGuinness et al. (2026) directly examined whether the relationship between insight phenomenology and output quality generalizes in the same way from convergent to divergent contexts. Their results show a relevant asymmetry. While in CT Aha! intensity was strongly associated with objective accuracy (consistent with the insight-accuracy effect), in DT the relationship between Aha! and creative quality was more modest, whereas the relationship with participants' own judgments of creativity was considerably stronger. The authors termed this discrepancy the "Aha! glow": experiencing an Aha! can make an idea feel particularly creative even when the corresponding advantage in externally evaluated creativity is smaller. Thus, while the Aha! experience appears to provide a relatively well-calibrated cue to correctness in constrained problem solving, its relation to quality becomes more complex when the criterion itself is open-ended.

Taken together, these findings suggest a potentially important parallel between CT and DT, but not a simple equivalence between accuracy and creativity. In CT tasks, insight distinguishes candidate solutions that are likely to satisfy an externally specified criterion: the correct solution. In DT tasks, the emerging evidence indicates that Aha!-accompanied ideas can also be more original or creative, while simultaneously showing that the subjective experience of insight may amplify creators' evaluations of their own ideas.

Two complementary explanations may account for the relationship between Aha! experiences and creative quality. First, the processing preceding sudden idea emergence may facilitate access to more remote associations or alternative representations, producing ideas that are more original at the moment they become available. Second, the Aha! experience may influence subsequent processing by increasing attention to an emerging idea and motivating its elaboration. On this account, part of the creative advantage associated with Aha! could arise from the downstream effects that follow the experience. The first explanation concerns the processes that generate an idea; the second concerns what happens after the idea has emerged. Distinguishing these possibilities will require separating the quality of an idea from the effects of subsequent elaboration and evaluation. Restructuring provides one possible mechanism for this relationship, but it remains to be demonstrated directly in DT. The available evidence primarily concerns ideas accompanied by an Aha! experience rather than independently identified continuous and discontinuous routes. Whether the advantages associated with insight reflect the underlying trajectory of idea generation, representational restructuring, or the subsequent effects of insight phenomenology remains to be established. This distinction is particularly relevant to the present model, because the route through which an idea emerges and the processes that determine its subsequent fate may interact without being identical.

5 Downstream Consequences of Insight: Decision-Making, Memory, and Learning

Once an idea becomes available to awareness, it is evaluated and may then be developed and potentially translated into action. In other words, once we have an idea, what do we do with it? More importantly, does the manner in which an idea is generated influence what happens to it next? This question extends the Dual-Route Model beyond idea generation to the consequences of how ideas emerge into awareness. A relatively small body of research on the downstream effects of insight provides an initial basis for addressing it. Early evidence comes from Salvi and Bowden (2019), who showed that problem-solving accuracy and confidence are related to subsequent judgments and decisions. Building on this broader question, Yu et al. (2024) investigated whether experiencing an Aha! influences subsequent decision-making. After solving verbal problems, participants chose between a certain monetary reward and an uncertain reward offering a potentially higher payoff. Choices of the uncertain option were more frequent following solutions accompanied by an Aha! experience than those without one, suggesting that the Aha! experience may temporarily increase risk preference. Extending these findings, Becker et al. (2023) replicated the behavioral effect and found greater activation in the nucleus accumbens, a region implicated in reward processing, following high-compared with low-Aha! experiences. Thus, the rewarding phenomenology of insight may have motivational consequences extending beyond the problem that elicited it. This finding has important implications for creative practice because pursuing an idea often involves uncertainty, particularly in DT, where there may be no immediately verifiable correct solution. Here, the Aha! experience may encourage individuals to pursue an idea whose value is not yet known, potentially promoting exploration of unconventional possibilities that might otherwise be dismissed. However, the same mechanism could also increase commitment to an idea simply because its emergence felt compelling. To date, there is little evidence establishing how Aha! experiences influence subsequent risk-taking, idea selection, or willingness to invest in ideas generated during DT. This represents both a potential adaptive function and a potential vulnerability of the Aha! experience: increased willingness to pursue unexpected ideas may facilitate creative exploration, but it may also encourage investment in ideas whose value has not yet been established. In this sense, the Aha! experience resembles Alice's decision to follow the White Rabbit into Wonderland: an unexpected discovery may open possibilities that would otherwise remain unexplored, but the initial sense of curiosity and excitement offers no guarantee that the path will lead somewhere worthwhile!

Beyond their influence on decision-making, Aha! experiences also have consequences for memory, a relationship supported by a substantial body of empirical evidence. Several studies have demonstrated that problem solutions associated with an Aha! moment tend to be better remembered (i.e., the so-called insight memory advantage). Specifically, Danek et al. (2013) demonstrated that these solutions were better recalled two weeks later than solutions reached without that experience. Subsequent research has examined whether this advantage reflects the cognitive processes involved in reaching a solution or the subjective experience accompanying it. Kizilirmak and colleagues (2016) found that both spontaneous and induced insights were associated with better subsequent memory.

In induced-insight trials, participants are given the solution rather than discovering it themselves, which can elicit a sudden realization resembling an "Oh, of course!" or "D'oh! experience". Further, Danek and Wiley's (2020) later results showed that solution correctness, subjective certainty, and pleasurable affect contributed to later recall, whereas restructuring did not account for the memory advantage. Although these findings emphasize the contribution of insight phenomenology, other evidence suggests that changes in the underlying representation may also influence subsequent memory. Becker et al. (2025) investigated insight during the identification of ambiguous visual images and found that stronger Aha! was associated with greater changes in solution-relevant neural representations in the visual cortex, alongside increased activity in the hippocampus and amygdala. Importantly, both representational change and hippocampal activity were associated with better subsequent memory for correctly identified objects, even when accounting for solution time. Further, this memory advantage extends beyond the solution itself. Salvi et al. (2024) found that incidental scholastic information encountered around Aha! and D'oh! experiences was better remembered than information encountered when these feelings were missing. The consequences of insight may therefore extend beyond retaining a newly generated idea to facilitating learning from information encountered around the same time.

How can we explain these effects? One explanation concerns the rewarding experience accompanying insight. The sudden change in understanding characteristic of insight is often accompanied by positive affect, confidence, and a sense that the new interpretation is meaningful. These features may engage reward-related processes that contribute to the encoding and retention of newly acquired information. A computational account proposed by Laukkonen et al. (2023) offers a framework for understanding this relationship. According to this account, representational restructuring generates a higher-order prediction error, while dopaminergic precision-weighting contributes to the confidence, pleasure, and motivational salience associated with the Aha! experience. The resulting combination of representational change and reward-related signaling may help explain why information acquired through insight is particularly memorable. Within the Dual-Route Model, these findings suggest that the consequences of idea generation may depend on both the processes that produce an idea and the experience accompanying its emergence. As illustrated in Figure 1, a consciously available candidate may subsequently undergo evaluation, verification, elaboration, selection, or implementation, depending on the individual's goals and the demands of the task. These processes are conditional rather than necessary stages. The Aha! experience may influence which ideas receive further attention, how willing individuals are to pursue them, and what information is retained for later use. The convergence between attention, decision-making, and memory raises a broader question. Why should a sudden change in understanding simultaneously feel rewarding, command attention, promote action, and facilitate learning? One possibility is that these effects are different expressions of an adaptive mechanism for updating internal models. If insight marks a consequential change in representation, its characteristic phenomenology may help prioritize and stabilize that change so that it can influence future cognition and behavior.

6 Insight as Model Updating

While the Dual-Route Model proposed in this chapter aims to explain how ideas emerge during creative cognition, the processes associated with insight may serve a broader function beyond creativity. For example, sudden changes in understanding have been described across problem solving, perception, psychotherapy, social understanding, and meditation (Ke et al., 2026; Tulver et al., 2023). Across these contexts, having an insight reflects a more general capacity to reorganize available information, allowing a new interpretation to revise an existing one. This raises the possibility that insight may serves a broader adaptive function: updating the internal models through which our mind interprets information available in the world. Predictive-processing accounts provide a potential theoretical framework for this interpretation. Cognition can be conceived as relying on generative models that organize incoming information and generate predictions about the world (Friston, 2010). When incoming information can no longer be accommodated by an existing model, prediction errors create pressure for updating. Such updating can occur gradually, as accumulating evidence progressively modifies the existing model, or via insight (Laukkonen et al., 2023). Friston et al. (2017) relate insight to Bayesian model reduction, whereby a more parsimonious model can emerge through the removal of redundant or poorly supported parameters. Building on this framework, Laukkonen et al. (2023) propose that restructuring through Bayesian reduction generates a higher-order prediction error associated with the content of insight. Dopaminergic precision-weighting of this prediction error is proposed to contribute to the confidence, pleasure, and attentional capture characteristic of the Aha! experience. Recent computational work provides a proof of principle for this possibility. Using an active-inference model, Doulfoukar et al. (2026) demonstrated how gradual evidence accumulation can precede an abrupt reorganization of an internal model through Bayesian model reduction, accompanied by discontinuous changes in behavior and confidence, a transient increase in Bayesian surprise, and increased expected precision. In sum, restructuring concerns the change in the representation, whereas the Aha! experience may signal the significance of that change to the cognitive system. This perspective offers a potential explanation for the convergence of findings considered in the preceding sections. If insight marks a consequential change in representation, the positive affect, confidence, and motivational salience accompanying its emergence may help prioritize the new interpretation, while reward-related neuromodulatory processes may create conditions favorable to its consolidation. The relationship between insight and memory is particularly relevant here. When restructuring produces a more adequate representation, retaining that update may improve the individual's ability to interpret subsequent information and guide future behavior. The memory advantage associated with insight may therefore reflect, at least in part, mechanisms that favor the stabilization of newly reorganized representations. As a consequence, insight might transiently promote plasticity around a successful model update, strengthening the newly reorganized representation and facilitating learning from information encountered around the same time. While this proposal could account for the convergence among insight, reward, memory, and learning, it remains (so far) a hypothesis. Whether insight itself produces a transient increase in neural plasticity, and whether dopaminergic mechanisms causally mediate such an effect, has yet to be experimentally established. Importantly, this possibility also raises the question of whether the Aha! experience contributes to the stabilization of a new representation independently of the restructuring that produced it. Distinguishing the effects of representational change from those of its accompanying phenomenology would help clarify whether the memory advantage reflects successful model updating, the rewarding experience of insight, or their interaction. This perspective also clarifies why insight is useful without being infallible. If the Aha! experience evolved or developed as a probabilistic signal of successful model revision, it need not perfectly discriminate correct from incorrect representations. When the signal tracks a genuine improvement in the model, prioritizing and retaining the new interpretation is adaptive. When the same phenomenology accompanies an erroneous restructuring, however, confidence, reward, and enhanced retention could instead stabilize a false interpretation. The mechanisms that allow insight to rapidly revise an inadequate model may therefore also help explain why false insights can become unusually compelling and resistant to revision (Laukkonen et al., 2020). Evaluation and verification remain critical precisely because the subjective strength of the update cannot guarantee its objective validity. This broader interpretation is also relevant to creative cognition. The discontinuous route may contribute to the generation of new ideas by allowing previously dominant organizations of a problem or semantic space to be escaped, permitting information to be recombined or interpreted from a different perspective. When this transition involves genuine restructuring, the resulting Aha! experience may help the new idea capture attention and remain available for further development.

In open-ended creative thinking, therefore, the potential adaptive function of Aha! may lie in marking a candidate representation as worth exploring. Its eventual value must be established downstream through evaluation, elaboration, testing, and, where appropriate, verification. This returns us to the architecture of the Dual-Route Model. The continuous and discontinuous routes describe how candidate ideas develop and become available to awareness; they do not themselves determine what happens afterward. Illumination marks the sudden emergence of an idea through the discontinuous route, while insight refers to the more specific case in which that emergence reflects a change in representation and is accompanied by an Aha! experience. Once available, the candidate enters a different problem: whether it should be retained, elaborated, tested, implemented, or rejected. The phenomenology of insight may bias this downstream competition by attracting attention, encouraging exploration, and strengthening retention, but it does not replace evaluation.

The route by which an idea reaches awareness and the fate of that idea after it arrives are therefore distinct, but potentially interacting, components of creative cognition. The deeper question is whether the cognitive machinery underlying insight evolved more generally to enable consequential and stable changes in how an organism understands, remembers, and acts upon its world.

7 Future Directions

The most immediate test of the Dual-Route Model is whether the processing dynamics identified for insight in convergent problem solving generalize to DT. Much of the evidence reviewed in this chapter for discontinuous processing comes from CT tasks: insight solutions are characterized by limited access to intermediate states, abrupt changes in perceived proximity to solution, distinctive attentional and physiological dynamics, and, under some conditions, representational restructuring.

Recent studies demonstrate that Aha! experiences also occur during DT and are associated with more creative ideas (Smith et al., 2026; Moroshkina et al., 2026; McGuinness et al., 2026), but the presence of an Aha! experience alone does not establish that the same underlying processes are involved.

The critical next step is therefore to examine the trajectory surrounding individual Aha!-associated ideas in open-ended problems. This will require testing whether the behavioral, metacognitive, and neurocognitive markers established in CT can also identify discontinuous emergence during DT. These markers should be examined at the level of individual ideas, using converging measures of perceived progress, response timing, gaze and attentional dynamics, pupil responses, neural activity, and changes in search trajectory. The critical prediction concerns access to the generative trajectory: during continuous processing, intermediate states should remain sufficiently accessible to be monitored and manipulated, whereas before discontinuous emergence, critical intermediate states should remain comparatively inaccessible until their product becomes consciously available. Importantly, the model does not predict that the processes preceding discontinuous emergence are themselves instantaneous. Substantial processing may unfold over time before culminating in an abrupt transition into awareness, as suggested by both the empirical evidence reviewed here and recent computational modeling (Doulfoukar et al., 2026). The question goes beyond demonstrating that Aha! experiences occur during DT, to demonstrating if the two routes are (for example) accompanied by the same physiological markers of discontinuous processing observed in CT.

A second challenge is to establish restructuring in DT. Aha! experiences during DT show that sudden, phenomenologically salient idea emergence is not confined to CT problems (Smith et al., 2026; Moroshkina et al., 2026), but they do not demonstrate that restructuring has occurred. Future studies should therefore seek independent evidence of representational change, for example through changes in the organization of successive responses, semantic search trajectories, or experimental manipulations of the representation through which a problem is approached. This distinction is essential for determining when illumination occurs within DT. It is equally important for testing whether restructuring itself predicts the quality of an idea, or whether some of the advantages associated with insight arise just from the Aha! experience and its downstream effects.

A third question concerns what happens after an idea becomes consciously available. Evaluation and verification should not be assumed to follow every instance of idea generation, but may depend on epistemic motivation, task demands, external incentives, anticipated consequences, and the need to act. Future work should examine whether the route through which an idea emerges influences its subsequent fate: whether it is remembered, selected, elaborated, tested, implemented, or rejected. This question may be particularly important in open-ended creative and design contexts, where an Aha! experience can make an idea feel compelling in the absence of an immediately available criterion of correctness. The emerging evidence on memory, confidence, and risk preference therefore generates a testable prediction: ideas accompanied by stronger Aha! experiences may receive preferential attention and investment even when their objective quality is held constant. Establishing when this prioritization is adaptive, and when it instead contributes to premature commitment to a compelling but inadequate idea, is an important direction for future research and practical applications.

A particularly important question concerns the developmental origins of the two routes. The distinction between continuous and discontinuous processing does not imply that they emerge simultaneously in ontogeny. Predictive processing is evident early in infancy: six-month-olds show pupillary responses to violations of learned predictions (Zhang et al., 2019), and nine-month-olds show enhanced theta activity to unexpected physical and social events, consistent with early predictionerror processing (Köster et al., 2021). Violations of expectation can also enhance subsequent learning and hypothesis-testing behavior in 11-month-old infants (Stahl and Feigenson, 2015), while infants' increased exploration following surprising events is reduced when they are subsequently provided with a plausible explanation (Perez and Feigenson, 2022). Together, these findings suggest that mechanisms for detecting discrepancies between predictions and incoming information, and for using such discrepancies to guide subsequent learning and information seeking, are present well before mature executive and metacognitive abilities. The developmental literature on insight similarly suggests that some components of discontinuous processing are present relatively early. Aha! experiences have been reported in young children, and their frequency appears relatively stable between four and eight years even as children's understanding of what those experiences represent becomes more sophisticated (Prenevost et al., 2025). Children as young as four associate Aha! experiences with positive affect and the generation of a new idea, whereas the tendency to attribute the experience specifically to an internal cognitive event rather than to its external outcome develops later (Haugen et al., 2025). Thus, the capacity for discontinuous emergence may precede the capacity to understand or report the metacognitive significance of that transition. These results raise the question of whether continuous and discontinuous processing follow different developmental trajectories. Mature continuous processing requires sustained access to intermediate states that can be monitored, evaluated, and deliberately manipulated, whereas discontinuous emergence does not necessarily require conscious access to the computations producing its product. Discontinuous processing could reflect an earlier-developing form of cognition that is progressively complemented by greater conscious access to generative processing. Alternatively, both processing dynamics may be present early and become increasingly differentiated and strategically regulated over development. Distinguishing these possibilities would help establish whether developmental changes occur primarily in the mechanisms that generate new representations, in access to those processes, or in the metacognitive ability to recognize and evaluate their products. Testing these hypotheses will require measures that do not depend exclusively on verbal reports of insight. Pupillometry provides one promising approach because it can be applied across developmental stages. In adults, pupil dilation has been observed shortly before solutions reported as insight (Salvi et al., 2020c). In children, the nonlinear dynamics of pupil fluctuations distinguish those who eventually solve an insight problem from those who do not (Vásquez-Pinto et al., 2023). In six-month-old infants, pupil dilation is sensitive to violations of learned predictions (Zhang et al., 2019). These findings suggest that physiological responses relevant to detecting unexpected events can be measured before an Aha! experience can be verbally reported. Combining such measures with independent behavioral evidence of representational change could therefore help distinguish prediction-error detection from model updating, and model updating from its later metacognitive characterization as an Aha! experience.

The same question can ultimately be extended across species. If insight serves a broader adaptive function in model updating, some of its component processes may predate the human capacity to describe an Aha! experience. Converging changes in gaze, pupil dynamics, behavior, and subsequent memory could provide nonverbal evidence for sudden changes in representation without assuming that nonhuman animals experience or metacognitively represent those changes in the same way as humans. Comparative studies could therefore begin to separate the evolutionary histories of the processes considered together in human insight: prediction-error detection, representational change, discontinuous emergence, physiological or affective marking of the transition, and preferential retention of its consequences. This would provide a way of testing the broader possibility raised in the preceding section: that insight builds upon an evolutionarily older capacity for consequential model updating, while the human Aha! experience provides a metacognitive signal through which such changes become subjectively salient and can influence subsequent judgment and behavior.

8 Conclusions

Illumination has long occupied a central place in theories of creativity, yet how an idea transitions into conscious awareness remains one of the less well-specified aspects of the creative process (Sadler-Smith, 2015; Pinkow, 2023). The Dual-Route Model developed in this chapter proposes that creative ideas can reach awareness through different processing trajectories. The central contribution of the model is therefore to locate the distinction in access to the generative trajectory, rather than in the type of task, the individual, or the speed of the underlying cognitive processes. These routes are not independent cognitive systems or fixed properties of individuals: the same person may generate different ideas through either route, even within the same creative task. This distinction has practical consequences for engineering and design, although which route is more effective depends partly on the kind of problem being solved. Optimizing the weight of a component while preserving its load-bearing capacity, comparing materials against explicit performance criteria, or iteratively improving the efficiency of an existing mechanism lends itself to continuous processing because intermediate solutions can be inspected, tested, and modified. By contrast, when the existing formulation of a problem constrains its solution, further optimization within that representation may be insufficient. A designer confronting an unusable interface may need to reconsider how the user interacts with the system rather than continue refining the existing layout; an engineer facing an apparently unavoidable mechanical trade-off may recognize that an assumed constraint can be removed or that the same function can be achieved through a different architecture. In such cases, stepping away from the problem, changing context, relaxing focused attention, or exploring beyond the dominant solution space may create conditions favorable to unexpected alternatives. The practical implication is that different problems, and different moments within a design process, may benefit from different modes of idea generation. A productive design process should leave room both for developing the current solution and for discovering that the problem might be approached differently.

What happens after an idea emerges is equally important. A suddenly emerging solution may feel unusually coherent or promising and may therefore attract greater attention, investment, or willingness to act. That subjective signal can be useful when it directs resources toward a genuinely valuable idea, but it can also encourage premature commitment when the idea has not yet been adequately tested. For engineers and designers, the practical lesson is to preserve the motivational value of an Aha! without treating it as a substitute for verification: an idea that feels worth pursuing should still be subjected to the same feasibility, safety, and performance criteria as one developed step by step.

Beyond creativity, the framework connects illumination with the broader possibility that insight contributes to model updating, learning, and belief revision. If an Aha! experience helps mark a consequential change in representation as worthy of attention, action, and retention, its combination of confidence, pleasure, salience, and memorability may have an adaptive function. Yet precisely because this signal is probabilistic rather than infallible, the processes that make a productive insight compelling can also make a false insight difficult to abandon. Generation and evaluation therefore remain complementary: one determines which possibilities enter consideration; the other determines which deserve to remain.

The central question for future research is how different ideas reach awareness and when their mode of emergence predicts their eventual value. The engineer introduced at the beginning of this chapter may sometimes reach a solution by constructing it piece by piece and sometimes by suddenly seeing the problem differently. Creative cognition needs both possibilities. Some ideas are built through a sequence of steps we can follow; others feel discovered because the processes that brought them into existence remain hidden from awareness. The Aha! experience may signal that what we have discovered is worth pursuing, but it is the work that follows that determines its value.
