Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background and aims: In most self-exclusion programs, leaving is the gambler's own decision. In Chile, by contrast, revocation requires the consent of a sponsor whom the participant nominates at enrollment and may later replace. We investigated whether the relationship with the first sponsor, and later changes of sponsor, relate to how long participants remain excluded.

Methods: We analyzed records from Chile's self-exclusion registry (2019-2025; N = 3,825 participants; 1,753 revocations). Time to first revocation was examined using Kaplan-Meier curves and time-varying Cox models including the relationship with the first sponsor, the direction of replacements, and prior rejected requests, accounting for the three-month waiting period after a replacement.

Results: Participants whose first sponsor was a close family member had a lower revocation hazard than those who nominated a partner (HR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.59-0.76); their median time to revocation was 40.4 months, against 20 to 22 months in the other groups. More importantly, replacing a non-family sponsor with a close family member was followed by a lower hazard (HR = 0.59), whereas the reverse change showed no detectable difference. Each rejected request, and each replacement once the waiting period had ended, was associated with a higher hazard, with many revocations right after that period.

Discussion and conclusions: Having a close family member as sponsor, whether from enrollment or after a replacement, is associated with longer exclusions. These results are discussed in the light of the sponsor's role as a social commitment device and the points at which participants may benefit from support.

1 Introduction

Consider a person who has been losing money for months in casinos and decides to ban himself from coming back to every casino in the country. This, which may be beneficial for the individual and their close family, is what voluntary self-exclusion (VSE) programs allow. In general, after a period of time the ban expires or the person requests to be removed from the list of self-excluded gamblers.

So far, most of the evidence on the decision to self-exclude has come from studies of participants' self-reported motives for enrolling and their gambling behavior prior to enrollment. In early descriptive work, Ladouceur et al. (2007) and Hayer and Meyer (2010) found that participants frequently cited financial distress, gambling-related problems, and difficulty controlling their gambling as reasons for enrolling. In a systematic review, Motka et al. (2018) likewise identified financial difficulties and a perceived inability to stop gambling as common motives, along with pressure from significant others, particularly in land-based venues. More recently, Hopfgartner et al. (2022) used online account records to show that enrollment is often preceded by high deposits, repeated limit changes, prior exclusions, and the use of multiple payment methods. Although these observations clarify the circumstances under which people enter self-exclusion, what is less clear is how those circumstances relate to the subsequent duration of the exclusion.

Studies following participants after enrollment suggest that the course of an exclusion can differ from what their initial motives would lead one to expect. In an online poker sample, Luquiens et al. (2018) found that self-reported motives did not clearly distinguish subsequent gambling trajectories, and that returning to gamble, or revocation, and new exclusions were common. In a land-based setting, McCormick et al. (2018) observed that some participants even attempted to re-enter casinos during an active exclusion, with smaller improvements in gambling symptoms among those who made such attempts compared to those who did not attempt it. Ending an exclusion, however, does not necessarily mean that a person returns to harmful gambling: Slutske et al. (2010) documented recovery from pathological gambling without complete abstinence and Turner et al. (2021) found that people who reinstated after completing an online tutorial at the end of their exclusion gambled more than during the exclusion, but less than before it.

In this paper we focus on additional circumstances that may influence the decision to revoke an exclusion, and how they relate to the duration of self-exclusion. One such circumstance concerns the involvement of a sponsor. In most self-exclusion programs, sponsors or significant others may provide support, receive information, or be contacted when someone attempts to enter a venue (Gainsbury, 2014; Nowatzki and Williams, 2002), but they hold no veto power over the participant's decision to revoke and go back to gambling. The Chilean program is a special case, in which the sponsor's consent is required for revocation. This opens the possibility that the relationship with the sponsor may influence the duration of exclusion, both by influencing the participant's willingness to request revocation and the sponsor's willingness to consent.

In Chile, following the 2005 Gambling Law and the expansion of the casino sector, the Superintendencia de Casinos de Juego (SCJ; Superintendency of Gaming Casinos) was established to regulate casinos and implement harm-minimization policies (Nazer and Llorca-Jaña, 2021). Under its centralized VSE program, launched in August 2019, a gambler who self-excludes cannot enter any licensed casino in the country for an indefinite period. At enrollment, the participant must nominate a sponsor and declare their relationship to that person, typically a close family member (such as a parent, sibling, or child), a spouse or partner, or a friend. To leave the program, the participant must wait at least six months from enrollment and submit a revocation request co-signed by the sponsor; if the sponsor does not agree to sign, the exclusion remains in force. This veto power is the critical feature that distinguishes the Chilean program from most other VSE programs, and it is the feature that we exploit in this paper. 1While an exclusion is in force, the participant can also replace their sponsor at any time (or a sponsor may resign), transferring the power to authorize a revocation to a new person. Because a gambler whose current sponsor refuses to sign could use this rule to appoint someone more permissive, the regulation imposes a three-month waiting period after any sponsor replacement during which revocation is not allowed. If a participant submits a revocation request that does not meet these requirements (for example, before completing the mandatory six-month period, during the three-month waiting period after a replacement, or with an incomplete form), the SCJ rejects the application and the exclusion continues. Once a revocation is approved, the person is removed from the list of self-excluded gamblers, though they can enroll again in the future if they decide to ban themselves again.

Requiring the sponsor's consent provides a direct mechanism through which the relationship with the sponsor may influence the duration of exclusion. Research on commitment devices suggests that involving another person can help people maintain a commitment (Bryan et al., 2010) and, as we have already mentioned, pressure from significant others may also play an important role (Motka et al., 2018). In the present setting, a parent, sibling, or child may be less willing to authorize a return to casinos than a partner or a friend, or the participant may be more reluctant to ask a close family member for permission to revoke. Either process should contribute to longer exclusions among participants who nominate close family at enrollment. This is one of the hypotheses we explore in this paper.

An important difficulty in interpreting differences across initial sponsors, however, is that participants choose whom to nominate when they enroll. Gamblers who nominate a close family member may differ from those who nominate a partner or a friend in ways that the registry does not record, such as the severity of their gambling problems or how much they want to stop gambling; this is a classic self-selection process. Those data are not observable. However, the fact that participants can replace their sponsors during an exclusion allows us to look at the same question in a different way. Although replacements are also chosen by participants, they occur after the first sponsor has been nominated. To the extent that the relationship with the sponsor matters, a participant who replaces a partner or a friend with a close family member should become less likely to revoke after that, whereas those that replace a close family member with a partner, a friend or somebody else outside the family should become more likely to revoke. Likewise, replacing a sponsor or having a request rejected are also markers of intent to leave the program. Therefore, both of these events should be followed by a higher likelihood of revocation. These are the other hypotheses we examine in this paper.

To test these predictions, we analyzed the records of Chile's centralized self-exclusion registry, which include all enrollments, sponsor replacements, and revocation requests between August 2019 and June 2025. We first compared the time to first revocation of participants whose first sponsor was a close family member with that of participants who nominated a partner, a friend, or someone else.

We then used time-varying Cox models to examine whether the likelihood of revocation changed after a participant replaced their sponsor. We distinguished three types of replacement: those in which a close family member became the sponsor, those in which a close family member was replaced by someone outside the family, and those in which a friend became the sponsor. These models also took into account how many times each participant had replaced their sponsor and how many of their revocation requests had been rejected. Because revocation is restricted during the three months that follow a replacement, we examined the likelihood of revocation once those three months had ended. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine how the relationship with a sponsor in a context where the sponsor holds veto power over revocation relates to the duration of self-exclusion.

2 Methods

2.1 Data sources and study period

We analyzed records of self-exclusion events from Chile's centralized registry, covering the period from its launch in August 2019 to 12 June 2025. The SCJ maintains this registry as part of its regulatory responsibilities, recording enrollments, revocations, and changes of sponsor. Access to the records was obtained under a data-use agreement between ISCI and the SCJ and was facilitated by one co-author (M.S.), who is affiliated with the SCJ. National identifiers were used only to link records across events and were removed before analysis.

2.2 Sample

On 12 June 2025, the registry listed 3,401 participants who were currently self-excluded and 1,454 whose exclusions had been revoked. Many of these participants had entered or left the program more than once: of the 3,401 who were self-excluded, 690 (20.3%) had revoked at least one previous exclusion, and of the 1,454 whose exclusions had been revoked, 305 (21.0%) had already been self-excluded at least once before.

The survival analyses followed each participant from their first accepted enrollment, and the analysis sample was obtained in four steps. First, after removing duplicate records, we excluded 17 participants whose revocation was signed by a sponsor other than the one on record and 103 participants whose accepted enrollment or revocation had no sponsor recorded; of the remaining participants, 4,734 had an accepted enrollment that was followed for more than one day. Second, we excluded 903 participants whose first record was loaded on 9 August 2019 from the earlier casinoby-casino system, because their recorded enrollment date reflects the date of that administrative transfer rather than when they originally enrolled, and most of these records lack a valid birth date.

Third, we excluded four participants whose age was missing. Fourth, we excluded two participants whose revocation was recorded after 50 and 120 days, well before the mandatory six-month period, which we regarded as recording errors; revocations recorded a few days before the six-month mark were kept, since they are likely to reflect differences in how the six months were counted. The final analysis sample therefore comprised 3,825 participants, of whom 1,753 revoked their first exclusion before 12 June 2025. Of these participants, 453 enrolled in the six months before 12 June 2025 and therefore could not yet have revoked. We left them out of the descriptive comparisons between participants who revoked and those who remained excluded (Table 1 and Figure 1), but kept them in the main survival analyses, in which they contribute only the time during which they were observed.

2.3 Variable definitions

The primary outcome was the time from a participant's first accepted enrollment to their first accepted revocation, recorded in days and reported in months. Participants who had not revoked by 12 June 2025 were censored at that date. Since Circular 102 came into force in August 2019, self-exclusion has been indefinite, with a mandatory minimum period of six months before revocation can be requested.

Our main predictor of interest was the relationship between the participant and the sponsor nominated at first enrollment (the first sponsor), classified as partner (including spouses and domestic partners), close family member (parent, sibling, child, grandparent, or grandchild), friend, or other.

We focused on the first sponsor because this relationship is fixed when follow-up begins, whereas the sponsor in office at a later point in time partly reflects replacements made during the exclusion, which we examined separately. We also included three demographic characteristics of the participant: sex, age at enrollment, and whether the participant lived in the Metropolitan Region, which includes Santiago (17.6% of the sample). Although we had no specific predictions for these demographic variables, we included them as controls because they may be related both to the choice of sponsor and to revocation; for example, women nominated a close family member more often than men did, and a partner less often. Because the administrative registry records participants as male or female, we use the term "sex" throughout.

Four counts of events occurring during the exclusion were entered as time-varying covariates: effective sponsor replacements, in which a different person became the sponsor; fictitious replacements, in which the replacement request named the existing sponsor; sponsor resignations; and rejected revocation requests. 2 Rejected revocations were included as a marker of intent to leave the program: because a request can only be rejected if it was submitted in the first place, this count captures active attempts to revoke, and its hazard ratio should not be interpreted as a causal effect of the rejection itself. All covariates were specified a priori without stepwise or data-driven selection, with partner, male, and residence in the rest of the country serving as the reference categories.

2.4 Sponsor replacement rules

Under Circular 122 (in force since 20 August 2021), a participant may replace their sponsor at any time, and no limit is imposed on the number of replacements. However, revocation cannot occur within three months of a replacement. In practice, this three-month waiting period is intended to prevent participants from replacing a sponsor simply to obtain immediate revocation. Before that date, the regulation did not impose a waiting period after a replacement.

2.5 Survival analysis

To examine time to first revocation as a function of the relationship with the sponsor, we used survival analysis. In survival analysis, the variable of interest is the time until an event occurs, in our case the number of days from enrollment to revocation. This allows us to compute the probability that a participant remains excluded beyond any given time and the hazard, that is, the probability of revoking at a given time among those who are still excluded (Therneau and Grambsch, 2000). Participants who had not revoked by the end of the observation period were treated as right-censored, meaning that their records contributed information up to that date without assuming that they would remain excluded after that. We restricted the survival analyses to the first revocation because subsequent exclusions may occur under different circumstances.

We first used Kaplan-Meier curves (Kaplan and Meier, 1958) to describe the probability of remaining self-excluded over time according to the relationship with the first sponsor, comparing the four groups with a log-rank test. We then fitted time-varying Cox proportional hazards models (Cox, 1972) to examine whether these differences held after adjusting for the covariates described above. Because the counts of events change during follow-up, each participant's record was split into consecutive intervals within which all covariates remained constant (Andersen and Gill, 1982; Therneau and Grambsch, 2000), and the hazard for individual i at time t was specified as where h 0 (t) is the baseline hazard, which is left unspecified.

Family i , Friend i , and Other i are indicators for the relationship with the first sponsor (with partners as the reference group); Female i is an indicator for women; Age i is the participant's age at enrollment in decades; and Metropolitan i is an indicator for residence in the Metropolitan Region. The remaining terms vary over follow-up: E i (t) and F i (t) are the cumulative counts of effective and fictitious replacements (see below for when each replacement enters these counts), R i (t) is the count of sponsor resignations and J i (t) the count of rejected revocation requests before t, and W i (t) equals 1 during the three months that follow any replacement made from 20 August 2021 and 0 otherwise. Each exponentiated coefficient, exp(β) or exp(γ), gives a hazard ratio: values below 1 indicate that participants with that characteristic, or with one additional event of that kind, were less likely to revoke at any given time, whereas values above 1 indicate that they were more likely to do so.

Our main hypothesis concerns β 1 . To the extent that a close family member is less willing to consent to revocation, or the participant is less willing to ask a relative for it, participants whose first sponsor was a close family member should be less likely to revoke than those who nominated a partner; that is, β 1 < 0 (a hazard ratio below 1). Similarly, if some participants replace their sponsor in order to obtain consent, effective replacements should be followed by a higher hazard of revocation once the waiting period has ended (γ 1 > 0). Because a rejected request can only occur once a participant has applied to revoke, prior rejections should likewise be associated with a higher hazard (γ 4 > 0). We had no specific predictions for friends and other sponsors, for fictitious replacements and resignations, for the waiting period, or for sex, age, and region, which were included as controls.

Note that counting a replacement from the day it was recorded would combine the three-month period during which revocation is restricted with the unrestricted period that follows. We therefore entered each replacement made on or after 20 August 2021, effective or fictitious, into E i (t) or F i (t) three months after it was recorded, while W i (t) captured the three-month waiting period itself.

Replacements made before that date, when there was no waiting period, were entered from the day they were recorded. For example, if a participant replaced their sponsor on day 300 of the exclusion, W i (t) took the value 1 from day 300 to day 390, and the replacement was added to E i (t) from day 391 onwards. In this way, the replacement coefficients describe the hazard once the waiting period has ended.

To test whether changes of sponsor during an exclusion had a similar effect, we extended Equation 1 by adding one or more of three time-varying indicators for the direction of effective replacements: Towards i (t), equal to 1 once the participant had replaced someone outside the family with a close family member (towards family); Away i (t), equal to 1 once the participant had replaced a close family member with someone outside the family (away from family); and FriendEntry i (t), equal to 1 once the participant had replaced a sponsor who was not a friend with a friend (friend entry). In all three definitions, partners were classified as outside the close family. Each indicator took the value 0 before the first replacement of that kind and 1 from then on, starting when the three-month waiting period ended (or on the day of the replacement, for replacements made before 20 August 2021), and remained 1 if the participant later changed sponsor again. Under the hypothesis that the relationship with the sponsor matters, the coefficient on Towards i (t) should be negative and that on Away i (t) should be positive. Because these models also include E i (t), each directional indicator compares replacements in one direction with replacements in other directions, rather than with making no replacement. Importantly, these labels describe only the change in relationship category; the sponsor's willingness to consent was not measured. Ties were handled using Efron's method (Efron, 1977). Because each participant contributes multiple intervals to the analysis but at most one revocation, standard errors were clustered by participant (Lin and Wei, 1989), and all confidence intervals are based on these clustered standard errors.

The proportional-hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals (Grambsch and Therneau, 1994). Sensitivity analyses (i) counted all replacements from the day they were recorded, without the three-month lag; (ii) replaced the first sponsor with the sponsor in office at each point in time; (iii) stratified the baseline hazard by enrollment before or after 20 August 2021 to allow for the change in regulation; (iv) excluded participants with any rejected revocation request; (v) omitted all counts of events during the exclusion; and (vi) excluded the 453 participants who enrolled in the six months before 12 June 2025.

Analyses were conducted in R 4.6.0 using the survival (version 3.8-6) and survminer (version 0.5.2) packages (Therneau, 2024; Kassambara et al., 2025).

Because self-exclusion is indefinite under the current regulation, there is no automatic expiry that could act as a competing event, and the primary censoring event is the end of the observation window (12 June 2025). Although death or permanent moves abroad could in principle act as competing risks, these events are not recorded in the registry and their incidence is expected to be small relative to revocation; we therefore treat the data within a standard right-censored survival framework (Putter et al., 2007; Austin et al., 2016).

3.2.1 Kaplan-Meier curves

Because participants entered the registry at different times, some had been observed for longer than others when follow-up ended; for example, a person who enrolled in 2020 was observed over a longer window than someone who enrolled in 2022. After that, the curve for close family separates clearly from the other three: half of the participants whose first sponsor was a close family member remained excluded after 40.4 months (95% CI 33.3-47.9), compared with 22.3 months (19.0-24.8) for partners, 20.1 months (17.9-22.8) for friends, and 20.8 months (16.5-29.7) for other sponsors (χ 2 (3) = 51.3, p < .001, log-rank test).

3.2.2 Time-varying Cox proportional hazards model

Figure 3 presents the estimates from the main time-varying Cox model (3,825 participants, 1,753 revocations). 4 Discussion

The present study shows that, when leaving a self-exclusion program requires the consent of a sponsor, the relationship with that person is closely related to the duration of the exclusion. This was the case both for the sponsor nominated at enrollment and for the sponsor brought in during the course of the exclusion. Participants whose first sponsor was a close family member had a lower revocation hazard than those who nominated a partner, and half of them were still excluded more than three years after enrolling, nearly twice as long as participants in the other groups. More importantly, changes of sponsor during the exclusion pointed in the same direction: participants who replaced a sponsor outside the family with a close family member had a lower hazard after the change than those who made other replacements, whereas replacing a relative with someone outside the family was not reliably followed by a higher hazard. By contrast, replacements in general were followed by a higher hazard once the three-month waiting period had ended, and revocations were concentrated in the days immediately after that period.

Previous reviews of self-exclusion programs have documented reductions in gambling behavior and problem severity, along with improvements in psychological wellbeing (Gainsbury, 2014; Kotter et al., 2019). In spite of these benefits, outcomes vary considerably across studies, and some participants try to enter casinos while they are excluded or continue gambling. Because much of this evidence comes from observational studies without control groups, it is often difficult to separate the contribution of self-exclusion from other changes in participants' lives. For example, McCormick et al. (2018) observed reductions in gambling symptoms during exclusion, but noted that these improvements were smaller among participants who tried to enter casinos while they were excluded.

In this regard, an outstanding question is what leads participants to maintain, or end, an exclusion once they have enrolled. When Pickering and Blaszczynski (2022) compared people who renewed their self-exclusion with those who discontinued it, they found no detectable differences across the demographic, gambling, and psychological measures they examined, and called for further research on social and environmental influences on this decision. Here, we examined one such influence: the relationship with the person whose consent is required to revoke.

Chile's registry makes this analysis possible by recording initial sponsor relationships, subsequent replacements, and revocation histories within a program where exclusion does not expire automatically.

In the broader literature on gambling treatment and recovery, Petry and Weiss (2009) and Penfold and Ogden (2024) have shown that social support is associated with lower gambling severity and better quality of life. The present findings concern a more specific arrangement, in which another person holds formal veto power over the decision to revoke. Under this view, a parent, sibling, or child may be less willing to authorize revocation, or the participant may be more reluctant to request it from that relative. Either possibility is consistent with the idea that involving another person can help people maintain a commitment (Bryan et al., 2010).

An alternative explanation, however, can be drawn from the fact that participants choose their own sponsors. Those who nominate a relative at enrollment may have less severe gambling problems, may be more motivated to stop gambling, or may have different family circumstances than those who nominate a partner, and these baseline differences could produce longer exclusions without any active role for the sponsor. Although our observational records cannot rule out self-selection of this kind, the analysis of sponsor replacements makes a simple baseline account harder to accept.

Replacements occur after the first sponsor has been chosen, and our models compared replacements in different directions while adjusting for the relationship with the first sponsor. Even so, participants who brought in a close family member had a lower hazard after the replacement than those who made other replacements, and this difference disappeared when the sponsor in office at each point in time was used instead of the first sponsor; in that model, having a close family member in office was itself associated with a lower hazard. In other words, the lower hazard was related to having a close family member as sponsor, whether from enrollment or after a replacement. Participants also choose whom to bring in, of course, but unmeasured differences between participants would then have to produce the same relationship pattern at two different points of the exclusion.

What is less clear is why the two directions of replacement differed. Replacing a relative with someone outside the family was not reliably followed by a higher hazard, whereas bringing in a friend was. One possibility is that choosing a close family member as the first sponsor reflects how much the participant wants to stop gambling, and that this does not change when the relative is later replaced. Because our models include the relationship with the first sponsor, this effect remains after the replacement, and replacing the relative adds little to the hazard. By contrast, those who bring in a friend may more often do so in order to obtain consent to revoke. Because the registry does not record the reasons for replacements, these ideas can only be regarded as informal speculations.

Prior rejected requests were also strongly associated with revocation. Naturally, this result should not be taken to mean that a rejection causes revocation: a rejected request can only occur once a participant has applied to revoke, and it therefore indicates that the participant has already decided to leave the program. In many cases, the participant simply applies again after correcting an administrative error or waiting until the time requirements are met. The same applies to sponsor replacements. Once the three-month waiting period had ended, the hazard after an effective replacement was more than three times higher, and a large number of revocations took place in the days immediately after that period expired. Before Circular 122 came into force in August 2021, by contrast, revocation could follow a replacement without delay, and most participants who revoked after a replacement made in that period did so within three months of it. While it is not unreasonable to think that many participants replace their sponsor in order to revoke, some replacements are likely to follow changes in the participant's life that the registry does not record, such as the death of a relative or the end of a relationship. Moreover, many participants entered and left the program more than once: on 12 June 2025, one in five of those who were self-excluded had revoked a previous exclusion.

In practical terms, these findings suggest several ways in which programs that involve a sponsor might be strengthened. Because how long an exclusion lasts depends on the sponsor's consent, sponsors should receive clear guidance at enrollment about their role and the conditions under which revocation can be requested. Ideally, this should be subject to continuous follow-up to ensure the sponsors are updated about their role. Working directly with sponsors, including those of participants who remain excluded and of those who revoked, as well as sponsors who were replaced and those who replaced them, would also be valuable. Not only would this help to strengthen the sponsor-gambler commitment, but also help regulatory and research institutions understand the underlying causes of each type of event. In addition, events recorded during the exclusion can serve as early signals that a participant is preparing to leave the program. Because a rejected request marks an active intent to revoke, it identifies participants who may benefit from both clarification of the application procedure and additional support. Similarly, the large number of revocations at the end of the three-month waiting period indicates that this restriction delays, rather than prevents, many of the revocations that follow a replacement; the replacement request itself may therefore serve as an opportunity to offer support. These considerations are in line with recommendations to provide support alongside self-exclusion (Gainsbury, 2014) and with reports from Pickering and Blaszczynski (2022) that participants often feel insufficiently supported when deciding whether to renew or discontinue their agreements.

Several limitations should be noted. Because participants choose both their initial sponsors and any subsequent replacements, the differences we observe across relationship categories may partly reflect unmeasured differences in gambling severity, motivation to stop gambling, or family circumstances, as well as the underlying reasons for changing sponsors rather than the new sponsor's response to a request. Furthermore, because rejected requests reflect an active intent to leave the program, adjusting for them may take away part of the association of other variables, such as the relationship with the sponsor, that act by making participants less likely to request revocation in the first place. The proportional-hazards assumption also did not hold for some covariates, including the relationship with the first sponsor, meaning that the corresponding hazard ratios should be interpreted as average differences over the follow-up period.

Finally, ending an exclusion need not imply a return to harmful gambling, just as remaining on the register need not imply abstinence (Slutske et al., 2010). As shown by Turner et al. (2021) in their study of gamblers reinstating after an exclusion, directly assessing post-exclusion gambling behavior is essential for evaluating harm-minimization outcomes. Because our survival analyses were restricted to the first revocation and relied solely on administrative records, following participants across successive exclusions and linking registry events to clinical or behavioral measures of gambling would help clarify how sponsor barriers relate to long-term recovery.

To summarize, in this paper we have tested the idea that the relationship with a sponsor may influence the duration of gambling self-exclusion. Our findings show that when leaving selfexclusion requires the consent of another person, nominating or switching to a close family member is associated with remaining excluded for substantially longer, whereas sponsor replacements and rejected requests mark moments at which participants are likely to leave the program. Chile's registry allows these social barriers to be tracked over time, and suggests that the relationship with the sponsor is a variable that is important to consider in the design of self-exclusion programs.
