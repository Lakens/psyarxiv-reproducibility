Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract

People often seek advice when choosing a film, a book, or another experience whose value depends on their tastes. Large language models (LLMs) provide a convenient way to obtain such advice. We test a simple method for improving their predictions, inspired by the wisdom of the inner crowd. An LLM first predicts a person's movie rating from that person's past ratings. It then predicts how the general public, given the same history, would predict the same person's rating. Averaging takes the arithmetic mean of these two responses. We evaluated 12 GPT model snapshots using MovieLens 1M. A history-size study tested 1-20 ratings for 55 users and extended the comparison to 55 ratings for 32 eligible users. Averaging improved mean squared error relative to the first response even at the longest history. A subsequent evaluation used 10 target films per person with 10 fixed history ratings. Across 320 user-movie pairs, mean squared error was 1.218 for the first response, 1.147 for the public-perspective response, and 1.150 for Averaging. Additional analyses showed that Averaging reduced errors of at least 1.5 rating points, whereas the first response produced more exact matches. These advantages did not extend to every error threshold or metric. The findings connect inner-crowd research with a simple conversational method for preference prediction and show why the choice of response should reflect the prediction objective.

1. Introduction

Which film should we watch tonight? Will a friend enjoy the book we plan to give them? Such questions require predictions about preferences. There is no universally correct film or book: the same experience can delight one person and disappoint another. Yet people have information that may help them choose well. They remember past experiences, observe other people's choices, and ask for advice. Research on matters of taste examines how these sources of information help people anticipate what they will enjoy (Yaniv et al., 2011; Müller-Trede et al., 2018).

Using that information is not straightforward. People can overestimate how well their friends know their preferences, and familiarity with a partner does not ensure accurate predictions of that partner's tastes (Gershoff & Johar, 2006; Lerouge & Warlop, 2006). Conversely, another person's reported experience can be informative even when the person is unfamiliar. Studies of affective forecasting show that others' reactions can improve predictions of one's own reactions, although people may underestimate their value (Gilbert et al., 2009; Walsh & Ayton, 2009). A stranger's opinion can sometimes be as useful as a friend's (Eggleston et al., 2015). Thus, knowing someone well and knowing how to use an opinion are different problems.

Previous choices also reveal information about whose opinions will be useful. Yaniv et al. (2011) showed that reliance on similar advisers and majority opinions depends on taste discrimination. Gershman et al. (2017) further showed that people infer latent groups from observed choices and use this structure to decide whom to follow. This process goes beyond comparing two people's choices in isolation. Preference research also examines when dissimilar opinions can inform prediction (Fujisaki & Yang, 2025) and, in ongoing work, whether response time can help select a single aesthetic judgment to represent a crowd (Fujisaki, in preparation). Together, these approaches ask how information about a person, an adviser, or a judgment can make advice more useful. The degree of shared taste also varies across aesthetic domains (Vessel et al., 2018).

LLMs provide a new interface for this everyday problem. A person can list films they liked and disliked and ask whether they would enjoy another film. Studies have examined recommendation through natural-language prompts, including rating prediction and the ranking of candidate items (Liu et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2023). Kang et al. (2023) directly tested whether LLMs can predict an unknown rating from a user's previous ratings. Their work establishes the feasibility of the task while showing that pretrained models can lag behind collaborative-filtering approaches. Fluency in discussing a film therefore does not establish accurate personalization. Here we ask whether a small change in the conversation can improve the prediction made from the information already supplied.

Our approach draws on the wisdom of the inner crowd. Several estimates from one person can contain useful information beyond a single estimate. Averaging them can therefore improve accuracy, even without consulting another person (Vul & Pashler, 2008; Herzog & Hertwig, 2009 , 2014a). Research has examined how this benefit depends on memory, repeated sampling, and the interval between judgments, using both laboratory tasks and large natural experiments (Hourihan & Benjamin, 2010; Rauhut & Lorenz, 2011; Steegen et al., 2014; van Dolder & van den Assem, 2018). The benefit varies with the questions, the judge's expertise, and the way the second estimate is elicited (Müller-Trede, 2011; Fiechter & Kornell, 2021; Gaertig & Simmons, 2021). An inner crowd is therefore a procedure for making better use of available judgments, rather than a guarantee that any second answer will help.

Generating judgments and deciding what to do with them are also distinct steps. People do not always recognize when combining their own estimates would be useful (Fraundorf & Benjamin, 2014; Herzog & Hertwig, 2014b). Research comparing averaging with the selection of a single confidence judgment similarly shows why the evaluation criterion matters (Litvinova et al., 2020). The approach extends beyond factual estimation. Barneron et al. (2019) found benefits from combining repeated essay evaluations by the same rater. This suggests that repeated judgments can improve evaluation even when the task involves subjective assessment. Fujisaki et al. (2022) made the connection to matters of taste directly. Participants rated paintings or music from their own perspective and estimated how much the public would like the same items. Averaging these two ratings improved the prediction of other participants' preferences relative to using the participant's own rating. Averaging also outperformed the public estimate for paintings; the corresponding difference was not established for music. This procedure offers a simple way to obtain a more informative judgment from one adviser. Its central idea is to elicit another perspective and combine the resulting responses.

A related possibility now exists within an LLM. Chuang et al. (2025) showed that aggregating multiple responses from a model can improve numerical guesstimation. Their work links LLM decoding to crowd wisdom in tasks involving quantities and forecasts. The present study examines individual preferences and elicits two responses through different instructions. First, the model predicts a target person's rating from that person's history. Second, it estimates how the public, seeing the same history, would predict that same person's rating. We compare the first response (R1), the second response (R²), and their arithmetic mean (Averaging). Throughout, the target remains one person's rating of one film.

We first varied the number of history ratings, from 1 to 55, to test whether the method remained useful as information about the person increased. We then predicted 10 films per person using a fixed history of 10 ratings. This second study broadens the test beyond a single selected film. The main analyses concern mean squared error (MSE). Additional analyses examine mean absolute error (MAE), exact matches, and the differences between R² and Averaging. This order separates the basic improvement over a direct prediction from the further question of which response is most useful for a particular purpose.

2. Method and results

>Method and results

2.1. Prediction task

Figure 1 illustrates the basic procedure. For each prediction, the model received a target user's previous movie ratings, the corresponding titles and genres, and the title and genres of a target movie. It then produced two predictions about the same target rating. All history ratings preceded the target rating, which was hidden. R1 asked for a direct prediction of the target user's rating. R² was generated in the same conversation after the model's complete R1 response. It retained the same history and R1 response, then asked how members of the public would predict the same user's rating from that information. It requested the mean of these imagined predictions. The question concerned the public's prediction about this user, not the public's own liking of the movie. Both responses could use decimals from 1 to 5. Averaging was calculated as (R1 + R²) / 2, with no fitted weights.

We used MovieLens 1M, which contains actual movie ratings on an integer scale from 1 to 5 (Harper & Konstan, 2015). MovieLens has also been used to study variation in recommendation accuracy across users (Analytis & Hager, 2023) and to evaluate LLM prediction of user ratings (Kang et al., 2023). It allows us to assess predictions against an observed preference, rather than an investigator's judgment of whether a recommendation seems appropriate.

We evaluated 12 fixed GPT model snapshots. Each user-film-model combination started with a fresh context. The model received no outcome feedback, and predictions were not exchanged across models or films. No model was fine-tuned; history size refers to the examples included in the prompt. We first calculated each model's squared prediction error, then averaged errors across models and users. Thus, the main analysis describes individual-model performance. It is distinct from first averaging predictions across 12 models. Paired confidence intervals resampled users 10,000 times, keeping their model and condition observations together. Detailed settings and sampling rules appear in Appendix A; the original prompts appear in Appendix B.

R² estimates how the general public, given the same history, would predict the same user's rating of the same movie; it does not estimate the public's own liking of that movie. R² is generated in the same conversation after the complete R1 response. Averaging is the arithmetic mean, (R1 + R²) / 2. 2.2. Increasing the amount of history

The first study used one target film per person and nested histories of 1, 3, 5, 10, and 20 ratings. Of 56 sampled users, 55 had complete responses in every condition. For these users, the person, target film, and true rating were fixed across history sizes. All three methods improved as history increased (Figure 2A). From one to 20 ratings, MSE fell from 1.688 to 0.838 for R1, from 1.331 to 0.831 for R², and from 1.460 to 0.804 for Averaging. R² had the lowest MSE with one, three, or five history ratings. Averaging had the lowest MSE with 10 or 20 ratings.

The benefit of Averaging over R1 was larger when little history was available. The MSE reduction was 0.228 at one rating, 95% CI [0.117, 0.349], and 0.034 at 20 ratings, [-0.009, 0.074]. The change in this benefit was -0.194, [-0.322, -0.078]. Additional information therefore reduced the initial advantage of Averaging. A constant prediction of 3 had MSE 1.945, higher than every method at every tested history size.

We next extended the history to 55 ratings for the 32 users who had sufficient earlier ratings for their original target. The comparison used these same users at every history size (Figure 2B). At 55 ratings, MSE was 0.976 for R1, 0.892 for R², and 0.910 for Averaging. R² reduced MSE relative to R1 by 0.085, 95% CI [0.006, 0.170]. Averaging reduced it by 0.067, [0.026, 0.110]. R² had the lowest point estimate at all six history sizes in this subset. Thus, the usefulness of a second prediction remained at the longest history tested.

The two samples must be kept distinct. At 20 ratings, Averaging ranked first in the full 55-user sample, whereas R² ranked first in the 32-user subset. Moreover, the change in the Averaging benefit between 20 and 55 ratings was uncertain, -0.008, 95% CI [-0.057, 0.043]. The results show persistence with longer histories; they do not establish a constant benefit across history sizes or users.

2.3. Predicting ten films per person

The second study tested whether the result depended on the single target initially selected for each person. We retained the 32 users and predicted 10 films for each, using 10 fixed history ratings per user. The target set included the original film and nine additional eligible films. All predictions were newly generated. This gave 320 user-movie pairs covering 284 distinct movies and 7,680 valid model responses, with no missing responses. Each film was predicted in a separate conversation from the same history.

Both R² and Averaging improved MSE relative to R1 (Figure 3; Table 1). Across 3,840 predictions per method, MSE was 1.218 for R1, 1.147 for R², and 1.150 for Averaging. The reduction relative to R1 was 0.071 for R², 95% CI [0.018, 0.125], and 0.067 for Averaging, [0.039, 0.096]. At the user level, Averaging improved MSE for 25 of the 32 users. The improvement therefore extended beyond the original one-film task.

The originally selected films were easier in the observed data: R1 MSE was 0.950 for the original targets and 1.248 for the nine added targets. Nevertheless, the MSE benefit of Averaging over R1 was almost identical, 0.0673 and 0.0674, respectively. The intervals for the original-versus-added differences included zero. These results support the broader ten-film evaluation while cautioning against treating the initial one-film errors as stable properties of individual users. Note. The first three rows average 3,840 individual-model errors. Each reference prediction is evaluated on the same 320 user-movie pairs. Other-user film means use population ratings that were not supplied in the prompts. The all-period mean is a retrospective reference. The constant-3 reference had higher MSE than all three methods. R² and Averaging also had lower point estimates than the mean of the supplied history. However, the other-user film means were more accurate. The result therefore concerns an improvement to LLM predictions from the supplied history, rather than superiority over recommendation methods that use additional population data.

3. Additional analyses

>Additional analyses

3.1. Mean absolute error

MAE in the ten-film evaluation was 0.837 for R1, 0.827 for R², and 0.829 for Averaging (Figure 4A). Although both alternatives had lower point estimates than R1, the differences were small and uncertain. The R²-R1 difference was -0.010, 95% CI [-0.036, 0.016], and the Averaging-R1 difference was -0.008, [-0.021, 0.006]. The evidence for improvement is therefore clearer for MSE than for MAE.

History-size MAE results are shown in Figure S1. In the full 55-user sample at 20 ratings, R1 had the lowest MAE (0.662), followed by Averaging (0.666) and R² (0.674). In the matched 32-user sample at 55 ratings, MAE was 0.663, 0.624, and 0.642 for R1, R², and Averaging. All three methods had lower MAE than the constant-3 reference in both history samples and in the ten-film evaluation. These findings retain the favorable benchmark comparison without implying that R² or Averaging always improves MAE over R1.

3.2. Exact and near matches

R1 produced the most exact matches: 787 of 3,840 predictions (20.49%), compared with 483 for R² (12.58%) and 246 for Averaging (6.41%; Figure 4B). The same ordering held for predictions within 0.5 points: 47.14%, 42.68%, and 39.19%. Thus, R²'s lower MSE did not arise from more exact hits.

These comparisons use unrounded predictions. Actual ratings are integers, whereas models could predict decimals, and Averaging can introduce additional fractional values. Exact-match rates consequently describe both accuracy and the numerical form of the responses. The present analysis does not establish the same ordering for an integer-constrained or categorical prediction task.

3.3. Comparing R² with Averaging

The two methods had similar MSE point estimates. Averaging minus R² was 0.003, 95% CI [-0.022, 0.029]. The corresponding MAE difference was 0.002, [-0.010, 0.015]. These intervals do not establish equivalence, but neither comparison clearly favored one method. Averaging had lower MSE than R² for 17 users and higher MSE for 15. Across the 12 models, Averaging had lower MSE than both component responses in four: GPT-4o mini, GPT-4o, GPT-4.1 mini, and GPT-5 nano. These are descriptive model rankings.

A clearer advantage for Averaging appeared at the prespecified large-error threshold of 1.5 points. The rate fell from 18.15% under R² to 15.57% under Averaging, a reduction of 2.58 percentage points, 95% CI [1.82, 3.41]. This is a 14.2% relative reduction. Twenty-nine users had fewer such errors after Averaging, three tied, and none had more. Eleven of the 12 models also improved. The R1 rate was 20.52%.

The advantage depended on the error boundary (Table 2; Figure 5). Averaging reduced errors of at least two points, from 9.09% to 7.50%. For errors strictly greater than two points, however, the rate increased from 4.48% to 5.65%. There were 177 errors exactly equal to two points under R² and 71 under Averaging. This concentration at the boundary explains the reversal between the inclusive and strict thresholds. Averaging reduced errors crossing particular tolerances, rather than improving every part of the extreme-error distribution. Note. Each denominator is 3,840. The ≥1.5 threshold was specified before the ten-film run; other thresholds were examined in follow-up analyses. Predictions were not rounded.

3.4. Averaging across models

We also averaged the 12 models' predictions for each user-movie pair before scoring them. Ensemble MSE was 1.126 for R1, 1.060 for R², and 1.082 for Averaging. The corresponding MAEs were 0.824, 0.809, and 0.813. These values describe 320 ensemble predictions, rather than the average error of 3,840 individual-model predictions. Averaging all model-specific means is algebraically identical to averaging the R1 and R² ensemble predictions.

The large-error advantage of Averaging over R² did not extend to the ensemble. Both methods had 45 errors of at least 1.5 points out of 320 cases. An improvement in a single model's tworesponse procedure should therefore be distinguished from an improvement in a multi-model ensemble. Appendix E reports the full error bins and variance summaries for the individual-model predictions.

4. Discussion

A short second question improved LLM preference prediction. When the model estimated how the public would predict the same person's rating, MSE was lower than for the direct response. Averaging the two responses also improved on direct prediction. This benefit remained with 55 history ratings in the eligible sample and when the task expanded to 10 target films per person. The findings connect the wisdom of the inner crowd to a common human-AI interaction: supplying a preference history and asking for advice about a new item.

The result also clarifies what Averaging contributes. In Fujisaki et al. (2022), combining personal and estimated public preferences improved prediction relative to the adviser's own preference. Here, R² was already a strong predictor and often had the lowest MSE. Our task differs because R1 is a prediction about the target person, rather than the adviser's own liking. Averaging therefore combines two predictions about the same person. It cannot be assumed to beat the better component.

Its additional value in the ten-film task was a lower frequency of errors crossing the specified 1.5point tolerance.

This distinction matters for practical use. Lower average squared error, more exact hits, and fewer errors beyond a tolerance are different goals. R² had the lowest MSE point estimate in the full ten-film evaluation. R1 produced more exact matches, whereas Averaging produced fewer errors of at least 1.5 points. These results favor a choice guided by the intended loss rather than a single ranking of methods. They concern the accuracy of rating predictions; whether users make better choices or enjoy the resulting recommendations remains to be tested.

4.1. History and differences between people

The history-size results connect to social learning in matters of taste. Analytis et al. (2018)showed that the usefulness of broad aggregation and similarity-based strategies changes with experience. Our full sample displayed a related pattern: the benefit of Averaging over R1 declined as history increased from one to 20 ratings. However, the benefit did not disappear in the matched extension to 55 ratings. More examples can improve direct prediction without removing the value of a second judgment.

Differences between people may explain part of this pattern. Mainstream taste is associated with performance differences in collaborative filtering (Analytis & Hager, 2023), and taste typicality is relevant to the usefulness of both blended and dissimilar opinions (Fujisaki et al., 2022; Fujisaki & Yang, 2025). These findings motivate testing whether LLM predictions, and the benefit of Averaging, depend on how typical a person's preferences are. The current data do not identify that relationship: users had different target films, and the 32-user extension was selected by history availability. Typicality should be measured from ratings separate from the target outcomes used to evaluate prediction. This would distinguish a predictable person from an easy set of films.

4.2. Future studies

Why ask about the general public? We used this instruction as a simple, tested way to elicit another perspective, drawing on Fujisaki et al.'s work on preference prediction and inner-crowd estimation (2022, 2023). Perspective-taking has also improved the use of advice in human judgment (Yaniv & Choshen-Hillel, 2012). The present design does not establish that the public perspective is the best instruction. The second response could benefit from the public perspective, from reconsidering the first answer, or from another opportunity to generate a prediction. A sameperspective second response and a public-perspective response generated without R1 would help separate these possibilities. R²'s smaller prediction variance is compatible with more moderate responses, but it does not identify the underlying process.

Other methods may elicit more useful second judgments. Dialectical bootstrapping asks a person to reconsider assumptions and generate an alternative estimate (Herzog & Hertwig, 2009). Fujisaki et al. (2023) compare estimated public opinion with repeated estimation and dialectical bootstrapping, emphasizing both accuracy and convenience. Van de Calseyde and Efendić (2022) ask people to adopt a disagreeing person's perspective; subsequent work shows benefits for difficult questions and costs for easy ones (Van de Calseyde & Efendić, 2025). Gomilsek et al. (2024) use Fermian strategies that decompose an estimation problem into subtasks and combine their solutions. Fujisaki et al. (2025) combine several thinking styles across repeated estimates. These approaches provide concrete alternatives for an LLM preference task: changing the imagined adviser, reconsidering the inferred taste profile, or separately predicting relevant aspects of a film before combining them. Whether these adaptations improve preference prediction is an empirical question.

The combination rule also deserves attention. Human studies show that selecting a preferred estimate and averaging estimates need not produce the same benefit (Fraundorf & Benjamin, 2014; Herzog & Hertwig, 2014b; Litvinova et al., 2020). Our results make this issue concrete for LLMs. An equal-weight average helps relative to R1 while being close to R² in MSE and different in its error distribution. Future comparisons can evaluate alternative elicitation and combination rules against a specified loss on held-out users and films. The relevant aim is a useful improvement for the person seeking advice, rather than additional diversity by itself.

4.3. Scope of the evidence

This study used one archival dataset, Japanese prompts, a modest number of users, and 12 model snapshots from one provider. The history and ten-film studies developed sequentially and were exploratory. The ten-film evaluation reused the users eligible for the long-history extension. Longer histories also included older ratings, so their number and age were not separated. Bootstrap intervals preserve dependence within users but do not cover all uncertainty across models, films, or prompting procedures.

Although target ratings were excluded from prompts, exposure to public MovieLens data during model pretraining cannot be ruled out. The other-user film means also show that additional population information can improve prediction beyond the procedure tested here. The contribution is a simple way to improve predictions in a history-based LLM interaction. Testing new ratings, other preference domains, and users' actual choices would establish how far that contribution extends.

MovieLens 1M is available from GroupLens at https://grouplens.org/datasets/movielens/1m/. The study archive retains sampling manifests, prompts, model snapshots, requests, responses, errors, and analysis outputs. No new human participants were recruited for these analyses.

A.1. Data and model settings

MovieLens 1M contains 1,000,209 integer ratings from 6,040 users. We supplied movie titles, genre labels, and the target user's selected earlier ratings. We did not supply demographic information, the target user's database identifier, or other users' ratings. Every history rating had a timestamp strictly earlier than the target rating, and history and target movies did not overlap. Timestamps record the submission of ratings rather than necessarily the viewing of films. The outcome is a recorded expression of preference, rather than a direct measure of enjoyment at the time of the model prediction.

Each of the 12 fixed snapshots in Table C1 supplied one R1-R² pair per condition. No repeated pairs were generated for selection by accuracy. Responses used a strict structured format containing a number and a one-sentence basis. The GPT-4 models used temperature 1, top-p 1, and an output allowance of 400 tokens. GPT-5 models used low reasoning effort, default sampling settings, and an output allowance of 8,192 tokens. Settings were constant across studies. They do not equate computation across models; the roster comparison describes performance under the reported configurations.

R² received the original history, target information, and the complete R1 answer. It received neither the true rating nor another model's prediction. Other user-film pairs, including other films for the same user, were predicted in separate contexts. Communication errors, timeouts, rate limits, and server errors allowed at most two attempts per trial. Incomplete or invalidly formatted content was not regenerated.

A.2. History-size sampling

After an initial pilot, 56 new users were sampled from users with at least one eligible target having 20 strictly earlier ratings. Initial-pilot users were excluded. One eligible target was sampled per user with seed 2026091501. Nested histories contained the latest 1, 3, 5, 10, or 20 ratings before this target. Larger histories therefore extended farther into the past; history count and age were not independently manipulated.

The design required 6,720 responses. We obtained 6,718 valid responses. A GPT-4o R1 response at history size 5 was incomplete, and its dependent R² was not sent. Primary analyses retained the 55 users with complete data at every history size, giving 6,600 responses. All available responses were preserved in the archive. The 55-rating extension retained the 32 users who had at least 55 earlier ratings for their original target. All 768 new responses were valid. The matched comparison at sizes 1-55 reused these users' earlier responses at sizes 1-20. The extension is consequently not an independent replication of those earlier conditions.

A.3. Ten-film sampling

The ten-film study retained the 32 users from the long-history extension. For each user, we fixed a 10-rating history and retained the original target movie. Nine additional targets were randomly sampled from movies rated strictly after the history cutoff, using seed 2026091505. For one user, the original cutoff left only five eligible future movies. We moved that user's cutoff to the latest earlier timestamp that provided at least 10 history ratings and 10 eligible targets. The original target was retained. No target rating or model accuracy was used in this selection rule. This yielded 320 user-movie pairs and 284 distinct movies. Models supplied new responses for every target, including the original targets. There were 7,680 valid responses from 7,680 attempts, with no missing observations or API failures. The same 10 history ratings were used for all 10 targets within a user.

A.4. Scoring and uncertainty

For a prediction p and actual rating y, signed error was p -y. MSE was the mean of (p -y)² and MAE was the mean of |p -y|. An exact match had |p -y| ≤ 10 ⁹; a near match had |p -y| ≤ 0.5, ⁻ allowing numerical tolerance. The prespecified large-error indicator was |p -y| ≥ 1.5. Predictions were not rounded. Other thresholds were examined after the main analysis and are exploratory.

For individual-model performance, errors were calculated before averaging across models. For ensemble performance, the 12 predictions were averaged within a user-movie pair before scoring. Each user received equal weight. In the ten-film study, user-level scores averaged across the user's 10 films and the 12 models. The 12 snapshots are fixed model configurations, not independent human participants.

Paired bootstrap intervals resampled users 10,000 times. Models, films within a user, and repeated history conditions were retained together. We used percentile 95% intervals without adjustment for multiple comparisons. Bootstrap seeds were 2026091502 for the full history study, 2026091504 for the matched extension, and 2026091506 for the ten-film study. Intervals quantify variation across sampled users conditional on the tested roster and target sets. They do not estimate variation across all possible models or fully account for movie identities shared between users. These exploratory studies were not publicly preregistered; protocols and analysis definitions were saved before their corresponding runs.

The constant-3 and history-mean references use no additional population ratings. Film-mean references exclude the target user and average other users' ratings of the target movie. One uses all timestamps and the other only ratings strictly earlier than the target timestamp. Both have access to information absent from the prompts. Audits verified source ratings, temporal ordering, nested histories, fixed within-user histories in the ten-film study, response contexts, missingness, and the distinction between individual-model and ensemble errors.

Appendix D. Study development and execution records

Mean individual-model MSE was 1.276 for R1, 1.186 for R², and 1.200 for Averaging. These users were excluded from the history-size study. The pilot motivated the subsequent history-size comparison and is not pooled into its estimates.

The history-size study recorded 6,778 attempts and 6,718 valid responses. Its 60 unsuccessful attempts comprised 48 interrupted requests, ten timeouts, one URL-related failure, and one incomplete response. The run resumed under the declared retry rule after a recorded timeouthandling correction, without changing obtained responses or the sample. The 55-rating extension added 768 valid responses with no new failures; failures attached to reused requests belong to the earlier run. The ten-film study added 7,680 valid responses with no failures.
