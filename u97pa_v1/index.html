To improve Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .849 This additional analysis did not change the results, and no relationship was found between relationship status and neither GAL-polarization (p = .849), nor TAN-polarization (p = .247) in this younger sample either.
p = .247 This additional analysis did not change the results, and no relationship was found between relationship status and neither GAL-polarization (p = .849), nor TAN-polarization (p = .247) in this younger sample either.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Polarization can be described as when opinions and attitudes discontinue existing on a continuum and are drawn to opposing extremes. Affective polarization refers to the positive feelings and associations towards one's own political ingroup, in combination with negative feelings and associations that arise towards the outgroup on the other end of the spectrum. Recent studies have shown that polarization is increasing in several countries. This change is happening simultaneously with increases in singleness and loneliness. Moreover, a historically significant political gap can be observed in many Western countries between young men and women, raising questions regarding heterosexual relationship formation. The current study set out to examine differences in affective polarization in men and women, and to test loneliness, age, and relationship status as predictors of affective polarization in a Nordic multiparty democracy. We used a population-based sample of 7,357 Finnish adults who responded to an online survey. In line with previous findings, men were found more likely to be TAN (traditional-authoritariannationalist)-polarized, and women more likely to be GAL (green-alternative-libertarian)polarized. In addition, polarization was found to be more common among younger respondents. Surprisingly, relationship status was not found to be a significant predictor of affective polarization. Loneliness was found to be more common in GAL-, but not in TANpolarized respondents, indicating that less moderate political alignment may be associated with loneliness in general.

Introduction

The present study examined affective polarization, the gender political gap, and associations with pair bonds and loneliness in a Finnish population-based sample collected in 2022. Previous research indicates that political polarization has significantly increased in several countries, and that affective polarization has increased in some regions but not in others (Boxell et al., 2021; Emanuele & Marino, 2024; Garzia et al., 2023; Geiger, 2014; Brenan, 2025). Affective polarization refers to when individuals have increasingly positive feelings towards their own political group, while having increasing animosity or increasingly negative feelings towards their political opponents.

A gendered political gap has been observed in several countries, that is, a seemingly significant difference in political attitudes between men and women (Burn-Murdoch, 2024; Dassonneville, 2021). Simultaneously to this shift having occurred, romantic relationships appear to have been on the decline, with rates of not only marital, but also nonmarital and non-cohabitating relationships decreasing (Lei & South, 2021; United States Census Bureau, 2023), especially among young people (Saad, 2015). Concurrently with an increased proportion of the population being single, loneliness is reported as an increasingly common experience and has increasingly been identified as a public health threat (Batanova et al., 2024; Leland, 2022). There is limited research regarding loneliness and political party support, but findings suggest that lonely individuals may be more likely to support radical or populist parties (Langenkamp & Bienstman, 2022; Peterson et al., 2025).

Furthermore, romantic couples tend to have similar political views (Gordon et al., 2024), and previous findings indicate that political affiliations can impact one's rated attractiveness and dating prospects (Bagel, 2024; Huber & Malhotra, 2017; Indikator Opinion, 2025; Nicholson et al., 2016). In summary, current research raises the question of whether increasing political dissimilarity between men and women poses a threat to heterosexual relationship formation.

In light of these findings, a need for studying the interplay between affective polarization, relationship formation and loneliness becomes apparent, particularly as it appears that young adults are forming fewer lasting romantic relationships than previously (Rauer et al., 2013; United States Census Bureau, 2023).

Political Alignment, Polarization and the Gender Gap

Politics functions as a link between people and government and has differing definitions; however, a commonly referenced definition of politics is "who gets what, when, how" (Tansey & Jackson, 2014; Lasswell, 2018). Most political scientists' definitions of politics postulate that it pertains to the social exercise of power (Tansey & Jackson, 2014). Political polarization can be described as a phenomenon where differences in attitudes, opinions or interests in a given society or group discontinue existing on a continuum, and rather become divided in concentration to opposing extremes (Merriam-Webster, 2025). While research on political polarization has mostly focused on preferences regarding policy, partisan polarization can also be studied by measuring a person's animosity towards members of other political parties, a phenomenon called affective polarization. Iyengar et al. (2012) demonstrated that partisans' affective polarization has significantly increased, with negative out-party stereotyping among American Democrats and Republicans having increased by almost fifty percent from 1960 to 2010. This same dramatic effect was not observed in Great Britain (Iyengar et al., 2012), indicating that there are regional differences in the affective polarization of individuals. A potential consideration when studying regional differences in affective polarization is that different countries have differently structured political systems. Research on affective polarization has often been conducted in areas with real or de facto two-party systems, such as the United States. The United Kingdom also has a de facto two-party system, however, there are smaller parties with substantial and growing support (Cracknell et al., 2025). Finland has a multi-party system where all governments are coalition governments, and the government exercises the nation's supreme executive power with its decisions being overseen by the Parliament (Finnish Government, 2025).

Recent data have shown a significant and increasing gap in political attitudes between young men and women in several affluent countries (Burn-Murdoch, 2024). Polls have shown noticeable differences in questions of political policy between men and women (Saad et al., 2024). In the United States, a notable gender gap has been observed in the number of women and men who identify as liberal, with a higher number of women identifying as such. This difference between American men and women was considerably smaller a decade earlier (i.e., in the early 2010s; The Survey Center on American Life, 2022). An increasing political gap between men and women has also been observed in South Korea, Germany and Great Britain (Burn-Murdoch, 2024). A study by Langsæther and Knutsen (2025) found a steady gender gap in women's and men's political attitudes in the majority of the studied Western democracies. Data from 14 European countries showed that women were less traditionalist, more pro-immigration, more economically left-wing, more likely to highlight the environment over the economy, and more inclined to prioritize gender equality. The gap signifies a historically relevant shift in the political sphere, as women's voting preferences leaned somewhat more right and conservative than men's up until the late 1980s (Duverger 1955; Norris, 1999; Oskarson & Ahlbom, 2021).

Studies on affective polarization have mainly been conducted in the United States, which has a de facto two-party system. Studying affective polarization in a multi-party system requires a different approach and can be more demanding than in a two-party system (Wagner, 2021). It is yet somewhat unclear what drives fluctuations in affective polarization. However, it seems that affective polarization differs from country to country. Garzia et al. (2023) found that affective polarization has grown in some, but not all Western democracies, and that it has grown most in the United States. In Boxell et al., (2021), northwestern European countries, including Finland, had lower levels of polarization compared to southern European and central Eastern European countries. As affective polarization may look different in differing political systems, with varying cultural and country-specific influences, studying polarization in a Nordic multi-party system becomes of interest. A study by Kawecki (2022) found that levels of affective polarization have increased in Finland between 2003 and 2019. Isotalo et al. (2019) found increased polarization between the Finnish parties, but did not find evidence for mass polarization in the population, as the ideological center was still very popular.

Previous research has shown that individuals have a consistent proclivity to perceive the ingroup more positively than the out-group; a phenomenon called intergroup bias (Hewstone et al., 2024). This phenomenon is in accordance with the typical self-serving attributional bias, that is, the proclivity to interpret successes as due to one's own performance, but failures as due to external factors (Gioia & Sims Jr., 1985). This bias is ubiquitous in intergroup relations, and an individual's favorable social identity can be built on comparisons between the own in-group, and certain outgroups, where the purpose is to highlight one's own group as positively distinct, as compared to others (Hewstone et al., 2024; Tajfel & Turner, 2004).

The political sphere is no exception to intergroup bias. It has been shown that intergroup bias is common among voters, and that partisan identity can motivate affective polarization (Sheffer, 2020). Robison and Moskowitz (2019) studied how social group polarization is related to affective polarization, finding that polarization between social groups (i.e., not only political parties) is a driver of affective polarization. Studies on group biases and group effects have shed light on how affective polarization functions. When studying affective polarization, it is not merely the positive feelings and associations towards the in-group which are measured, but also the negative feelings and associations towards the out-group. Research on intergroup bias has highlighted the importance of considering the attitudes towards the outgroup; Negational categorization (i.e. defining one's identity by what one is not rather than what one is) affects and shapes one's own social identity and can increase outgroup derogation (Zhong et al., 2008). Furthermore, studies have concluded that humans have a proclivity for noticing, using, and learning from negative information over positive information (Kahneman & Tversky, 1984; Peeters & Czapinski, 1990; Schupp et al., 2004; Vaish et al., 2008). Therefore, studying polarization not only in terms of individuals' own political value orientations, but also in terms of negative evaluations and affect toward others', becomes increasingly relevant. An individual's own political value orientation can be defined as the person's beliefs on how society should function and be structured. A two-dimensional model is often used to visualize individuals' political value orientation, where one dimension describes ideological differences in the role of economic redistribution and state intervention on a left-right axis. The other dimension is cultural and non-economic, and has been described through different concept pairings, such as libertarianism vs authoritarianism; liberalism vs conservatism; and post-materialism vs materialism.

The group of these three concept pairings has been labeled as GAL-TAN, that is, green-alternativelibertarian vs traditional-authoritarian-nationalist (Söderlund, 2023). In contemporary Western Europe, GAL-views tend to be consolidated with the economic left, and TAN-views tend to be consolidated with the economic right (Brigevich et al. 2019). This is crucial to point out, as much of the research on political views has used the left-right axis for measuring political differences.

Although the present study uses the two-dimensional GAL-TAN model, it is worth noting that research shows that the GAL/TAN-categorization is not entirely dichotomous, and that parties can simultaneously have some values from one category and other values from another (Brigevich et al., 2017).

Politics and Romantic Relationships

Humans are innately and consistently motivated by the need for social belonging and seek positive relational experiences within stable and caring relationships (Baumeister & Leary, 1995).

Positive social experiences and relationships are consistently associated with health and longevity.

Relational experiences predict physical health both positively and negatively, so that positive relational experiences are associated with health improvement and longevity, while negative relational experiences have the potential to harm one's physical health (Don et al., 2024; Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010; Rook, 2014). There is robust evidence that stable romantic relationships are associated with individuals having better health and increased levels of happiness (Huntington et al., 2022; Kawamichi et al., 2016). Single individuals report lower life satisfaction and wellness than those who are married or in a relationship, and those who are married have the highest reported life satisfaction (Lehtonen, 2024). The positive effects of romantic relationships and marriage are, however, mediated by relationship satisfaction, and the impact of marital satisfaction on health has been shown to be on par with the health outcomes of a healthy diet (Robles et al., 2013).

Nevertheless, the proportion of single individuals has increased in several countries over the past decades, and romantic relationships are becoming less prevalent. The decline in romantic relationships can be observed in that nuptiality has significantly decreased in several countries (Tilastokeskus, 2024; United States Census Bureau, 2023). The number of one-person households has also been on the rise globally (Cohen, 2021), and in several countries, people are both increasingly living alone or in their parents' home (Hemez m.fl., 2024; Statistics Canada, 2022). Changes in living arrangements have also occurred in Finland; the number of Finns living alone has doubled between 1990 and 2022 (Tilastokeskus, 2023). However, the decrease in romantic relationships is not only seen in the declining number of marriages, but nonmarital and noncohabiting romantic relationships have also become less common (Lei & South, 2021), especially among youth (Statistics Canada, 2022). These changes together indicate that the phenomenon of decreased nuptiality is not fully explained by nonmarital relationships replacing marriages. Instead, romantic relationships overall are seemingly on the decline, and when romantic relationships are formed, they tend not to last as long as previously (Rauer et al., 2013; United States Census Bureau, 2023). Individuals in romantic relationships tend to have similar political views to their partner, but a non-negligible minority of couples with differing political views exists (Gordon et al., 2024). Even among politically dissimilar couples, extreme differences in political views are still uncommon (Gordon et al., 2024). Couples who perceive their relationships as more politically dissimilar report slightly lower perceived relationship quality (Gordon et al., 2024). Van Duyn (2024) found that in politically dissimilar couples, partners affected the others' exposure to news content, and that news gave rise to conflict in the relationship. Curiously, compared to married individuals, a larger gender gap has been observed in political alignment between never-married men and women in the United States (Nadeem, 2024). This suggests that on group level, married men and women are slightly more aligned politically than their never-married counterparts.

Experimental studies investigating how political views affect the formation of relationships have indicated that political stances may influence the evaluation of potential partners. A study by Huber and Malhotra (2017) found that potential dating partners were evaluated more positively when they had political similarities to those making the evaluation. Nicholson et al. (2016) found that participants evaluated images of people as less attractive based on dissimilar preferences in presidential candidates. They also reported that in female evaluators, a similar political preference increased perceived attractiveness of the individuals in the pictures, but this effect was not found in male evaluators. When looking at observational data, political similarities were also associated with the tendency to reach out to potential dating partners (Huber & Malhotra, 2017).

Polls measuring the significance of political alignment in a potential partner also indicate that political views affect partner preference, again seemingly more so for women. A survey by dating app Coffee Meets Bagel found that 69% of daters would filter by political alignment if possible, and that 83% of users would consider leaving a relationship due to different political views. Especially women and the left-leaning found political compatibility important in a relationship, when compared to men and right-leaning individuals (Bagel, 2024). According to a 2023 poll by Yle, 49% of Finns could not imagine themselves being in a serious romantic relationship with a person who votes for the Finns Party, a right-wing populist party currently in government (Takamäki, 2023). A 2025 poll measuring the importance of similar political views found that a notable proportion of both men and women viewed political similarity as important (Indikator Opinion, 2025). However, a noticeable difference was detected in the level of importance men and women attributed to having similar political views in a romantic relationship. This gender difference was most pronounced among the younger respondents: 66% of women under age 30 considered similar political views to be quite important or very important, compared to 42% of men under 30 (Indikator Opinion, 2025). Additionally, a multinational study using survey data from 144 countries found that the importance that women placed on political similarity in a romantic relationship increased along with how extreme their own political views were-women with more extreme political views found political similarity to be more important (Kuschel et al. 2025). In light of these findings, it appears likely that individuals' political views could impact their dating prospects when looking for a partner.

Loneliness and Its Association with Politics

Loneliness has been defined as a discrepancy between the desired levels of social relations and the levels of social relations which have been achieved by the individual (Perlman & Peplau, 1981). Loneliness is currently regarded as a significant public health issue in many affluent societies, and the notion of a present-day epidemic of loneliness is regularly referenced in media (Leland, 2022; M. Ross, 2024; Turunen, 2020; Fagerudd, 2025). Concerns over negative effects of loneliness are wellfounded, as it is apparent that loneliness and social isolation have far-reaching negative effects on the health of individuals and societal resources. Contemporary evidence has shown that loneliness is associated with detrimental health effects, in terms of mental, physical, cardiovascular, and neurological health (Kuiper et al., 2015; Manemann et al., 2018; Richard et al., 2017; Valtorta et al., 2016 ), while positive relational experiences, on the other hand, are associated with longevity and good health (Don et al., 2024; Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010).

A large-scale meta-analysis found that loneliness levels in emerging adults have increased linearly over time, potentially raising legitimate concern, but the review found the label "loneliness epidemic" to be excessive (Buecker et al., 2021). In Finland, the proportion of the population who were 16-years-old or older who felt lonely sometimes, most of the time or all the time, grew from between 2018 and 2022. In these data, elderly people were most often lonely, and young people the second loneliest demographic (Tilastokeskus, 2024). There is also evidence for regional differences in loneliness: for example, loneliness was more prevalent in Eastern European countries than in Nordic European countries in a 2022 study (Surkalim et al., 2022).

Research on the relationship between political leanings and loneliness is limited. Current findings pertaining to politics seem to suggest that loneliness is both associated with supporting a radical or populist political party and with political disengagement. A study by Langenkamp and Bienstman (2022) found an association between weak social belonging and voting for populist parties on the right, but the association between social belonging and support for left-wing populist parties was weak and inconsistent. Peterson et al. (2025) studied the association between loneliness and support for the radical right, finding a statistically significant association between the two.

Additionally, the same study found that left-wing populist and anti-establishment parties were also noticeably lonelier than average, suggesting a general association between support for populism and loneliness. An experimental study by Renström et al. (2020) indicated that those who were more sensitive to being rejected by others and who also experienced social exclusion, were more prone to display willingness to engage with a radical group or identify with one. Whilst a connection between populist support and loneliness appears to be emerging in the evidence, loneliness is also tied to being politically disengaged. Lonely individuals are less likely to vote in elections and experience a lower sense of voting duty (Langenkamp, 2021), also believing that they have a low influence on politics (Langenkamp & Stepanova, 2024). In sum, it appears that lonely individuals are more likely than non-lonely individuals to both show political disengagement and support for populist or more radical parties.

The Current Study

The study of affective polarization has markedly increased since about a decade ago, but several knowledge gaps remain (Halperin et al., 2024; Torcal et al., 2023). While affective polarization in Finland and other Nordic countries has been found to be lower than in many other Western countries, such as the United States and Central Eastern and Southern European countries (Boxell et al., 2021; Reiljan, 2020), these studies show that multi-party systems can also have impactful levels of affective polarization. It is still unclear how affective polarization could potentially affect loneliness and relationship status. The current study set out to explore these phenomena in a multi-party democracy with moderate levels of affective polarization. This study is the first, to the best of my knowledge, to examine the association between relationship status and affective polarization, and the first Finnish study to examine the association between affective polarization and loneliness.

Based on previous findings, I postulated the following hypotheses: (1) Men will be more likely to feel warmth towards parties with TAN-values, while simultaneously having negative feelings towards parties with GAL-values (i.e., to be TAN-polarized).

Women will be more likely to feel warmth towards parties with GAL-values, while simultaneously having negative feeling towards parties with TAN-values (i.e., be GAL-polarized); (2) Younger respondents will be more likely to be affectively polarized; (3) Singleness will be more common in polarized respondents than in non-polarized respondents; and (4) Loneliness will be more common in polarized respondents than in not polarized.

Method

Participants and Procedure

The data used in the present study stemmed from a large, population-based sample of Finnish twins, as well as siblings and parents of twins. Twin families and their postal addresses were obtained from the Digital and Population Data Services Agency of Finland, which maintains the national population registry. In total, addresses to 50,771 individuals were obtained from the registry in the summer of 2021. Over the period from October 2021 to February 2022, these individuals were sent an invitation letter by postal mail to participate in an anonymous online survey. Each individual was assigned a randomly generated, eight-character personal code, which they could use to log on to the survey (these randomly generated codes were subsequently used to match individuals belonging to the same family, however, so that personal information and survey responses were never combined or stored together). Individuals who did not respond in any way were sent a reminder letter 2-3 weeks after the arrival of the first invitation letter. In order to incentivize participation, invitees were offered the possibility to participate in a raffle for 100 gift cards worth €25 apiece to S-Ryhmä, a business conglomerate operating, for example, shops, grocery stores, hotels, restaurants and petrol stations across Finland (an individual could only win one gift card). In total, 12,269 individuals responded (24%) and of these, 854 individuals declined to give informed consent, leaving us with responses from 11,415 individuals. It should be noted, however, that this response rate likely represents an underestimate. For example, according to national statistics, up to 10% of Finns move each year (https://www.stat.fi/). We do not know how many of the potential participants we failed to reach (e.g., due to changing addresses or death) between when the addresses were obtained from the population registry and when the invitation letters were sent out. The final sample for the current study was 7,357 respondents, after selecting one participant per family to eliminate confounding effects of familial resemblance and excluding two participants due to them reporting simultaneous GAL-and TAN-polarization in their responses.

Measures

Demographic Characteristics

The survey collected demographic information on the participants. This information included age, level of education, current socioeconomic status and relationship status. Relationship status was assessed with the question: "Are you in a romantic or sexual relationship with someone at the moment?", with the response options being "no relationship", "I have a sexual partner, but I'm not in a relationship" or "Yes, I am in a romantic relationship". Participants who reported being in a romantic relationship were categorized as such, participants who selected one of the remaining two responses were categorized as single. Information about the gender of participants was obtained from the Central Population Registry (legally registered as male or female).

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured with the UCLA-3-item-loneliness scale, which has been shown to measure loneliness with adequate reliability and validity (Hughes et al., 2004). Respondents were asked three questions; "How often do you experience a lack of companionship?", "How often do you feel you're left on the outside?" and "How often do you feel isolated from others?" and could select responses on a three-point Likert-scale of "Barely ever", "Every now and then", and "Often". Standardized Cronbach's Alpha for the UCLA-3-item-loneliness scale was 0.738 in the current study.

Correspondingly, the majority of the Finns Party voters had a negative perception of the idea of dating voters of the Greens or the Left Alliance in the poll (Takamäki, 2023). Please use the following scale, where 0 means very coldly (very negatively), 50 means that you don't feel negatively or affirmatively (or that you don't have an opinion), and 100 means very warmly (very affirmatively)."

The answers were recoded to a Likert scale of 1-11. Three groups were then identified for the statistical analyses. The GAL-polarized group included respondents who had rated the Left Alliance and/or the Green Party very affirmatively (10 or 11 points on the 11-point scale) while also giving a very negative rating (1 or 2 points on the 11-point scale) to the Finns Party or the Christian Democrats. The TAN-polarized group included respondents with the inverse scores to the aforementioned group (i.e., very negative ratings to the Left Alliance and the Green Party, and very affirmative ratings to the Finns Party and the Christian Democrats). The control group in the study consisted of non-polarized respondents, that is, respondents who did not meet criteria for neither GAL-nor TAN-polarization.

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore affective polarization in Finland and its associations with gender, age, relationship status and loneliness. This question was of particular interest due to reports of increases in polarization and a gender gap in politics, especially among young adults, simultaneously to a decrease in the proportion of the population who are in committed romantic relationships, especially among young adults. The first hypothesis, proposing that men will be more likely to be TAN-polarized, while women will be more likely to be GAL-polarized, was supported with a moderate effect size. The second hypothesis, proposing that young respondents will be more likely to be affectively polarized was also supported: younger participants were more likely to be both GALand TAN-polarized. The third hypothesis, that being single will be more common in polarized respondents was surprisingly not confirmed -no significant relationship was found between affective polarization and relationship status. The fourth hypothesis, positing that loneliness will be more common in polarized respondents was partially supported; a relationship was found between loneliness and being GAL-polarized, but not being TAN-polarized.

Main findings and Interpretations

As predicted, a gender gap was found in our sample; Men were more likely TAN-polarized and women were more likely GAL-polarized, and this finding is in line with previous research (Burn-Murdoch, 2024; Saad et al., 2024). Considering that the tendency for women to vote more conservatively and religiously than men has been observed in several Western countries historically, with men notably identifying themselves as more left-leaning than women until the 1980s (Duverger, 1955; Norris, 1999; Oskarson & Ahlbom, 2021), these results indicate a historically relevant shift in the political sphere. Regarding the results of the current study, it is worth noting that the sample consisted of equally many TAN-and GAL-polarized men, meaning that men in our sample were equally likely to be polarized on both ends of the spectrum. As Finnish men vote for the selected TAN-parties combined at higher rates than the selected GAL-parties combined (Paloheimo 2024; Suuronen et al., 2020; Yle, 2022), this result could indicate a potential sampling bias. This sampling bias is unlikely to have over-exaggerated the gender gap, rather it may have potentially underestimated it.

Several factors have been suggested to explain the gender gap. Women's religiosity has decreased (Shorrocks 2018), and fewer gender gaps have been observed in religious societies (Langsæther and Knutsen, 2025). Another potential explanatory factor is women's increased participation in waged labor, as women's work participation is associated with women being more left-leaning (De Vaus & McAllister, 1989). Women having become more liberal and left is a consistent finding in recent decades, while men on the other hand, in part have also become more liberal, and in part have become more right-wing.

It seems to be both policy questions and voters' identity which can affect their voting choices.

There are certain policy questions which appear to differ more strongly between men and women;

Henriksson (2023) finds the largest differences in questions regarding sexual and gender minorities, but also questions regarding public service cuts and climate action show some differences between the genders. Nevertheless, research on social sorting has found that affective polarization can be driven by social identity and party identity aligning, irrespective of policy questions. Through the process of social sorting, social identity can become increasingly linked with a party, and this has been found to drive strong emotions to all threats and reassurances (Mason, 2016). Therefore, while women and men have differing opinions on certain policy questions, affective polarization can further be strengthened by social and party identity, even when opinions do not differ (Mason, 2016).

Furthermore, affective polarization could potentially be increasingly exacerbated by factors such as media and increased usage of social media (Lane et al., 2023; Van Bavel et al., 2021), economic decline (Rodríguez-Pose et al., 2023), and negational group categorization increasing social divides (Zhong et al., 2008).

In the current study, affective polarization decreased with age. The result is in alignment with previous studies indicating that political differences between men and women are larger in the younger generation (Burn-Murdoch, 2024; Isotalo et al., 2024; Langsæther & Knutsen, 2025). Shorrocks (2018) also highlights that the gender gap is different based on cohorts, and that in younger individuals, men lean more to the right and women lean more to the left. However, it has also been suggested that general affective polarization would increase with age (Phillips, 2022), which the results of the current study contradict. Similarly to the first hypothesis, the decrease in religiosity could be one explanatory factor for this result. Other potential factors could be the previously mentioned economic decline, media and increased usage of social media (Lane et al., 2023; Rodríguez-Pose et al., 2023; Van Bavel et al., 2021). An increased polarization in younger adults is noteworthy, as events occurring during teenage and early adult years appear to be particularly influential for voting preferences (Ghitza et al., 2023).

In the current study, no association was found between being single and being affectively polarized. As previous research has indicated that political values can affect partner choice, how attractive one is perceived as, and one's dating prospects (Bagel, 2024; Huber & Malhotra, 2017; Indikator Opinion, 2025; Nicholson et al., 2016), and that dissimilar political affiliation in a romantic relationship is less common (Gordon et al., 2024), it would seem plausible that heterosexual romantic relationship formation could be affected by affective polarization and the political gender gap.

Although the current study affirmed the gender gap, the third hypothesis, that singleness and affective polarization would be related, was not confirmed. To the best of my knowledge, no prior research has investigated whether there is an association between relationship status and affective polarization, making the present study the first to do so.

Several perspectives could be taken into account to potentially explain the unpredicted result. As the gender gap and singleness appear to be more common among youth, and failing to account for cohort differences can underestimate gender differences (Rauer et al., 2013; Shorrocks, 2018; United States Census Bureau, 2023), I decided to replicate the analysis but only with participants below the age of 30. No association between relationship status and affective polarization was found in this additional analysis either, disproving the hypothesis that generational differences would have been the cause for the unconfirmed third hypothesis. An alternative explanation could be party dislike not transferring strongly to voter dislike. In a 2023 study, Strandberg asked Finns how willing they would be to be friends with a supporter of a certain party, finding that Finns were relatively willing to be friends with a supporter of another party. Most people had only one party whose supporters they were most unwilling to be friends with. The fact that the strength of party dislike did not directly translate into the same level of strong unwillingness to be friends with a party supporter, could indicate that the polarization between political parties is stronger than the hostility between individuals supporting those parties. Potential difficulties in finding a romantic partner due to political views could also be mediated by dating prospects within the same cluster, or in dating the supporters of a party which is least disliked in the opposing cluster.

A multi-party political system might have a protective effect against involuntary singleness caused by difficulty in finding a romantic partner with shared political views. In order to achieve power in a multi-party system, governments have to be formed of parties with somewhat different ideologies collaborating, potentially strengthening sympathy to voters in the same cluster, rather than only the same party. Thus, considering that affective polarization may look different depending on the specific country and political system, studying of the third hypothesis in a different context, particularly one with a two-party system, could be of interest.

A relevant perspective to be taken into consideration is the previously mentioned potential sampling bias in the current study. National polls from the same time period as when invitations to the survey used in the present study were sent out, show approval ratings that are dissimilar from the views reported in the present study (Orjala, 2022). National polls consistently show respondents having higher or similar levels of support for the selected TAN-parties collectively, than the selected GAL-parties collectively (Paloheimo, 2024; Suuronen et al., 2020; Orjala, 2022). In our data, respondents reported more positive views towards the selected GAL-parties than towards the selected TAN-parties, indicating some sampling bias. It seems that TAN-polarized individuals were underrepresented in our data, resulting in a potential sampling bias affecting the outcomes of the analyses. It is therefore possible that affective polarization could have a true association with relationship status, especially if the effect size is small and TAN-rather than GAL-polarization is the driver of this hypothesized association. If this was the case, it could come to light in future research using a more representative sample.

It is also worth noting that singleness is increasingly common in the modern day. A notable proportion of singles are not looking for a romantic partner, and many are reporting that being single is according to their liking (Brown, 2020; Lei & South, 2021). Potential difficulties in finding a romantic partner experienced by polarized individuals, i.e. involuntarily singleness, might not come to light in the analysis, if the general population is electively single to a higher degree than previously.

Furthermore, as relationship status at a given point is a limited measure of romantic relationships, future research could expand the understanding of affective polarization in relation to romantic relationships by studying features such as relationship quality or duration.

In the present study, GAL-polarization was significantly associated with higher levels of loneliness, but TAN-polarization was not. Previous research on political leanings and loneliness is limited, however some studies have found an association between loneliness and support for populist parties and the radical right (Langenkamp & Bienstman, 2022; Peterson et al. 2025).

However, a study by Rydgren (2009), found that those who had voted for populist or radical rightwing parties were not more socially isolated and did not have lower social capital. The association between left-wing populism and loneliness is not entirely conclusive. Langenkamp and Bienstman (2022) found an association with weak social belonging and voting for populist parties on the right, but only a weak and inconsistent association with voting for the populist left. However, Peterson et al. (2025) found similarly high levels of loneliness in supporters of the populist radical right, supporters of the far-left populist party and a left-wing party with anti-establishment rhetoric. The results of the current study present evidence for an association between loneliness and support for left-wing parties. A previous study showed that rejection-sensitive individuals who are rejected are more apt to identify with and support a radical group (Renström et al., 2020), providing a hypothesis for the association between loneliness and more radical political support.

The results of the current study strengthen the hypothesis for an association between loneliness and affective polarization or support for less moderate parties generally. The results highlight that loneliness may also be more common among GAL-polarized individuals. A general tendency for polarized individuals and those supporting less moderate parties to be lonelier would explain why the GAL-polarized individuals in the current study were more likely to report higher degrees of loneliness, but not why the TAN-polarized were not lonelier than non-polarized individuals. The previously mentioned sampling bias may have affected the results, as the TANpolarized individuals were underrepresented. Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that most of the parties selected for the current study are parties with a fair amount of support. The party with the least support of the ones selected was a religious center-right party, which previous research focusing on populist or radical right support could be less applicable to. In general, it is worth noting that the Finnish political field is considered to have low levels of ideological polarization, low levels of conflict, and to be consensus-driven (Kawecki, 2022; Kekkonen & Ylä-Anttila, 2021).

Limitations and Recommendations for Future Research

Women were overrepresented in the sample of the current study (64.8%), and as mentioned above, it seems that some groups of participants (particularly TAN-polarized men) were underrepresented, potentially skewing the results. In the present study, respondents indicated how warmly (affirmatively) or coldly (negatively) they feel towards the most common Finnish parties, but these feelings were not further explored. For future research, it would be of interest to study to which degree these feelings translate to the level of animosity or friendliness towards the voters of other political parties in a multi-party system, as previous research has shown that party dislike does not translate into voter dislike at the same strength (Strandberg, 2023).

Future research is still needed to determine whether being affectively polarized plays a role in relationship formation. Relationship status at a given point in time is a limited measure of one's romantic experience. Future research could expand the understanding of the relationship between affective polarization and romantic relationships through studying other aspects of romantic relationships, such as relationship quality in current or previous relationships, and individuals' own evaluations of their relationship status and dating prospects. Future research could explore whether affectively polarized individuals are more likely to remain single, and whether they, once partnered, have higher levels of conflict, and whether being affectively polarized is associated with an increased or decreased likelihood of romantic relationships ending. Furthermore, it could be of interest for future research to investigate whether the relationship between affective polarization and romantic relationships differs in a two-party political system, in contrast to the multi-party system of the current study.

A relevant question for future research is how strongly affective polarization between political parties transfers to hostility, conflict or negative consequences in romantic and platonic relationships, and daily social interactions. Considering that some studies have found younger adults to be more polarized than older adults (Shorrocks, 2018; United States Census Bureau, 2023), future research could take this into consideration by studying age cohorts separately. Finally, previous research has found that negative categorization increases outgroup derogation, potentially increasing levels of polarization (Zhong et al., 2008). This finding brings out the importance of practicing discretion when studying and reporting on affective polarization.
