What we found

We found 9 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Full text

Abstract

Callous-unemotional (CU) traits characterize a severe group of antisocial youth. Secondary CU traits, distinguished by co-occurring anxiety, are theorized to be acquired when emotionally sensitive children experience prolonged adversity. We tested this acquired hypothesis from age 10 to 16 in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (baseline N = 11,860, Mage = 9.96, 47.8% female).

Dimensional models in the whole sample regressed CU traits at 16 on baseline emotional sensitivity, cumulative adversity, and their interaction. Retrospective models tested whether sensitivity and lifetime adversity predicted classification at 16 as secondary CU (anxiety T ≥ 60) rather than primary CU (anxiety T = 50) or anxiety-only. Prospective models followed sensitive youth (≥ 90th percentile) with no CU traits at age 10 (n = 364). Contrary to the acquired hypothesis, the sensitivity × adversity interaction on later CU traits was negative (B = -.076, p < .001): adversity predicted later CU traits, but more weakly in more sensitive youth. Sensitivity and adversity did not interact to predict secondary over primary CU (B = .004, family-bootstrap 95% CI: -.11, .21), and in sensitive youth without CU traits, no form of adversity predicted CU traits at 16. Results held across alternative measures of sensitivity and adversity, anxiety cutoffs, and covariate sets. Between ages 10 and 16, these data do not support an adversity-driven pathway to CU traits in emotionally sensitive youth; results were consistent instead with comorbid clinical severity or overlapping trait liabilities. These conclusions, however, do not preclude an acquired pathway operating earlier.

Introduction

Youth conduct problems pose a significant public health issue. The youth themselves often face serious downstream consequences, including higher rates of trauma exposure, institutionalization and incarceration, and premature mortality (Fairchild et al., 2019), while also imposing great harm to others (De Brito et al., 2021; Gatner et al., 2023) and substantial economic costs (Gatner et al., 2023; Rivenbark et al., 2018). Thus, delivery of the timeliest, most effective interventions for these youth is critical, and the development of such interventions hinges upon a precise, nuanced understanding of the developmental pathways to youth conduct problems.

Callous-unemotional (CU) traits, characterized by deficient empathy, remorse, and affect, and theorized to capture the affective facet of psychopathy in adulthood (Hare & Neumann, 2008), are particularly helpful for designating a distinct pathway to conduct problems and have been codified in diagnostic systems with the Limited Prosocial Emotions (LPE) specifier for conduct disorders (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; World Health Organization, 2019). Elevated CU traits distinguish a severe group of antisocial youth who warrant specialized treatment (Frick et al., 2014a (Frick et al., , 2014b)), as these youth often display more harmful and versatile misconduct, including proactive aggression, and are at elevated risk for antisocial personality features in adulthood (De Brito et al., 2021; Frick et al., 2014a Frick et al., , 2014b;; Ritchie et al., 2022). These youth also differ from antisocial counterparts with non-elevated CU traits on distinct socioemotional and neurobiological correlates, indicating unique etiological factors (Frick & Kemp, 2021). Although youth with elevated CU traits respond to standard treatments for conduct problems, they tend to begin and end treatment with more severe behavior than peers without elevated CU traits (Perlstein et al., 2023). Although CU-tailored treatment modifications (e.g., PCIT-CU) show promise (Fleming et al., 2022; Kimonis et al., 2019), clarifying how CU traits develop is needed to inform intervention, particularly for adolescents, for whom PCIT is no longer appropriate and trait, behavioral, and contextual factors may have become increasingly fixed, severe, and complex (Steinberg, 2014).

One way to enhance therapies for these youth is to better disentangle causal mechanisms.

Deficient empathy development is considered central to the etiology and expression of CU traits (Frick & Kemp, 2021). However, like most psychopathological phenomena, CU traits present with multiple forms.

The primary variant (the "prototypical" form) has historically received the most attention, is thought to be largely inherited, and is characterized by emotional, physiological, and neurobiological (e.g., amygdala) hyporeactivity to socioemotional cues along with low trait fearfulness or anxiety (Craig et al., 2021). In contrast, the secondary variant is characterized by CU traits that co-occur with high levels of anxiety and hyperreactivity to emotional cues (Craig et al., 2021; Todorov et al., 2025). Distinguishable primary and secondary variants have been found across reporter types, levels of analysis, and youth and adult samples (Cassart et al., 2025; Cecil et al., 2018; Craig et al., 2021; Todorov et al., 2025). Youth with secondary CU traits are also distinct from anxious-or internalizing-only peers, showing less empathy, greater interpersonal problems, and more aversive parenting (Cassart et al., 2025); across comparisons, they often show the worst behavioral outcomes, including higher aggression (Craig et al., 2021). This clinically severe profile raises questions about its development.

Whether anxiety-defined primary and secondary groups represent distinct developmental phenotypes, rather than differences in broader internalizing problems, measurement, or symptom configuration, remains an open question, particularly in community samples and at younger ages. Nor is it clear why CU traits and anxiety co-occur. There are three main hypotheses. The first comes from the psychopathy literature and Karpman (1941), who described primary psychopathy as inborn while the secondary form was theorized to be acquired in response to environmental stress. Contemporary models of secondary CU traits have similarly emphasized adversity-driven pathways, seemingly because youth with secondary CU traits often report more extensive maltreatment histories (Cassart et al., 2025; Cecil et al., 2018; Craig et al., 2021 Craig et al., , 2023;; Todorov et al., 2025). For instance, Kimonis (2023) developed the Emotionally Sensitive Child-Adverse Parenting Experiences-Allostatic Load (ESCAPE-AL) model, which proposes that children with high trait emotional sensitivity who experience prolonged adversity develop CU traits as a defense mechanism (i.e., emotional/empathic numbing) to regulate overwhelming distress. Similarly, Craig et al. (2023) emphasize histories of "colder" parenting among youth with secondary CU traits. In this acquired hypothesis, the secondary variant presents a kind of acquired "phenocopy" of primary CU in which a "callous façade" develops over time as a maladaptive trauma response leading to troubling consequences like conduct problems.

Though the acquired hypothesis remains dominant, it is important to acknowledge alternatives.

One holds that co-occurring anxiety is a consequence of more severe behavior, flipping the directionality of symptoms. For example, Robertson et al. (2023) found that in high-CU youth, higher aggression predicted later increases in anxiety, suggesting co-occurring anxiety marks behavioral severity rather than an independent pathway. Here, behavior is the link between CU traits and later anxiety: aggression and risk-taking raise exposure to victimization and trauma, which may in turn precipitate anxiety, plausible given that secondary CU traits co-occur with both greater trauma histories and more severe, wide-ranging behavior problems (Craig et al., 2021).

A third possibility is a dual-risk or overlapping liability hypothesis. Children may inherit broad risk factors that predispose them simultaneously to elevations on both CU traits and internalizing problems marked by anxiety, heightened emotional reactivity, or negative affect. In this view, the secondary variant is not adversity-driven, and neither anxiety nor CU traits follow the other; rather, it reflects the co-occurrence of two trait liabilities that may have been present from the start. These hypotheses, acquired, behavioral consequence, and dual-liability, are not mutually exclusive, and none has been established as definitive (Frick & Dickens, 2006; Robertson et al., 2023).

We focus on the acquired hypothesis because it remains the prevailing framework and, if correct, carries distinct clinical implications: it would prioritize screening for and treating emotional sensitivity and adversity exposure (e.g., via emotion-regulation or trauma-focused intervention), in contrast to existing CU-specific modifications that target deficient emotional reactivity and prosocial behavior (Fleming et al., 2022; Kimonis et al., 2019).

This study used both retrospective and prospective analyses to test the acquired hypothesis for secondary CU traits. Using the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, a large, epidemiologically informed longitudinal cohort, we tested the acquired hypothesis from age 10 to age 16 in three ways. First, dimensional tests in the whole sample asked whether baseline emotional sensitivity and cumulative adversity interact to predict CU traits at 16; the acquired hypothesis predicts that adversity raises CU traits more in sensitive youth. Second, retrospective tests used the cutoffs applied in prior work, which we treat as that literature's operationalization rather than as validated subtypes, to distinguish primary CU (elevated CU traits, no co-occurring anxiety), secondary CU (elevated CU traits with elevated anxiety) and anxiety-only presentations at 16, and asked whether baseline sensitivity and lifetime adversity interact to predict secondary over primary CU. Third, prospective tests followed highly sensitive youth with no CU traits at baseline and asked whether cumulative adversity predicted the increase or onset of CU traits.

Importantly, we do not assume adolescence is the primary etiological period for CU traits but treat it as a theoretically informative window in which socioemotional systems, adversity exposure, and regulatory demands expand and reorganize (Pollmann et al., 2025; Steinberg, 2014). If CU traits can be acquired through prolonged adversity in emotionally sensitive youth, adolescence should be a detectable period for such processes; null findings would suggest the pathway operates earlier, is confined to more severe clinical contexts, or is less central than theorized.

Participants

Participants were drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, a longitudinal cohort that recruited 11,880 children aged 9-11 from 21 sites across the United States between 2016-2018. The current study used data from the https://doi.org/10.82525/8f3w-5260 which includes data collected at baseline and annually over seven years (i.e., eight total timepoints). Analyses

All analyses used R version 4.5.1, and the code will be made available on GitHub upon publication. Siblings are nested within families; linear and logistic models used family-clustered standard errors, and multinomial models are reported with family-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (1,000 resamples of families) for the interaction terms beside the model-based standard errors.

Analyses tested the acquired hypothesis for secondary CU traits in three complementary ways.

Dimensional analyses in the whole sample asked whether baseline emotional sensitivity and cumulative adversity interact to predict CU traits at age 16. Retrospective analyses asked whether baseline sensitivity and lifetime adversity predicted later classification as having secondary CU traits, relative to primary CU traits or an anxiety-only presentation. Prospective analyses asked whether emotionally sensitive youth with no CU traits at baseline developed CU traits following cumulative adversity exposure.

Dimensional tests: Does adversity raise later CU traits more in sensitive youth?

CU traits at age 16 (summed score) were regressed on baseline emotional sensitivity, cumulative adversity (unit-weighted composite), their interaction, and the covariates, using linear regression with family-clustered standard errors; negative binomial models were fit as a check on the count outcome.

Sensitivity and adversity were standardized before forming the product, and simple slopes of adversity were probed at -1 SD, the mean, and +1 SD of sensitivity. Per the acquired pathway, we predicted a positive interaction: adversity should raise later CU traits more in sensitive youth. Using the cutoffs applied in prior work (Hawes et al., 2021; Kahn et al., 2013), youths were classified at age 16 as primary CU (CU sum ≥ 4, anxiety T = 50, the scale floor), secondary CU (CU sum ≥ 4, anxiety T ≥ 60), or anxiety-only (CU sum < 4, anxiety T ≥ 60). CU-elevated youth with T = 51-59 fall between the primary and secondary definitions and were not classified. Sensitivity analyses used a stricter anxiety cutoff (T ≥ 65) for the secondary CU and anxiety-only groups and classified the T = 51-59 youth as primary.

Baseline emotional sensitivity, lifetime adversity (general adversity and aversive parenting, separately), and their interaction were regressed onto group classification using multinomial regression with secondary CU as the reference group, as the study purpose was to test its distinction from primary CU and anxiety-only. Three models were fit: Model 1, sensitivity, adversity and their interaction; Model 2, the main effects with covariates; Model 3, Model 2 plus the interaction, which is the test of the hypothesis. Sensitivity and adversity were mean-centered before forming the product. Supplemental logistic regressions assessed main and interactive effects on secondary CU (vs. not) and primary CU (vs. not) classification (Supplemental Table S5). Per the acquired pathway, we predicted the sensitivityby-adversity interaction would uniquely predict greater probability of secondary CU classification.

Because the models adjust for baseline CU traits, they test whether the interaction predicts the group difference arising between ages 10 and 16, not a process begun earlier.

Prospective tests: Do emotionally sensitive youth with no CU traits develop them when exposed to adversity?

A highly sensitive (90th percentile on PCA-derived emotional sensitivity) group with no CU traits (all items "not at all true"; sum = 0) at baseline was identified (n = 364). Within this group, each cumulative-adversity component (general, poverty, aversive parenting, PTEs) and the unit-weighted composite across ages 11-15 was regressed onto CU traits at age 16 using linear regression, first alone and then with covariates (baseline aggression, family history of antisocial behavior). We predicted adversity would be positively associated with CU traits. Sensitivity analyses used alternative cutoffs (75th and 80th percentiles; 1, 1.5, 2 SDs above the mean). To address prospective hypotheses with greater power, supplemental logistic regressions in the full baseline subsample with no CU traits and no sensitivity constraint tested whether the sensitivity-by-adversity interaction predicted the onset of elevated CU (vs. not) and of secondary CU (vs. not) by age 16 (Supplemental Table S6).

Robustness analyses

The headline estimates were re-estimated under each of the following, with results in Supplemental Table S7: the three alternative sensitivity measures; alternative summaries of the same adversity measures (a cumulative-risk count of domains at or above the 75th percentile, and threat and deprivation sub-indices following McLaughlin and Sheridan, 2016); the 18-item aggression T-score as the covariate; baseline anxiety, sex, and household income as added covariates; the alternative group definitions above; and past-year rather than lifetime event counts.

Results

Descriptives and baseline (age 10) differences based on age-16 group classification

At baseline, parents reported a mean of 0.5 lifetime PTEs (SD = 0.9; upper decile, 1). By the 5year follow-up, parents had reported a mean of 1.3 negative life events on the lifetime checklist (upper decile, 4) and youth a mean of 2.5 (upper decile, 5). Exposure was higher on every count in the highly sensitive subgroup with no baseline CU traits (n = 364): PTEs M = 0.9 (upper decile, 2); parent-reported events M = 2.3 (upper decile, 5); youth-reported events M = 3.1 (upper decile, 7; Table 1). Thus, the sample, and this subsample in particular, contained the repeated adversity the acquired hypothesis requires.

At age 16, 794 youth (8.5% of those assessed) had elevated CU traits and anxiety data. Of these, 252 were classified as primary CU, 192 as secondary CU, and 350 (44%) had anxiety T = 51-59 and were not classified; 885 youth were classified as anxiety-only. Group differences at baseline are shown in Figure 1; descriptives for the total sample and groups, with family-clustered, Holm-adjusted contrasts, appear in Supplemental Table S3, and correlations in Supplemental Table S4. Descriptives and correlations for the sensitive no-CU subsample appear in Table 1.

The age-16 secondary CU group was already the most elevated group at age 10. They exceeded both other groups on emotional sensitivity (M = 1.84 vs -0.05 for primary CU and 1.21 for anxiety-only), anxiety (T = 60.3 vs 51.9 and 58.8), depression (T = 59.2 vs 53.3 and 57.7), irritability (3.71 vs 2.00 and 2.56) and aggression (4.60 vs 2.58 and 2.55; all contrasts p ≤ .037). On CU traits, secondary youth did not differ from primary youth (2.14 vs 1.91, p = .20), and both exceeded anxiety-only youth (0.93, p < .001).

Secondary youth had the highest family history of antisocial behavior (47% vs 32% and 38%) and, with anxiety-only youth, of anxiety (42% vs 26% and 42%). Lifetime general adversity did not differ across the groups (F = 1.46, p = .23). Aversive parenting ran the other way: primary CU youth were highest (0.35 vs -0.03 for secondary and -0.07 for anxiety-only; p = .013 and < .001), and secondary youth did not differ from anxiety-only youth (p = .73). The groups also differed by sex (28% of primary, 42% of secondary, and 57% of anxiety-only youth were female; χ²(2) = 69.5, p < .001), by household income (37.9%, 33.3%, and 27.0% below $50,000; χ²(2) = 11.5, p = .003), but not by age.

Dimensional tests: Does adversity raise later CU traits more in sensitive youth?

In the whole sample (n = 8,875), the sensitivity × cumulative adversity interaction on CU traits at 16 was negative (B = -0.076 per SD × SD, SE = 0.016, p < .001). Adversity therefore predicted later CU traits more strongly in less sensitive youth. One SD more cumulative adversity predicted 0.21 more CU points (SE = 0.03, p < .001) for youth 1 SD below the mean on sensitivity, 0.13 (SE = 0.02) at the mean, and 0.06 (SE = 0.02, p = .016) for youth 1 SD above it (Figure 2). The simple-slope lines cross near +1 SD of adversity: sensitive youth had modestly higher predicted CU traits at low adversity but gained less with increasing adversity than less sensitive youth. The effect of adversity is attenuated in sensitive youth, not reversed. The interaction was likewise negative for the general-adversity component (B = -0.062, p < .001) and null for the parenting, poverty and PTE components, and negative binomial models gave the same pattern (composite interaction p < .001).

Prospective tests: Do emotionally sensitive youth with no CU traits develop them when exposed to adversity?

In the emotionally sensitive (top 10%) subgroup with no baseline CU traits (n = 364; 266 with the age-16 outcome), no form of cumulative adversity across ages 11-15 was associated with CU traits at 16 (all p ≥ .11; Table 3). Neither was baseline aggression (p = .076 to .17); family history of antisocial behavior was the only consistent predictor (B = 0.33 to 0.37, p = .035 to .076). With a broader definition of "sensitive" (75th percentile; n = 827), the unit-weighted composite did predict later CU traits, positively (B = 0.28, p = .007), and the more stringent cutoffs (1, 1.5 and 2 SDs) were null (Supplemental Table S6). These effects are consistent with the whole-sample model, which implies a positive adversity slope through most of the sensitivity range that approaches zero in its upper tail, where the 90th-percentile subsample lies; they do not indicate an effect specific to sensitive youth. In the full no-CU subsample (n = 5,028-5,436), the sensitivity × adversity interaction on the onset of elevated CU (n = 191-206 cases) or secondary CU (n = 44-46) by age 16 was negative in every model and significant for the composite (elevated CU: B = -0.18, p = .007; secondary CU: B = -0.20, p = .017; Supplemental Table S6).

Robustness

The conclusions did not depend on the choices examined in Supplemental Table S7. The dimensional interaction remained negative and significant under every alternative covariate set, adversity index, and life-event window (i.e., past-year or incident event scoring in place of lifetime checklists); it was smaller with the EATQ-R (B = -0.038, p = .026) and non-significant with the youth-reported BIS (B = -0.024, p = .17) as the sensitivity measure, and in no specification was it positive (Supplemental Figure S1). The retrospective interactions remained null throughout. In sum, no operationalization of sensitivity or adversity produced the positive sensitivity × adversity interaction on later CU traits that the acquired hypothesis predicts, whether looking backward from age 16 or forward from age 10.

Discussion

Can CU traits be acquired through adversity in emotionally sensitive youth? We tested this from age 10 to age 16 in the full ABCD cohort, with dimensional, retrospective, and prospective designs and with several theoretically and empirically driven operationalizations of sensitivity and adversity. Within that window we found no support for it. In the whole sample, adversity predicted later CU traits across the probed range of sensitivity, but less strongly for more highly sensitive youth, the opposite of the acquired prediction. Sensitivity and adversity did not interact to predict who was classified as secondary rather than primary CU at 16. Sensitive youth who had no CU traits at age 10 did not acquire them in proportion to the adversity they experienced. These conclusions held under alternative sensitivity measures, adversity indices, anxiety cutoffs and covariate sets, and with family-clustered inference. This pattern converges with longitudinal community evidence from earlier in development, using a comparable anxiety-based classification: in the ELISA cohort, with temperament and parenting measured at age 4 and variants classified from ages 5 to 10, early environmental risk did not selectively predict secondary CU; punitive parenting and lower parental warmth predicted both variants, whereas fearlessness uniquely predicted the primary variant (Álvarez-Voces et al., 2026). The dimensional finding deserves a plain statement.

Adversity was associated with higher CU traits at 16, by about 0.1 to 0.2 points per SD. That association was attenuated, not reversed, in sensitive youth. One reading is that emotionally sensitive children respond to adversity with the anxiety and dysphoria the sensitivity composite indexes, rather than with the callousness the acquired model proposes, so that adversity's association with CU traits is carried by less reactive youth. We note the alternative that the composite, built from parent-reported symptoms, is a poor index of the temperament the model has in mind; the results with the EATQ-R and the youth-reported BIS were weaker but in no case in the predicted direction.

Our findings are more consistent with the dual-risk or overlapping liability hypothesis. Youth classified as secondary CU at 16 were already the most impaired group at age 10 on every symptom measure: CU traits equal to those of primary youth, and higher anxiety, depression, irritability, and aggression than both comparison groups. The retrospective models adjust for these baseline levels, so they test whether sensitivity and adversity predict the group difference that arises between ages 10 and 16, among youth comparable at 10; within that window they do not. The same ordering held at age 16, where secondary youth were the only group whose aggression rose over the follow-up. Results in the sensitive no-CU group are also consistent: neither adversity nor baseline aggression predicted later CU traits, and family history of antisocial behavior was the only consistent predictor. These profiles are compatible with co-occurring heritable or temperamental risk (Tomlinson et al., 2026) and with an acquired pathway that was complete before age 10; they do not distinguish between them. Neither the dual-liability nor the behavioral-consequence account (Robertson et al., 2023) was tested here. Testing the consequence account requires repeated measures of CU traits, behavior, exposure and anxiety and models that separate within-person change from stable differences. This design tests the acquired pathway between ages 10 and 16. Because every model adjusts for, or selects on, CU traits at age 10, it cannot detect a process completed earlier in childhood, and we do not claim otherwise. Adolescence is nonetheless a period in which the acquired account makes predictions: exposure to adversity outside the family increases, CU traits are commonly assessed in clinical and justice settings at these ages, and most of the evidence for secondary variants comes from adolescent samples (Craig et al., 2021). Our sample carried the exposure the hypothesis requires, with a tenth of parents reporting four or more negative life events and a tenth of youth five or more by age 15, and higher rates in the sensitive subsample. If sensitive youth can acquire CU traits through adversity in this period, that process should have been observable here, and it was not. It remains possible that the acquired hypothesis, as proposed in ESCAPE-AL (Kimonis, 2023), applies to earlier childhood, to the most severe clinical populations, or to particular forms of adversity, such as sexual abuse, that this study could not capture (McLaughlin & Gabard-Durnam, 2022). Testing it there will require cohorts followed from early childhood with temperament measured before CU traits emerge.

The cutoff-defined groups should be read as the operationalization used in prior work, not as validated subtypes. In this cohort, 44% of youth with elevated CU traits at 16 fell between the primary and secondary anxiety cutoffs and were classified by neither, and classifying them as primary did not change the conclusions. Identifying which features, such as anxiety, emotional reactivity, trauma exposure, and their timing, mark a distinct secondary variant is a precondition for testing developmental models of how such a variant arises, and the present results suggest that this work is not finished. Several limitations should be considered. All measures were parent-reported except where noted, and the same parent reported CU traits, anxiety, and aggression, so shared method contributes to their associations; future work should incorporate multi-method indices of emotional sensitivity, anxiety, and CU traits. The CU measure is brief and parent-reported: four items, three of them reverse-scored prosocial items, validated in this cohort (Hawes et al., 2020) and the only CU measure available for the full sample at every wave, but it does not cover the full content of established instruments and has a marked floor ( 53to 59% of youth score zero at each wave). Models using lifetime adversity lost about 30% of cases listwise because life events were first assessed at the 1-year follow-up, more among primary youth, and retained youth had somewhat more PTEs and higher sensitivity than excluded youth. Finally, ABCD is a community cohort, and the pathway may operate only in the more severe clinical or justice-involved contexts in which secondary variants have mostly been described; replication in clinic-referred or justiceinvolved samples is warranted.

The findings bear on clinical research rather than on practice, because observational associations between adversity and symptom change do not estimate treatment effects. They suggest that, at least in this period, reducing adversity exposure alone would not be expected to prevent secondary CU traits, and that trials comparing trauma-focused components with approaches that address co-occurring CU traits and anxiety directly, such as emotion regulation and anxiety management (Linehan, 2020) alongside coaching empathic and prosocial skills, would be informative for youth presenting with both. Evidence on whether the variants respond differently to treatment is limited and mixed, with differences emerging after PCIT-CU in young children (Fleming et al., 2023) but not after functional family therapy or multisystemic therapy in adolescents (Thøgersen et al., 2022), underscoring the need to tailor and test interventions by variant.

In conclusion, between ages 10 and 16 in a large community cohort, we found no support for an adversity-driven pathway to CU traits in emotionally sensitive youth. Adversity predicted later CU traits, but less so in the most sensitive. Youth with secondary CU traits were already the most impaired at age 10, consistent with pre-existing temperamental and/or genetic liabilities. The acquired account may yet hold for earlier childhood or for more severe populations, but in the window these data observe it did not.

Disentangling these mechanisms remains crucial for improving prevention and treatment for antisocial youth with CU and anxiety features.
