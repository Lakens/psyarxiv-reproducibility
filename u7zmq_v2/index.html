Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Natural disasters, pandemics, armed conflict, and economic upheaval inflict lasting harm on mental wellbeing, yet what determines who recovers and who deteriorates remains poorly understood across crisis types.

Objectives: This review maps how crisis exposure shapes wellbeing across four crisis typologies, two surveillance modes (NLP-based and survey/administrative), and three impact directions (favorable, adverse, or mixed).

Eligibility criteria: Peer-reviewed journal articles and conference papers published in English between 2016 and 2026, applying machine learning, NLP, or deep learning methods to a population exposed to one of the four crisis typologies, and reporting at least one measurable mental health outcome.

PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore, supplemented by forward and backward citation searching and targeted keyword searches.

Factors were charted using the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health framework, adapted for the health system where the framework's coverage is thinnest. They were sorted into tiers, subgroups, and subdimensions with maximum possible fidelity to the original framework.

Results: Across 338 code-able determinants, Intermediary Determinants, particularly psychosocial and behavioral-biological factors, accounted for two-thirds of coded determinants, while NLP-based methods proved most effective at capturing structural and contextual signals (e.g., governance, discoursebased constructs) rather than the clinical burden measures that dominate closer to the individual. Favorable, protective constructs were both rarer and less computationally detected than adverse ones, concentrating almost entirely in mastery, self-concept, coping, and social capital. Forty-five factors were set aside as Outcome Measures because they described prior symptoms, diagnoses, or the study's own outcome construct, with 8 being circular with the study's stated target. Fewer studies examined economic downturns, and conflict studies had almost no NLP presence.

Conclusions: These findings demonstrate this field has matured methodologically in adverse detection but remains structurally limited in reach. This is most evident in armed conflict contexts and in the surveillance of protective, resilience-oriented signals, highlighting the need for digital phenotyping, expanded NLP coverage, and wider adoption of explainable AI methods.

1. Introduction

Mental health concerns have reached unprecedented levels, with more than one billion people living with one or more mental disorders (World Health Organization [WHO], 2025a). Given a global count of 727,000 suicides each year among individuals aged 15 to 29 (WHO, 2025b), mental health problems have grown in prominence in the global health and development agenda. Recent longitudinal research indicates that overlapping global crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the Russia-Ukraine War, and economic recession result in functional impairments that persist long after these events resolve (Richter et al., 2024). The cumulative psychological toll of recent epidemic crises, including SARS-CoV-1, Ebola, Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS), and the Anthrax threat, has been studied (Esterwood & Saeed, 2020; Xiong et al., 2020). A review of crisis intervention strategies has also shown how intervention causes meaningful and significant improvements in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression outcomes (Hu et al., 2025).

Moreover, understanding why some individuals are less resilient to crisis-induced psychological harm than others has driven a growing body of research in this field across diverse domains. Yet, existing reviews of the factors causing crisis-induced psychological harm have been limited in scope. They focus on single crisis types such as pandemics (Lieneck et al., 2021; Kunzler et al., 2021) and economic turmoil (Stein et al., 2023). They rely on survey-based and epidemiological primary studies, without integrating computational or data-driven methods, which have been shown to enable population-scale, real-time monitoring of mental health during crises (Tušl et al., 2022) and allow early detection of psychological distress signals before clinical presentation, supporting timely intervention during and after crisis events (Shatte et al., 2018).

To address these gaps, this review treats wellbeing more comprehensively, examining predictive factors across four crisis typologies: natural disasters, pandemics and infectious disease outbreaks, armed conflict and forced displacement, and economic upheavals. This paper adopts a scoping review methodology to systematically map the breadth of evidence across crisis types and wellbeing dimensions. A scoping review is appropriate because the field is heterogeneous in methods, populations, and outcomes, and the aim is to map breadth and identify gaps rather than pool effect sizes (Arksey and O'Malley, 2005).

2. Methods

This review followed PRISMA-ScR (Tricco et al., 2018) and the Arksey and O'Malley (2005) framework as refined by Levac et al. (2010). A preregistered protocol is on OSF (https://osf.io/9jcs7, from 3 July 2026). Using the PCC framework (Peters et al., 2022), this review targets adults and communities exposed to population-scale crises (military, civilian, healthcare worker, refugee, and disaster-survivor populations), aligned with WHO Mental Health Action Plan 2013 -2030 priorities (WHO, 2021).

Studies were eligible if they applied a machine learning, NLP, deep learning, or spatial modeling method with a predictive, classification, or clustering function; examined one of the four crisis typologies; and reported at least one mental health outcome. We conducted a systematic search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore on 27 June 2026, combining crisis, method, and outcome term blocks (2016-2026, English only), yielding 7,940 records (Supplement A). Supplementary searching (citation chasing and keyword searches) identified 11 further records (forward citation n=3, backward citation n=3, keyword search n=5; Supplement B). All results are archived in RIS/CSV on OSF.

The search strategy was structured around three core concepts: crisis exposure, mental health and wellbeing outcome, and computational method. Crisis exposure terms included pandemic, natural disaster, armed conflict, refugee, and economic turmoil. Mental health and wellbeing terms included PTSD, depression, anxiety, and resilience, as well as positive wellbeing terms such as flourishing and life satisfaction. Computational method terms included machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing, and named algorithms such as random forest and support vector machines. Terms within each concept were combined using OR and truncation, and the three concepts were combined using AND; a 2016 to 2026 date limit and English language filter were applied across all databases. Full search strings for all four databases are provided in the Supplements.

Two reviewers conducted title/abstract and full-text screening independently, resolving disagreements through discussion or referral to a third reviewer. Agreement at full-text screening was κ = 0.904 (6/133 disagreements). Screening is summarized in Figure 1. Findings were synthesized narratively, grouped by crisis typology and analyzed across three areas: the wellbeing and non-wellbeing dimensions of outcomes and their associated factors (5.1), patterns of adverse, favorable, and mixed factors across crisis types (5.2), and computational methods and explainability (5.3). Each factor was classified into named tiers, subgroups, and subdimensions adapted from the WHO CSDH framework (Table 1).

Given study heterogeneity, meta-analytic pooling was not possible; instead, each study's factors were tallied by direction, dimension, crisis typology, and computational method (NLP-based versus survey/administrative), so patterns could be compared within and across these groupings rather than pooled into a single effect size. Studies reported factor prominence on two non-equivalent bases: outcome-linked (e.g., SHAP, permutation importance, regression coefficients), tied to a fitted model predicting a defined outcome and typically accompanied by a performance metric; and frequency-or association-based (e.g., topic/emotion frequency in NLP studies, association-rule support/confidence or causal-forest split weighting in survey studies), reflecting how often or strongly a theme or association appeared with no outcome variable modeled. Findings are compared with two prior non-computational reviews in Section 5.3. Data were extracted using a standardized charting form, piloted on five included studies. Charted variables included bibliographic information, crisis typology, country/setting, study design, data source type, sample size, computational method(s), evidence type (prediction-focused vs. pattern-focused, following Tu et al., 2025), primary outcome(s), and predictive factor classification. 3.1 The CSDH Framework and Its Adaptation for Crisis Research

This review adopts the conceptual framework for action on the social determinants of health developed by Solar & Irwin (2010) for the World Health Organization Commission on Social Determinants of Health. It organizes health inequities along a causal chain moving from socioeconomic and political context through structural determinants to intermediary determinants of health. The framework has since been applied and extended in later work, including a systematic review of disaster health literature (Nomura et al., 2016) and a recent refinement of the original World Health Organization structure using empirical case material (Karatekin et al., 2024 (Karatekin et al., , 2026)).

The framework organizes crisis determinants into three causal tiers: (1) Structural Determinants (governance, policy, social position) generate a person's socioeconomic position and socio-political context, which determine differential exposure to risk, differential vulnerability once exposed, and differential consequences of illness; (2) Bridging Determinants (social cohesion and social capital) carry that structural position down to the individual, or fail to; and (3) Intermediary Determinants (material, psychosocial, behavioral, health-system) convert this into the lived health outcome. CSDH fixes the direction of this pipeline and the four intermediary categories, but leaves open how each subdimension is operationalized, and how finely subdimensions are split.

3.2 Fidelity to the Source Framework

The present taxonomy proposed in the paper follows the textual authority of Solar & Irwin to guide classification. Every subdimension corresponding to a construct named in the source text, whether stated without qualification or introduced through a hedged example list, was retained faithfully and traced to its originating passage. This procedure follows directed content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005) and the deductive qualitative content analysis process described by Elo & Kyngäs (2008), in which theory-derived categories are applied to a corpus as predetermined codes .

The coding scheme draws its subgroups and sub-dimensions from Solar & Irwin's (2010) CSDH framework. Under Socioeconomic Position: Age, Occupation, Gender, Education, Race/Ethnicity, and Income are all taken verbatim from the stratifying list in Section 5.5.8. Under Socioeconomic & Political Context: Culture & Societal Values is verbatim from the six-point context list in Section 5.4. Under Material Circumstances: Financial Means, Housing, and Material Work Environment are all verbatim from Section 5.6.1. Under Health System: Access and Responsiveness/System Performance are both verbatim from Section 5.6.4. Under Social Cohesion & Social Capital: Bonding, Bridging, and Linking Social Capital are all verbatim, originally from Szreter & Woolcock (2004) as cited in Solar & Irwin's Section 5.6.6, though Linking Social Capital had zero occurrences in the actual corpus.

Governance, as coded here, consolidates state action across three registers: direct governance and restriction measures, macroeconomic policy, and public policy content. These are combined into a single subdimension because crisis literature items along macroeconomic and public policy lines were too sparse individually to support their own subdimensions and share a common state action character with governance content proper. Epidemiological Conditions has been modified to track the same information for all types of crises, not just epidemics as it did originally. Renamed Contextual and Environmental Conditions, it tracks the crisis's objective, measurable scale, case counts, environmental hazard severity, and so on, distinct from the state's political response.

3.3 Points of Extension

Solar & Irwin acknowledge directly that a full characterization of the socioeconomic and political context was beyond the scope of their paper. An earlier draft of the framework had attempted to include every element in a single diagram, and the authors report that this approach was abandoned as unhelpful. The need for extension is greatest precisely where the source framework's own coverage is thinnest. The health system discussion, although substantive on financing and equitable access, never engages with psychiatric care, psychological support, or mental health service delivery at any point in the document. This asymmetry follows directly from the framework's origins in socioeconomic epidemiology and intersectoral policy rather than clinical or psychological science. This review has followed the CSDH framework with maximum fidelity, taking most of its constructs verbatim. Because the framework provides an overarching structure whose subdimension content is illustrative rather than exhaustive, applying it to a review of crisis literature necessarily surfaces content the source framework does not itself name. This is an expected consequence of applying a general health equity policy framework outside the context of its original construction (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). Category development followed the taxonomy development method of Nickerson et al. (2013), alternating between testing framework-derived categories against the corpus and defining new categories from corpus content the framework could not accommodate. This mirrors both Karatekin et al. (2026), who refined their agent and power-centered social determinants framework after case material could not be accommodated within its earlier version, and Nomura et al. (2016), who derived 35 disaster health themes from within CSDH's existing three-domain structure.

Rather than developing an original account of trust and reciprocity, Solar & Irwin explicitly adopt Szreter & Woolcock's (2004) bonding, bridging, and linking typology, crediting the source and carrying its vocabulary directly into the framework's own subdimension labels. This review anchors the psychosocial determinants in Pearlin's Stress Process Model (Pearlin et al., 1981) because it provides an empirically validated account of how discrete Life Events and Chronic Strains erode self-concept and elevate psychological distress, mediated by the individual's Coping mechanisms and ideals of Mastery and Self Concept. CSDH's own text supplies anchors for four of Pearlin's five roles in one passage. It is defined as "psychosocial stressors (for example, negative life events and job strain), stressful living circumstances (e.g., high debt) and (lack of) social support, coping styles, etc.", which covers discrete events and job strain as Life Events, ongoing debt as Chronic Strains, and coping directly. CSDH also anticipates Mastery and Self Concept and Personality Traits, describing how "people perceive the events related to their health as controllable (internal control) or as controlled by others (external control)". CSDH anchors the Behavioral and Biological subdimension firmly to the body and to conduct, never to relational or cognitive experience. Its definition is entirely physical: smoking, diet, alcohol consumption, and lack of physical exercise, with genetic factors folded in among the biological factors. Nothing in this definition touches relationships or cognition, just as nothing in the Psychosocial definition touches physiology. That asymmetry marks the boundary between the two subdimensions: one is oriented to the person's perceived and relational environment; the other to the person's physiology and conduct. 4. Outcomes of Crises and Relevant Factors Identified Through Computational Methods

This section maps the 70 included studies by crisis type, population, computational method, analytical basis, and reported key finding. Associated factors that significantly contribute to mental wellbeing outcomes following crisis exposure can be split into three types: favorable, mixed, and adverse factors. Adverse factors increase the probability of wellbeing deterioration, while favorable factors buffer against it. Mixed or non-monotonic factors may increase the probability in some situations and decrease it in others.

4.1 Pandemics and Infectious Disease Outbreaks

Of the 23 studies in this category, 11 use NLP or text-based methods (Table 2) and 13 use survey or administrative data (Table 3). Method choice tracks data type: text-based studies predominantly use transformer architectures (BERT, RoBERTa, or fine-tuned variants) for emotion and sentiment classification, while survey-based studies predominantly use tree-based ensembles (random forest, gradient boosting, XGBoost) to rank candidate variables. Populations range from general social media users (Ou et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2023) to specific subgroups such as healthcare workers (Zhou et al., 2022; Elyashar et al., 2021), pregnant women (Nichols et al., 2022; Dhankar & Katz, 2023), and older adults (Xiang et al., 2025). 4.3 Natural Disasters

Emotional factors dominate the NLP subset (Table 6), since nearly all target negative emotion or sentiment directly. This crisis type spans the widest methodological and population range in the review: military cohorts, civilian survivors, first responders, and refugees (Table 8 and Table 9). Clinical and affective outcomes dominate as primary targets (PTSD, depression, anxiety). Across military populations, predeployment symptom burden and social disconnection are the most consistent adverse predictors, while unit cohesion, resilience, and strong social networks are favorable (Papini et al., 2023; Kessler et al., 2024). Among conflict-exposed civilians, coerced loss of personal agency and unmet basic needs are the most distinctive adverse factors (Conrad et al., 2017; Jayawickreme et al., 2020). Among refugees, structural and material conditions such as income, housing, and time since displacement recur as predictors, alongside family contact as a favorable factor (Figueiredo & Ndiaye, 2025; Keller et al., 2026).

Non-NLP methods, for the same reason as Age and Gender, mainly collected it. Race and Ethnicity and Income remained too small to analyze. However, Financial Means under the Material Circumstances subgroup also captured the same signals as Income.

Financial Means (n = 15, e.g., financial worries, income insecurity, asset loss), which is under the Intermediary Tier and the Material Circumstances subgroup but is being discussed here, was almost entirely adverse and appeared across all four crisis types in a fairly even distribution. This shows the universal relevance and impact of economic hardship regardless of any crisis context. It was mineable from news coverage of price surges, shortages, and economic shocks.

Socioeconomic & Political Context (n = 35, 18 Adv, 8 Fav, 9 Mix).

This refers to the governance, policy, and environmental conditions surrounding a person, rather than anything about the individual directly. Governance (n = 12, e.g., government stringency measures, lockdown, misinformation & criticism) was coded as adverse, as it was more often treated as a source of distress than support. It was concentrated in pandemic and natural disaster research (n = 11 combined), where it plays an active intervening role. NLP detected it in 10 of 12 instances, since lockdown orders and government reactions are directly reported in media and social media text.

Contextual and Environmental Conditions (n = 16) skewed adverse (e.g., number of cases, deaths, severe weather and natural hazards), as case counts, death tolls, and hazard severity are likely to elicit negative mental impacts. This appeared only in pandemic (n = 6) and natural disaster research (n = 10), the two crisis types in which an objective, publicly reported magnitude of the event itself is routinely tracked. They were NLP-dependent, with 11 of 16 instances found by NLP, since case counts and hazard figures appear in both mined news text and structured records such as epidemiological or meteorological data. Culture and Societal Values (n = 7) showed a mixed valence, split across pandemic and armed conflict research, and was well detected by NLP. Armed conflict research also shows a gap that the other three crisis types do not share. It has zero coverage of Governance or Contextual and Environmental Conditions, meaning the political context around conflict has not been studied at all.

It was the most evenly balanced tier in the taxonomy, as shown in Table 11. Bridging Determinants sit in the middle of the CSDH tier structure and refer to factors that either carry the protection of a favorable structural position down to the individual or fail to do so. All subdimensions are almost entirely coded as adverse, and almost no item is coded as favorable. This total absence of favorable coding across the subgroup follows directly from how these constructs are defined: a burden, an illness, or a biomarker abnormality has little room to be recorded as a positive outcome in the first place. Moreover, they are captured almost entirely through non-NLP methods (21 of 25, 84%), since self-rated health and diagnosed conditions are typically collected as clinical or survey items rather than mined from open text.

Cumulative Physiological Burden (n = 25, e.g., self-rated health, hypertension, cancer) was predominantly adverse (17 of 25). It was concentrated in armed conflict research (n = 11) and pandemic research (n = 12). This was because maladaptive and health risk behaviors were tracked as a consequence of crisis stress. Some of its discourse surfaced in mined social media text (e.g., leisure-or substance-related language), similar to psychosocial circumstances. This refers to a person's contact with, and receipt of support from, formal health and psychosocial services during a crisis. It is the only intermediary category in which favorable codes outnumber adverse ones. Access (n = 8, e.g., medical needs past 30 days, distance to pediatric ICU, distance to alcohol/drug treatment center) appeared only in natural disasters (n = 3) and armed conflict (n = 5) research. It was captured entirely administrative surveys and clinical methods. Responsiveness and System Performance (n=1, e.g., receiving psychological support material) had too few observances to discuss further.

Outcome Measures (n = 45, 44 Adv, 0 Fav, 1 Mix). These items sit outside the three CSDH determinant tiers because they describe prior diagnoses, current symptoms, or the study's own outcome construct rather than a cause of wellbeing. They represent the study's output rather than factors leading to it. These items are often symptoms or related outcomes of the very distress or disorder that the study is designed to predict. Eight of these outcomes are circular with the study's own target.

5.2. What are the explainability or ranking methods to order factors?

The analytical basis linking factors to outcomes was recorded for all 69 studies. Non-NLP studies overwhelmingly fit a formal statistical or machine learning ranking: 50 of 51 non-NLP studies use SHAP, feature or variable importance, regression or LASSO coefficients, or a comparable technique, and only one study (Li et al., 2024) reports no ranking basis at all but only has 6 factors. Every SHAP-based study in the corpus, 9 of 9 including the one study combining SHAP and LIME (Bashar et al., 2025), is non-NLP.

NLP studies show a pattern close to the opposite. Of the 17 NLP studies with a recorded basis, only 5 report a formal statistical measure, limited to correlation (Ou, de Bruijn & Schulz, 2025) or regression and path coefficients (Wu et al., 2023; Bathina et al., 2021; Elyashar et al., 2021; Karami et al., 2026), and none use SHAP, permutation importance, or any tree-based importance measure. Other NLP studies mostly characterize factors through purely descriptive frequency measures, operationalizing importance almost entirely as how often a topic or sentiment appears in the text rather than how strongly it predicts an outcome. Comparison with previous studies Schäfer et al. (2024), Kunzler et al. (2021), and Newnham et al. ( 2022) remain the most relevant prior reviews of crisis mental health, each methodologically sound within its own remit. None includes a computational or machine learning method as an inclusion criterion, and this difference in scope produces a difference in what each review can structurally find. Schäfer et al. restricts to regression models in highincome countries; Kunzler et al. restricts to early pandemic survey data; Newnham et al. explicitly excludes conflict-and violence-related trauma. This review's machine learning and NLP inclusion criterion is what surfaces actually impacted populations such as refugees, disaster-exposed civilians, Ugandan and Sri Lankan war survivors, and constructs that regression-based synthesis in high-income cohorts is not designed to reach.

Moreover, this review coded factors under the CSDH framework, extended by stress process models, to build the organizing taxonomy. This produced 338 determinants sorted into Structural, Bridging, and Intermediary tiers across 24 subdimensions, a resolution none of the three prior reviews approach. Every coded item was then cross-tabulated by crisis typology and by detection modality (NLP vs. non-NLP). This double stratification showed where the burden is concentrated by crisis type and how invisible that burden is to computational detection.

On age, it proves consistently unstable rather than resolving to one direction. Education diverges between the two prior reviews (Kunzler protective vs. Schäfer largely null), and the present review leans closer to Kunzler, with a favorable tilt. Emotion regulation/psychological flexibility, favorable in Schäfer et al., becomes far more heterogeneous in the present review's 29-item Mastery and Self Concept subdimension. Governance, Occupation, Financial Circumstances, Housing, and the biological/behavioral subdimensions were untested by all three prior reviews because each was restricted by a different eligibility axis (high-income countries, early pandemic data, or non-conflict disaster/pandemic exposure), and none stratified by detection method.

6. Synthesis, Future Directions, and Limitations

>Synthesis, Future Directions, and Limitations

6.1. Synthesis of findings

Across the evidence base, 338 code-able determinants were charted using the WHO CSDH, with Intermediary Determinants (psychosocial circumstances and behavioral and biological factors chief among them) accounting for 66.3% of the total. In contrast, Structural and Bridging Determinants together account for just over a third.

Armed conflict is captured almost entirely through structured clinical instruments rather than textbased methods, unlike pandemic and natural disaster research, which has more instances of NLP detection. Armed conflict research also shows zero coverage of governance or environmental conditions, leaving the effects of political context on trauma in conflict settings unexamined. Prior trauma and pre-existing psychiatric burden are the most consistent predictors of later distress, more so than any feature of the conflict exposure itself. This suggests that harm accumulates on prior vulnerability rather than arising fresh from a single event.

In pandemics, the dominant psychosocial pattern is prolonged, unresolved uncertainty rather than a discrete shock. Coping and resilience constructs are also studied far more heavily here than in any other crisis type, giving pandemic research the most developed evidence base in the review for protective psychosocial mechanisms. In economic upheavals, occupational and income disruption is adverse almost without exception across the available studies, showing how economic disruption itself is the central pathogenic factor in this crisis type. This crisis type is also the least evidenced in the review, with only five studies across both NLP and survey methods combined. Natural disasters show that harm extends well beyond the disaster event itself, driven mainly by prolonged material and residential disruption such as property loss, displacement, and relocation. NLP-based methods capture governance and contextual signals well, with 10 of 12 governance items and over half of contextual items derived from mined text. Structural Determinants carry the highest NLP rate of any tier. The tiers closest to the individual are captured almost entirely through non-NLP clinical or survey instruments. Categories drawn from discourse, such as governance and ethnicity, are NLP-heavy, while every demographic, biomarker, clinical, and administrative factor is drawn from non-NLP sources.

Fifty of 51 non-NLP studies apply a formal statistical or machine learning ranking to justify their factors. Eleven of 17 NLP studies base their justification on descriptive frequency counts. Rather than fitting formal rankings, these NLP studies mostly operationalize importance as how often a topic or sentiment appears in the text. Only 9 studies overall use SHAP (Table 14), so this field requires wider adoption of explainable AI techniques for more interpretable results.

Adverseness dominates 6 of the taxonomy's subdimensions at 85% or higher. It spans life events, chronic strains, financial means, housing, material work environment, and direct biomarker panel. Favorable signal concentrates almost entirely in mastery, self-concept, and coping constructs, together accounting for 25 of the corpus's 72 favorable codes. Six subdimensions, including Life Events, Chronic Strains, and Housing, show zero favorable coding across all methods. Prior symptoms, diagnoses, and the study's own outcome construct sit outside the three determinant tiers; eight of these are circular with the study's own stated target.

6.2. Future Research Directions

Computational methods and NLP approaches should be expanded specifically into the crisis types and constructs where they remain structurally absent. They also need to detect protective, not only adverse, constructs. Researchers at the intersection of AI, business, and social economics need to study economic downturns more. Armed conflict is captured almost entirely through structured clinical instruments rather than text-based methods, in contrast to the subdimensions where NLP already dominates in pandemic and natural disaster research. Armed conflict research also shows zero coverage of governance or environmental conditions, leaving the effects of political context on trauma in conflict settings unexamined.

Digital phenotyping should be used more widely across all crisis types, as its value for tracking behavioral and mental health signals is well established in the clinical literature (Onnela & Rauch, 2016). This involves collecting passive data from phones and wearables, such as movement, screen time, sleep, and typing behavior. This would allow chronic strain and health behavior to be tracked continuously without repeated surveys, which is especially useful in conflict zones or disaster areas where survey access is often limited. The broader contextual and semantic range of large language models (LLMs) makes them more capable of picking up the subtler, less standardized language of mastery, self-concept, and coping. GPT-based models, for example, have already been shown to detect psychological constructs from text more accurately than traditional dictionary-based methods across multiple languages (Rathje et al., 2024). LLMs can also pick up signals from armed conflicts in real time, as demonstrated by their use in detecting human rights violations in social media posts during the Russia-Ukraine war (Nemkova et al., 2023).

These gaps point toward constructing a knowledge graph that integrates evidence across the CSDH tiers into a unified computational structure, and toward bringing formal explainable AI methods more broadly into crisis mental health research. A graph linking structural determinant data, bridging determinant data, and intermediary outcome data would allow new crisis events to be mapped onto the existing evidence base, identifying at-risk populations and intervention priorities across tiers rather than within a single subgroup in isolation. Moreover, regardless of whether the underlying model is NLP-based, adopting purpose-built explanation methods such as SHAP, LIME, or causal machine learning (rather than relying on frequency counts or a model's native feature importance as a stand-in) would raise the evidentiary standard across the field.

6.3. Limitations of This Review

This review has four main limitations. First, consistent with PRISMA-ScR guidance for scoping reviews, we did not conduct a formal quality or risk-of-bias appraisal, since the review's purpose was to map the breadth of the evidence rather than weight it. As a result, this synthesis represents methodologically stronger and weaker studies on equal footing. Second, our characterization of certain factors as the "strongest" predictors reflects how consistently they rank highly across studies, not a pooled effect size: importance scores and odds ratios are not directly comparable across different methods and study designs. This vote-counting approach is standard for scoping reviews and well suited to mapping breadth, but it cannot quantify any single factor's contribution relative to others.

Third, the literature coverage is not exhaustive. The search was supplemented by forward and backward citation searching, consistent with recommended scoping review practice (Levac et al., 2010). Still, some relevant studies may have been missed, particularly those published in non-English venues or in regional computer science conference proceedings not indexed in the four databases searched. Fourth, and most consequentially, this evidence base relies heavily on social media data. Across all four crisis types, included studies drew predominantly on Twitter, Reddit, Weibo, and YouTube, which capture people with internet access and digital literacy rather than the populations a crisis affects most severely, such as older generations or lower-income groups. These biases compound with population gaps that differ by crisis type. Across all four crisis types, low-and middle-income countries are underrepresented relative to high-income contexts.

11. Conclusions

This scoping review mapped 70 studies applying machine learning, NLP, deep learning, and spatial modeling methods to mental health and resilience outcomes across four population-scale crisis types: pandemics, economic upheavals, natural disasters, and armed conflict or displacement. Current computational systems largely detect distress only once it has produced a structured clinical record, rarely extending to the governance, contextual, and coping constructs this review finds carry the most computationally accessible signal upstream of that record. Crisis mental health research has matured methodologically but not yet diversified in what it measures or whom it reaches. Closing the burden visibility gap, linking structural and contextual signals to individual-level clinical outcomes, expanding the evidence on economic upheavals, extending NLP into armed conflict contexts, and detecting more protective constructs represent the clearest paths toward more equitable computational crisis mental health surveillance.
