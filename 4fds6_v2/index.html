Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

In the absence of face-valid indicators, particularly in children, liability to substance use/addiction requires quantification. The Transmissible Liability Index (TLI) was developed for this purpose by employing a high-risk design and item response theory and quantifies this liability in childhood, before substance exposure. Because the TLI places liability to substance use disorder on a common scale, it can also facilitate harmonization across datasets collected from different populations. This study extends prior research by examining genetic and environmental contributions to TLI and its relationship with liability to the initiation of licit and illicit substance use. The study utilizes data of TLI, and substance use tracked from age 11 to age 25 from longitudinal twin studies in Virginia and Minnesota. Results showed that TLI is highly heritable and that substance use initiation (SUI) reflected a mixture of genetic and shared environmental influences varying across substance classes, consistent with the principle that initiation is more environmentally sensitive than progression. The genetic correlations between childhood TLI and lifetime SUI are consistently stronger than the environmental correlations and support a developmental interpretation in which early heritable liability manifests in SUI and, further downstream, in disorder. In addition to confirming TLI's high heritability, predictive validity, and utility for dataset harmonization, it supports the feasibility of identifying high-and average-resistance samples, to be used for selecting specific indicators of resistance to substance use/addiction.

This study suggests that a specific set of behavioral characteristics assessed in childhood and adolescence is correlated with the initiation of substances such as alcohol, nicotine, cannabis and illicit drugs partly because of the same genes influencing both. These characteristics could be used to predict who will likely initiate substances.

Introduction

Substance use frequently leads to substance use disorders (SUDs) (i.e., addiction), characterized by persistent use despite various negative consequences. Severe consequences due to substance use do not require the diagnostic threshold of SUD to be met. Therefore, any substance use behavior presents a significant public health and social hazard (Vanyukov et al., 2023). The diagnostic categorization of SUDs arbitrarily dichotomizes the continuous latent (unobserved directly) trait of liability to substance use that underlies variation in the behaviors pertaining to substances, including those that represent asymptomatic/"normal" phenotypes. Further, the prevalence of substance use behaviors is higher than that of SUDs. Consequently, understanding the etiology of SUDs may also benefit from subclinical phenotypes which include substance use behaviors that precede a diagnosis of SUDs as well as the liability underlying SUDs.

Use of a TLI to Study Underlying Etiology of SUD

SUD liability has been dimensionally quantified in children-before potential exposure to substancesby applying a high-risk/family paradigm and item response theory (IRT) to derive a TLI (Kirisci et al., 2009 (Kirisci et al., , 2012;; Vanyukov et al., 2003a Vanyukov et al., , 2003b Vanyukov et al., , 2009;; Vanyukov & Tarter, 2000). Transmissibility is the portion of the liability variance that is correlated between parents and offspring, as per the tau model in nuclear family data (Rice et al., 1980). IRT (e.g., (Embretson & Reise, 2000)) provides models that relate responses to individual items (e.g., in questionnaires) to the latent trait underlying their variation. Development of the TLI in the Center for Education and Drug Abuse Research (CEDAR) used behavioral characteristics that discriminate between children of SUD-affected and -unaffected parents as indicators of children's transmissible SUD liability. These characteristics were assessed by items comprising psychological and diagnostic questionnaires, reflecting the behavioral foundations of common (non-specific to particular substances) liability to substance use/addiction (Vanyukov et al., 2012).

Genetic Epidemiology of TLI

Prior twin studies show that TLI is highly heritable (h2 = 0.70 to 0.76), with the remainder of the variance primarily due to unique environmental influences in the Minnesota Twin and Family Study (MTFS) (Hicks et al., 2012; Vanyukov et al., 2009). These properties have been replicated in two twin samples, supporting the robustness of TLI's genetic architecture across studies. Further, the genetic component of TLI assessed in childhood accounts for the entire phenotypic relationship between TLI and subsequent SUD outcomes in adolescence and young adulthood, with no unique environmental factors shared between them. These results validate TLI as a measure of transmissible -primarily heritable -SUD liability in children and support its utility for early-age genetic and mechanistic studies of addiction. Importantly, because IRT places individual scores on a common metric irrespective of sample and subset of items used, TLI can also be scored across independently collected datasets: applying the CEDAR-derived item parameters to MTFS data yielded scores closely convergent with those obtained from calibration within MTFS itself (r = 0.91, Spearman ρ = 0.95), with equivalent prediction of adult diagnosis (Vanyukov et al., 2015). SUI (i.e., first engagement of substance use at any point in the lifetime) is a common and important behavior in the development of SUD since any substance use behavior is contingent on having started use. In 2018, the prevalence of initiation for alcohol, cannabis, and tobacco was between 13.4% -26.3% in American adolescents ages 12-17 and 51.5% -79.7% among young adults ages 18-25 (Volkow et al., 2021). The prevalence of illicit drug initiation including cocaine, methamphetamines and heroin ranged from 1.3%-11.4% in Americans ages 18-25 (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2025).

Genetic Epidemiology of SUI

The role of genetic and environmental factors on SUI varies across drugs and across the life course. Twin studies in adolescents aged 12-19 consistently report that additive genetic (15-75%) and shared environmental factors (30-84%) account for a substantial proportion of the variance in the liability to initiation of nicotine and alcohol (Huizink et al., 2010; Kochvar et al., 2023; Maes et al., 1999 Maes et al., , 2017;; Prom-Wormley et al., 2021). The pattern for liability to initiation of illicit substances during adolescence is similar to that of licit substances. For initiation of any illicit drug (i.e., cannabis, cocaine, stimulants, sedatives, hallucinogens, and opiates), genetic contributions ranged from 15% to 61%, whereas shared environmental contributions accounted for 32% to 59% of the variance (Agrawal & Lynskey, 2006; Zellers et al., 2024). Further, the factors contributing to SUI vary over the life course, with stronger shared environmental contributions in early adolescence and an increasing magnitude of genetic factors by late adolescence and young adulthood (Do et al., 2015; Maes et al., 2017; McGue et al., 2000; Agrawal et al., 2005; Huggett et al., 2018; Maes et al., 2004; Richmond-Rakerd et al., 2016).

The magnitude of genetic and environmental contributions for SUI also varies by sex. Variance due to additive genetic effects for initiation was higher in females for conventional cigarettes (females = 32-95%, males = 19-24%), alcohol (females = 21-48%, males = 1-29%), and cannabis (females = 18-72%, males = 22-44%). The contribution of shared environmental effects was more prominent among males, suggesting that the role of genetic effects on liability of SUI may be higher among females, while the magnitude of shared environmental contributions is higher among males. (Richmond-Rakerd et al., 2016) Although the phenotypic and genetic properties of the TLI have been evaluated previously (Hicks et al., 2012; Kirisci et al., 2009; M. Vanyukov et al., 2009 M. Vanyukov et al., , 2015)), neither its utility for data harmonization across multiple samples nor its ability to serve as an index of liability to initiation of substance use, a key step in SUD development, have been examined. A combined dataset of twins provides additional opportunities to obtain more precise estimates of model parameters, extended evaluation of sex differences, as well as detailed analyses of various aspects of substance use in their relationship with the childhood measure of liability and its further validation as a tool for identifying high-resistance individuals. This article reports the results of biometric genetic analyses of the TLI and SUI in the harmonized twin data from two studies.

Methods

Participants

Data from two samples of male and female same-sex monozygotic (MZ) and dizygotic (DZ) twins reared together, were assessed across adolescence into young adulthood in Virginia and Minnesota. The Virginia sample included opposite-sex twins. All procedures in the contributing studies were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Virginia Commonwealth University and the University of Minnesota. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians, and assent from participating children, at each assessment; participants provided their own consent upon reaching the age of majority.

Virginia Twin Study of Adolescent Behavioral Development Participants included 2,775 twins from the Virginia

Twin Study of Adolescent Behavioral Development (VTSABD), a population-based cohort-sequential longitudinal study (Hewitt et al., 1997; Meyer et al., 1996), and its young adult follow-up studies, comprising six assessment waves conducted between 1990 and 2006. The sample included twin pairs born between 1974 and 1984 from Virginia, with follow-up assessments spanning from childhood through early adulthood, initially assessed between the ages of 8 and 16 and followed up at ages 9-17, 12-17, 14-17, 18-28, and 22-32. The retention rate across the six waves was greater than 80%. A detailed description can be found elsewhere (Eaves et al., 1997; Maes et al., 2007).

Minnesota Twin and Family Study

Participants included 3365 twins from the Minnesota Twin and Family Study, MTFS, a population-based longitudinal study of twins born between 1972 and 1984 and first assessed in 1989 (Iacono et al., 1999; Malone et al., 2002). The sample consisted of two cohorts of same-sex twins from Minnesota. The older cohort, born between 1972-1977, was initially assessed at age 17 and followed up at ages 20, 24, 29, 35. The younger cohort, born between 1977-1984, was initially assessed at age 11 and followed up at ages 14, 17, 20, 24, 29 (Wilson et al., 2019). Retention rate across cohorts and waves was greater than 87%, with limited bias attributable to substance use and related psychopathology. A detailed description can be found elsewhere (McGue et al., 2000; Wilson et al., 2019).

Measures (see Supplemental Materials)

Transmissible Liability Index The rationale for TLI development and its derivation procedure has been described in detail elsewhere (Vanyukov et al., 2003a (Vanyukov et al., , 2003b (Vanyukov et al., , 2009;; Vanyukov & Tarter, 2000). Data from the VTSABD and MTFS were matched to 45 CEDAR-generated items and to one another to generate a version of the TLI optimized for these two samples.

Substance Use Initiation

Six variables representing lifetime SUI for specific substances (nicotine, alcohol or cannabis) or a group of substances (illicit drug use without or with cannabis, any substance use) were studied.

Although the specific items asked of participants varied by study, they were harmonized to reflect the same concept of engaging in any lifetime use of a specific substance. These six variables were coded as binary variables with the maximum value taken across study waves and raters (i.e. twin's self-report and parental report).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R 4.6.1 ("Happy Hop") (R Core Team, 2026) using the open-source R package OpenMx 2.21.11 (Neale et al., 2016).

Univariate Twin Models Standard univariate ACE models were fitted to twin data to quantify genetic and environmental contributions to individual differences in TLI and SUI. These models partition variance in additive genetic (A), common environmental (C) and nonshared (or unique) environmental (E; including measurement error) factors (e.g., Neale & Cardon, 1992). Structural equation models were fit to raw data using the direct symmetric approach (Verhulst et al., 2019). Likelihood ratio tests and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) were used to select the best fitting models. Whilst models for continuous data were fit to TLI scores, a threshold model was assumed for the binary SUI measures to allow partitioning of the underlying liability to initiate substances.

Prior to ACE model fitting, we evaluated model assumptions in saturated models. We further tested whether variance components could be equated by sample and sex.

Bivariate Twin Models

To evaluate the sources of covariance between TLI and SUI, we fitted a correlated factors model which quantifies the ACE sources of (co)variance to the joint continuous (TLI) and binary (SUI) data.

These models assessed the degree to which the observed covariance between TLI and SUI can be explained by genetic and environmental factors shared between them. We present genetic (rA), shared (rC) and unique (rE) environmental correlations from the joint continuous-binary model (Figure 1). To account for the possibility that TLI (variable x) has a direct causal effect on SUI (variable y) (or vice-versa), we also tested causal models where TLI was a cause of SUI (byx), and where the causal direction was reversed such that SUI caused TLI (bxy). We thus took a more agnostic approach by testing all possible identified models with up to three out of the five possible sources of covariance (rA, rC, rE, byx, bxy) and used multimodel inference methods to summarize the goodness-of-fit of the models and to calculate weights used in model averaging to obtain the 'best' estimates of the parameters of interest (Kirkpatrick et al., 2015; Maes et al., 2021). These estimates are derived from weighted averages of the parameter values across models, by summing the products of Akaike weights with the estimates from each model.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

Analyses were based on data from 6,140 individual twins from two samples. The VTSABD sample of 2,775 included 630 male and 822 female MZ twins, 362 male and 374 female DZ twins and 587 twins from opposite sex pairs. The MTFS sample of 3,365 included 1,041 male and 1,121 female MZ twins and 561 male and 642 female DZ twins. Means and variances of TLI by sample and sex are presented in Table 1, which includes the counts and percentages for lifetime ever use of each of the substances. TLI was significantly higher in males versus females in both samples. The prevalences of tobacco, cannabis and illicit drug initiation were significantly higher in males in both samples. Prevalence for alcohol and any substance initiation did not differ significantly by sex within either sample. For TLI, DZ correlations were approximately half the MZ correlations, consistent with substantial additive genetic contributions. For the SUI variables, DZ twin correlations were generally higher than half the MZ correlations, suggesting contributions of both additive genetic and shared environmental factors to variation in liability of substance initiation (see Supplemental Table 4).

Univariate Twin Modeling of TLI and SUI by Sample and Sex

Model assumptions, including equality of means/thresholds and variances by twin order and zygosity were met for each variable when tested separately by sample. Since only the Virginia sample included opposite sex twin pairs, we fitted a univariate sex limitation model to the TLI data. Neither qualitative nor quantitative sex differences were significant for TLI. The best fitting model was an AE model, resulting in a heritability estimate of 0.72 (95% CI = 0.67; 0.75) and the residual E variance component estimated at 0.28 (95% CI = 0.25; 0.33) for TLI when variance component parameters were equated across sex. In the Minnesota sample, an AE model was also the best fitting model including significant quantitative sex differences in variance components, with heritability estimates of 0.74 (95% CI = 0.70; 0.78) for males and 0.59 (95% CI = 0.52; 0.64) for females. When combining the VTSABD and MTFS samples, estimates for the AE variance components were statistically different by sex and between samples (Table 2).

Univariate genetic analyses of binary SUI phenotypes suggested that both additive genetic and shared environmental factors contributed to familial resemblance (Table 2). Heritability estimates of liability to substance use ranged from 0.17 to 0.28 for nicotine initiation, -0.02 to 0.46 for alcohol initiation, 0.17 to 0.41 for cannabis initiation, 0.34 to 0.57 for initiation of illicit drugs (not including cannabis), 0.19 to 0.30 for drug use initiation (cannabis or other drugs) and -0.17 to 0.48 for initiation of any substance. Estimates of contributions of shared environmental factors to SUI liability were almost consistently higher than heritability estimates and ranged from 0.47 to 0.71 for nicotine, from 0.41 to 0.90 for alcohol, from 0.43 to 0.64 for cannabis, from 0.10 to 0.53 for other illicit drugs, from 0.32 to 0.53 for use of any drugs and from 0.40 to 0.96 for any substance use.

Unique environmental factors, which include measurement error, explained mostly between 10 and 25% of the variance in liability to SUI. We opted to present estimates of variance components from the full ACE sex limitation model to provide more consistent comparisons across substances, sex and sample. In each case, at least one of the sources of familial resemblance (A or C) contributed significantly to the liability of substance initiation. The few instances of negative estimates of the additive genetic variance component, for alcohol and any substance initiation, reflect cases where the respective variance component can be dropped from the model without significant loss of model fit. Supplementary analyses constraining twin correlations and variance components across sex and sample, while allowing for main effects of sex and sample, confirmed the pattern of results (see Supplemental Table 5).

Bivariate Genetic Analysis of TLI and SUI

We first fitted ACE correlated factors models to TLI and each of the six SUI variables to estimate the role of genetic and environmental factors to the variance and covariance of TLI and SUI. The results for the variance decomposition of the two variables mirrored those of the univariate analyses, as expected. We thus focus here on estimates of the genetic and environmental correlations between TLI and initiation of each substance (Table 3).

As shared environmental factors did not contribute significantly to the variance of TLI, in contrast to their contributions to SUI liability, we could not estimate rC's. In addition, for variables with negative estimates of heritability, rA's were not estimated.

The estimated rA's were, with few exceptions, consistently higher than rE's. The highest rA's were observed between TLI and initiation of nicotine (0.27 to 0.59), followed by any drug use (0.28 to 0.32), illicit use excluding cannabis (0.24 to 0.31) and cannabis (0.23 to 0.24) in males, whilst the rA's between TLI and alcohol initiation or any substance use were not significantly different from zero. In contrast, estimates of rE's showed the highest values between TLI and alcohol or any substance initiation. Estimates were broadly consistent across samples with the largest discrepancy for correlations between TLI and nicotine initiation. The pattern of rA's between TLI and SUI in females was similar to that in males but estimates were lower in both samples. rE's were low and non-significant in both samples as well as across sex.

As covariation between TLI and SUI could also arise from direct causal effects between TLI and SUI, we extended analyses to testing all possible identified models including one or both causal paths (b>: from TLI to SUI, or b<: from SUI to TLI) and/or any of the possible confounding paths (rA or rE in this case) (see Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 6). The results for the model-averaged estimates of the correlations are broadly consistent with those of the correlated factors models, showing mostly higher rA'a compared to rE's for initiation of nicotine and any illicit drug use, and higher rE's than rA's for initiation of alcohol and any substance.

Estimates of causal paths from TLI to SUI were generally higher than reverse causation paths from SUI to TLI in males, but rather variable or in the reverse direction in females. These trends are strongest for nicotine and any illicit drug initiation and for males compared to females. When data were combined across sample and sex (see Supplemental Table 7), trends of higher rA versus rE correlation, and of higher causal paths from TLI to SUI than the reverse were confirmed.

Discussion

This study extends prior research on the TLI by examining its genetic and environmental architecture and relationship with SUI. The study was conducted in the VTSABD and the MTFS twin samples, the harmonization of which was enabled by the TLI. Results elaborate upon previous evidence for TLI's high heritability and predictive validity, demonstrate the feasibility of IRT-based dataset harmonization across studies, confirm that variation in SUI is influenced by a combination of genetic and shared environmental factors, and establish that the genetic link between childhood TLI and initiation of substance use is consistently stronger than the environmental link across most substances. The results strengthen the case for TLI as a quantitative, early-childhood index of transmissible SUD liability.

IRT-Based Harmonization Across Studies

This study demonstrates that TLI can be successfully used to calibrate across datasets from different populations using an anchor-item IRT approach. By identifying 13 common items across CEDAR, VTSABD, and MTFS (29% of original TLI item pool), sufficient to serve as a psychometric bridge, TLI scores were placed on a common metric that minimized instrument-related and study-design variance. This follows established principles of IRT-based harmonization, where invariant anchor items enable linking of nonequivalent groups onto a shared latent scale (Gibbons et al., 2014). The replication of the TLI heritability estimates and the structural consistency of bivariate TLI-SUI results across samples further validate the approach. As the field increasingly relies on large multi-site consortia for molecular genetic studies, the ability to harmonize a childhood liability index across diverse studies substantially expands the scope of applicable samples (Conway et al. 2010). The DIF analyses further ensured that harmonized scores were not biased by sex-specific item functioning -a meaningful concern given known sex differences in endorsement rates for externalizing items in childhood (Achenbach & Edelbrock, 1983).

High Heritability of the TLI and Between-Sample Differences in Variance Components

Results are consistent with a previous study from CEDAR and closely parallel those reported in the MTFS alone using IRT-derived TLI scores (Vanyukov et al., 2015), a sum-of-z-score approach for TLI items (Hicks et al., 2012), and with the heritability of the broader externalizing spectrum (Krueger et al. 2002). The qualitative conclusion of high heritability without shared environmental contribution held across both studies, as did estimates in males. In contrast, in females, statistically significant quantitative differences in variance components between the VTSABD and MTFS, with heritability estimates higher in the former, warrant consideration. While these differences cannot be explained by higher liability in the VTSABD sample (the rates of initiation are not substantially different), several factors may contribute to these differences. First, the two studies were conducted in different geographic regions, and environmental shifts in substance availability, social norms, and parental monitoring practices can alter the relative magnitude of genetic and environmental variance (Dick et al., 2007). When shared environmental variance is systematically higher in one region -for example, due to greater homogeneity in community-level influences -heritability estimates will be correspondingly lower. Second, developmental timing differences may play a role, as heritability of externalizing traits is known to strengthen across adolescence alongside their increasing expression (Iacono et al., 1999), and the two studies assessed twins at somewhat different ages and across different numbers of waves. Third, residual measurement non-equivalence cannot be excluded: despite the IRT harmonization, non-anchor items in each sample may cover different portions of the liability continuum with different precision, and differential measurement error is absorbed into the E component, which may artificially deflate heritability in the sample with noisier measurement. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and consistency of the qualitative results across samples suggests that between-study differences reflect degree rather than kind.

Quantitative sex differences in TLI heritability, with higher estimates in males than females observed in MFTS, replicate prior observations (Vanyukov et al., 2009 (Vanyukov et al., , 2015) ) and are consistent with a pattern seen across substance use phenotypes and externalizing traits (Krueger et al., 2002). The mechanisms are unclear but may reflect the greater prevalence and heritability of externalizing and disinhibitory traits in males, or genuine sexspecific pathways to addiction liability. Despite DIF adjustment, residual quantitative sex differences suggest that some portion of TLI variance is due to sex-specific genetic or environmental factors, a question warranting further investigation in larger samples.

SUI: Modest Heritability and Prominent Shared Environmental Effects

In line with an extensive literature on substance use twin studies, heritability estimates for lifetime SUI were in most cases, lower than for SUDs. Across substances, shared environment accounted for a larger proportion of SUI variance than additive genetic factors, replicating previous results (Fowler et al., 2007; Maes et al., 1999). This is consistent with the observation that environmental influences -peer behavior, substance availability, parental monitoring, and social norms -are relatively more influential for whether an individual ever tries a substance, while genetic factors exert stronger influence on variation in progression to heavier use/disorder (Agrawal & Lynskey, 2006; Maes et al., 2004; Verweij et al., 2010). For cannabis initiation specifically, environmental availability has been shown to account for a large proportion of shared environmental variance (Gillespie et al., 2009) consistent with the high estimates observed here. This pattern is particularly salient in the context of prior TLI-SUD bivariate results (Vanyukov et al., 2015). In that study, the variance for adult SUD diagnosis in the MTFS was dominated by shared environmental factors, with a smaller additive genetic component. The present results extend this pattern earlier in the SUD trajectory: across substances, SUI showed similarly prominent shared environment, while the contribution of additive genetic factors increases as the phenotype moves from initiation toward disorder. Heritability estimates for alcohol initiation were particularly low and, in some cells, nominally negative, reflecting well-documented results indicating that variation in whether one ever tries alcohol is almost entirely driven by social and family environment, with genetic factors becoming more influential for later stages of consumption and problem development, i.e., higher liability levels (Maes et al., 2017; Verhulst et al., 2015). Heritability estimates were somewhat higher for illicit drugs other than cannabis, consistent with a role for substance-specific genetic mechanisms that co-vary with disinhibitory traits (Kendler et al., 2003). The few instances of negative estimates of variance components likely reflect cases where the respective variance component can be dropped without significant loss of model fit.

Genetic Connections Between TLI and SUI

The bivariate analyses show a theoretically coherent pattern: genetic correlations between childhood TLI and lifetime SUI were positive across most substances and substantially higher than the corresponding unique environmental correlations, consistent with TLI's rationale as an index of the transmissible -primarily genetic -component of SUD liability. These results complement the bivariate results reported previously in the MTFS (Vanyukov et al., 2015), where the genetic variance of TLI accounted for more than half of the genetic variance in adult SUD diagnosis, with the genetic correlation accounting for the entire phenotypic relationship between the two measures. The present results address an earlier point in the same phenotypic development and show that the genetic signal from childhood TLI is already detectable at the initiation stage, before disorder has developed.

The substance-specific pattern of genetic correlations is informative and broadly consistent across the two samples. The highest rA was observed for nicotine initiation, followed by any drug use initiation, illicit drugs excluding cannabis, and cannabis. Estimates for rA's of alcohol initiation and any substance initiation were unstable and, in some cases, hit the lower or upper bound, likely resulting from the very high prevalence of alcohol initiation and the very low or negative heritability estimates for these measures. The gradient of rAs closely mirrors the known gradient in heritability of initiation across substances -nicotine and illicit drug use share more genetic architecture with the externalizing/disinhibitory spectrum that TLI indexes than does normative alcohol initiation (Agrawal & Lynskey, 2006).

A notable result is that rA values for nicotine in the VTSABD are comparable to those reported previously (Vanyukov et al., 2015) for adult SUD diagnosis. This is at first sight surprising, since disorder is more severe, and liability to it is more heritable relative to initiation. It suggests that the genetic connection between childhood TLI and nicotine, as well as illicit drug involvement, is already near its maximum at the initiation stage, with the transition to disorder phenotypes adding substance-specific variance rather than amplifying the shared genetic signal from TLI. The somewhat lower rA values in the MTFS for several substances likely reflect the between-sample differences in TLI heritability discussed above, particularly in females, where a lower heritable variance in TLI constrains the maximum possible genetic correlation with any outcome. In general, since TLI is designed to index liability to adult illicit SUD, this may also suggest that smoking has become almost as nonnormative as illicit substance use.

The non-significant and near-zero rA for alcohol initiation and any substance initiation deserves attention.

Alcohol initiation is common -over 80% in both samples -and so dominated by shared environmental influences (C = 0.78 in males and 0.71 in females) that the heritable variance available to correlate with TLI's genetic component is minimal in the VTSABD. The between-sample and between-sex differences in alcohol rA could be attributable to sampling variability and statistical imprecision, given that rA estimates for lowheritability-particularly for binary traits-may be imprecise and unstable (Neale & Cardon, 1992). More broadly, results suggest that TLI's genetic reach extends to alcohol involvement more strongly at the disorder stage than at initiation. The rE values between TLI and SUI were generally small and non-significant but showed a substantively interesting pattern. In males, rE was positive for most substances in both samples. The highest values were for precisely the phenotypes with the highest shared environmental variance and lowest heritability. This suggests that in males, some unique environmental experiences independently co-vary with both the behavioral precursors captured by TLI and the likelihood of initiating substance use, most plausibly early behavioral problems leading to environments where substances are encountered, independent of the heritable liability pathway. In females, rE values were near zero or negative across most substances and samples, suggesting that the unique environmental factors associated with higher TLI scores do not co-vary positively with SUI.

The rA estimates were most consistent across the VTSABD and MTFS for cannabis and illicit drugs excluding cannabis. These data further support the validity of the IRT harmonization. Taken together with data for SUD diagnosis (Vanyukov et al., 2015) and for adolescent substance abuse composite scores (Hicks et al., 2012), these results provide converging evidence across three different SUD-related outcomes and two independent twin cohorts that the genetic component of childhood TLI systematically predicts substance involvement across the developmental continuum, with the strength of the genetic connection reflecting the heritability of liabilities to substance use-related developmental outcomes.

Causal Modeling: TLI as Predictor of SUI

Extending the correlated-factors approach to causal modeling, the model-averaging results suggested that the direction of effect predominantly runs from TLI to SUI in males, with higher causal path estimates in that direction than the reverse. This is consistent with the developmental logic underlying TLI's construction and with prior bivariate results (Vanyukov et al., 2015), where the temporal ordering of childhood TLI and adult SUD diagnosis was interpreted in explicitly directional ontogenetic terms: TLI measures liability at an earlier developmental stage, and its genetic variance propagates forward to influence liability as indexed by the later diagnosis. Because TLI was assessed in childhood while SUI aggregates subsequent waves, a path from SUI to TLI is not developmentally interpretable; the female estimates are more plausibly attributable to low heritability and threshold measurement of the binary SUI variables than to reverse causation. The present causal modeling extends this framework to SUI as an intermediate outcome, in line with longitudinal evidence that childhood externalizing behavior and behavioral disinhibition prospectively predict substance use onset (Iacono et al., 1999; King et al., 2004; Samek et al., 2017; Vanyukov et al., 2015) and that polygenic scores for behavioral disinhibition in childhood forecast later substance use across multiple substances (Schaefer et al., 2023).

In females, causal estimates were more variable, potentially reflecting genuine sex differences in the pathways from childhood liability to initiation, or reduced statistical power due to lower SUI prevalence and lower TLI heritability in females. Prior results (Vanyukov et al., 2015) noted greater uncertainty in female bivariate estimates, attributed in part to power limitations. This instability cautions against assuming that the same developmental pathways operate equally across sexes (Baker et al., 2011) and underscores the need for sexstratified analyses with adequate statistical power.

Implications for Identifying High-Resistance Populations

A primary motivation for this research is the identification of high-resistance individuals -those at the low end of the SUD liability distribution -who can serve as a contrast population for the discovery of resistance factors, the poorly understood counterpart of risk, as well as indicators specifically of the resistance aspect of liability (Vanyukov et al., 2016 (Vanyukov et al., , 2023)). Resistance factors may have greater translation potential than risk factors, because it is those factors that prevent a disorder on the background of the ubiquity and ready availability of psychoactive/addictive substances, from licit to increasingly decriminalized illicit ones. Nonetheless, it is risk factors that continue to be the common focus of research: e.g., research efforts are applied to the mechanisms of return to opioid use rather than those of persistent abstinence (Luo et al., 2024). Examples of implementation of a resistance approach are rare in general and in substance use in particular. One reason for that is the prevailing "disease model," which by definition targets the risk aspect. In addition, behavioral traits are "complex" meaning that variation in them is determined by numerous genetic and nongenetic factors rather than by a single factor such as exposure/non-exposure to an environmental agent or in monogenic traits. In complex traits, including substance use and the multifactorially inherited, and thus likely normally distributed liabilities to behavioral/psychiatric disorders, the absence of disorder or of its symptoms does not necessarily correspond to high resistance to it. As unaffected individuals comprise 90-95% of the population, their average liability is close to the population average. The identification of genuinely high-resistance individuals requires a continuous liability metric such as the TLI. The present results strengthen the empirical foundation for this approach in several respects. Confirming that TLI variance is predominantly genetic validates TLI scores as reflecting heritable differences. Establishing that TLI is genetically correlated with SUI across multiple substance classes confirms that TLI covers the same liability dimension underlying the outcomes of interest. Demonstrating feasibility of cross-study harmonization indicates that low-TLI individuals can be identified from pooled existing datasets, providing the statistical power needed to characterize a phenotypically unusual group. This supports the ongoing Resist! research program (Prom-Wormley et al., 2022; Vanyukov et al., 2023).

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. Residual between-study heterogeneity in TLI variance components, despite IRT harmonization, may reflect genuine population differences, cohort effects, or residual measurement non-equivalence that the anchor-item approach could not fully eliminate. SUI was coded as a lifetime binary variable, which does not capture timing or developmental context of first use; prior research indicates that age at first use carries important genetic information and independently predicts SUD risk (Richmond-Rakerd et al., 2016). The samples are predominantly of European ancestry. Prior work has noted that TLI items selected in European-American samples may not perform equivalently in African-American samples, where transmissibility of SUD liability may be differently structured (Vanyukov et al., 2009).

Conclusions

This study demonstrates high heritability, predictive validity, and suitability of the TLI for cross-study harmonization to generalize across two independent longitudinal twin cohorts. Quantitative between-sample differences in heritability estimates most plausibly reflect cohort, regional, developmental timing, and residual measurement factors rather than fundamental differences in genetic architecture. SUI reflects a mixture of genetic and shared environmental influences varying across substance classes, consistent with the principle that initiation is more environmentally sensitive than progression. Genetic connections between childhood TLI and lifetime SUI are consistently stronger than environmental connections and support a developmental interpretation in which early heritable liability manifests in SUI and, further downstream, in disorder. These results validate TLI as a tool for quantifying the full range of SUD liability -including both risk and resistance -and support its application in identifying high-resistance samples as a complement to the established high-risk research paradigm.
