Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Social media use is increasingly suspected of harming attention, but the evidence remains too limited to draw any firm conclusions. One obstacle is the lack of a framework specifying how such use might affect attention afterwards, without any device. To address this gap, we draw on emotional attention, the affective mechanism that prioritises what matters to individuals. The Relevance Rate Model rests on the relevance rate, the frequency with which an environment delivers concern-relevant stimuli. Drawing on predictive coding, we suggest that social media raises the rate that users expect, and that this expectation may persist after each session and, with repeated use, across daily life. When an activity delivers less concern-relevant information than expected, sustained attention to the ongoing task may be disrupted as emotional attention is recruited to address the shortfall. The model specifies four measurable parameters of use, from which we derive a research agenda.

[div-01]

The growing use of social media over the past decade has become a major societal issue, particularly among adolescents and young adults 1 . Questions have been raised regarding the links between social media use and various psychological, educational, and developmental outcomes, including psychological distress, academic performance and developmental trajectories 2 . Restrictive policies are thus being implemented even as the consequences of this use remain a subject of debate 3 . Attention plays a central role in this debate, given its importance for daily functioning and mental health 4 . Social media cues are well known to capture attention, with notifications diverting resources from the task at hand while they are present 5 . Whether social media use also impairs attention once use has ended, when no cue is present, is less clear. Despite widespread belief in such a persistent effect, current evidence is too limited to settle the question 6 . In the broader digital media literature, associations with attention are similarly weak and inconsistent 7, 8 , partly for lack of a mechanistic framework specifying which properties of exposure affect which attentional processes 6, 9.

Regarding usage, the evidence linking social media use to attention remains difficult to interpret, largely because of how use is measured. When participants estimate their cumulative time of use, results are mixed 10, 11 , partly because such estimates correlate only moderately with logged use 12 . Logging cumulative time objectively addresses this issue, yet its links with attention remain small and not always in the expected direction 13, 14 , possibly because the same total can conceal very different patterns of use 15 . Studies that distinguished such patterns have reported effects tied to several candidate parameters. Exposure to dense or rapidly updated content disrupts attention after use [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] . Attentional effects vary from moment to moment, and in strength across individuals, as a function of session length and fragmentation, that is, with how use is distributed across the day [22] [23] [24] . Finally, effects observed after a reduction or interruption of use, or after weeks of assigned use, suggest that chronic use may produce more persistent changes 14, 25, 26 . Whether social media use affects attention may thus depend less on its total duration than on what it delivers and how it unfolds over hours and days.

With regard to attention, these studies have examined several processes, including inattention symptoms 10, 11 , sustained attention 14, 19, 25, 26 , momentary distraction 22 , the monitoring of delayed intentions 18 , inhibitory and proactive control 17, 23, 24 , and the prefrontal activity and frontoparietal connectivity that support it 20, 21 . Although distinct, each can be read as a failure to keep limited resources on the ongoing activity, and thus as a diversion of endogenous attention, which voluntarily sustains that activity, whether reading a text or following a train of thought 27.

Existing frameworks explain several aspects of digital media use, from the costs of media multitasking 8, 28 to reward-driven engagement 29, 30 , online vigilance 31, 32 and effort recalibration 33 (Box 1). None, however, aims to explain what diverts endogenous attention once use has ended, nor how the properties of exposure would shape this diversion across timescales.

To answer these two questions, we develop the Relevance Rate Model (RRM), grounded in affectivism, an approach that places affect at the heart of cognition 37 . Account 4. Effort recalibration.

Effort recalibration proposes that repeated engagement with low-friction digital activities offering immediate rewards increases the subjective cost of cognitive effort 33 . This change may persist after digital activities are removed, reducing the relative attractiveness of effortful alternatives. The account therefore explains changes in effort allocation and activity selection, rather than attentional lapses within an ongoing task. its density, but also by its quality. It focuses on concern-relevance, defined as the probability that a stimulus will satisfy or dissatisfy a major concern of the organism 38 , such as its needs, goals, values or social standing 39 . Traditionally, social media content is described in terms of reward, that is, the positive feedback users receive 29, 30 . Concern-relevance, by contrast, carries no polarity 40 . News of a conflict or of climate obstructs concerns as surely as a like promotes them, and negativity even drives the consumption of online news 41 . Social media content is not neutral in this respect, as algorithms personalise it according to each user's past engagement 42 . We therefore propose to characterise exposure by the rate at which concern-relevant stimuli are encountered, whether conducive or obstructive, which we term the relevance rate.

With regard to attention, this framework draws on appraisal theories, according to which emotion arises from the ongoing appraisal of what events mean for the individual 43, 44 . Concern-relevant stimuli are automatically given attentional priority, in order to allocate resources to assessing their consequences and the appropriate response 45 . This mechanism is termed emotional attention 46 . Whereas endogenous attention is guided by current task goals 27 , emotional attention prioritises concern-relevant stimuli whether or not they serve the task at hand 47 . The two rely on partially dissociable neural systems, the dorsal frontoparietal network for endogenous attention and the amygdala for emotional attention [48] [49] [50] . Yet both bias the competition for the same limited processing capacity, so that concern-relevant stimuli can draw away the resources needed to pursue current goals 51, 52 . Emotional attention can also be triggered by internal stimuli, such as thoughts 53, 54 , making it a natural candidate for diverting endogenous attention after use, in the absence of an external signal.

To explain how usage would bring about this diversion, the RRM proposes that the brain learns the relevance rate of its environment, just as it learns other regularities through predictive coding 55 . Exposure to a high relevance rate would thus increase the rate at which the brain expects to encounter concern-relevant stimuli (Fig. 1). When a subsequent activity, such as reading, delivers fewer such stimuli than expected, the resulting relevance-rate mismatch would itself become concern-obstructive, so that concern-relevance comes into play twice, in the stimuli that shape the expectation and in the mismatch that frustrates it. Its appraisal would recruit emotional attention, diverting resources from the endogenous attention that sustains the activity, even without any social media cue. We ground each step of the RRM in evidence, examine how patterns of use and individual differences shape it, and derive a research agenda to test it.

The Relevance Rate Model

This section examines the four steps of the mechanism: social media delivers concernrelevant stimuli at a high rate, exposure calibrates the expected rate, this expectation persists after use, and the resulting mismatch recruits emotional attention (Fig. 1). For each step, we review the evidence and indicate where the model extends it (key terms are defined in Box 2). Most of this evidence concerns reward, a special case of concernrelevance 56 . The amygdala, proposed as a hub for concern-relevance detection 46, 49, 50 , serves as a common thread across these steps.

Box 2 | Glossary

Concern-relevance.

The extent to which a stimulus is appraised as likely to bear on one of an individual's current concerns, such as needs, goals, values or social standing, whether by promoting or obstructing them 39, 40.

Emotional attention.

The allocation of processing resources to stimuli appraised as relevant to the individual's current concerns, enabling the organism to assess their meaning and prepare an appropriate response 43, 45 . It can be engaged rapidly and involuntarily 47.

Endogenous attention.

The voluntary orientation of attention according to current goals, which sustains performance on an ongoing task 27 . In the RRM, it is a distinct source of attentional control that shares limited capacity with emotional attention.

Relevance rate.

The rate at which an environment delivers stimuli appraised as concern-relevant. It is a property of the environment, which depends on how often new items arrive and on the likelihood that they bear on the individual's concerns.

Expected relevance rate. The internal expectation, learned from exposure, of how often concern-relevant stimuli are likely to arrive. It is revised on two timescales. It rises quickly during use and falls back gradually afterwards, towards a resting level, the habitual expectation, which reflects daily life as a whole and shifts more slowly with the pattern of use.

Persistence. The carryover of an elevated expectation beyond use. It takes two forms, the after-effect of a session, which fades within minutes to hours after use, and a raised habitual expectation, which a sustained pattern of use maintains over days.

Precision.

The inverse of the variance of a signal or of a prediction. In predictive coding, the precision of an incoming signal relative to that of the prediction sets how strongly a prediction error revises a belief.

Relevance-rate mismatch. The shortfall of the rate an activity delivers below the expectation carried into it. Because a rate is estimated over time, it accumulates from intervals with few concern-relevant events as much as from those that deliver them. Although concern-obstructive, it is a signal about the rate rather than an event the activity delivers, and so does not enter the rate it measures.

Social media as a high-relevance-rate environment

Initially designed for active use, such as posting, messaging or reacting within existing networks, social media has since shifted towards passive browsing, as feeds began to mix friends' content with recommended posts 57 . These recommendations are selected to match each user 42 , which increases the likelihood that each piece of content bears on their concerns. Whether active or passive, usage thus exposes the user to a succession of content that may relate to their concerns. A like may signal approval from others 29, 30 , a message may affect a relationship, a post may alter a sense of status or belonging, and a political video may reinforce or threaten a belief. The implications of each piece of content for the user must therefore be appraised, which is precisely the role of emotional attention. Several findings suggest that emotional attention is recruited during use. Notifications disrupt attention in proportion to the relevance attributed to them 58 . Browsing a feed produces a largely neutral affective baseline, punctuated by brief peaks of physiological activation 59 , which may reflect responses to concernrelevant stimuli.

Several features of today's platforms are increasing the frequency of such content. Short-form formats increase the amount of content encountered per unit of time, and endless feeds eliminate natural stopping points 42 , whilst notifications fragment the day into repeated episodes of evaluation and the low cost of access makes it effortless to return 60 . Users are said to scroll through around 90 metres of content per day, roughly the height of the Statue of Liberty 61 . Other environments also deliver concernrelevant stimuli, from work and relationships to a gripping novel or a binge-watched series, but rarely combine this density, personalisation and ease of access.

How exposure calibrates an expected relevance rate

A high relevance rate can affect subsequent attention only if the brain learns this regularity and the resulting expectation outlasts use. For the first condition, calibration, predictive coding offers a general mechanism. The brain does not simply react to incoming events but anticipates them, deriving expectations from regularities in past input and registering departures from those expectations 55 . Regularities in an event stream can be learned incidentally, without intention or awareness 62 , and expectations about upcoming events, including their timing, are updated as the statistics of the environment change 63 . Expectations about when events will occur can, in turn, guide the temporal allocation of attention 64 . The RRM extends this general learning capacity to the frequency of events appraised as concern-relevant. Repeated exposure to a high relevance rate may therefore calibrate an expectation about how frequently such events are likely to occur. We propose that this calibration is fast. An expectation formed in a calm activity predicts few concern-relevant events, with little variation around that number. A dense stream delivers many more from its first minutes, which signals that the environment has changed rather than fluctuated, a condition under which expectations are revised faster 63.

The amygdala offers a possible substrate for this expectation, as its function has recently been described in predictive terms 65, 66 . In non-human primates, amygdala neurons respond to a reward cue only when it signals a higher reward rate than the prevailing one 67 . Amygdala activity also tracks the timing of reward delivery 68 and, in humans, encodes expected values updated through experience 69, 70 . Extending these findings to concern-relevance, we propose that exposure to a high relevance rate raises the expected rate against which the amygdala evaluates incoming events.

How expectations persist beyond social media use

Persistence is the second condition. Leaving the stream also signals a change in environment, which should speed the downward revision, yet expectations about concern-relevant events appear to resist it. In Pavlovian conditioning, the learning rate during extinction is lower for concern-relevant stimuli, whether threatening or positive, than for neutral stimuli 71 . Similarly, estimates of reward rates are revised more slowly when rewards become scarcer than when they become more frequent 72.

Evidence suggests that persistence operates on two timescales. The first, shortterm, follows each session: distraction fluctuates from hour to hour with the fragmentation of use 22 . The second, long-term, spans several days: the effects observed following a reduction or prolonged interruption in usage 25, 26 and the connectivity changes measured the day after four weeks of assigned use 14 indirectly suggest changes that do not depend on a recent session. To account for this, the RRM proposes that, following a session, the expectation falls back within minutes to hours. This constitutes the session's after-effect. However, it does not drop back to a fixed baseline, but rather to a resting level, the habitual expectation, which reflects the rate delivered by daily life as a whole. This resting level rises slowly with each session that maintains the expectation above it, so that after several days of repeated use, a mismatch may arise in the absence of a recent session, and this level only returns to normal when the usage pattern changes. A similar combination of fast and slow processes governs motor adaptation 73.

One might object that learned regularities remain tied to the task in which they were acquired 74 . The expected relevance rate, however, is abstract, as users anticipate only that concern-relevant information arrives at roughly a certain rate, not what it will be. Expectations of this degree of generality would occupy the higher levels of the predictive hierarchy, from which they constrain more context-specific predictions 75 . Furthermore, average reward rates transfer between tasks that share a relevant structure 76 . Here, the shared structure, events relating to concerns that occur at a certain rate, is common to all activities, so that the transfer requires no social-media-related cues. What persists is therefore an expectation, not an attentional state. During use, emotional attention is engaged by the content itself, with no ongoing task to disrupt. After use, only the elevated expectation remains, and endogenous attention would be diverted only when a subsequent task delivers less than this expectation predicts.

From persistence to relevance-rate mismatch

Persistence gives rise to a relevance-rate mismatch when the expectation brought into an activity exceeds the rate that activity delivers. It could stem from either form of persistence, though more strongly from the after-effect than from a raised habitual expectation. In both cases, the mismatch is greatest when the subsequent activity is calm. In terms of predictive coding, the mismatch is a prediction error 55 . In Bayesian terms, the expectation carried into the activity is a prior, itself drawn towards the habitual expectation, that the concern-relevant events of this activity update. Each posterior becomes the prior for the next interval, so that as long as the prior remains above what the activity yields, the shortfall recurs. The mismatch is therefore not a single event but a prediction error that persists throughout the activity (Fig. 2). The amygdala responds to such violations, in humans when an expected reward is delayed or absent 77 or when a neutral outcome replaces an expected appetitive or aversive one 78 , and in other animals for both kinds of outcome 79, 80.

The prior is now held with confidence, so each interval revises it only slightly and the mismatch shrinks slowly. Because the estimate concerns a rate, distributions are wider at higher rates, and intervals with few concernrelevant events contribute evidence that the rate is lower than expected.

From mismatch to attentional disruption

Like thoughts, the mismatch is an internal stimulus that can engage emotional attention 53, 54 . It would become concern-obstructive as it disrupts what the brain has become accustomed to, namely appraising concern-relevant stimuli at a certain rate. A single calm interval carries little evidence of this, but successive intervals make the shortfall increasingly precise, and therefore increasingly weighted (Fig. 2). A related state, termed frustrative nonreward, arises when an outcome is omitted or smaller than expected 81 . In humans, a decrease in the rate of positive feedback during a cognitive task induces frustration and can impair performance 82 . In rats, a sufficient reduction in reward activates specific regions of the amygdala 83 . Boredom, which signals a discrepancy between expected and actual engagement 84 , offers an example drawn from social media, as it rises once scrolling through short videos is no longer possible 85.

Once concern-relevant, the mismatch is appraised to determine how to resolve it 38, 46 . The mismatch can be resolved in two ways (Fig. 1). The expectation may be revised downwards to match the current activity, or the received rate may be raised to match the expectation, by returning to social media or by generating concern-relevant thoughts. The effort recalibration framework already considers the choice between continuing a task and turning to digital media in terms of effort cost and opportunity cost 33 . The RRM focuses instead on the competition that precedes this choice. At the start of a task, the goal of performing it dominates, and endogenous attention sustains it. As the shortfall gains precision, the concern it obstructs would gain weight relative to this goal, until it recruits emotional attention. Resources would then go to appraising the shortfall, its consequences and the response it calls for. Because emotional and endogenous attention compete for the same limited capacity 51, 52 , these resources would be withdrawn from the ongoing task, whichever route is eventually taken. As this shift occurs when one goal overtakes the other, it should produce intermittent lapses rather than a steady decline in performance.

This mechanism offers a common reading of reported effects. Without competing stimuli, the diversion would appear as momentary distraction, inattention, reduced sustained attention or failures to monitor intentions 10, [17] [18] [19] 25 . With them, it would weaken inhibitory control 23, 24 , with reduced lateral prefrontal activity and default mode-frontoparietal coupling as its neural trace 20, 21 . Effects observed shortly after use [16] [17] [18] 20 would reflect the after-effect, and those observed after an interruption, a reduction or weeks of assigned use 14, 25, 26 a raised habitual expectation.

How patterns of use set the expected relevance rate

The studies reviewed in the introduction point to four candidate parameters of use, namely the within-session relevance rate, which combines the density and concernrelevance of the feed [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] ; the duration and fragmentation of sessions [22] [23] [24] ; and the chronicity of usage 14, 25, 26 . When these parameters are studied separately, their interactions may be overlooked. The RRM integrates them as a leaky bucket, which each session fills and which empties between sessions down to a resting level, the habitual expectation, itself rising with sustained use.

The within-session relevance rate sets the level the expectation approaches during use. It depends on both the rate at which new content arrives and its relevance to the user's concerns, although how the two combine over the course of a session remains to be established. Speed is increased by continuous feeds and short-form content 42 , as well as by the users themselves, through rapid scrolling, skipping or fast-forwarding 85 . Concern-relevance is enhanced by recommendation systems, which personalise content based on past engagement 42 . Session duration and fragmentation are two sides of the same coin. Because calibration is fast, duration should matter only until the expectation reaches the rate of the stream. Fragmentation then takes over, since a return maintains the expectation only if it comes before the expectation has subsided, so that the spacing of sessions matters more than their number. For the same total duration, fragmented use would therefore keep the expectation elevated for longer than a single long session (Fig. 3).

Chronicity operates on a slow timescale. Each session that keeps the expectation above the habitual level raises that level slightly. For the same usage pattern, a user who has maintained it for a week rather than a day therefore returns to a higher resting level between sessions. 2. After their morning session (10:00), both users carry the same elevated expectation, because it reaches the stream's rate early in a session. By 16:00, it has returned to its resting level for the concentrated user, whilst the fragmented user's final session has reinstated it. For the same total duration, fragmentation thus prolongs the mismatch throughout the day. Clock times and durations are illustrative, as the timescales of calibration and decay remain to be estimated.

This view also exposes the limits of current usage metrics. Cumulative time adds up sessions regardless of their distribution, although the level of the bucket may differ for the same total (Fig. 3). The study that separated duration from fragmentation used isolated one-hour windows 22 , which cannot show how the two combine across the day. Questionnaires derived from existing frameworks (Box 1) measure simultaneous use, problematic use or online vigilance rather than the parameters that calibrate the expectation. They also rely on self-report, which diverges from logged behaviour 12.

Who the parameters place at risk

Individual differences and transient states may increase vulnerability by making an elevated expected relevance rate more likely to be reached, maintained or acted upon. Boredom proneness 85 , need to belong 86 , anxiety 87 and FoMO 88 may raise the withinsession relevance rate, by increasing scrolling speed and the likelihood that content bears on the user's concerns. Because expectations about concern-relevant events resist downward revision, the same dispositions may also lengthen the after-effect, thus acting twice. Others may increase between-session fragmentation by raising the likelihood of picking up the phone. Momentary fatigue 89 and emotion-regulation difficulties 90 may do so by making the current state harder to tolerate, and attention-deficit traits 4 by making it harder to stay with a task other than the phone.

Adolescence may displace several parameters at once. Peer approval and social evaluation become dominant concerns, raising the expected concern-relevance of social streams 91 and making the expectation resist downward revision once the stream is left. Fragmentation may also increase, as still-developing sustained attention 92 favours returning to the stream over holding a task, and younger users indeed show more fragmented smartphone use 93 . Calibration may in turn be faster, since adolescents learn from social feedback at a higher rate than adults 94 . Faster learning would also speed the decay of the expectation after use, but with frequent returns to the stream, it should mainly serve calibration, each new session quickly bringing the expectation back up. These influences therefore converge on greater exposure, making adolescence, and the early adulthood into which these changes extend 95 , a period of heightened vulnerability.

Later adulthood may also carry vulnerability. Isolation becomes more likely at this age 96 , and unmet belonging needs heighten the monitoring of social cues 86 , which may raise the expected concern-relevance of social streams. Yet social media use may also reduce loneliness at this age 97 , and the patterns of use that sustain the expectation have not been documented in this population, so its net effect remains open.

Predictions and research agenda

The questions this literature has to address are whether social media use leaves attention impaired once a session has ended, and whether that impairment persists. The RRM makes both questions answerable by turning them into two quantities. The first is the after-effect, the elevated expectation that follows a session and falls back within minutes to hours. The second is the habitual expectation towards which it falls back, built up over days of repeated use and declining over days once use stops.

In both cases, sustained attention and attentional lapses may be measured with tasks that are well established 19, 25, 26 , scored for moment-to-moment stability rather than mean performance 98 . What changes is when they are administered, after a fixed period of use, say ten minutes, or after a fixed period of full abstinence as used in restriction studies 25 . The model also predicts when the cost should be greatest. A session taken as a break from a calm task, such as studying, should disrupt attention on returning to that task more than a session followed by a denser activity. Conversely, the more the ongoing task matters, through stakes or deadlines, the later the shortfall should overtake the goal of performing it, and the later lapses should appear. Administered in the scanner, these tasks would show whether windows of unstable performance coincide with amygdala activation, whose connectivity with attention networks already relates to sustained-attention performance 99 . Unstable windows without amygdala involvement would qualify the role attributed to emotional attention.

What is most urgently needed, though, is a better measure of use, one that would serve this literature whether or not the mechanism proposed here proves correct. The four parameters can be derived from scrolling logs and from the times at which sessions start and end, which tracking tools make increasingly feasible to collect 100 . Only the concern-relevance of content escapes the log, and could be approached through experience sampling. Future studies could combine these parameters into an exposure index that behaves like the leaky bucket described above, rising during sessions, draining between them and drifting with sustained use. Its weights and timescales can be estimated, and the index compared against simpler descriptions of use down to cumulative time. If cumulative time accounts for attention best, the model's central claim is rejected. Should the model fail, the same comparison would still identify which description of use is best supported, including descriptions the model does not privilege.

What remains open is how far the model extends across individuals. The dispositions discussed above are starting points rather than predictions. Age differs, because several parameters move together in a direction the model can specify [92] [93] [94] . Two comparisons follow. Across the patterns of use that individuals actually display, adolescents and young adults should show stronger associations between the expected relevance rate and attentional disruption than older adults. At matched patterns of use, comparing the after-effect across ages would show whether faster learning from social feedback or the greater concern-relevance of social content dominates. A shorter aftereffect in younger users would indicate that faster learning dominates. This prediction matters because age-based restrictions are being implemented despite the absence of supporting experimental evidence 3.

Conclusion

Whether social media affects attention has been debated for over a decade, and one obstacle has been the lack of an account specifying both the relevant exposure and the mechanism at stake. The RRM offers one such account. It proposes that the brain learns the relevance rate of its environment, like other statistical regularities. Streams that deliver concern-relevant stimuli rapidly and repeatedly may thus calibrate upwards the rate users expect. When this expectation carries over into activities that deliver a lower rate, the resulting mismatch may recruit emotional attention away from the ongoing task. Because emotional and endogenous attention are distinct sources of control, this cost depends on their sharing a limited capacity. If they drew on independent resources, the mismatch would leave the ongoing task intact, which makes attentional disruption after use a direct test of this shared capacity.

The contribution is both theoretical and methodological. Theoretically, the model identifies one way in which digital environments might affect cognition. Rather than depleting attentional capacity, they may shift what the brain has learned to expect from its surroundings. Unlike reward-based accounts, the model counts obstructive events as much as conducive ones. Methodologically, the model characterises exposure through four parameters that can be computed from logged use, rather than through time spent alone. In doing so, it turns broad questions about the effects of social media into estimable ones. Which patterns of use affect attention, over what timescale and for whom? These questions are worth pursuing whether or not the mechanism proposed here proves to be the right one.
