study_design_overview This is a qualitative, observational, cross-sectional study using a contemporary iterative approach to thematic analysis. Data are drawn from semi-structured interviews and focus groups with Spanish adults. In the initial phase, expert reviewers conducted inductive coding to identify culturally grounded everyday life scenarios in which people in Spain feel loved. In the current preregistered phase, a team of graduate coders will apply a primarily deductive coding scheme to the full transcripts using Atlas.ti, identifying (1) the presence of the expert-generated scenarios and (2) the relational tie involved (e.g., family member, friend, partner, stranger, acquaintance). Coders will also be encouraged to identify new scenarios inductively. Intercoder agreement (ICA) will be assessed at three time points: early, mid, and late in the coding process. Final scenarios will be thematically grouped to inform item development for a culturally grounded Felt Love Questionnaire in the subsequent quantitative phase.

The semi-structured interviews and focus groups followed a consistent protocol, prompting participants to describe specific moments in which they felt loved, and to reflect on how love is expressed and recognized in Spanish culture. Example prompts included:

–When you think of “love,” what comes to mind?
–Think back to a time when you “felt loved.” Can you tell me what that felt like for you?
–Do you think Spanish society has a particular way of expressing love?

statistical_tests This preregistration describes a qualitative phase of a mixed methods study. No inferential statistical models (e.g., ANOVA, regression) will be used. Instead, we will conduct a thematic analysis using a contemporary iterative approach that combines deductive and inductive coding. Coders will analyze transcripts using Atlas.ti to identify (1) the presence of predefined everyday love scenarios and (2) the associated relational tie (e.g., family, friend, partner, stranger, acquaintance).

We will compute descriptive frequency counts for how often each scenario and relational tie appears in the dataset. We will also assess intercoder agreement (ICA) using percent agreement, calculated at three time points (early, mid, and late in the coding process) to monitor consistency. These results will guide thematic refinement and item selection for the development of the Felt Love Questionnaire in the quantitative phase. 		The cultural beliefs and individual differences of the expressions of love in daily life will be derived using the cognitive psychometric models rooted in the Cultural Consensus Theory framework (Batchelder et al., 2018; Oravecz et al., 2014). Models from this framework have been used in previous studies exploring beliefs about love (Oravecz et al., 2016; Heshmati et al., 2019).

First we will extract eigenvalues (up to the first 10) from the correlation matrix of responses from the participants for each of the Felt Love Questionnaire (FLQ) items. We will plot these to visualize changes in the eigenvalues (see, e.g., Batchelder et al., 2018). This is a general first step done in CCT research. This will help us test our first hypothesis. If there is a single underlying culture, we will see a sharp drop after the first eigenvalue and only smooth change afterwards.

After this, we are going to fit the Continuous Response model (CRM, Anders et al., 2014) to the participants’ responses to the FLQ items. While hypothesis 1 assumes that there will be a single underlying culture behind the FLQ responses, the CRM can accommodate more than one underlying culture (i.e., it can fit multiple culture solutions). That is to say that with this model we can derive different consensus truths for the FLQ items based on cultural ‘membership’ (person-specific), where membership is derived from the FLQ data. The CRM will derive the culturally shared consensus answers for each item in the Felt Love Questionnaire. It will also derive for each participant an estimate for their:
a. cultural knowledge level
b. response bias shift
c. response bias scale.

a. attachment style
b. Personality style
c. cultural value related to interpersonal concern and conformity
d. Sex
e. How much they report expressing love in daily life in general.
f. How much they report feeling loved in daily life in general.
g. how much they feel loved from different sources (such as family etc.)

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Using a sequential mixed-method approach guided by Cultural Consensus Theory, we examined shared agreement about daily experiences that evoke feelings of love in the Spanish culture. In Study 1, we conducted semi-structured interviews (n = 54), two-person interviews (n = 6), and focus groups (3-5 participants; n = 40). Qualitative analyses found recurring experiences centered on explicit affection, dependable support and care, quality time, and thoughtful actions.

In Study 2, a representative sample of Spaniards (N = 502) evaluated these experiences to assess cultural agreement elicit feelings of love. Cognitive psychometric analysis revealed strong shared agreement. Experiences most likely to evoke love involved support and dependability, shared quality time, and explicit expressions of love, particularly within close relationships such as family, partners, and friends. In contrast, controlling or possessive behaviors from romantic or familial ties were unlikely to elicit love. These findings advance social psychological theory by demonstrating that everyday love can be understood as a form of shared cultural knowledge, specifically a relational social schema that can be empirically identified through Cultural Consensus Theory.

Cultural Models of Everyday Love in Contemporary Spain: A Mixed-Methods Cultural

Consensus Study

People use the word love to describe a wide range of experiences, from affection and care to moral connection. These meanings are deeply shaped by cultural, social, and relational contexts. Social psychological research has long sought to define love, most often through the lens of romantic or familial relationships (e.g., Aron & Aron, 1997; Berscheid, 1994; Fehr, 1988 Fehr, , 2006 Fehr, , 2019;; Hazan & Shaver, 1987; Sternberg, 1986). These frameworks emphasize attachment, intimacy, and commitment as the essential building blocks of close relationships (see Heshmati et al., 2025 for a comprehensive review of psychological research on love). While such models have advanced understanding of how love maintains strong interpersonal bonds, less is known about how love is recognized and enacted in everyday life across sociocultural contexts.

Research on lay conceptions of love offers an important shift in perspective. Rather than imposing researcher-defined models, this tradition examines how ordinary people conceptualize love in their own terms. Prototype research demonstrates that people consistently describe love through relational and moral features (e.g., trust, caring, honesty, and friendship) rather than passion or sexual desire (Fehr, 1988 (Fehr, , 2006 (Fehr, , 2019;; Fehr & Russell, 1991). Complementary social psychology perspectives suggest that lay understandings of love are shaped by implicit narratives and social categories that organize different varieties of love (Meyers & Berscheid, 1997; Sternberg, 1998), as well as essentialist beliefs about caring for another's well-being as a core feature of love (Hegi & Bergner, 2010). From a social-cognitive perspective, these lay understandings can be viewed as shared schemas or social scripts that guide how people interpret interpersonal behavior within specific relationships (Baldwin, 1992; Holmes, 2000). Thus, whether behavior is recognized as loving may depend on the behavior itself, the relational role of the person enacting it, and its cultural meaning. However, this literature primarily captures how people broadly mentally represent love (i.e., its defining features and categories) rather than how those shared meanings are specifically enacted and recognized in everyday life.

Complementing these conceptual approaches, recent work conceptualizes love as a dynamic, momentary process that unfolds in daily life. The everyday love perspective views love not as a static trait or enduring bond, but as recurring micro-moments of connection expressed through gestures, words, touch, and mutual responsiveness (Fredrickson, 2013; Oravecz et al., 2020). Empirical studies demonstrate that frequent experiences of felt love are associated with greater well-being, lower loneliness, and psychological flourishing (Major et al., 2018; Dickens et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022). These findings underscore that love is embedded in ordinary exchanges across diverse relational contexts, not only romantic partnerships, but also interactions with family members, friends, colleagues, and even strangers (West et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2025c). Yet despite growing recognition that love operates through daily experiences, little research has examined whether the situations that elicit feelings of love are culturally shared within a society.

Culture plays a central role in shaping emotional meaning. What signifies love in one cultural context may not carry the same significance in another (Karandashev, 2015). Crosscultural research demonstrates that relational norms, communication styles, and value systems influence how emotions are expressed and interpreted (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Mesquita & Leu, 2007). For example, collectivistic contexts tend to emphasize responsiveness, obligation, and relational harmony, whereas individualistic contexts prioritize autonomy and personal expression in close relationships (Dion & Dion, 1993). These differences suggest that everyday loving gestures, such as offering practical help, expressing verbal affirmation, or engaging in physical affection, may carry culturally specific meanings as signals of love. However, most prior research compares predefined constructs across societies rather than identifying culturally grounded understandings from within a single population. Far less is known about whether members of a given cultural group converge on a coherent, shared model of what makes people feel loved in everyday life.

Cultural Consensus Theory (CCT; Romney et al., 1986) provides a framework for identifying such shared cultural models. Rather than assuming universal definitions, CCT conceptualizes culture as a system of shared beliefs and interpretive norms within a group.

Rooted in cognitive anthropology and cognitive psychology, CCT allows researchers to analyze patterns of agreement across individuals, determine whether members of a group converge on a common cultural model, and estimate the degree to which individuals align with that model (Weller, 2007). Although CCT did not emerge specifically from social psychology, it complements social psychological approaches by providing a formal method for examining shared social knowledge, such as shared relational schemas (Holmes, 2000) about everyday loving behaviors. In domains where no objective "correct" answer exists-such as abstract emotional constructs like love-consensus reflects collectively learned social knowledge rather than factual truth.

Importantly, CCT is traditionally embedded within mixed-method designs and bridges emic and etic approaches: qualitative methods are used to elicit culturally meaningful content, which is then evaluated quantitatively to test for structured consensus (Romney et al., 1986; Weller, 2007). Contemporary extensions of CCT use formal cognitive and Bayesian psychometric models to estimate the culturally shared model (Batchelder et al., 2018; Oravecz et al., 2015; Weller et al., 2023).

CCT has been applied to examine everyday experiences of love in the United States using an instrument composed of brief descriptions of everyday experiences known as the Felt Love Questionnaire (FLQ; Heshmati et al., 2019). Participants evaluated descriptions of everyday experiences beginning with the prompt "Most people feel loved when…," allowing researchers to model culturally shared beliefs about love-eliciting situations. Findings revealed strong convergence on small relational gestures-such as receiving compassion or affectionate closeness-as prototypical experiences of feeling loved, indicating the presence of a coherent cultural model. Subsequent work extended this approach across developmental stages, demonstrating that although specific expressions may shift with age, the underlying cultural structure of everyday love remains detectable (Ellis et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2025c). Similar convergence has also been observed among culturally diverse U.S. immigrant samples (Heshmati & Rombaoa, 2025), underscoring CCT's capacity to identify shared emotional meaning systems within heterogeneous populations. However, these investigations have been largely confined to North American contexts, leaving open the question of how everyday love is structured in other cultural settings.

Spain provides a compelling context for extending this work. Spanish society has been described as strongly oriented toward interpersonal bonds, dense social networks, and the centrality of family life (Uskul et al., 2023). Rather than reflecting simple "higher collectivism," recent scholarship suggests that Mediterranean cultures are characterized by distinct forms of interdependence and relational dependence that structure social and affective life in specific ways. Within the family domain, emotional bonds remain central, even as diversification of family structures and changing relational trajectories reflect a dynamic and evolving society (Roche Cárcel, 2021). Although Catholic tradition historically shaped Spain's cultural configuration, contemporary processes of secularization suggest that family-and communityoriented values are now expressed as broadly shared cultural orientations beyond formal religious practice (Herranz de Rafael & Fernández-Prados, 2024; Rosa González & Cabrera, 2023). In emotional terms, Spanish culture combines relational interdependence with distinctive patterns of emotional openness and expressiveness (Kitayama & Salvador, 2024; Salvador et al., 2024; Uskul et al., 2023). These include normative physical affection (e.g., hugging, kissing, and touch as everyday expressions of care), direct verbal expressions of warmth, and the embedding of love in shared daily routines such as preparing food and spending time together. Together, this literature positions everyday love not only as a personal feeling or dyadic bond, but as culturally shared relational knowledge (social schema) that structures how people within a culture recognize and enact connection in daily life.

The Present Research

Prior work on lay conceptions of love in everyday life highlights that love is not merely an emotional connection in romantic relationships, but a culturally shaped understanding of what it means to care for, value, and connect with others (Heshmati et al., 2019; Williams et al, 2025b). Yet, the majority of this research has been conducted in North American contexts, limiting our knowledge of how everyday experiences of love are structured in societies with different relational and moral beliefs. The present research addressed this gap through a sequential exploratory mixed-method design conducted in Spain, a culture characterized by communal sociality, expressive emotional norms, and strong family bonds. Study 1 used qualitative interviews and focus groups to identify naturally occurring situations in which people living in Spain feel loved across different types of relational ties (e.g., family, friends, romantic partner, strangers). Study 1 used qualitative interviews and focus groups to identify recurring everyday experiences of feeling loved and the relational contexts in which they occurred. These findings informed development of a culturally grounded set of everyday love experiences for Spain (FLQ-Spain). These experiences were then evaluated in Study 2, which quantitatively examined the extent of shared agreement among a large representative group of people living in Spain on what makes 'most people' feel loved using a cultural consensus modeling approach.

Together, these studies provide a comprehensive understanding of love as both a lived experience and a collectively held cultural meaning system adopted by people living in Spain.

Study 1

Understanding everyday experiences of love requires methods that capture cultural nuance and lived meaning beyond quantitative measurement. Qualitative research is particularly suited to examining the variation and depth of culturally situated emotional experiences (Creswell, 2013; Karasz, 2011; Levitt et al., 2018 Levitt et al., , 2021)). Rather than seeking statistical generalization, qualitative research focuses on uncovering the variation and depth within a phenomenon that could reveal how emotions are understood and enacted within specific cultural contexts (Karasz, 2011; Levitt et al., 2018). Guided by this perspective, and as the first step of our CCT investigation, we conducted a qualitative study to explore how people living in Spain experience love in their everyday lives and to identify the diverse ways loving experiences showed up in people's lives as they went about their day.

To achieve this goal, we used semi-structured interviews and focus groups with a diverse sample of adults. We analyzed the data using a flexible iterative deductive-inductive coding process (Braun & Clarke, 2006; Merriam & Tisdell, 2016) and utilized qualitative data analysis (QDA) software (Deterding & Waters, 2021)-specifically, ATLAS.ti (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2025). This nonlinear analytic approach involved repeated phases of coding, interpretation, and refinement to ensure that themes accurately depict the heterogeneity of participants' love beliefs and experiences (Small & Calarco, 2022). The primary objective of Study 1 was to generate a detailed culturally grounded inventory of everyday love experiences as experienced in Spain. Specifically, we sought to (a) identify the situations that elicit feelings of being loved, (b) examine the relational ties through which these experiences occur (e.g., family, friends, romantic partners, acquaintances, strangers), and (c) explore the co-occurrences of these experiences across different relational ties. The findings from this qualitative phase provided the empirical basis for deriving the Spain version of the Felt Love Questionnaire (FLQ-Spain), a set of culturally grounded everyday scenarios used in Study 2 to estimate shared cultural beliefs about love in everyday life in Spain.

Reflexivity practices involve critically examining the role of the researchers and the influence of their perspectives on the research. Some reflexive approaches emphasize minimizing the perspective of the researchers (e.g., Glaser & Strauss, 2017), whereas other approaches leverage researchers' perspectives to enrich interpretation of findings (e.g., Gentles et al., 2014). Sample Size Rationale and Recruitment.

We planned approximately 10-12 focus groups of 3-5 participants, stratified by age and gender, supplemented by individual interviews. This multiformat design captured both individual reflection and group interaction across key demographic dimensions. Recruitment was facilitated through community referrals and snowball sampling, following guidance from Creswell & Poth (2018) on engaging local networks for diverse qualitative samples.

Participants engaged in individual semi-structured interviews (n = 54), paired interviews (n = 6), or one of 11 focus groups of 3-5 participants (n = 40). Procedure and Materials

Study 1 involved a four-week period of in-person qualitative fieldwork in Spain in January 2025, during which participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling. Spanish adults were recruited through flyers, word of mouth, and social media.

Eligibility required participants to be (1) ≥18 years old, (2) living in Spain, and (3) Spanishspeaking. Interested participants completed an online interest form and a basic demographics form (e.g., age, gender, relationship status). Eligible participants completed an in-person session in a private office or university classroom in Madrid. At least one of the principal investigators or graduate research assistants was physically present for all interviews and focus groups with inear live (AI) translation devices, while trained bilingual English/Spanish interviewers conducted the interviews in Spanish. All interviews and focus groups were conducted in-person in a private classroom or closed office room.

The semi-structured interviews and focus groups followed a consistent protocol, prompting participants to describe specific moments in which they felt loved, and to reflect on how love is expressed and recognized in Spanish culture in response to open-ended questions.

Example prompts included: (1) "Think back to a time when you "felt loved." Can you tell me what that felt like for you?" (2) "Can you describe a time that you showed love to someone else?" and (3) "Do you think Spanish society has a particular way of expressing love?" The full protocol and final question wordings for both interviews and focus groups is provided in the online Supplemental Materials. After completion of the study, participants received €30.

Individual/paired interviews lasted 25-60 min (M = 40), and focus groups lasted 35-90 min (M = 55).

Recordings were transcribed in Spanish using a secure AI-assisted service, anonymized, and reviewed by eight Spanish-English bilingual research assistants for semantic accuracy.

Transcripts were then AI-translated into English and reviewed by the same bilingual team for translation accuracy and conceptual equivalence, including colloquial and culturally specific expressions. The finalized, anonymized interview and focus group transcripts (Spanish) and translations (English) are available upon request.

Qualitative Analysis Process

We used an iterative approach for qualitative coding that combined deductive and inductive coding. Coding was conducted in ATLAS.ti (version 25.0.1) using a flexible coding approach (Deterding & Waters, 2021; see also Abramson et al., 2026; Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). This approach is appropriate for a relatively large qualitative dataset (N > 30) and for our use of both theoretically informed a priori codes and inductively identified patterns.

Unlike approaches in which codes are developed solely from participants' narratives (e.g., grounded theory approach), flexible inductive-deductive coding allowed us to begin with theoretically informed a priori codes and refine the codebook as researchers engaged with the cultural guides and the participants' data (Deterding & Waters, 2021).

The initial codebook was developed deductively from prior research on love in everyday life (e.g., Ellis et al., 2024; Heshmati et al., 2019; Oravecz et al., 2016), particularly scenarios from the U.S. Felt Love Questionnaire (FLQ-US; Heshmati et al., 2019). These prior scenarios provided theoretically informed sensitizing concepts for identifying related experiences in the Spanish interview and focus-group data. After conducting several of the first few interviews, relational context emerged as an important additional coding domain since participants frequently indicated that whether an experience conveyed love depended on who enacted it.

Relational ties were therefore incorporated alongside everyday love scenarios in the final codebook.

Four trained research assistants coded the English-translated transcripts through a flexible iterative process (Deterding & Waters, 2021) using ATLAS.ti (version 25.0.1). Before independent coding, coders completed a three-stage training process consisting of codebook review, joint practice, and independent trial coding. Weekly calibration meetings were held during the two-month coding period to discuss coding decisions and operational definitions, with discrepancies resolved through consensus and, when necessary, the principal investigator serving as the final arbiter.

Coders analyzed sentence-and paragraph-level segments on two domains: (1) everyday love experiences and (2) associated relational ties, including family, friends, romantic partner, stranger, acquaintance, and general/nonspecific ties. Passages could receive more than one lovescenario or relational-tie code. Coded data were organized for comparisons of everyday love experiences and relational ties (Abramson et al., 2018 (Abramson et al., , 2026)). Consistent with a flexible coding approach (Deterding & Waters, 2021), we used ATLAS.ti frequency table features to descriptively summarize how often each love scenario code and relational tie code appeared across the data. These frequency counts were used to identify commonly mentioned and less frequently mentioned scenarios and relational ties, rather than to conduct formal statistical inference. Details for frequencies of love scenario codes and relational ties can be found in the online Supplemental Materials in Tables S3-S4.

Intercoder agreement (ICA) was assessed using percent agreement (Halpin, 2024) at three stages of coding-early, midpoint, and late-to monitor coding consistency. ICA was calculated using a partial-overlap method in which partial matches received proportional credit, exact matches received a score of 1.0, and non-overlaps received a 0. For example, if the master code contained two subcodes ("warm hug," "kiss on cheek") and a coder identified only "warm hug," the overlap score would be 0.50. Coders' independent coding was compared with the consensus master code at three stages: early (after training), midpoint (approximately 50% of the data coded), and late (approximately 85% coded). Agreement remained high across stages: ICA values for love scenario codes were .86, .85, and .87, and values for relational tie codes were .97, .98, and .90, respectively.

Results

Consistent with the APA reporting guidelines (Levitt et al., 2018), we provide select data extracts related to prominent themes directly quoted from the interviews and focus groups. Given the sequential design, we focus here on the qualitative patterns most central to the development and interpretation of the FLQ-Spain, rather than providing exhaustive coverage of all qualitative results from Study 1. Specifically, we summarize the most frequent and salient everyday love experiences, the relational ties through which these experiences were described, and how these qualitative findings informed the items for the FLQ-Spain tested in Study 2.

Prevalent Everyday Life Loving Experiences

Participants described a wide range of everyday love experiences, most frequently involving physical affection (e.g., hugs and kisses), quality time, care and support during difficult moments, and personalized consideration (e.g., thoughtful gifts, remembering personal details).

Verbal and Physical Expressions of Love.

Participants frequently described love as it is expressed verbally and through physical affection, particularly hugging, cuddling, and touch.

Verbal expressions most often involved the literal phrase "I love you," especially from parents to children across situations such as daily check-in phone calls, moments of physical affection, and difficult times. These expressions were also frequently described in romantic relationships, followed by friendships. Physical gestures were often framed not as intimate or sexual, but as warm, casual, and embedded in daily interactions, such as greetings or expressions of support.

For example, one participant described Spanish expressions of love through the idea of "campechanidad":

For me…a very key word is: "campechanidad." We are not only close, friendly people.

When we treat each other, when we approach people, when we want to make others feel comfortable and loved with our bodies. [For example,] "I'm going to play with your hair, oh, how beautiful it is", we cuddle and kiss and hug. And I think that, in some cultures, publicly, this not only does not happen because it doesn't happen naturally, but it is also often socially punishable.-Non-binary, 27 years old Participants also discussed "kissing on the cheeks," or giving "two kisses," as a culturally familiar form of physical affection. However, this practice was interpreted in different ways. Some viewed it as a routine social norm that did not necessarily communicate love, whereas others viewed it as a culturally embedded expression of affection across different kinds of relationships. Explicit references to kissing romantic partners appeared less frequently. Thus, physical affection emerged as a salient feature of everyday love in Spain, while also depending on relational and situational context. Quality Time. Spending quality time with others was also frequently brought up by participants when describing daily expressions of love. Participants emphasized that feeling loved often emerged when others expressed a desire to spend time with them, invited them into shared activities, or adapted their own preferences to participate in something meaningful to the other person. As one participant explained: But I imagine, uh, I don't want to love soccer [fútbol]. And he loves that I go with him to watch soccer. Well, from time to time, I go with him to watch soccer because I know it's something he's looking forward to, even if it's not my favorite thing to do, but sometimes it's something like that to accompany someone who maybe doesn't have another person to accompany him in what he likes. Well, it makes you feel loved.-Female, 25 years old Participants also described quality time through ordinary social routines or everyday activities, including walking together, watching a movie, shopping, going to the beach, sharing drinks, eating together, or preparing food for others. Although food was sometimes described as care or nourishment, participants most often emphasized the relational meaning of these activities as opportunities for connection, accompaniment, and shared presence.

Instrumental Support and Care. Additionally, participants frequently recounted moments in which love was conveyed through offering or receiving support, care and help, especially during difficult times. These accounts went beyond verbal reassurance and included more active efforts to reduce others' burdens or provide assistance. Participants often emphasized that support felt especially loving when it was offered without needing to be requested. For example, one participant described setting aside daily responsibilities to care for their mother during a moment of distress:

There was a moment where she broke down… So my sister and I decided that "today we are going to stop everything." No studying, no work, nothing… And even though in that moment, my mom didn't want to talk, we just did that for her.-Non-binary, 20 years old Although support and care were most often described within close relationships, participants also recalled meaningful examples involving acquaintances and strangers, such as someone offering a seat, holding a door, paying for something, or helping during a stressful public moment.

Gift Giving.

Participants discussed giving and receiving gifts more ambivalently than other expressions of love. Some described material or obligatory gift exchanges as less meaningful, especially when they lacked personal intention. However, gifts were more likely to be interpreted as loving when they reflected thoughtfulness, effort, or knowledge of the recipient's preferences and needs (e.g., concert ticket to favorite artist, a new kitchen tool to replace a broken one). Participants also described small, handmade, or food-related gifts as meaningful because they communicated that the giver had remembered and considered them personally.

Personal Details, Attentiveness, and Genuine Interest. The qualities that made giftgiving feel loving-thoughtfulness and attentiveness to the other person-also appeared repeatedly in other expressions of love. Participants described feeling loved when others listened carefully, remembered personal details, checked in about important events, or adjusted their behavior in response to the participant's needs. One participant described listening as a way of communicating care: Listening, letting the person express themselves. Say a few words of comfort. I believe that by doing that, you show the person that you care. There are many times when you don't need to say anything, just listen and let it flow.-Female, 51 years old Participants also often described feeling loved when others remembered details about them, ranging from simple preferences, such as their favorite color, to more deliberate recollections of opinions or needs they had shared previously. These experiences often appeared alongside other loving actions, such as gift-giving, shared activities, checking in before an exam, or preparing food the person liked. More broadly, participants described genuine interest in their thoughts, feelings, and well-being as loving because it made them feel considered, included, and personally known.

Less Frequent but Culturally Meaningful Scenarios.

While the most frequently mentioned experiences centered on physical affection shared time, support, and attentiveness, participants also described a range of less common but culturally meaningful experiences. These included emotional comfort and reconciliation, guidance and protection, respect for autonomy, spiritual or nature-based experiences, and collective moments of belonging such as being welcomed by a group, cheering together at a sporting event, or singing in harmony. Although less frequent, these scenarios enriched the conceptual space of the FLQ-Spain by highlighting the frequent and the subtle expressions of love that emerged from participants' lived experiences and narratives.

Relational Ties and Frequently Mentioned Loving Scenarios

Participants described loving interactions across diverse relational ties, but most examples involved family, friends, and romantic partners. Even when the prompts did not specify a particular relational tie, participants drew on examples from these intimate ties, underscoring their centrality in how love is experienced and expressed in daily life in Spain.

Family examples frequently centered on parent-child dynamics across the lifespan, parents expressing affection toward young children through hugs and care to adult children seeking support or life advice with their parents during major life transitions (e.g., first pregnancy, attending university) and difficult times (e.g., breakups, job burnout). Friendship examples often emphasized companionship (e.g., accompanying to health appointments), shared quality time (e.g., traveling far for a birthday or wedding), emotional support (e.g., joining for a mental health walk), and the significance of non-romantic affection. Romantic partners were also frequently mentioned, with romantic relationships and passionate scenarios often being the first examples participants raised when describing their general understanding of love.

When prompted about loving experiences with less close relational ties like acquaintances and strangers, participants were able to provide many detailed and valuable examples. These included welcoming interactions with neighbors, coworkers noticing distress, and strangers offering unexpected help. For instance, one participant recalled forgetting her bus pass and being unexpectedly comforted by a stranger: And he [the driver] told me no, to get off the bus. Then, the lady behind me said, "No, don't worry, I'll pay for it." And she was trying to calm me down all the time because I was nervous and telling her that I was going to pay her back in cash and so on. But she kept trying to calm me down; she gave me a hug, and she talked to me….-Female, 21 years old Many of the loving accounts were also described without any relational specificity, focusing more on the scenario or behavior itself rather than on who it occurred with. Participants frequently specified a loving scenario with the focus on the actions-such as Spaniards displaying love through giving other people kisses, offering help, showing affection, or expressing genuine concern-indicating that these examples can be applied in any relationship.

Co-Occurrence of Relational Ties and Everyday Love Experiences

We conducted a co-occurrence analysis to examine how frequently each everyday love scenario appeared alongside different relational ties (see Figure 1). Overall, family and romantic partners were the most frequent relational contexts in which participants described feeling loved, followed by friends, with relatively fewer mentions of acquaintances and strangers. This pattern further highlights the centrality of close relationships in Spaniards' everyday experiences of love.

Distinct patterns also emerged across themes. Family references most often co-occurred with scenarios involving offering or giving help, support during a hard time, guidance or advice, and caring when sick, reflecting love as dependable care and instrumental support. Romantic partners were closely linked with unconditional acceptance, spending quality time, and physical affection, underscoring intimacy and mutual responsiveness as hallmarks of romantic love.

Mentions of friends clustered around sharing meals, opening up about personal problems, and insisting on spending time together, suggesting companionship and reciprocity as defining features of friendship love. Mentions of acquaintances and strangers were rare, but when they occurred, they typically involved small gestures of kindness, such as smiling warmly or offering unexpected help, indicating that even brief encounters can convey a sense of warmth, connection, and love.

Across relational contexts, support during a hard time emerged as the most pervasive expression of love, showing strong co-occurrence with family, friends, and romantic partners.

Together, these findings indicate that in Spain, love is relationally enacted through reliable support, shared time, and embodied care; these are behaviors that anchor emotional connection within everyday life.

Discussion

The results from Study 1 advance the understanding of love as a culturally embedded emotional experience. Consistent with prototype research demonstrating that laypeople organize love around relational and moral features such as care, trust, and responsiveness (Fehr, 1988 (Fehr, , 2019)), the Spanish narratives illustrate how these features are enacted through culturally patterned practices of presence and accompaniment. Prior U.S. FLQ research documented strong consensus around reliable care, emotional support, and affectionate connection (Heshmati et al., 2019); the present findings extend this work by showing how these experiences are organized across family, friendship, and romantic contexts in Spain. Because the FLQ-US did not examine love experiences within specific relational ties, whether a similar relational organization would emerge in U.S. samples remains an important direction for future cross-cultural research.

Moreover, when asked what makes Spanish expressions of love distinct from people in Spain, many participants described Spaniards as "affectionate," "touchy," and "passionate," highlighting how physical contact and emotional intensity are socially normative ways of showing care and love. This resonates with research on familism and relational interdependence in Mediterranean and Latin cultures (Calzada & Brooks, 2013), as well as broader anthropological accounts of high-contact, emotionally expressive societies. These findings also position passionate gestures (e.g., hugging, kissing, touching) as everyday expressions of warmth and love across relational contexts, not just limited to romantic settings.

In line with everyday love perspectives that conceptualize love as unfolding through ordinary micro-moments of connection (Fredrickson, 2013; Oravecz et al., 2020), participants located love in daily routines such as sharing meals, coexisting in the same space, and offering help without being asked. These findings also converge with research on gratitude, in which thoughtfulness and responsive, other-focused behavior serve as meaningful relational signals of care and connection (Algoe et al., 2008 (Algoe et al., , 2016)). Such behaviors may similarly communicate that a person is known, valued, and loved. Importantly, these expressions appear culturally patterned rather than universally generic. Consistent with scholarship describing Mediterranean societies as structured by dense interpersonal networks and relational interdependence, physical affection, food sharing, and even certain forms of directive care emerge as culturally legible markers of commitment and responsibility across relational contexts (Uskul et al., 2023). Together, these patterns suggest that love in Spain is sustained through everyday practices of accompaniment, embodied warmth, and communal engagement within a broader relational ecology.

Having first identified culturally meaningful scenarios of felt love through in-person fieldwork, coding, and co-occurrence analyses, the second step (Study 2) examined the extent to which these experiences reflect shared cultural beliefs about love among Spaniards

Study 2

Study 2 focused on eliciting layperson judgments about the everyday love experiences identified in Study 1 and their cultural meaning within contemporary Spain. We asked a large representative sample (N = 502) of people living in Spain to judge these 61 experiences in terms of how likely or unlikely they would make most people living in Spain feel loved. Our first aim was to determine whether responses reflected a single shared cultural consensus or multiple subcultures in understandings of everyday love. Our second aim was to characterize which experiences were more or less likely to elicit feelings of love and to examine how these evaluations varied across relational ties.

Following completion of Study 1 and development of the FLQ-Spain, we preregistered six hypotheses (relevant for the current paper) for Study 2 prior to quantitative data collection and analysis. Specifically, we hypothesized that (1) responses would reflect a single underlying cultural consensus; (2) scenarios involving support for needs and goals would be judged as likely to elicit love; (3) scenarios involving physical expressions of love would be judged as likely to elicit love; (4) family-related scenarios would be judged as likely to elicit love; (5) friend-related scenarios would be judged as likely to elicit love; and (6) scenarios involving controlling behavior and jealousy would be judged as unlikely to elicit love. For Hypotheses 2-5, "likely" was operationalized as a 95% credible interval for the consensus estimate above 50; for Hypothesis 6, "unlikely" was operationalized as a 95% credible interval below 50. Details for the preregistration for Study 2 and all the preregistered hypotheses are available in the Supplemental Materials and OSF.

Methods

Participants

We used the Qualtrics Participant Panels service (Qualtrics, 2024) to recruit participants to complete an online survey, which was composed of demographic questions as well as questions related to daily life experiences. We recruited a representative sample of 502 adults living in Spain in July 2025, with regional quotas approximately reflecting the national population distribution-30% Center (e.g., Comunidad de Madrid), 20% East (e.g., Comunidad Valenciana), 5% Northeast (e.g., Cataluña), 25% North (e.g., País Vasco), and 20% South (e.g., Andalucía). Additionally, 94% of the sample indicated White as their primary racial or ethnic group while 6% indicated Non-White or Mixed. Within the sample, there were also approximately 50% male and female participants. Anders et al. (2014) demonstrated adequate parameter recovery with as few as 20 participants and 30 items. We additionally conducted simulations with 100 participants and 61 items, which showed low bias and RMSE and approximately 95% coverage across 100 simulated datasets. These results indicated that a sample of 100 would be sufficient for reliable estimation; however, we recruited approximately 500 participants to better capture demographic and geographic diversity across Spain.

Procedure

Participants completed the FLQ-Spain, demographic questions, and additional trait measures not analyzed in the present study through an online Qualtrics survey. Qualtrics Panels service implemented standard data-quality procedures, including identity verification and removal of incomplete and unusually fast responses. We also implemented preregistered data exclusion processes and three embedded attention checks within the Qualtrics survey. Full details on these attention check processes are available in the online Supplemental Materials. The study was approved by the [BLINDED] IRB (STUDYXXXXXXXX).

Measure

Felt Love Questionnaire-Spain. The 61-item Felt-Love Questionnaire-Spain (FLQ-Spain) was developed through a comprehensive process of fieldwork and qualitative analysis in Study 1, to ensure cultural grounding in Spanish culture. This questionnaire consisted of a variety of scenarios that describe concrete situations in which people may feel loved. Derivation of experiences was primarily informed by the qualitative findings from Study 1, with additional reference to established scenarios from prior U.S.-based research on felt love (Oravecz et al., 2016; Heshmati et al., 2019). The complete list of the 61 items is available in Table S5 in the online Supplemental Materials.

Participants were first prompted with the instructions "We will ask you about your opinion on what most people think of as loving. As you read about the everyday life scenarios in the next pages, focus not on your own feelings, but on whether you believe these experiences would make MOST PEOPLE feel loved." Then, participants rated each scenario following the stem 'Most people feel loved when…' on a visual slider from 0 (Unlikely) to 100 (Likely), with the starting point set directly in the middle to reduce survey acquiescence (bias towards the positive). This was then repeated for all scenarios, yielding a response from each participant for each experience. Moreover, all 61 scenarios were presented in a random order for each participant in order to reduce ordering effects for item presentation.

The FLQ-Spain scenarios included specific relational ties for most of the scenarios, which could be categorized into: (1) stranger (e.g., "a person they don't know does something unexpectedly nice for them"); ( 2) acquaintance (e.g., "an acquaintance hugs them warmly," "a coworker celebrates their accomplishments"); (3) friend (e.g., "their friend insists on spending time with them"); ( 4) family (e.g., "their parent offers guidance on what is best for them," "their child is happy to spend time with them"); (5) romantic partner (e.g., "their partner wants to know where they are all the time"); (6) general, or non-specified ties (e.g., "someone shows genuine interest in what they think or do," "they cheer together at a sporting event"); and ( 7) other (e.g., "the sun is shining," "they feel one with the universe").

Analysis

We chose the Continuous Response Model (CRM; Anders et al., 2014) from the CCT framework to determine if the participants came to a single shared agreement on the experiences in everyday life that made most people feel loved, and at what degrees were the daily life experiences likely to make Spaniards feel loved. This model has been applied in previous studies where participants evaluated scenarios on a continuous response scale, such as the probability of future events and the importance of healthy eating and living practices (Anders et al., 2014).

For characterizing how much each experience would make Spaniards feel loved, the CRM analysis yielded estimates about the shared agreement on a scale of 0-100% likely (i.e., from very unlikely to very likely to elicit love), as well as a 95% probability interval around each estimate that allowed for comparisons across love experiences. When deriving these estimates, the analyses also took into account possible individual differences in the knowledge levels of each of the participants regarding the shared consensus on daily life signs of love. Furthermore, person-specific response tendencies were also considered, such as if an individual had a tendency to give 'extreme' ratings (the ends of the scale) or if they had some general tendencies towards rating experiences unloving versus loving. More details on the model are provided in Figures S1-S2 in the online Supplemental Materials.

General Discussion

Across two empirical studies, this sequential mixed-method research combined in-person interviews and focus groups with Bayesian psychometric modeling to examine shared understandings of everyday love in Spain. The exploratory qualitative phase generated 61 culturally grounded experiences that informed the FLQ-Spain and subsequent Study 2 hypotheses, which were preregistered after Study 1 completion and before Study 2 quantitative data collection and analysis. Cultural Consensus Theory modeling then demonstrated shared agreement across these experiences. From a social psychological perspective, these findings show how love is recognized through shared meanings by laypeople, relational schemas, and culturally-patterned everyday interactions. Together, these studies offer an integrated account of love as both lived experience and shared cultural knowledge.

Existence of a Strong Cultural Consensus on Love

The single-culture consensus indicates that Spaniards share a coherent cultural schema for recognizing how love unfolds in everyday life. Within this shared cultural model, instrumental support and dependability emerged as central components. Scenarios describing receiving support during a difficult time, being cared for when sick, and selfless giving reflect a model grounded in reliable care and mutual responsibility. These experiences were also among the most highly endorsed in prior U.S.-based research (Heshmati et al., 2019), suggesting that reliable care and instrumental support in times of need may represent a broadly shared foundation of felt love rather than a culturally specific feature. This suggests that loving experiences function not only as connection, but as core mechanisms of coping and social support. Everyday supportive behaviors are known to buffer stress and promote resilience (Feeney & Collins, 2015).

Spending time with others underscored the cultural value placed on shared presence in Spain. Across relational ties, prioritizing time together was perceived as loving. This pattern aligns with research linking frequent, high-quality interactions to well-being (Ryff & Singer, 2008) and resonates with Spain's cultural emphasis on convivencia, or living and connecting together in everyday life.

The strong consensus that explicit verbal affirmations, such as "parents say 'I love you'", are loving, suggests that direct verbal expression occupies a central place in how love is recognized in this cultural context. Direct verbal expressions complement embodied affection rather than replace it: saying "I love you" functions as an extension of emotional expressiveness, a way to make felt connection visible and reaffirm relational closeness. Notably, this item was also among the most strongly endorsed expressions of felt love in prior U.S.-based research (Heshmati et al., 2019), suggesting that explicit verbal affirmation may be part of a relational vocabulary shared across cultural contexts rather than a feature unique to this sample. At the same time, the consistency with which verbal affirmations were endorsed alongside embodied and presence-based experiences (e.g., spending time together, physical closeness) points to a cultural model in which verbal and nonverbal expressions of love operate together rather than as substitutes for one another (Rojas, 2024).

The Importance of Family Ties for Experiencing Love in Spain

Within the shared cultural model, family emerged as the strongest and most consistent source of love. Both qualitative and quantitative results converged on the centrality of parentchild and intergenerational bonds as the primary context for felt love. Scenarios such as "parents say 'I love you,'" "family supports them in a difficult time," and "child is happy to spend time with them" were highly endorsed. While the FLQ-US did not have most of its items framed around specific relational ties, comparable family-related experiences (e.g., a child snuggling up to them, spending time with family) also received high endorsement in prior studies (see, e.g., Heshmati et al., 2019; Ellis et al., 2024), suggesting that centrality of family to felt love may not be unique to this sample. At the same time, the family-related scenarios with a high consensus in the present study are diverse in experiences, capturing the full range of behaviors through which familism is expressed. These include verbal expressions of love, receiving support and guidance, providing care, and spending joyful time together. Notably, family-based directive and involvement behaviors (e.g., a parent insisting on spending time together, wanting to know a child's whereabouts) were also rated as moderately to highly loving (see Figure 4), suggesting that within this cultural model, parental guidance and involvement are themselves read as expressions of care rather than as constraints on autonomy. The strong agreement that love is felt when a child willingly spends joyful time with a parent further suggests that parental love in this sample is grounded in mutual affective engagement rather than obligation alone, with love reinforced through shared positive affect rather than duty (Fernández et al., 2005).

Romantic Relationships: Agreement on Boundaries of Love

Romantic relationships in Spain appear to be embedded within the same broader cultural model emphasizing security and compassion. The strongest romantic indicator of feeling loved, "they feel unconditionally accepted by their partner," reflects companionate love rather than romantic intensity alone (Fehr, 1994). This suggests that romantic relationships in Spain do not have a distinct cultural script characterized by intensity and romance, but instead align with the same relational principles that structure love in family and friendship contexts. Consistent with this interpretation, participants strongly agreed that "their partner shows respect for their space or time" makes people feel loved, reinforcing that healthy romantic love is understood as supportive rather than intrusive (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2010).

At the same time, the data reveal a clear cultural boundary between caring involvement and controlling behavior. Scenarios involving jealousy and surveillance such as "their partner wants to know where they are all the time" and "their partner shows signs of jealousy" were among the least likely to be perceived as loving. This reaffirms the compassionate nature of love in everyday life for people in Spain as these controlling behaviors are antithetical to the trusting and accepting characteristics of compassionate love (Goetz et al., 2010; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2017; Fehr et al., 2014). Importantly, not all directive behaviors were evaluated negatively.

Scenarios in which their friend/parent/partner "insists on spending time with them," were generally perceived as loving. This pattern likely reflects cultural differences in how "insistence" is interpreted in relation to autonomy and relational identity. Within an interdependent selfconstrual, the self is defined largely through close relationships rather than individual independence (Markus & Kitayama, 1991). Accordingly, in the Spanish cultural context, such behaviors may signal a strong desire for closeness and connection rather than an infringement on personal autonomy. This contrasts somewhat with prior U.S.-based research, where a comparable "insistence" item was rated as more neutral with respect to felt love (Heshmati et al., 2019); given differences in item wording and relational specificity across studies, this contrast should be interpreted with caution, but it is consistent with the possibility that insistence carries different relational meaning across cultural contexts varying in interdependence.

Similarly, the two directive scenarios that were also evaluated as loving, "their partner is protective of them" and "their partner tells them how to live a healthier life," appear to be interpreted as genuine care and investment in a loved one's well-being. This interpretation aligns with cultural models of familism, in which guidance, protection, and involvement are normative ways of expressing concern and responsibility toward close others (Marin & Marin, 1991). The finding that a partner's encouragement toward healthier behavior was perceived as loving diverged from prior U.S.-based findings, where a comparable item was rated as more neutral (Heshmati et al., 2019); however, slight differences in item phrasing across studies limit the inferences we can draw from this comparison.

The strong consensus on non-romantic, compassionate scenarios and the emphasis on unconditional acceptance in romantic relationships demonstrate that the Spanish love prototype prioritizes security, reliability, and emotional safety over passionate intensity. This pattern aligns with prototype research identifying acceptance and care as central features of love (Fehr, 1994 (Fehr, , 2006)).

Love Beyond Close Human Ties

Beyond close human ties, the consensus results show that Spaniards also locate "felt love" in a small set of nontraditional sources (e.g., pets, nature, etc.). Consensus on these experiences varied. The clearest non-human exception was: "their pets are happy to see them"

which received one of the highest consensus estimates across all scenarios, indicating that Spaniards widely recognize affectionate responsiveness from pets as a prototypical loving experience. This finding aligns with the broader pattern observed throughout the FLQ-Spain, in which love is defined less by abstract meaning and more by moments of mutual orientation, warmth, and responsiveness, core features of positivity resonance (Fredrickson, 2013).

Experiences involving nature occupied an intermediate position. Feeling close to nature or noticing that "the sun is shining" were viewed as moderately likely to elicit feelings of love, indicating that embodied positive states and environmental comfort can support feelings of warmth and well-being, but are not sufficient on their own to constitute love. A similar pattern was observed in prior U.S.-based research, where experiential and ambient scenarios, including feeling close to nature, also received relatively lower endorsement compared to interpersonally grounded experiences (Heshmati et al., 2019). This cross-context consistency suggests that the interpersonal contingency of felt love may be a feature that extends beyond the Spanish cultural context. Prior research shows that contact with nature reliably enhances positive affect and connectedness (Capaldi et al., 2014), yet these effects lack the interpersonal contingency that characterizes love as conceptualized in the present framework.

Additionally, the national-anthem scenario was closest to neutral and also showed the largest variability, implying that national symbolism may reliably evoke collective emotion for some, but it does not function as a culturally consensual "love cue" in the same way as care, closeness, and responsive relationship moments (Sullivan, 2018).

Scenarios referencing shared religious/spiritual belief contexts (e.g., feeling connected to a higher being, feeling one with the universe) showed only moderate endorsement and noticeably higher dispersion. From a CCT perspective, this pattern suggests that spiritual and religious experiences represent a subcultural rather than population-wide pathway to felt love in Spain.

Spain is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in religious identification and practice-53% of Spaniards declaring themselves as Catholic and only 17% as practicing (Centro de Investigaciones Sociológicas, 2025)-such that spiritual cues may be meaningful for "some people" but are not part of the shared default understanding of what makes "most people" feel loved. Notably, experiences involving shared belief systems-such as being surrounded by others who share the same religious or spiritual beliefs-did not elicit strong agreement as loving experiences. This suggests that shared identity or collective meaning, in the absence of direct emotional exchange, is not central to how love is recognized in everyday life in Spain.

Finally, self-directed experiences further clarify this boundary. Although participants showed relatively high agreement that people feel loved when "they feel love towards themselves," this scenario did not reach the levels observed for relationally responsive experiences. This pattern suggests that self-love is acknowledged as emotionally meaningful, but is not treated as equivalent to being loved by another. In line with theories of positivity resonance (Fredrickson 2013 (Fredrickson , 2016)), love in Spain appears to be understood primarily as a relational process, one that is co-created through mutual presence, responsiveness, and care, rather than as a purely intrapersonal state.

Theoretical and Methodological Implications

From a social psychological perspective, these findings contribute to understanding love as a socially recognized and relationally situated category of experience. Love is not merely an individual feeling or internal state, but a relational phenomenon shaped by culturally shared expectations about how care, connection, and responsiveness are enacted in everyday life.

Building on prototype perspectives of love that show that love is primarily composed of key abstract features of care, affection and trust (Fehr, 1988 (Fehr, , 2019;; Fehr & Russell, 1991), the present findings show that love can be recognized through concrete everyday scripts and behaviors involving instrumental support, shared quality time, verbal and physical expressions, genuine interest, and relational role expectations. Also, the findings reveal unique patterns of interpersonal relatedness for the culture in Spain, which aligns with social-cognitive perspectives for how certain relationships and interaction patterns can influence people's relational schemas (Holmes, 2000). More broadly, this project serves as a proof of concept for integrating culturally grounded qualitative research with quantitative consensus estimation in the study of emotions. By grounding measurement development in lived experience and then formally testing shared understanding at the population level, this approach bridges a longstanding divide between qualitative and quantitative traditions in emotion and social psychological science. Applied to love, a construct often treated as either deeply personal or theoretically diffuse, this mixedmethod framework demonstrates how culturally embedded emotional processes can be empirically specified, compared across groups, and studied as shared social phenomena rather than solely private states.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of the present research should be acknowledged alongside directions for future work. First, the qualitative phase was conducted primarily in an urban setting and may not capture regionally specific expressions of love. Although participants represented diverse ages, socioeconomic backgrounds, and relational experiences, future research should extend qualitative fieldwork to rural and regionally distinct communities. Importantly, Study 2 tested the resulting scenarios in a nationally representative sample across Spain.

Second, CCT assesses shared cultural knowledge rather than individuals' personal or momentary experiences of love. Future research could integrate diary or experience-sampling methods to examine how culturally shared love cues translate into lived experiences across time and contexts, and parallel FLQ instruments could test the model cross-culturally. In addition, cross-cultural comparisons using parallel FLQ instruments could test whether the Spanish cultural model of love, centered on relational responsiveness, warmth, and dependable care, differs from models observed in more individualistic or lower-contact cultural contexts.

Third, although the FLQ-Spain scenarios were developed to capture a wide range of everyday love experiences, the final set necessarily represents a subset of the scenarios articulated during the qualitative phase. Scenario selection was constrained by conceptual overlap and practical considerations related to questionnaire length and participant burden, which required prioritizing the most salient and broadly interpretable experiences. As with any experience-based measure, this process may privilege more observable or interactional expressions of love over subtler or context-dependent forms. Future research could expand or rotate item pools or incorporate complementary elicitation approaches (e.g., narrative vignettes or visual prompts) to further explore how love is recognized in more ambiguous contexts while remaining grounded in culturally shared understandings.

Conclusion

Across two studies, this research demonstrates that love in everyday life in Spain is not merely an individual feeling but a shared cultural understanding of how connection is enacted.

Using qualitative fieldwork and Bayesian Cultural Consensus Theory, we showed that Spaniards converge on a single cultural model of what makes "most people" feel loved. This shared model is centered on relational responsiveness, dependable care, and shared presence. Love is most clearly recognized in moments of emotional orientation, supported in times of need, and invited into warm, responsive interaction. These findings align with conceptualizations of love as a dynamic, co-created process, emerging through micro-moments of mutual care and emotional synchrony (Fredrickson, 2013; Oravecz et al., 2021 , Williams et al., 2025a).

At the same time, the findings reveal clear cultural boundaries around what does not count as love. Symbolic, identity-based, or meaning-oriented experiences (e.g., national symbolism or spiritual connection) were not widely recognized as loving. Similarly, controlling or possessive behaviors were largely rejected, highlighting that love in Spain is grounded in respect and trust rather than surveillance or obligation. By grounding this work outside of the United States, the present research extends a literature on love that has been largely shaped by North American samples and assumptions. Importantly, the findings reveal substantial convergence with prior U.S.-based research: the core of the Spanish consensus (reliable care, support during difficulty, shared presence, and explicit verbal affirmation) closely mirrors the most highly endorsed experiences of felt love documented in the U.S. (Heshmati et al., 2019).

This convergence itself is a meaningful finding, suggesting that certain expressions of felt love are shared between these two cultural contexts. At the same time, the present research makes a distinct contribution by documenting how this shared core is organized relationally in Spain, differentiated across interpersonal ties, and shaped by cultural norms around involvement, guidance, and interdependence, and by demonstrating that the cultural consensus framework can be successfully extended beyond North American contexts.

More broadly, these findings advance social psychological theory by positioning everyday love not merely as an individual emotion or dyadic relationship property, but as a form of shared cultural knowledge that can be formally identified, mapped, and compared across societies.

Studying love as shared relational meaning requires methods that capture both lived experience and cultural coherence. CCT provides this framework by testing whether a shared cultural model exists and how strongly specific experiences are embedded within it. In the present studies, the identification of a one-culture solution provides empirical evidence that everyday experiences of love in Spain are organized around a shared cultural schema, rather than fragmented individual interpretations. This moves the study of love beyond subjective reports toward a formal assessment of collectively held cultural knowledge. Methodologically, the use of the Bayesian Continuous Response Model (CRM; Anders et al., 2014) represents a significant advance for research on shared relational schemas. Unlike traditional consensus approaches, the CRM accommodates continuous judgments, estimates uncertainty around each scenario, and separates shared cultural knowledge from individual-level variability in response tendencies and cultural competence. This distinction is particularly important for emotional constructs such as love, where personal experiences may vary widely even when underlying cultural beliefs are shared. The CRM therefore allows researchers to model consensus with greater precision while preserving meaningful individual differences.
