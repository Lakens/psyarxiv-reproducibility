Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Others' affective reactions can be the only available information about how good an outcome might be, yet little is known about how people use them to inform their own valuation. Across three preregistered experiments, we tested how observers use others' reactions to value unseen lottery outcomes, and whether social group membership changes this affect-based value inference. Participants observed facial reactions of "demonstrators" to hidden lottery outcomes, reported how good they believed the demonstrator felt about the outcome (inferred affect), and stated their incentive-compatible willingness to pay (WTP) for these lotteries. In Experiment 1 (N = 416), happy relative to neutral expressions increased both inferred affect and WTP, and inferred affect accounted for most of the valuation difference. Experiment 2 (N = 1,127; informed by piloting, N = 419) introduced group membership via political agreement. In-group demonstrators were inferred to feel slightly better, and happy expressions increased WTP more when they came from in-group than from out-group demonstrators. Experiment 3 (N = 1,461) replicated these findings and added angry expressions, which reduced WTP without significant group differences. Across Experiments 2 and 3, group differences in reported inferred affect partially accounted for the group differences in valuation. Our findings extend affect's role as a social signal to economic valuation and show that social group membership changes how others' reactions inform observers' valuation of unseen outcomes. Affect may thus be one route by which preferences spread differently within identity-aligned groups.

Group Membership Changes How Others' Affect Informs Observers' Valuation of

Unseen Outcomes Imagine passing a restaurant you have never tried and catching the smile of someone leaving. Their smile alone might leave you more inclined to try the restaurant yourself. Others' affective reactions can be the only available source of information about the value of an outcome (e.g., food or reward), and yet relatively little is known about how people use others' affective reactions to inform their own valuation when no alternative source of information is available. In parallel, people do not treat all social sources of information equally, frequently discriminating based on coalitions and group identities. Imagine that the person leaving is someone you recognize as similar to you, or someone who clearly is not. Would their perceived similarity or group membership change your valuation of their outcomes?

Here we induce these considerations to intersect, allowing us to ask both how others' affective expressions inform observers' subjective valuation of outcomes, and whether social group membership changes how these expressions inform valuation. To address these questions, we combine approaches from affective science, social cognition, and behavioral economics to test whether observers use others' facial expressions to inform their subjective valuation. We measure both how good observers think another person feels about their outcome (inferred affect) and how much observers are willing to pay for that outcome. Inferred-affect ratings serve to approximate how observers interpret affective reactions, allowing us to probe associations between this form of social inference and observers' own valuation of outcomes.

Others' Affective Expressions Convey Information

A large body of work demonstrates that others' affective expressions serve as social signals that help observers to guide their own judgments and behavior (Hareli & Hess, 2012; Keltner et al., 2019; Keltner & Haidt, 1999; Van Kleef, 2009; Van Kleef & Côté, 2022). Observers use others' emotional reactions to infer whether situations are cooperative or competitive (Van Doorn et al., 2012), whether norms have been violated (Hareli et al., 2013; Heerdink et al., 2019) and how to respond to unfamiliar objects or situations (Stenberg & Forslund, 2020; Walle et al., 2017). Infants use caregivers' expressions toward novel objects to decide whether to approach or avoid them (Klinnert et al., 1986). Similarly, adults are more likely to avoid objects that they have previously observed paired with fearful rather than happy expressions (Meffert et al., 2015).

Together, this work establishes that affective expressions signal evaluative information that observers can use to guide their own behavior in novel contexts. But do affective expressions also inform observers' formally measured valuation of unseen outcomes, beyond such measures of approach and avoidance?

Affective Expressions as a Signal for Subjective Valuation

Subjective value refers to how much an option is worth to an individual and is central to theories of learning and choice (Juechems & Summerfield, 2019; Rangel et al., 2008). In reinforcement learning, subjective value (as estimated future reward) allows organisms to learn from experience and choose options that maximize expected reward (Juechems & Summerfield, 2019; Polanía et al., 2019; Rangel et al., 2008). In the study of economic choice, subjective value (commonly measured via willingness to pay [WTP]; Becker et al., 1964) guides preferences between competing alternatives (Kable & Glimcher, 2007; Padoa-Schioppa & Assad, 2006; von Neumann et al., 1944). By focusing on subjective value measured as WTP, the present investigation extends beyond asking whether observed affect influences behavior and asks instead whether observed affect can serve as a signal that acts on the underlying valuation.

Why might another person's affective reaction provide information relevant to observers' own subjective valuation of an unseen outcome? Work on experienced affect suggests that affective reactions compactly encode information relevant to valuation. Positive outcomes generally elicit more positive affective reactions than negative outcomes (Forbes & Bennett, 2024; Mellers et al., 1999; Parr et al., 2026; Shenhav, 2024). Moreover, how good someone feels about an outcome (i.e., valence) tracks with how that outcome compares to their expectations (Chew et al., 2021; Rutledge et al., 2014), effectively approximating their subjective valuation of that reward (Vollberg & Sander, 2024). Observed affect may therefore provide observers with usable information not only about an objective outcome value, but about how that value was subjectively experienced and evaluated by another person, or, in other words, information about objective outcomes through another person's subjective lens. Whether observers rely on this information may well depend on the extent to which they expect the other person's preferences to align with their own. Such expectations could be cued by shared group identity, which people can infer based on preference similarity in other domains (see next section ; Gershman & Cikara, 2020; Lau et al., 2018).

Observers may also use others' affective reactions to infer value when the outcome that elicited the reactions is hidden. To do so, observers may infer how the demonstrator feels about the experienced outcome (which people readily infer from facial information; Frank & Stennett, 2001; Klingner & Guntinas-Lichius, 2023; Lange et al., 2022), and draw on that inferred affective state when valuing the outcome for themselves. Put simply, an outcome that makes another person feel good may also be good for them. We refer to the use of another person's affective reaction to infer the value of outcomes one has not personally experienced as affect-based value inference. The first aim of the present work is to establish whether observers can use demonstrators' facial expressions to infer how good the demonstrator feels about a hidden outcome, in turn, adjust their own formally measured valuation of that outcome, and to probe how much of this valuation can be accounted for by inferred affect.

Intergroup Biases in Value Inference

People categorize others into social groups with shared social identity based on shared preferences (Gershman & Cikara, 2020), including political opinions (Lau et al., 2018), and favor members of their own social group (in-group; Amodio & Cikara, 2021; Hewstone et al., 2002; Tajfel, 1970). Such in-group favoritism also extends to learning. Both adults (Boeltzig et al., 2023; Golkar et al., 2015; Golkar & Olsson, 2017; Kang et al., 2021) and children (Buttelmann et al., 2013; Howard et al., 2015) learn more readily from in-group than out-group members. For example, people observing the actions and outcomes of others in a reinforcement learning task learn less from individuals who oppose (i.e., outgroup) rather than share their political views (i.e., in-group members; Kang et al., 2021). Importantly, this deficit is explained by impaired learning from out-group actions, whereas learning from out-group outcomes remains intact, suggesting that group membership can selectively affect specific channels of social information when observers learn what value to expect from observing others.

Group membership can induce biases in domains unrelated to the content of the group identity and distort valuation and value updating. People have been shown to learn from politically like-minded others, even when tasks are unrelated to politics (Marks et al., 2019). Similarly, social stereotypes distort valuation and value updating during repeated social interactions. Even when objective rewards are equivalent across interaction partners, people favor positively over negatively stereotyped group members and incorporate positive feedback from negatively stereotyped group members less strongly into their preferences (Schultner, Lindström, et al., 2024; Schultner, Stillerman, et al., 2024). In other words, social group information can distort both what value observers expect from others and how new information updates those expectations.

Group membership may also change how rewarding others' outcomes are for observers. Observing others receive rewards engages reward-related processing (Lockwood et al., 2015), and this signature of vicarious reward is more pronounced for others whom observers perceive as sharing their attitudes and values (Mobbs et al., 2009).

These lines of work show that group membership can influence how people learn from and evaluate information provided by others. Most of the previous work, however, has focused primarily on actions and outcomes as social information. Here, we ask whether ingroup favoritism also manifests in value inferences from observing affective reactions of others, particularly to outcomes that are hidden. This question is most relevant for situations in which affective expressions are observable but objective outcomes are not.

How could group membership influence affect-based value inference? Two nonmutually exclusive possibilities follow from the reasoning above. First, observers may infer different affect from the same expression and value the outcome accordingly. Second, group membership may change how the inferred information informs observers' own valuation, for example because a positive reaction from someone who shares one's preferences says more about how much one would value the outcome oneself. By measuring inferred affect, we can probe the first possibility directly and ask how much of the group effect on valuation (if any) can be accounted for by inferred affect. A group effect on valuation that remains after accounting for inferred affect would point to the second possibility (without confirming it). The second aim of the present work is therefore to test whether social group membership influences affect-based value inference and, if so, to describe how its effects on inferred affect and valuation are related.

Overview of the Present Work

Experiment 1 (N = 416) tested whether observers can use others' affective reactions to hidden outcomes to inform their subjective valuation of those outcomes. Participants observed a demonstrator reacting to two hidden lottery outcomes with either a neutral or happy facial expression (Ma et al., 2015), reported how positively they inferred the demonstrator to have felt about the outcome, and reported their WTP for a ticket to the lottery using an incentive-compatible Becker-DeGroot-Marschak procedure (BDM; Becker et al., 1964). We hypothesized that happy (vs. neutral) expressions would lead observers to infer more positive affect (H1) and higher WTP (H2), and probed how much of the effect of expression on WTP could be accounted for by inferred affect.

Experiment 2 (N = 1,127) built on extensive piloting (N = 419; see Supplementary Materials) to examine whether and how affect-based value inference is influenced by social group membership. Participants observed both an in-group and out-group demonstrator, operationalized via political agreement (Gershman & Cikara, 2020; Kang et al., 2021), reacting to two hidden lottery outcomes each with a neutral and a happy expression. We hypothesized that in-group demonstrators would be inferred to feel more positive than outgroup demonstrators at the same expression level (H3), and that happy expressions would increase WTP more when coming from in-group compared to out-group demonstrators (H4). We further examined how much of the in-group effect on WTP could be accounted for by the in-group effect on inferred affect.

Experiment 3 (N = 1,461) extended the previous experiments by testing how people use negative reactions (i.e., anger) to inform their subjective valuation of unseen outcomes, and whether social group membership influences affect-based value inferences from such negative expressions. Each participant observed two in-group and two out-group demonstrators reacting with neutral, happy, and angry expressions. We tested whether Experiment 2's findings replicated in this setting and whether group membership also changed the effect of angry expressions on WTP.

Experiment 1: Establishing Affect-Based Value Inference From Facial Expressions

Experiment 1 tested whether observers can use demonstrators' facial expressions to infer how positively the demonstrator feels about a hidden lottery outcome, and whether these expressions and inferences about them influence the observer's own subjective valuation for a ticket to that lottery measured via WTP (Becker et al., 1964). Each participant observed one demonstrator reacting to two lottery outcomes with either a neutral or happy facial expression.

Method Participants

We recruited 420 participants via Prolific. Participants' first language had to be English and their current country of residence had to be the United States. Participants were compensated at a base rate of £9 per hour and earned an additional bonus based on their WTP responses (see Design and Procedure). Participants who did not provide informed consent or failed the attention check were excluded (n = 4), resulting in a final sample of N = 416 participants (n = 217 female, n = 197 male, n = 2 other; age: M = 42.62, SD = 14.20, range = 18-80). Data were collected on March 18, 2026. The design, hypotheses, and analysis plan were preregistered prior to data collection.

Sample size was determined a priori via Monte Carlo power simulation targeting H2 (the effect of expression valence on WTP). Because no prior estimate was available, simulations were based on synthetic data with a smallest effect of interest of 0.5 pence (WTP difference between happy and neutral expressions), and we targeted the conventional 80% power. The simulation model included random intercepts for participant and demonstrator, as preregistered (see Supplementary Materials for details). With 1,000 iterations per candidate sample size, simulations indicated that N = 400 participants would yield approximately 80% power to detect this effect at α = .05. We recruited 420 participants to account for potential exclusions.

Stimulus Selection From Chicago Face Database

Demonstrator faces were selected from the Chicago Face Database (CFD; Ma et al., 2015). To be suitable for the experimental design, models were required to have a neutral, a happy closed-mouth, and (for Experiment 3) an angry expression available, which restricted the selection pool to Black Female, Black Male, White Female and White Male models.

We used a three-stage selection procedure to select three models from each of these four groups: First, eligibility filtering retained only models with the requisite expression sets and unambiguous gender and ethnicity classifications, operationalized as classification probabilities of at least .95 on the relevant CFD norming variables (FemaleProb or MaleProb ≥ .95; BlackProb or WhiteProb ≥ .95). Second, based on the CFD norming data, models rated more than 0.5 SD above their group mean on "unusualness" and 0.5 SD below their group mean on "prototypicality" were excluded. Third, the remaining models were matched across groups on thirteen CFD norming dimensions: perceived age, attractiveness, trustworthiness, dominance, threat, babyfacedness, suitability as a research stimulus, and the perceived intensity of six emotions on the neutral face (happy, sad, angry, afraid, disgusted, surprised). Each variable was z-scored across the full filtered dataset, and the Euclidean distance from each model to the cross-group centroid was computed. Within each group, models were ranked by their distance to this common centroid (Supplementary Materials Table S4). In Experiment 1, the closest model of each group served as the demonstrator for the main trials and the neutral expression of the second-closest model as the example-trial stimulus.

Design and Procedure

Each participant observed a single demonstrator displaying both a neutral and a happy closed-mouth expression across two total trials (within-participants manipulation of expression valence). On each trial, participants rated how good they thought the demonstrator felt about a lottery outcome and how much they would be willing to pay for a ticket to that lottery. WTP responses were incentivized using a BDM procedure (Becker et al., 1964) to ensure participants reported their true valuation of the ticket.

After providing informed consent and completing a general attention-check item ("How many times have you personally visited the planet Mars?" ; single-choice options 0-9), participants were introduced to the main task. Participants were told that they would observe a person reacting to outcomes from different lotteries paying between 1 and 10 pence (i.e., £0.01 to £0.10), with some lotteries paying more than others. The instructions read: "In each round, you will see the person's facial reaction to their lottery outcome. Your task is to use this information to evaluate the lottery for yourself." No other information about the demonstrator or the purpose of their reaction was provided. Based on this information, participants were instructed to provide two ratings on each trial: a) how good they believe the person feels about their outcome (inferred affect), and b) how much they would be willing to pay for a ticket to that lottery (WTP). The BDM procedure was then explained: At the end of the experiment, one trial would be randomly selected and a random ticket price between 1 and 10 pence would be drawn. If the participant's WTP for that trial met or exceeded the drawn price, the participant would pay the drawn price from their 10 pence budget and receive the lottery's payout and their remaining budget as a bonus. If the participant's WTP was below the drawn price, no transaction would occur and they would receive their bidding budget of 10 pence as a bonus. Participants then completed an example trial followed by comprehension check questions, which had to be repeated until answered correctly before the main task began.

Each participant was randomly assigned to view a demonstrator from one of the four demonstrator groups described above, and the order in which the two expressions were presented was randomized across participants. On each trial, participants were shown a static image of the demonstrator displaying either a neutral or happy closed-mouth expression alongside both rating questions (Figure 1). Affect was rated on a 0-100 visual analogue slider anchored at 0 ("neutral", indicated by a flat-mouth smiley) and 100 ("very good", indicated by a smiling smiley) with the affect slider initialized at the midpoint. The currently selected slider value was displayed as a number above the slider. WTP was collected as a text-box response (0-10 pence as whole numbers) to avoid carry-over effects from the affect rating. The image of the facial expression and both questions remained on screen until participants proceeded to the next page. After completing both trials, participants reported which of the two lotteries they believed had paid more (first, second, both the same, unsure), completed demographic questions and open response questions about their experience in the study. Finally, participants were shown the outcome of the BDM procedure and their resulting bonus. Note that WTP was measured in pence to keep it consistent with the currency of participants' base payment and bonuses on Prolific, so that bids corresponded exactly to the amounts at stake. Analysis

All hypotheses were preregistered and tested using linear mixed-effects models fit with the lme4 package in R (Bates et al., 2015). Degrees of freedom were approximated with lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) and are reported rounded down to integers.

In all experiments, random-effects structures were selected by, starting from the most complex candidate structure, simplifying the structure until it converged, was not singular, and fit the data better than the next simpler structure (likelihood-ratio test, p < .20; Matuschek et al., 2017). This rule deviates from the preregistered random intercepts for participant and demonstrator group in Experiments 1 and 2. In Experiment 1, with two trials per participant, only random intercepts could be estimated; the demonstrator intercept was retained for inferred affect but not for WTP. All selected structures are listed in the Supplementary Materials (Table S2). Note that demonstrator identity and demonstrator group are equivalent in Experiment 1, since each race and gender combination was represented by a single Chicago Face Database model. All preregistered main hypothesis tests (H1-H4) led to the same conclusions under the preregistered structure (Table S3); where a follow-up analysis depended on the structure, we report this with the respective result.

We report unstandardized estimates with 95% confidence intervals. For WTP, we additionally report effects as a percentage of the response scale (0-10 pence in Experiments 1 and 2; 0-15 pence in Experiment 3) for comparability across studies.

Results

H1 | Observers Attribute More Positive Affect to Happy Expressions Than Neutral Expressions

As predicted, participants rated happy expressions as corresponding to more positive feelings than neutral expressions, intercept = 26.08, b = 54.90, SE = 1.11, 95% CI [52.71, 57.09], t(414) = 49.30, p < .001 (see Figure 1D). In other words, observers readily decoded the affective valence of demonstrators' facial expressions, confirming the stimuli are suitable for probing effects on subjective valuation.

H2 | Observers Are Willing to Pay More for Lotteries Associated With Happy Expressions

As predicted, participants were willing to pay more for tickets to lotteries associated with happy expressions than for lotteries associated with neutral expressions, intercept = 2.78, b = 3.41, SE = 0.10, 95% CI [3.22, 3.60], t(414) = 35.29, p < .001, corresponding to 34.1% of the response scale (see Figure 1E). Consistent with this pattern, 94.2% of participants reported the lottery associated with the happy expression to be the higherpaying lottery. Demonstrators' affective reactions can thus serve as signals that act on observers' formally measured valuation of hidden outcomes.

Can Inferred Affect Account for the Expression Effect on WTP?

Inferred affect was associated with WTP over and above expression valence. Each additional rating point of inferred affect was associated with 0.045 pence higher WTP (i.e., about 0.45 pence per 10 points), b = 0.045, SE = 0.003, 95% CI [0.039, 0.051], t(749) = 14.24, p < .001. The indirect effect via inferred affect was aE × b = 2.46 pence, 95% CI [2.10, 2.81], corresponding to 72% of the total effect (see Figure 1F). Inferred affect thus accounted for most of the expression effect on WTP, consistent with the idea that observers value an outcome to a substantial degree according to how good they infer the demonstrator to feel about it.

Discussion

Experiment 1 showed that observers used demonstrators' facial expressions to infer how good the demonstrator felt about a hidden outcome and adjusted their incentivecompatible valuation of that outcome accordingly, with inferred affect accounting for most of this effect (72%). We treat inferred affect-observers' reported inference about how good the demonstrator feels about the outcome-as a proxy for their interpretation of the demonstrator's affective response. However, that does not mean that inferred affect captures all the information observers extract from an expression. Observers may, for instance, also form inferences about the demonstrator (Dashti et al., 2026) or about how valuable the outcome was for the demonstrator. The latter inferences about value inherently overlap with inferred affect to the extent that affective valence reflects subjective value (Shenhav, 2024; Vollberg & Sander, 2024). Moreover, a single rating measures the inference with noise, and WTP may not change linearly with inferred affect. It is therefore not surprising that inferred affect did not account for the entire expression effect. That said, our results show that a single proxy of how good observers interpreted the demonstrator to feel predicted how they valued the outcome themselves, even beyond the expression category.

In Experiment 1, the demonstrators were anonymous strangers with no further information provided about them. Often, however, we know more about the people whose reactions we observe, such as whether they share our views or not. Experiment 2 therefore asked whether value inference from affective reactions changes with political similarity between participants and demonstrators.

Experiment 2: Group Membership Influences Affect-Based Value Inference From

Happy Facial Expressions Experiment 2 introduced political group-related information by telling participants whether the observed demonstrators shared their views. Before observing each demonstrator, participants learned whether the demonstrator agreed with most or with few of their own political opinions. Following prior work that used shared preferences and political agreement to induce group membership (Gershman & Cikara, 2020; Kang et al., 2021; Lau et al., 2018), we refer to demonstrators who largely agreed with the participant as in-group and to those who largely disagreed as out-group demonstrators. Each participant observed one in-group and one out-group demonstrator, each reacting to lottery outcomes with a neutral and a happy expression.

Method Participants

We recruited 1,168 participants via Prolific (same participation requirements and compensation as in Experiment 1). Group membership was operationalized via agreement between participant and demonstrators on a set of five political-opinion items (taxes, abortion, healthcare, firearms, and border policy; Supplementary Materials, Table S5; response distributions in Figure S1). Participants reported their own position on each item by indicating whether they agreed or disagreed with a statement. Each demonstrator was then presented with a profile of responses that randomly matched either four out of five of the participant's responses (ingroup) or only one out of five (out-group) at the beginning of their lottery trials.

Before the lottery task instructions, participants reported their own positions on the five political-opinion items.

Expression valence and group membership were manipulated within participants, so their effects on inferred affect and WTP (H1-H4) are experimental effects. Inferred affect, in contrast, was measured, so the path analyses describe how the experimental in-group effects on inferred affect and WTP are related and do not identify causal mediation (see Experiment 1). The analyses are nonetheless informative because they constrain explanations of the in-group effects. Specifically, if the in-group effect on WTP rested only on in-group demonstrators being inferred to feel better, inferred affect should account for it, provided that WTP changes linearly with inferred affect (non-linear mappings are tested in Experiment 3).

Discussion

In Experiment 2, whether demonstrators shared participants' political opinions (ingroup) or opposed them (out-group) changed affect-based value inference. Observers inferred in-group demonstrators to feel slightly better (H3), and happy expressions increased WTP more when they came from in-group demonstrators (H4), while the effects of expression valence on inferred affect and WTP replicated Experiment 1. The more positive inferred affect ratings for in-group demonstrators accounted for part of the in-group effect on WTP. The remaining in-group effect has at least two interpretations. In-group reactions may inform valuation differently (e.g., a premium for positive in-group signals), or the inferred-affect rating may not capture everything observers infer from a reaction. Experiment 2 thus shows that in-group reactions raised WTP beyond what inferred affect could account for, and this in-group effect was larger for happy than for neutral expressions.

Experiment 2 included only neutral and happy expressions, which leaves room to ask how observers use negative reactions to value unseen outcomes and whether group membership also changes valuation from such reactions. Moreover, it remained untested whether participants perceived agreeing demonstrators as closer to their own political party (as a conceptualization of a social in-group) and disagreeing demonstrators as members of the other party (out-group), rather than only as sharing or not sharing their opinions.

Angry Expressions To address these questions, we conducted a third experiment that included angry expressions. This allowed us to test how observers use negative reactions to value unseen outcomes, whether group membership also changes this valuation, and whether the results of Experiment 2 hold when negative reactions are part of the stimulus set. For every demonstrator, and before any expression was shown, participants also indicated which political party they believed the demonstrator felt closer to, and at the end of the study they rated the two parties, testing whether the agreement manipulation induced perceived political group membership. Finally, to increase the precision of within-participant estimates at an adequate study length, each participant completed twelve rather than four trials (four demonstrators with three expressions each).

Method Participants

We recruited 1,508 participants via Prolific (same participation requirements as in Experiments 1 and 2). Participants who failed any of three attention checks were excluded (n = 47), resulting in a final sample of N = 1,461 participants (n = 790 female, n = 656 male, n = 15 other; age: M = 41.53, SD = 13.12, range = 18-88). Data were collected on October 2, 2026. The design, hypotheses, and analysis plan were preregistered prior to data collection.

Sample size was determined a priori by simulation (1,000 simulations per specification) for the expression valence [happy] × group membership [in-group] interaction on WTP (H4), using variance components from a pilot sample (N = 97 after exclusions). To guard against overestimating this effect, we used a conservative estimate following Perugini et al. (2014), namely the lower bound of the 60% confidence interval of the Experiment 2 estimate under its preregistered random-effects structure (b = 0.23 pence, SE = 0.09), rescaled to the payout range of Experiment 3 (b = 0.19 pence). With N = 1,400 after exclusions, power for this effect was 84%-90% under the random-effects structures supported by the pilot data, and above 90% for the Experiment 2 point estimate (see Supplementary Materials, Table S6). We planned to recruit 1,490 participants to account for an expected exclusion rate of 6%; due to minor over-recruitment on Prolific, 1,508 participants took part.

Group Membership Manipulation and Manipulation Checks

Group membership was manipulated as in Experiment 2, with the following changes. First, each participant observed two in-group and two out-group demonstrators (four demonstrators in total). One in-group demonstrator agreed with all five and the other with four of the participant's five responses, and one out-group demonstrator agreed with one and the other with none of the participant's responses (agreement levels and agreeing items were randomly assigned). Second, the demonstrator's responses were shown all at once next to the participant's own responses, followed by the number of shared opinions. Third, after answering the opinion items, participants indicated which of the two major U.S. parties they felt closer to, and by how much, on a slider from "much closer to the Democratic Party" through "equally close to both parties" to "much closer to the Republican Party" (internally coded from -100 to +100 for analysis; a live readout showed the direction and strength unsigned, e.g., "Leaning Democratic 45"). After each demonstrator's opinion profile, and before any of the demonstrator's expressions, participants used the same slider to indicate which party they thought the demonstrator felt closer to. This perceived placement served as the main manipulation check. At the end of the study, participants rated how much they liked, valued, felt connected to, and trusted Democrats and Republicans in general (0-100) and, as in Experiment 2, reported their party identification (Democrat, Republican, or neither).

Design and Procedure

The design and procedure was similar to Experiment 2 but introduced several changes. Expression valence had three levels (neutral, happy, and angry), yielding a 3 (expression valence) × 2 (group membership) fully within-participant design with twelve trials per participant. Each participant observed four demonstrators. Two of the four demonstrator groups (Black female, Black male, White female, White male) were randomly drawn for each participant. The two models of each drawn group (the same eight models as in Experiment 2) were randomly assigned to the in-group and the out-group role, so that each participant saw one in-group and one out-group with the same race and gender. The example trial showed the neutral expression of one of these eight models from a group not otherwise shown to the participant. Demonstrators were introduced by first names that were randomly assigned within gender (Danielle, Stephanie, Nicole, Gabrielle; Kevin, Travis, Aaron, Derek). Names were chosen to be common and to fit Black and White demonstrators about equally well (Supplementary Materials, S5). The order of demonstrators and the order of expressions within each demonstrator block were randomized. Lotteries paid between 3 and 15 pence, and each trial showed a new lottery labeled by a random three-digit number. Inferred affect was rated on a slider from -100 (very bad) through 0 (neutral) to +100 (very good), and WTP was entered as a whole number between 0 and 15 pence. The BDM procedure could draw ticket prices between 1 and 15 pence. After the second and fourth demonstrator blocks, participants completed an attention check indicating which group the demonstrator they had just observed belonged to (Black Female, Black Male, White Female, White Male). Unlike in Experiments 1 and 2, participants were not asked which lottery had paid more. Median completion time was 16.1 minutes.

Results

Manipulation Checks

Participants placed in-group demonstrators on their own side of the party scale and out-group demonstrators on the other side (Figure 6A). With the own side defined by participants' own placement, in-group demonstrators were placed at M = 52.07 (SE = 1.04) and out-group demonstrators at M = -55.28 (SE = 1.20) on the -100 to +100 scale, a difference of b = 107.35, SE = 1.85, 95% CI [103.72, 110.98], t(1361) = 58.01, p < .001. With the own side defined by party identification, the difference was similar, b = 113.20, SE = 1.94, 95% CI [109.39, 117.01], t(1127) = 58.30, p < .001.

Participants also rated their own party more favorably than the other party on all four items and their mean (own minus other party, with the own party defined by own placement: 42.35 to 47.89 points; by party identification: 46.96 to 53.21 points; all ps < .001; Supplementary Materials, Figure S1C). For the composite, the difference was b = 44.12, SE = 0.82, 95% CI [42.51, 45.73], t(1362) = 53.78, p < .001 (own placement). Together, these results confirm that, as intended, participants perceived demonstrators who shared most of their opinions as members of their political in-group and demonstrators who shared few as members of their political out-group, while rating their political in-group more favorably in general.

As in Experiment 2, the opinion items also tracked with partisan identity. The closer participants placed themselves to the Republican Party, the fewer left-leaning opinions they reported, b = -1.59 per 100 points, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [-1.67, -1.51], t(1459) = -38.94, p < .001, and Democrats reported more left-leaning opinions than Republicans, b = 2.26, SE = 0.07, 95% CI [2.13, 2.39], t(1127) = 34.25, p < .001 (49.9% of participants identified as Democrat, 27.4% as Republican, and 22.7% as neither).

Replication and Extension of H1 and H2

Both hypotheses were supported for happy and angry expressions. Relative to neutral expressions (intercept = -5.61 on the -100 to +100 scale), happy expressions were rated as reflecting more positive feelings, b = 73.78, SE = 2.08, 95% CI [69.00, 78.56], t(8) = 35.50, p < .001, and angry expressions as reflecting more negative feelings, b = -61.89, SE = 5.72, 95% CI [-75.38, -48.39], t(7) = -10.81, p < .001. Accordingly, compared with lotteries associated with neutral expressions (intercept = 4.51 pence), participants were willing to pay more for lotteries associated with happy expressions, b = 4.15, SE = 0.12, 95% CI [3.90, 4.41], t(14) = 34.82, p < .001 (27.7% of the response scale), and less for lotteries associated with angry expressions, b = -1.48, SE = 0.15, 95% CI [-1.83, -1.13], t(7) = -9.83, p < .001 (9.9% of the response scale).

H3 | Group Membership and Inferred Affect

As in Experiment 2, in-group demonstrators were rated as feeling slightly better than out-group demonstrators showing the same expression, b = 1.50, SE = 0.31, 95% CI [0.89, 2.10], t(1455) = 4.87, p < .001 (additive model; Figure 6B). This in-group effect did not differ significantly between expressions (happy × in-group: b = 1.24, SE = 0.72, 95% CI [-0.17, 2.65], t(8749) = 1.72, p = .085; angry × in-group: b = 1.01, SE = 0.72, 95% CI [-0.40, 2.42], t(8749) = 1.40, p = .161; happy vs. angry: difference = 0.23, SE = 0.72, t(8749) = 0.32, p = .748). Within expressions, in-group demonstrators were rated as feeling better when they showed happy expressions, b = 1.99, SE = 0.52, 95% CI [0.97, 3.00], t(7478) = 3.85, p < .001 (Holm-adjusted p < .001), or angry expressions, b = 1.75, SE = 0.52, 95% CI [0.74, 2.77], t(7478) = 3.40, p < .001 (Holm-adjusted p = .001), but not significantly when they showed neutral expressions, b = 0.75, SE = 0.52, 95% CI [-0.26, 1.76], t(7478) = 1.45, p = .148 (Holm-adjusted p = .148). Taken together, angry expressions from in-group demonstrators were interpreted as slightly less negative, and happy expressions as slightly more positive, than the same expressions from out-group demonstrators.

H4 | Group Membership and WTP Replicating Experiment 2, happy expressions increased WTP more when they came from in-group demonstrators, happy × in-group: b = 0.22, SE = 0.06, 95% CI [0.11, 0.33], t(8755) = 4.00, p < .001 (1.5% of the response scale; Figure 6C). However, group membership did not significantly change the effect of angry expressions, angry × in-group: b = -0.10, SE = 0.06, 95% CI [-0.21, 0.01], t(8754) = -1.87, p = .062. Within expressions, WTP was higher for in-group than for out-group demonstrators when they showed happy expressions, b = 0.40, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [0.32, 0.48], t(5884) = 9.32, p < .001 (Holmadjusted p < .001), and neutral expressions, b = 0.18, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [0.09, 0.26], t(5882) = 4.15, p < .001 (Holm-adjusted p < .001), but not angry expressions, b = 0.07, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.01, 0.16], t(5884) = 1.74, p = .083 (Holm-adjusted p = .083). Put differently, participants were willing to pay about 4.7% more for outcomes associated with happy in-group than with happy out-group reactions (8.87 vs. 8.47 pence). Unlike in Experiment 2, in-group membership was thus also associated with higher WTP for neutral expressions. A preregistered exploratory analysis of the shift in WTP from neutral in the direction of each expression converged with these results. Group membership amplified this shift on average across happy and angry expressions (0.16 pence, p < .001), without a significant difference between them (p = .100).

Can Inferred Affect Account for the In-Group Effect on WTP?

We related the in-group effects on inferred affect and WTP with the same approach as in Experiment 2 (Figure 6D-I), now comparing neutral, happy, and angry expressions.

Are In-Group Demonstrators Inferred to Feel Better?

Across expressions, in-group demonstrators were inferred to feel 1.50 rating points better (p < .001; see H3), and this difference did not differ significantly between any two expressions (all ps ≥ .085; see H3; angry: aG = 1.75, neutral: aG = 0.75, happy: aG = 1.99; Figure 6D).

Is Inferred Affect Associated With WTP?

Across expressions, each additional rating point was associated with 0.028 pence higher WTP, b = 0.028, SE = 0.001, 95% CI [0.026, 0.030], t(1403) = 35.82, p < .001 (Figure 6E). (Because inferred affect was rated from -100 to +100 rather than from 0 to 100, and WTP from 0 to 15 rather than 0 to 10 pence, slopes are not directly comparable with Experiment 2.) Unlike in Experiment 2, the slope differed between expressions. It was steeper for happy (b = 0.048) than for neutral expressions (b = 0.025; difference = 0.023, 95% CI [0.020, 0.026], t(10406) = 15.01, p < .001) and for angry expressions (b = 0.021; difference = 0.027, 95% CI [0.024, 0.029], t(8833) = 19.21, p < .001), and slightly shallower for angry than for neutral expressions (difference = -0.0036, 95% CI [-0.0060, -0.0013], t(4913) = -3.04, p = .002). Because the slope increased from angry to neutral to happy expressions, we explored whether non-linear mappings across the rating scale described the relationship between inferred affect and WTP better than linear mappings (Supplementary Materials, Tables S15 and S16, and Figure S2). A power curve and a penalized spline fit better than one linear slope across the entire inferred affect range and than expression-specific linear slopes (ΔAIC = -76 and -115 relative to the latter), with WTP rising more steeply at higher inferred affect. Within the range of each expression's ratings, however, the non-linear mappings stayed close to the expression-specific linear slopes. The in-group effects reported below were essentially unchanged under these nonlinear mappings (e.g., direct effect for happy expressions: 0.31 to 0.32 pence vs. 0.31 pence with expression-specific linear slopes). Thus, the in-group valuation premium is unlikely to be an artifact of modeling the relationship between inferred affect and WTP as linear.

Is Increased WTP for In-Group Members Accompanied by More Positive Inferred Affect? (Indirect Effect)

Across expressions, the indirect effect was 0.042 pence, 95% CI [0.025, 0.059], corresponding to 19% of the total in-group effect on WTP (cG = 0.22 pence, p < .001; additive H4 model; Figure 6G). The indirect effect was largest for happy expressions (0.095 pence, 95% CI [0.047, 0.145]), smaller for angry expressions (0.037 pence, 95% CI [0.016, 0.059]), and not significant for neutral expressions (0.019 pence, 95% CI [-0.006, 0.043]). The indirect effect was significantly larger for happy than for neutral expressions (difference = 0.077 pence, 95% CI [0.023, 0.131]) and angry expressions (difference = 0.058 pence, 95% CI [0.005, 0.111]); the latter difference was not significant under the common randomeffects structure (Table S12). The indirect effects of angry and neutral expressions did not differ (difference = 0.019 pence, 95% CI [-0.014, 0.051]). For angry expressions, a significant indirect effect again occurred alongside a nonsignificant total in-group effect (cG = 0.07, p = .083), possibly for the reasons described for neutral expressions in Experiment 2. As in Experiment 2, the indirect path thus added a small positive increase to WTP for every expression for which in-group demonstrators were inferred to feel better. This indirect effect was largest for happy expressions.

Is There an In-Group Premium in WTP Above and Beyond Inferred Affect? (Direct Effect)

Across expressions, WTP was higher after in-group reactions at equal inferred affect, c′G = 0.18, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [0.13, 0.23], t(4293) = 6.98, p < .001 (Figure 6H). The direct effect was present for happy expressions, c′G = 0.31, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [0.24, 0.39], t(12385) = 8.21, p < .001, and neutral expressions, c′G = 0.15, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [0.07, 0.22], t(12444) = 3.84, p < .001, but not for angry expressions, c′G = 0.04, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.03, 0.12], t(12720) = 1.09, p = .274. The in-group valuation premium was larger for happy than for neutral expressions (difference = 0.17, 95% CI [0.07, 0.26], t(8524) = 3.32, p < .001) and than for angry expressions (difference = 0.27, 95% CI [0.17, 0.37], t(8695) = 5.36, p < .001), and smaller for angry than for neutral expressions (difference = -0.10, 95% CI [-0.20, -0.01], t(8669) = -2.07, p = .039).

Is Inferred Affect More Strongly Associated With WTP for In-Group Demonstrators? (Weighting)

At the same expression, and after allowing each expression its own in-group effect on WTP and its own slope of inferred affect, the association between inferred affect and WTP was not significantly stronger for in-group than for out-group demonstrators, wG = 0.0014, SE = 0.0009, 95% CI [-0.0003, 0.0031], t(12517) = 1.66, p = .096 (pence per rating point; Figure 6I). The weighting did not differ between any two expressions (all |t| < 0.04, ps > .970) and was not significant for any expression on its own (all ps ≥ .25; Table S12). With twelve trials per participant, the 95% CI excluded weighting differences larger than 0.0031 pence per rating point, about 11% of the overall slope of inferred affect. In the preregistered per-expression models, the weighting term reached significance for angry expressions (p = .038; Table S11). As in Experiment 2, the H4 pattern could also be described by a single steeper in-group slope of inferred affect across expressions (0.0020 pence per rating point, p < .001; Table S14).

Can Inferred Affect Account for the Expression Effects on WTP?

As in Experiments 1 and 2, inferred affect accounted for a substantial part of the expression effects on WTP. With three expressions, there is no single expression effect, so happy and angry expressions were each compared with neutral expressions. For happy expressions, the indirect effect was 2.07 pence, 95% CI [1.91, 2.23], or 50% of the total effect (cE = 4.15), and a direct effect remained, c′E = 2.12, SE = 0.07, 95% CI [1.98, 2.27], t(1321) = 28.74, p < .001. For angry expressions, the indirect effect, -1.73 pence, 95% CI [-2.06, -1.41], exceeded the total effect (cE = -1.48), so that the direct effect was positive, c′E = 0.24, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [0.14, 0.33], t(1091) = 4.80, p < .001. This follows from using one slope of inferred affect for all expressions in this analysis (b = 0.028). Applied to the much lower inferred affect of angry expressions, this common slope predicts a larger drop in WTP than observed, because the slope for angry expressions was in fact shallower (b = 0.021; see above), and the positive direct effect absorbs the difference. One possible contributor is that the lotteries paid at least 3 pence in our design, and mean WTP after angry expressions (about 3 pence) was close to this minimum. In other words, angry expressions lowered WTP less than their lower inferred affect would imply under a single slope for all expressions.

Discussion

Experiment 3 replicated both in-group effects of Experiment 2 with twelve trials per participant and additionally introduced angry faces. In-group demonstrators were inferred to feel slightly better, and happy expressions increased WTP more when they came from ingroup demonstrators. Angry expressions lowered WTP without a significant group difference, possibly because WTP after angry expressions was close to the minimum payout of 3 pence, leaving little room for group differences. Unlike in Experiment 2, WTP was also higher for in-group demonstrators showing neutral expressions, possibly because neutral expressions were no longer the most negative reaction. Inferred affect accounted for part (i.e., 19%) of the in-group effect on WTP. Most of the in-group valuation premium remained when accounting for inferred affect, and this premium corresponded to the positivity of the expression, that is, largest for happy, smaller for neutral, and not detectable for angry expressions. Non-linear mappings yielded the same premium, and group differences in the association between inferred affect and WTP within expressions were not significant. Together with Experiment 2, these results show that in-group reactions increased valuation beyond what could be accounted for by the intergroup difference in inferred affect, and that this in-group effect is larger the more positive the reaction.

General Discussion

Across three preregistered experiments, we tested whether observers use others' affective reactions to inform their own valuation of outcomes they have not experienced themselves, and whether social group membership, induced via political agreement, alters affect-based value inference.

Experiment 1 established that observers inferred happy facial expressions to reflect more positive affect than neutral expressions and were willing to pay more for lotteries associated with them, with inferred affect accounting for most of this expression effect. Experiments 2 and 3 showed that demonstrator group membership, induced via political agreement and perceived as shared partisan identity (Experiment 3), influences both affect ratings and WTP. In-group demonstrators were inferred to feel slightly better, and happy expressions increased WTP more when they came from in-group demonstrators, whereas group membership did not significantly change the effect of angry expressions. One explanation would be that observers valued in-group reactions more because they interpreted them as happier. Relating the in-group effects on inferred affect and WTP showed this explanation to be at least incomplete. While more positive inferred affect did account for part of the in-group effect on WTP, most of the in-group effect remained, especially for happy expressions. We did not observe reliable group differences in how strongly inferred affect was associated with WTP within expressions. In short, observers can use others' facial reactions to value outcomes they cannot see, while placing a valuation premium on positive reactions from political allies, which cannot be fully explained by how good they infer those allies to feel. Together, these findings extend the study of affective signaling to subjective value as measured in behavioral economics and show that the group membership of the person whose reaction observers see changes how that reaction informs their valuation of unseen outcomes.

A large body of work has established that others' affective expressions serve as social signals that observers use to infer situational features and norms and to guide their own behavior in novel contexts (Hareli & Hess, 2012; Stenberg & Forslund, 2020; Van Kleef, 2009; Van Kleef & Côté, 2022; Walle et al., 2017). Experiment 1 extends this tradition to subjective value as formalized in decision neuroscience and behavioral economics (Juechems & Summerfield, 2019; Padoa-Schioppa & Assad, 2006; Rangel et al., 2008). Observers inferred happy expressions to reflect more positive affect about an outcome and were willing to pay more for tickets to lotteries associated with happy reactions. Moreover, in all three experiments, a substantial part of the link between others' affective expressions and observers' WTP could be accounted for by how good observers inferred the demonstrator to feel. This pattern is consistent with observers using others' affective reactions as a cue to what an outcome is worth and updating their own valuation accordingly.

Our findings operationalize, at the interpersonal level, the proposal that affective valence indexes or even constitutes subjective value (Shenhav, 2024; Vollberg et al., 2025; Vollberg & Sander, 2024). Where prior work has shown that people's own affective reactions track with their subjective valuation of outcomes they experience (Chew et al., 2021; Forbes & Bennett, 2024; Mellers et al., 1999; Parr et al., 2026; Rutledge et al., 2014; Vanhasbroeck et al., 2021), the present results suggest that observers can use another person's affect as a window into how that person evaluated an outcome (Vollberg & Sander, 2024), and use this window to inform their own valuation. Because WTP was combined with an incentive-compatible BDM procedure, these shifts reflect valuation with monetary consequences. In doing so, the present findings extend beyond observed affect as a signal that influences approach and avoidance (Klinnert et al., 1986; Meffert et al., 2015) and demonstrate that it acts on the valuation that ultimately determines choice.

Across Experiments 2 and 3, observers inferred demonstrators who shared their political opinions to feel slightly better than demonstrators who opposed them, despite seeing the same expressions. Experiment 3 helps to interpret this shift. If in-group expressions were simply interpreted as more typical of the displayed emotion, in-group angry expressions should have been interpreted as expressing more negative valence. Instead, in-group angry expressions were interpreted as slightly less negative and in-group happy expressions as slightly more positive than the same expressions from out-group demonstrators. This pattern is consistent with a shift toward more positive inferred affect for in-group demonstrators. Whether it reflects more positive interpretations of in-group expressions, more negative interpretations of out-group expressions, or both, cannot be determined without an unaffiliated comparison.

These results complement prior work on observational learning and group bias (Boeltzig et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2021; Schultner, Lindström, et al., 2024; Schultner, Stillerman, et al., 2024). When rewards are hidden, observers can infer others' success from their observable behavior and selectively learn from those they infer to be successful (Hawkins et al., 2023). The present work shows that others' affective reactions can serve a similar function for valuation, and that group identity (here induced via political agreement) can influence valuation even when neither actions nor outcomes are available, leaving affective reactions as the only information available. In cases where outcomes are private (i.e., not yet experienced by observers) but reactions are public, positive reactions may therefore act more strongly on observers' valuation when they come from an identityaligned group than when they cross group lines. This points to affect as one route by which preferences for outcomes (e.g., products or causes) may spread differently within identityaligned social groups. We note that in-group effects were small relative to the effects of expressions themselves. However, consistent across experiments, observers were willing to pay about 4.5% more after happy reactions from in-group demonstrators relative to what they were willing to pay after happy reactions from out-group demonstrators. Such small effects can nevertheless have large consequences when they accumulate over many instances (Carey et al., 2023). When populations are divided and people see others' reactions frequently, a consistent premium for positive reactions from like-minded others could add up and reinforce the alignment of preferences within groups.

Relating the in-group effects on inferred affect and WTP constrains possible explanations for the in-group effect on valuation. Group membership influenced observers' reported inferences about the demonstrators' affective states, but the corresponding differences in inferred affect accounted for only part of the in-group effect on WTP. Explanations that rest solely on in-group demonstrators being inferred to be happier are thus insufficient. The remaining in-group valuation premium, largest for happy and absent for angry expressions, could reflect (i) systematic differences in how inferred affect informs valuation, (ii) additional evaluative inferences not captured by the inferred-affect rating, or both.

The first possibility, namely that inferred affect informs valuation differently for ingroup demonstrators, ties back to the reasoning that motivated this work. Observers may assume in-group members to share their preferences (particularly given political agreement; Gershman & Cikara, 2020; Lau et al., 2018) and extend this assumption to economic preferences (much as people prefer to learn from politically like-minded others even on nonpolitical tasks; Marks et al., 2019). A positive in-group reaction would then be more informative about how much observers themselves would value the outcome, so that the outcome is valued at a premium. Moreover, observing positive affect from an in-group member may itself be rewarding (Park et al., 2016), and vicarious reward is stronger for others perceived as similar (Mobbs et al., 2009), which may carry over to the outcome that elicited the reaction.

The second possibility is that the single (and noisy) inferred affect rating captures only part of what observers extract from an expression. If other inferences, such as how desirable the outcome was for the demonstrator or how credible the reaction seemed, also differ systematically between in-group and out-group demonstrators, they may explain portions of the remaining in-group effect.

Within expression categories, we found little evidence consistent with differential weighting of inferred affect into WTP, and non-linear mappings between inferred affect and WTP left the premium essentially unchanged. Whether the in-group valuation premium we observed is best described as a premium for positive in-group expression categories or as a steeper association across the whole range of inferred affect ultimately remains a question for designs with more finely graded expressions.

At first sight, adjacent findings from consumer research seem to contrast with the present results. Luxury goods received higher price estimates when sales assistants or advertising models showed neutral rather than smiling faces, which could be attributed to increased social distance that neutral faces signal (Zhu et al., 2022). In these studies, however, expressions were portraits or verbal descriptions of people greeting customers or advertising a product, and not a reaction to receiving the product like in our design. Moreover, whereas social distance or status seem relevant for the value of luxury products, they arguably play little role in the value of a monetary lottery outcome. Participants also estimated market prices in imagined purchase scenarios (i.e., how other people would value the outcome) rather than stating their own incentive-compatible WTP. Whether an expression raises or lowers valuation thus appears to depend on whether it reflects a response to the object and on what makes objects of that category valuable.

Constraints on Generality

Several features of our design constrain the generality of our findings and point to directions for future work. First, we operationalized group membership through political agreement, following recent work using similar manipulations (e.g., Kang et al., 2021; Vollberg & Cikara, 2024). Although participants perceived demonstrators who shared their opinions as political in-group members, our design cannot distinguish whether the observed effects depend on perceived group membership or on opinion similarity alone. Future work that manipulates group membership independently of preference similarity would also help establish whether affect-based value inference is influenced by group membership more generally or specifically when group membership involves shared preferences. Moreover, our manipulation varied preference similarity regarding political issues but did not probe opposing preferences regarding lottery outcomes. Whether observers can learn to "reverse" affect-based value inference when demonstrators have outcome preferences opposed to their own remains a relevant open question (Schultner et al., 2025). Although our design included Black and White demonstrators, we avoided operationalizing group membership through racialized features, given concerns about treating facial appearance as a direct indicator of racial categories (Martinez, 2025). Nevertheless, political positions, race and gender may interplay, particularly given evidence that race and gender can influence emotion perception (Craig & Lipp, 2018; Halberstadt et al., 2022; Summerell et al., 2025).

Second, our experiments isolated static facial reactions as the only available cue to the value of the hidden lottery outcomes. This allowed us to establish that observers use others' affective reactions to inform their own valuation when no other information is available, but not how strongly they rely on affective reactions when other informative cues are available. Testing how observers integrate expressions with other sources of information is therefore a potential next step, particularly because the interpretation of emotional expressions itself is known to depend on context (Anzellotti et al., 2021; Aviezer et al., 2017; Houlihan et al., 2023). Moreover, alternative signals such as dynamic expressions (Trautmann et al., 2009), verbal affect reports (Dashti et al., 2026) or even nonaffective reports could similarly inform valuation and be influenced by group membership. Relatedly, participants were told that demonstrators reacted to their own lottery outcomes, without any information about whether their expressions were intended to communicate with observers. Outside the laboratory, expressions may be regulated or strategically displayed (Eldesouky & English, 2023; Gross & Levenson, 1993; Teoh & Hutcherson, 2025), and observers respond differently to expressions perceived as inauthentic (Côté et al., 2013; Hideg & van Kleef, 2017). Such communicative intentions likely influence affect-based value inference, and how they do so remains an open question.

Conclusion

Across three preregistered experiments, observers used others' affective reactions to inform their valuation of hidden outcomes and were willing to pay more for outcomes associated with happy expressions and less for outcomes associated with angry expressions. Group membership, operationalized via political agreement, changed this affect-based value inference. Happy reactions increased WTP more when they came from an in-group member, whereas angry reactions showed no evidence of a corresponding group difference. Observers attributed more positive affect to in-group reactions, but this accounted for only part of their larger effect on valuation. Our results reveal how affect can substitute for missing information when outcomes are private but reactions are public, influencing economic valuation differently depending on social group membership. Taken together, these data point to affect as one route by which preferences may spread differently within identity-aligned groups.
