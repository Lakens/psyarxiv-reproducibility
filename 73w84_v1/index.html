Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Ethics Approval Check

Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, and it was present:

Since our participants are expecting parents and therefore do not yet need child-directed resources, we use the term ‘resources’ hereafter to refer to language-related, parent-directed resources.

As pregnancy represents a salient transition into parenthood during which expecting parents likely begin actively considering and preparing for their child’s upbringing, including decisions concerning language use, we predicted that most participants had made their language transmission decision during pregnancy (P1).

However, as noted above, all participants were raising their children multilingually: either with the two SLs or with one/both SL(s) and at least one HL. Ahooja et al. (2024) consequently suggested that both participant groups may have relied on largely the same resources, most of which were designed with the two SLs in mind.

Participants were recruited via local media, institutional newsletters, social media pages, as well as with posters and flyers placed in locations frequented by parents (e.g. healthcare practitioners, kindergartens, and libraries).

Additionally, snowball sampling was used: participants were asked to share the link to the questionnaire with other parents.

Based on preregistered inclusion criteria, we analysed responses from 520 out of the 642 participants in the original dataset.

Participants were included if they were between 18 and 59 years old, lived in Fryslân, reported at least minimal Frisian proficiency, were raising a young child, and responded to at least 77% of the key items related to the variables in this study.

This is likely related to the fact that the majority of participants had already decided which language(s) to transmit long before they were actually expecting a child (RQ1).

The resources whose influence we examined were thus provided at a point when most participants had already made up their mind regarding the IGT of Frisian.

While the Heit & Mem magazine and, to a lesser extent, the Heit & Mem website are resources for which expecting parents reported high levels of awareness, the available leaflet and booklets were relatively unknown among our participants while they were expecting.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

1. Introduction

Europe has long been characterised by a rich linguistic diversity that constitutes an important part of European cultural heritage, yet many of its languages are currently under threat: an estimated 221 of the languages spoken within the European Union are considered to be endangered (UNESCO 2010). Successful minority language maintenance and revitalisation depend heavily on intergenerational transmission (IGT) within the home (e.g. De Houwer 2021; Fishman 2001). Effective language transmission, in turn, is dependent on the language exposure -and specifically, the quantity and quality of the input -provided to young children by their parents and other caregivers (e.g. Byers-Heinlein et al. 2025). Recent research has shown that, to ensure their children receive sufficient minority language exposure, parents often make use of -or at least want to make use of -different types of language-related resources (e.g. Ahooja et al. 2024). This includes both parent-directed and child-directed resources: parent-directed resources are leaflets, books, websites, and other materials providing parents with information about raising children multilingually; child-directed resources are storybooks, games, digital applications, and other materials parents can use to engage with their children in the languages they wish to transmit in order to foster their acquisition (Ahooja et al. 2024). To date, parental engagement with resources has only been studied with regard to parents who are currently raising a young child. There is no known research regarding expecting parents' engagement with resources as a way to prepare themselves for the IGT of minority languages. We argue that this is an important area of empirical inquiry as it seems likely that parents decide on the language(s) they want to transmit prior to the birth of their children. The timing of (expecting) parents' language transmission decisions, however, has also not yet been investigated. The aim of our study is to begin filling these gaps in the research literature. The study examines both the timing of language transmission decisions as well as expecting parents' engagement with language-related resources to foster IGT of minority languages, and we approach these topics within the family language policy (FLP) framework.

FLP is the 'explicit and overt planning in relation to language use within the home among family members' (King et al. 2008: 1). Based on Spolsky's (2004) more general model of language policy, FLP comprises three inter-related components: language beliefs, language practices, and language management (e.g. King et al. 2008). Language beliefs comprise parents' ideologies (e.g. Curdt-Christiansen 2009), attitudes (e.g. Slavkov 2017), and concerns (e.g. Quirk et al. 2026) regarding language(s) and linguistic phenomena such as multilingual child-rearing. Language practices are everyday language behaviours within the family: for example, the languages parents choose to speak with their children, how regularly they use them, and the circumstances in which they do so (e.g. Surrain 2021). Language management consists of family members' efforts to influence or modify children's language acquisition and use, for instance by adopting specific family language strategies (e.g. Sander-Montant et al. 2025) and/or engaging with language-related resources (Ahooja et al. 2024). Previous research found that both language practices and language management shape the language exposure children receive, which plays a crucial role in their language acquisition and development (Hollebeke et al. 2023).

This paper examines parents' engagement with language-related resources, which, as noted above, constitute a form of language management (Ahooja et al. 2024) -or, for expecting parents, language management preparation. To date, research regarding parental engagement with both parentdirected and child-directed resources has only been conducted in the Canadian province of Québec. Ballinger et al. (2022) conducted a small-scale qualitative study into parents' engagement with resources while raising children multilingually. Building on this, Ahooja et al. (2024) conducted a large-scale questionnaire-based study, eliciting quantitative and qualitative data to further investigate the topic. Systematic research distinguishing between parents' engagement with parent-directed and child-directed resources in minority language contexts outside of Québec is currently lacking. We are also unaware of any previous research regarding expecting parents' engagement with language-related resources. Other small-scale, qualitative studies looked at engagement with resources to examine how parents obtain information regarding minority language maintenance (e.g. Lee et al. 2015 in the U.S.) or multilingual child-rearing (e.g. Barbeau-Morrison 2019 in Canada). However, these studies used different terminology and did not distinguish between parent-directed and child-directed resources. The present study addresses these gaps by examining expecting parents' engagement with resources in Fryslân, a province in the Netherlands. Since our participants are expecting parents and therefore do not yet need child-directed resources, we use the term 'resources' hereafter to refer to language-related, parent-directed resources.

The majority language in the Netherlands is Dutch. Approximately 2% of the country's population also speak West Frisian (Schmeets and Cornips 2026), most of whom live in the province of Fryslân, where they constitute roughly 64% of inhabitants (Dekker et al. 2025). Two other closely related Frisian languages are spoken in Germany: North Frisian, with approximately 4,000-5,000 speakers in the district of Nordfriesland (Versloot 2021), and Saterfrisian, the only surviving variety of East Frisian, with an estimated 2,000 speakers in the Saterland municipality (Litty et al. 2026). While all three share an ancestor, they are now classified as distinct autochthonous heritage languages (Litty et al. 2026) 1 that are either vulnerable (West Frisian) or severely endangered (North Frisian and East-/Saterfrisian) (UNESCO 2010). This study focuses solely on West Frisian, which is hereafter referred to as 'Frisian'. Almost all Frisian speakers also speak Dutch (e.g. Hilton et al. 2020), and some know additional languages such as English and/or immigrant HLs. In recent decades, Frisian has received increasing protection and promotion through language policies and planning measures. Consequently, the language benefits from greater institutional support, greater visibility in the media and the linguistic landscape, and inclusion in school curricula (see e.g. Kuipers-Zandberg and Kircher 2020, for an overview of the ethnolinguistic vitality of Frisian). Furthermore, systematic efforts have been made to encourage the IGT of Frisian in the home. Nonetheless, the number of parents who transmit Frisian to their children has not increased significantly since the 1970s (e.g. Klinkenberg et al. 2018).

To investigate expecting parents' engagement with resources to support them in the IGT of Frisian in Fryslân, we collected quantitative and qualitative questionnaire data from 520 Frisian-speaking parents of young children (aged 0-4 years) who reflected retrospectively on the period during which they were expecting. Our sample included both parents who ended up transmitting Frisian and parents who did not. The aim of the study is twofold. First, we aim to contribute to FLP theory by generating new knowledge regarding language management preparation in the form of expecting parents' engagement with resources. Second, we aim to provide practical information to policy makers and language planners to help shape future support measures for the IGT of Frisian. To do so, we investigate the following: first, the timing of minority language speakers' decision as to whether (or not) to transmit Frisian, and second, their engagement with resources. The latter includes expecting parents' awareness and use of, as well as their desire for, resources. Finally, we look at whether expecting parents' engagement with these resources has affected their decision (not) to transmit Frisian.

2. Background

Since this study addresses a previously unexplored stage of FLP, our initial approach was to address our research questions (RQs) in an exploratory fashion. Therefore, rather than formulating formal hypotheses, we stated predictions (Ps) regarding the patterns we expected to observe. To contextualise our study, we will first present the prior research that informed our study.

2.1 Timing

An important first step is to examine when parents decide what language(s) to transmit. Research on this topic is rare, and has focused primarily on how and why parents made language-related decisions rather than when the decision-making took place. In the U.S., a small-scale qualitative study on families raising Spanish-English bilinguals showed that FLP decisions were largely shaped by personal experience with the relevant languages and with multilingualism (King and Fogle 2006). Similarly, a small-scale qualitative study in Norway showed that parents' FLP decisions reflected prior experiences with multilingualism, and often also a desire for continuity across the lifespan (Purkarthofer and Steien 2019). However, little is known about the point in time at which parents typically decide which languages to transmit. Understanding decision timing, alongside expecting parents' engagement with resources, can help determine whether additional and/or different resources are needed, and whether awareness-raising regarding existing resources should be increased.

the latter (e.g. Byers-Heinlein et al. 2025). Following the classification of North Frisian by Litty et al. (2026), the present study likewise refers to West Frisian as an autochthonous HL.

To our knowledge, no previous research exists regarding when parents decide which language(s) to transmit. In Fryslân, after registering the birth of their child with the local municipality, parents receive the taalkado (language gift): a gift box of child-directed and parent-directed resources. Parents who register the birth online receive a digital 'collect card' that they can use to collect the gift at the town hall. The initiative was introduced in 2008 as the taaltaske (language bag), continued as the taalkado in 2017, and has since been available to families across the province. Additionally, the Heit & Mem (Dad & Mum) magazine, intended primarily for parents of young children, offers information on (multilingual) childrearing and advice on fostering children's acquisition of Frisian. Expecting parents also receive the special edition Lytse Heit & Mem (Little Dad & Mum) as part of the taalkado. However, little is known about the support expecting parents need and want -or when they would like to receive it. It remains to be ascertained whether the available resources reach parents early enough, and whether additional support for expecting parents would be beneficial. Therefore, our first RQ was: • RQ1: When did parents decide what language(s) to transmit to their child?

As pregnancy represents a salient transition into parenthood during which expecting parents likely begin actively considering and preparing for their child's upbringing, including decisions concerning language use, we predicted that most participants had made their language transmission decision during pregnancy (P1).

2.2 Awareness of resources

Parents' engagement with resources has only been studied systematically in the Canadian province of Québec, where many inhabitants speak HLs alongside one or both of the societal languages (SLs) -that is, the province's majority language, French, and English, which is a minority language in Québec but the majority language in Canada at large. Ballinger et al. (2022) and Ahooja et al. (2024) looked at differences between HL-transmitting parents (who transmitted one/more HLs in addition to one/both of the SLs) and non-HL-transmitting parents (who only transmitted the two SLs) regarding their awareness and use of, satisfaction with, and desire for resources. Their findings showed that non-HL-transmitting parents reported higher awareness of resources than HL-transmitting parents (Ahooja et al. 2024). However, these findings may not be directly transferable to the Frisian context. The previous studies examined awareness of resources without focusing on specific languages, whereas the present study focuses specifically on resources designed to support the transmission of the autochthonous HL Frisian. This led to ask: • RQ2: With regard to their awareness of existing resources, were there differences between expecting parents who did and who did not end up transmitting Frisian?

We reasoned that expecting parents who intend to transmit Frisian were more likely to seek out or pay attention to resources relevant to raising children multilingually with Frisian as they prepared for raising their child. In contrast, such resources may have been perceived as less relevant by expecting parents who do not intend to transmit Frisian. Consequently, we predicted that awareness of resources differed between the two groups, with expecting parents who ended up transmitting Frisian ultimately reporting greater awareness than those who ultimately did not transmit it (P2).

2.3 Use of resources

Regarding the use of resources, the aforementioned studies in Québec found no significant differences between HL-transmitting and non-HL-transmitting parents (Ahooja et al. 2024; Ballinger et al. 2022). However, as noted above, all participants were raising their children multilingually: either with the two SLs or with one/both SL(s) and at least one HL. Ahooja et al. (2024) consequently suggested that both participant groups may have relied on largely the same resources, most of which were designed with the two SLs in mind. At the same time, research elsewhere demonstrated that HL-transmitting parents actively sought information and support related to multilingual child-rearing. For example, the participants of a small-scale qualitative study in the U.S. reported obtaining information about HL maintenance through newspapers, magazines, schools, teachers, community programmes, paediatricians, and communication with other parents (e.g. Lee et al. 2015). Similarly, a small-scale qualitative study in Canada showed that HL-transmitting parents used resources when seeking advice on multilingual childrearing (Barbeau-Morrison 2019). Given the lack of previous studies regarding expecting parents, it remains unclear whether resource use differs between expecting parents who ultimately transmit a HL to their children and those who do not. Our third RQ therefore was: • RQ3: With regard to their use of existing resources, were there differences between expecting parents who did and who did not end up transmitting Frisian?

Existing research has compared parents raising their children with different combinations of SLs and HLs, but not parents who do and do not transmit an autochthonous HL. In the present study, the resources specifically concerned multilingual child-rearing and/or the transmission of Frisian. We assumed that these resources were more relevant to parents who were considering transmitting Frisian and may therefore have been more likely to seek them out or encounter them. By contrast, parents who did not (intend to) transmit Frisian may have perceived these resources as less relevant. We therefore predicted that expecting parents who ultimately did transmit Frisian reported greater resource use than those who ultimately did not transmit Frisian (P3).

2.4 Satisfaction with existing resources

As for the satisfaction with resources, the aforementioned Québec-based studies found significant differences between non-HL-transmitting and HL-transmitting parents. The former reported being satisfied with the available resources, the latter were less satisfied -presumably due to the dearth of available resources aimed at supporting the transmission of immigrant HLs in Québec (Ahooja et al. 2024; Ballinger et al. 2022). However, it is unclear whether this translates to the Frisian context. Our fourth RQ was thus: • RQ4: With regard to their satisfaction with existing resources, were there differences between expecting parents who did and who did not end up transmitting Frisian?

We postulated that resources concerning Frisian transmission were particularly relevant to parents who considered/planned to transmit Frisian, and that they were consequently more aware of these resources (see P2). We therefore expected that the suspected lower awareness of parents who ultimately did not transmit Frisianfoot_2 would result in lower satisfaction with the available resources. Hence, our prediction was that expecting parents who ended up transmitting Frisian would report higher satisfaction with existing resources than expecting parents who did not end up transmitting it (P4).

2.5 Desire for additional resources

Next, we turned to parents' desire for additional resources. Prior research amongst parents raising young children consistently demonstrated their desire for more resources to support their multilingual childrearing practices -and, in the case of HL-transmitting parents, the IGT of these HLs. For instance, in their small-scale focus group study in Québec's urban centre, Montréal, Ballinger et al. (2022) showed that both HL-transmitting and non-HL-transmitting parents wanted more information on multilingual childrearing (e.g. through various formats such as websites, weekly emails, apps, magazines, and public talks).

In addition, these parents also valued opportunities to connect with other parents through support groups.

Similarly, Ahooja et al. (2024) found that parents throughout Québec expressed a desire for more such resources. Other small-scale qualitative studies in the U.S. showed a comparable desire for more affordable and accessible materials supporting HL-transmission (e.g. Kwon 2017). This led us to ask: • RQ5: With regard to their desire for additional resources, were there differences between expecting parents who did and who did not end up transmitting Frisian?

We expected that parents who ultimately did not transmit Frisian perceived the available resources as less relevant for their language transmission decision (see P1), leading them to desire alternative resources that would better support their language transmission practices. In contrast, parents who ultimately transmitted Frisian may have perceived existing resources as more relevant to their language practices and therefore felt less of a need for additional resources. Based on this, we predicted that expecting parents who ultimately did not transmit Frisian retrospectively expressed a stronger desire for additional resources than expecting parents who ultimately transmitted Frisian (P5).

2.6 Language transmission decision

Finally, we turn to the relevance of resources to expecting parents' language transmission decisions. Specifically, we asked: • RQ6: Was the decision to transmit Frisian affected by the available resources?

Resources providing information and support regarding multilingual child-rearing and Frisian transmission may have contributed to parents' decision-making by informing them about the potential benefits, practicalities, and solutions to challenges regarding the IGT of Frisian. We therefore predicted that the resources they encountered played at least some role in the expecting parents' language transmission decision (P6).

>Methodology

3.1 Materials Data were collected online via the Adapted Resources for Raising Multilingual Children in Fryslân

Questionnaire (A-RRMC-F), based on the original RRMC by Ahooja et al. (2024). The A-RRMC-F was part of a larger survey, the Multilingual Family Language Policies in Fryslân Questionnaire (MFLPF), which also comprised questions eliciting qualitative and quantitative data pertaining to parents' attitudes towards Frisian (Kircher et al. 2026a; Heinzova et al. 2026; Dekker et al. in preparation) and towards childhood multilingualism (Kircher et al. 2026b; Heinzova et al. in preparation). Additionally, the MFLPF elicited quantitative and qualitative data regarding the engagement with resources among parents of young children (Dijkstra et al. in preparation). However, here, the focus is solely on expecting parents. The complete MFLPF, the preregistration, the R analyses code, and the supplementary materials for this study are available on the Open Science Framework: [https://osf.io/jteb6/].

3.2 Procedure

Participants were recruited via local media, institutional newsletters, social media pages, as well as with posters and flyers placed in locations frequented by parents (e.g. healthcare practitioners, kindergartens, and libraries). Additionally, snowball sampling was used: participants were asked to share the link to the questionnaire with other parents. Data were collected between February and June 2025. Prior to this, ethics clearance was obtained from the ethics board of the University of Groningen, Campus Fryslân.

3.3 Participants

Based on preregistered inclusion criteria, we analysed responses from 520 out of the 642 participants in the original dataset. Participants were included if they were between 18 and 59 years old, lived in Fryslân, reported at least minimal Frisian proficiency, were raising a young child, and responded to at least 77% of the key items related to the variables in this study. The final sample comprised parents from all regions in Fryslân except for the islands of Schiermonnikoog and Vlieland. The mean Frisian proficiency for understanding was 3.94 (SD=0.18) and speaking 3.69 (SD=0.65), measured on Likert scales where 0 meant not proficient at all and 4 meant highly proficient. Most participants spoke Frisian as their sole L1 (63.85%) or as one of their L1s alongside Dutch (23.65%). Most were raising their child with a partner (96.15%), identified as female (85,58%), and had chosen to transmit Frisian either as the sole language (44.61%) or alongside Dutch (49.23%). Out of the Frisian-transmitting parents, 92.12% of respondents indicated speaking Frisian to their child themselves and 77.50% indicated the co-caregiver transmitted Frisian. Participants' children were fairly balanced, and their ages are depicted in Figure 1.

3.4 Variables and analyses

The timing of participants' language transmission decision was elicited through a multiple-choice question (Q19) asking when they had decided which language(s) to raise their child with. Response options were "more than three months before the pregnancy", "three months or less before the pregnancy", "during the pregnancy", "within the first three months after my child's birth", "later than the first three months after my child's birth", and "I am unsure". Participants' awareness and use of resources were measured via multiple-select questions (Q49 and Q50, respectively). Response options were the materials included in the taalkado: the Talen kleuren je leven (Languages colour your life) leaflet, the Yn ferwachting (Expecting) booklet, and the Hoera in lytse poppe (Hurrah a little baby) booklet. The two other main resources that are available, namely the Heit & Mem magazine and the Heit & mem website, were also included. Additional answer options allowed participants to indicate that they were unaware of these resources (Q49) or had not used any of them before their child's birth (Q50). For Q50, participants could also indicate that they had used other resources, prompting an open-ended follow-up question to elicit details. Satisfaction with existing resources was measured via a five-point Likert item (Q51), ranging from "not satisfied at all" to "very satisfied". Participants' desire for additional resources was measured with a multiple-choice item (Q53), asking whether they would have liked additional resources while expecting their child, with the answer options "yes", "no", and "I am not sure". The selection of "yes" prompted another open-ended follow-up question, asking participants to elaborate on the additional resources they would have liked to receive prior to their child's birth. Finally, the effect of resources on transmission was measured via a fivepoint Likert item (Q52), ranging from "not at all" to "completely".

Several methods were used to address our research questions. RQ1 (decision timing) and RQ6 (effect of resources) were addressed using descriptive statistics: namely numbers and percentages. For awareness (RQ2), use (RQ3), satisfaction (RQ4), and desire (RQ5), we first computed average scores. For awareness (RQ2) and use (RQ3), these were the total number of resources participants selected in response to each question. The average satisfaction score (RQ4) was the overall satisfaction measured on the 5-point Likert item in Q51. Linear regression was then used to answer RQ2, RQ3, and RQ4, with the relevant average score as the dependent variable and language transmission decision as the independent variable. Participants with missing variables for the separate regression analyses were excluded for these analyses. In cases where strong deviations from normality and/or homogeneity of variance were observed, robustness checks using non-parametric alternatives (e.g. Wilcoxon rank sum tests) were additionally used to verify the stability of findings. To answer RQ5 (desire for additional resources), Fischer's exact test was used due to missing data. 3Following this, conventional qualitative content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005) was used on the qualitative data yielded by the open-ended pop-up question after Q53, to provide a more nuanced and in-depth understanding of expecting parents' desire for additional resources. 4 Analysis units consisted of individual participant responses. Responses containing multiple distinct desired resources were coded separately and therefore led to multiple codes per unit. Coding was inductive and data-driven, with codes remaining as close as possible to participants' wording while capturing the underlying type of support requested. Analyses followed three main stages: (1) familiarisation with the dataset and generation of initial descriptive codes; (2) comparison of initial codes across the complete dataset, grouping into broader conceptual categories, development of a coding manual with definitions, inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, and examples; and (3) application of the coding manual to the full dataset and possible revisions to the coding manual.

4. Results

>Results

4.1 Decision timing

In line with P1, our findings indicate that the decision to transmit Frisian was generally made before the birth of the child. However, this decision was made even earlier than we had assumed, namely well in advance of pregnancy, with more than three quarters of participants reporting that the decision had been made more than three months before pregnancy. A small group of participants made the decision during pregnancy, and only very few reported deciding after their child was born (see Table 1). Figure 2 shows that the Heit & Mem magazine was by far the most well-known resource among both participant groups. Awareness of the Heit & Mem website was high among participants who ended up transmitting Frisian but not among those who did not, and quite a few participants from both groups were also aware of the Yn Ferwachting booklet.

Awareness of the Hoera in lytse poppe booklet and the Talen kleuren je leven leaflet was higher among expecting participants who ended up transmitting Frisian than among those that did not. A small subset of both groups reported not being aware of any of these resources.

4.3 Use

In line with P3, participants who ended up transmitting Frisian reported using significantly more resources than those that did not (mean=0.94, sd=0.97, and mean=0.44, sd=0.72, respectively; β=0.498, SE=0.175, t(512)=2.846, p=.00461). The model explained a small proportion of the variance in use (R²=.016, adjusted R²=.014). A Wilcoxon rank-sum test, used as a robustness check for non-parametric data, indicated the same increased use by participants who ended up transmitting Frisian (W=5,432, p=.002865). Figure 3 shows the relative frequency of the type of individual resources selected per group. The Heit & Mem magazine was the most frequently-used resource among both participant groups. Those participants who ended up transmitting Frisian also reported relatively frequent use of the Heit & Mem website. The Talen kleuren je leven leaflet and the Yn ferwachting and Hoera in lytse poppe booklet were rarely used by either group, if at all. Most expecting parents who ultimately did not transmit Frisian reported using none of the listed resources, with a large group of expecting parents who ultimately did transmit Frisian reporting the same.

4.4 Satisfaction

Contrary to P4, there was no significant difference in the satisfaction with available resources between expecting participants who ended up transmitting Frisian and those who did not (mean=3.33, sd=1.26, and mean=2.96, sd=1.23, respectively). Both the regression analysis (β=0.365, SE=0.2447, t(470)=1.49, p=.137) and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test used as a robustness check for non-parametric data (W=5,023, p=.07739) indicated no statistically significant difference between the two groups. It is worth noting, however, that this question was answered by almost all expecting parents that did not end up transmitting Frisian (n=28) and the majority of this group (n=22) had previously indicated that they had not actually used any of the previously listed resources.

4.5 Desire

Although descriptive statistics indicated a greater desire for additional resources among participants who ended up transmitting Frisian than among those that did not (12.73% "yes" versus 0.00% "yes", respectively), and fewer "not sure" responses (27.10% versus 34.38%, respectively), these differences did not reach statistical significance (p=.060). The results therefore do not support P5 as there is insufficient evidence of transmission decisions being associated with participants' desire for additional resources.

To further explore participants' desire for additional resources, we conducted conventional qualitative content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005) on the answers to the aforementioned open-ended follow-up question. Fifty-three parents mentioned a total of sixty-one additional resources they would have liked to have access to. As noted above, several responses were coded multiple times because they contained multiple additional desired resources. The frequencies are presented in Table 2. The full coding manual is provided in the supplementary materials on the OSF.

4.6 Effect of resources on transmission

Contrary to P6, our results indicate that the available resources had little influence on most participants' decisions to transmit Frisian. More than half of them indicated that the question did not apply, and an additional fifth of them stated that the available resources had no influence on their decision. Only a small share of participants reported that the available resources had influenced their transmission decision to a considerable extent or completely (scores 4 and 5 combined; see Table 3).

5. Discussion

This study aimed to contribute to FLP theory by generating knowledge regarding expecting parents' engagement with resources as a form of language management preparation, as well as providing practical information to policy makers and language planners to help shape future support measures for the IGT of Frisian in Fryslân. As this is the first ever study of any aspect of FLP among expecting parents, our findings cannot be compared with research from other contexts. Accordingly, the discussion focuses on the findings of the current study and provides general recommendations.

5.1 Language transmission decision

We predicted that during the pregnancy would be the most commonly reported time during which minority language speakers decide whether (or not) to transmit Frisian (P1). Yet, our findings showed that this decision was often made even earlier: namely, in most cases, well before pregnancy. A parallel project investigating the IGT of Frisian in Nordfriesland revealed a similar pattern: the vast majority of minority language speakers had made the decision well before pregnancy, while considerably fewer made the decision during the pregnancy, and only a handful decided during the first few months of the child's life (Kircher and Heinzova, in preparation). The early timing of minority language speakers' language transmission decision suggests that, if they are to be helpful for individuals who are planning to have a family, relevant resources ought to be available well before pregnancy, rather than being introduced only during pregnancy or after birth (as is currently the case with the aforementioned taalkado). Moreover, the finding that Frisian speakers typically decide which language(s) to transmit more than three months prior to pregnancy points to the need to consider this early family planning stage as an important phase in the development of FLP.

5.2 Awareness

We predicted that expecting parents who ultimately ended up transmitting Frisian would report greater awareness of existing resources (P2). This was supported by our findings: expecting parents who ended up transmitting Frisian reported significantly greater awareness of resources than those who did not transmit the language. The Heit & Mem magazine was identified as the most well-known resource. The other resources, namely the leaflet and booklets included in the taalkado, were much less known by both groups of expecting parents. One possible explanation for this is that we asked parents about their retrospective awareness of the specific materials contained in the taalkado instead of asking whether they were aware of the taalkado itself. It is possible that parents were aware of the existence of a taalkado without being aware of the exact contents. Future research is necessary to find out about this.

5.3 Use

We predicted that expecting parents who ended up transmitting Frisian would report using significantly more resources than expecting parents who did not end up transmitting Frisian (P3). The findings supported our prediction. As the resources mentioned in the questionnaire specifically concerned raising children multilingually with Frisian, expecting parents who ended up transmitting Frisian likely used them for their intended purpose. By contrast, expecting parents who did not end up transmitting Frisian likely had less need for resources to support a language transmission decision they did not make. Out of all identified resources, the Heit & Mem magazine was the most frequently used among both groups of expecting parents. This is in line with the fact that it was the resource that the most expecting parents from both groups were aware of. The Heit & Mem website was also used relatively frequently by expecting parents who ended up transmitting Frisian. Both groups of expecting parents reported little use of the Talen kleuren je leven leaflet and the Yn ferwachting and Hoera in lytse poppe booklets. A possible explanation for this is that these materials arrived too late in the family language planning process, as the vast majority of participants indicated that -by the time they were expecting a child -they had already decided on whether (or not) to transfer Frisian. Again, more research is needed to ascertain this.

5.4 Satisfaction

We predicted that there would be a difference in satisfaction with existing resources between expecting parents who ended up transmitting Frisian and those who did not (P4). However, the findings did not support this: satisfaction with available resources did not differ significantly between the two groups. A possible explanation for this is the retrospective nature of the questionnaire. Participants may have evaluated their satisfaction with available resources based on their current circumstances rather than their experiences at the time they actually were expecting their child. We suspect this because, while the majority of the expecting parents that did not end up transmitting Frisian had indicated that they had used none of the aforementioned resources (see Figure 3), they did indicate being satisfied with the materials that they had used.

5.5 Desire for additional resources

Our findings do not support P5: there was no statistically significant association between Frisian transmission and expecting parents' desire for additional resources. Expecting parents who ended up not transmitting Frisian indicated no desire for additional resources, and only a small share of expecting parents who did end up transmitting Frisian indicated a desire for additional resources. Specifically, they would have liked to receive information on bilingual child-rearing, (earlier) access to the resources mentioned in the questionnaire, and child-directed entertainment materials. Some participants noted that they did not receive a taalkado because their child was born in hospital outside of Fryslân,foot_5 and they consequently had to register the child's birth in a different province. This made them ineligible to receive a taalkado. This is also related to the awareness of the taalkado: participants who expressed a desire for (earlier) access to the taalkado often indicated that, at the time, they did not know it existed, and that they would have liked to have received it. Although the need for additional resources exists, it is possible that we failed to find significant differences between the expecting parent groups simply due to the small sample size of the parents who did not end up transmitting Frisian. Future research could clarify whether this is the case.

5.6 Language transmission decision

Finally, the results revealed that, for most expecting parents, the available resources did not play a significant role in their decision of whether (or not) to transmit Frisian. These findings therefore do not support P6. More than half of the participants indicated that the question did not apply, while a fifth reported no influence at all. This is likely related to the fact that the majority of participants had already decided which language(s) to transmit long before they were actually expecting a child (RQ1). The resources whose influence we examined were thus provided at a point when most participants had already made up their mind regarding the IGT of Frisian. Still, there were some participants who indicated making the decision during pregnancy; and for minority language speakers like them, the qualitative data indicated a need for the resources that are currently offered post-birth to be provided sooner, to support the decision-making process of at least that share of expecting parents. There is a possibility that the resources, if provided earlier in the FLP process, could affect parents' language transmission decision.

5.7 Implications for policy makers and language planners

The present study was the first to examine expecting parents' engagement with resources as a form of language management preparation. Due to the non-representative nature of the participant sample, we make no claims about the generalisation of our findings. Nonetheless, the findings provide meaningful insights that -if they do generalise -have important implications for language policy and planning to promote the IGT of Frisian in Fryslân, and thereby the minority language's maintenance and revitalisation.

Notably, our finding that Frisian speakers tend to decide which language(s) to transfer to their children more than three months prior to pregnancy indicates that relevant resources aiming to foster the IGT of Frisian in Fryslân should be made available much earlier than is currently the case. Providing such resources to Frisian speakers who are in the early phases of family planning, before they are even expecting a child, ought to help them make informed language transmission decisions during the stage at which they actually tend to consider which language(s) to transmit.

Additionally, our results reveal the importance of increasing awareness of existing resources for expecting parents. While the Heit & Mem magazine and, to a lesser extent, the Heit & Mem website are resources for which expecting parents reported high levels of awareness, the available leaflet and booklets were relatively unknown among our participants while they were expecting. It is therefore advisable to design measures that raise awareness of the resources that are currently available for expecting parents as well as any resources that are developed in the future for Frisian speakers in the early stages of the family planning process. Raising awareness of these resources could support anyone who is unsure about whether to transmit Frisian.

Some parents retrospectively reflected on not having received the taalkado as they gave birth in hospital outside of Fryslân and consequently were ineligible for the taalkado. To counter this, we suggest making the taalkado available based on the family's place of residence rather than the child's place of birth. If there is a desire for the taalkado, it should ideally also be accessible to Frisian-speaking parents outside of Fryslân. Making these resources not only more visible but also more accessible at an earlier stage could also enhance awareness and use, thereby ensuring that expecting parents receive relevant information when they need it.

In addition to the aforementioned recommendations, we also recommend the organisation of regular public engagement activities on language science that are specifically aimed at expecting parents and parents raising young children. A holistic approach to language revitalisation should address both parents' knowledge and children's experiences with the target language. It has been shown that public engagement activities on language science, such as the Kletskoppen (Chatheads) festival, increase primary school-aged children's positive attitudes towards multilingualism (Unsworth and Knopp 2026). Implementing similar activities and meetings specifically for expecting parents and parents raising young children in Fryslân could bring families together, foster the creation of support networks, provide opportunities for positive interactions with and in Frisian, and contribute to the dissemination of evidencebased information about minority language transmission and multilingual child-rearing. Such events would also constitute an excellent venue for introducing (expecting) parents to existing resources and highlighting when and how these resources can be accessed.

These recommendations, alongside recommendations based on the aforementioned other components of our project, are currently being written up in a comprehensive research report. The aim of this research-informed science communication is to provide policy makers and language planners with the knowledge that is necessary to create effective support measures for families in Fryslân who wish to raise their children (multilingually) with Frisian. Previous research has shown that such support measures are most likely to be effective if they are tailored to families' specific situations (Fibla et al. 2022).

5.8 Strengths, limitations, and future directions

There are three major ways in which our study differs from previously-published research regarding resources. First, our study focuses on expecting parents' engagement with resources -so, prior to the birth of their children. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine expecting parents' awareness and use of, satisfaction with, and desire for resources of any kind and in any context. Second, while the aforementioned studies focused on parents who were raising their children multilingually with different language constellations, the current study is the first to compare parents who ended up raising their child multilingually or monolingually, with or without their HL. Third, our study is set in a previously unexplored context: namely the province of Fryslân in the Netherlands.

As the resources investigated in our study are specific to Frisian in Fryslân, and our sample was sizable but not representative, we make no claims regarding the generalisation of the study's findings to other minority language contexts. Especially the comparatively smaller sub-sample of expecting parents who did not end up transmitting Frisian does not allow for this. Despite repeated efforts to reach parents who were part of this target group, and even an extension of the time period during which our data were collected, we did not reach the sub-sample size we had aimed for during the preregistration. A potential explanation for this lies in the topic of the questionnaire: (expecting) parents who did not end up transmitting Frisian could have had less interest in participating in research on the transmission of Frisian, or they could have felt ashamed about their decision not to pass Frisian on to their child. Follow-up interviews with participants who did not end up transmitting Frisian, but who indicated a willingness to participate in further research, could help clarify the reasons, and such follow-up interviews could also reveal other ways to reach and recruit this target group.

Retrospectively looking at parents' engagement with resources during the time they were expecting a child constituted a very useful first step towards gaining insights into the FLP dynamics of expecting parents, and specifically their language management preparation. Ideally, future research should also examine data collected from minority language speakers at the time they were actually expecting a child. This will yield additional insights regarding their awareness and use of, satisfaction with, and desire for resources. Since there are no known studies of expecting parents in other contexts, future research could ascertain which of our findings are Fryslân-specific and which findings characterise expecting parents' language management preparation across contexts.
