To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 5 imprecise p values out of 50 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 10 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

How Code Check works

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

1 Introduction

Suicide is a major public health concern and the third leading cause of death among people aged 15-29 years globally (World Health, 2026). In Ireland, recent data from the Central Statistics Office reported 481 deaths by suicide occurring in 2024, of which 387 (80.5%) were among males (Central Statistics Office, 2026) . In addition, as outlined the Movember Institute's 'Real Face of Men's Health Republic of Ireland report, among males aged 15-34 years, external causes of injury, including suicide, account for more than half of all premature deaths, highlighting the disproportionate burden of suicide among younger men (Movember Institute, 2025). The consequences of such extend beyond those lives lost, affecting the mental health and wellbeing of bereaved families, friends and communities (Cerel et al., 2019; Pitman et al., 2014). Understanding the disproportionate burden of suicide mortality among males requires attention to the social conditions and gendered experiences that shape vulnerability and access to support among men and boys (WHO, 2018). A social determinants perspective situates suicide within the wider economic, social and environmental conditions of people's lives (Pirkis et al., 2017). Evidence linking unemployment, low income and relationship breakdown with higher suicide mortality among men reinforces the importance of considering material circumstances and relational disruption alongside individual psychological factors (Hughes et al., 2025). A complementary gendered lens considers how masculine norms and expectations shape men's experiences of distress, relationships and engagement with support, while recognizing differences among men across social circumstances and contexts (Nordin et al., 2024; Seidler et al., 2016; WHO, 2018). These perspectives provide a basis for examining suicide risk among men in relation to both their psychosocial experiences and the settings in which they live, study and seek support.

Universities provide an important setting in which to examine these social and gendered influences on mental health. For many students, entering university coincides with emerging adulthood, a period of identity exploration and changing roles and relationships (Arnett, 2000). The transition can involve increased academic demands, financial pressures and the challenge of establishing social connections, sometimes away from familiar support networks (Worsley et al., 2021). However, students span different ages and life stages, making it important to consider diverse circumstances and experiences of university life. Research highlights how teaching environments, accommodation and relationships with staff and peers are key considerations in shaping students' experiences of connection and support (Worsley et al., 2021). Within Irish higher education, university belonging and social connectedness are associated with better mental wellbeing, highlighting the importance of opportunities for connection in students' everyday university experiences (McGrath et al., 2026). These findings support attention to the social and organizational conditions that enable connection, participation and belonging within higher education (McGrath et al., 2026; WHO, 2015). For men attending university, this provides a rationale for examining psychosocial experiences alongside masculine norms, within an institutional setting that offers opportunities for both prevention and support.

Understanding these experiences within university settings requires attention to the social circumstances students encounter and the psychological resources available to them. Financial strain and stressful life events, including relationship breakdown, family conflict and employment difficulties, have been associated with suicidal thoughts and behaviors (Currier et al., 2016; Pirkis et al., 2024). Relational experiences are also relevant where loneliness is associated with subsequent suicidal ideation and behavior (McClelland et al., 2020), while lower interpersonal trust has been associated with subsequent suicidal ideation in population-based research (Kim et al., 2017). For boys and young men, the availability of family, friends or other potential sources of support does not necessarily mean that disclosing distress feels safe. Trust, familiarity and expectations about how others will respond influence informal help-seeking, alongside concerns about stigma and burdening others (Burke et al., 2022). Recent research with men further highlights how anticipated judgement and uncertainty about acceptable emotional disclosure can constrain access to peer support, even where peers are willing to help (Sharp et al., 2025). In addition accessibility, anonymity, affordability and responsiveness to men's circumstances are cited as important considerations for strengthening trust in This is a provisional file, not the final typeset article services (Movember Institute, 2025). These relational and institutional conditions provide a context for considering hopelessness and resilience: negative expectations about the future are associated with suicidal ideation and behavior (David Klonsky et al., 2012; Gómez-Tabares et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2020; O'Connor & Nock, 2014; Smith et al., 2008; Tsujii et al., 2020). while a greater perceived capacity to recover from stress has been associated with lower suicidal ideation (Kim et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2008). Examining these factors together enables consideration of men's social circumstances and psychological experiences without locating responsibility for managing adversity solely within individuals.

Indeed, these social and psychological experiences can also be gendered, with masculine norms shaping expectations about emotional expression, relationships and responses to distress. Masculinities are socially situated and negotiated through relationships and institutions, meaning that the expectations men encounter, and the consequences of conforming to or departing from them, vary across contexts (Galdas, 2026). Norms emphasizing self-reliance and emotional control may constrain disclosure and access to support where vulnerability carries a perceived social cost, while relationships that legitimize openness and mutual care can create opportunities for support (Galdas, 2026; McGrath et al., 2022; Sharp et al., 2025). Associations with suicide-related outcomes also differ across particular norms and how they are measured, cautioning against treating masculinity as a uniform risk factor (Starkey & Moore, 2026). Longitudinal evidence further suggests that particular masculine norms may relate to suicidal ideation through specific psychosocial experiences. Greater conformity to selfreliance predicted subsequent perceived burdensomeness, which in turn predicted suicidal ideation, whereas corresponding pathways involving thwarted belongingness were not supported (Gagliano et al., 2025). These findings strengthen the rationale for examining masculine norms alongside psychosocial factors while recognising that relationships may differ across norms and interpersonal experiences. Men's experiences of these norms are further shaped by age, socioeconomic circumstances, ethnicity and sexual orientation. For sexual minority men, attention to stigma, exclusion and expectations of heterosexual masculinity is therefore important, rather than interpreting sexual orientation itself as an inherent source of vulnerability (Fernandez et al., 2026; Galdas, 2026).

Contemporary measurement research also highlights that individual conformity scores capture particular dimensions of masculine norms and may inadequately represent their relational and cultural contexts (Fisher et al., 2026). University settings bring these questions into focus because students negotiate relationships, identities and expectations of achievement and independence within shared educational and social environments. Peer relationships and institutional practices provide contexts in which gendered expectations may be reinforced, challenged or accommodated, while universities offer opportunities to connect individual support with everyday conditions that enable belonging and participation (Galdas, 2026; McGrath et al., 2026; Newton et al., 2016). Examining masculine norms alongside psychosocial factors within this setting can therefore inform gender-responsive prevention, while recognising that individual measures capture only part of these wider processes.

An important question for prevention is whether specific masculine norms are associated with elevated suicide risk when considered alongside men's psychosocial experiences and social circumstances.

Earlier analysis of the survey underpinning this study identified associations between psychosocial factors, conformity to particular masculine norms, and suicide literacy, stigma and help-seeking attitudes among men at a university in Ireland ( O'Donnell et al., 2025). These findings highlighted the relevance of men's social and gendered experiences to their understanding of suicide and attitudes towards seeking support. They also provided a rationale for examining whether psychosocial factors and masculine norms were associated with elevated suicide risk itself, extending the focus from knowledge and attitudes to suicide-related thoughts and behaviors. The present analysis extends this work by focusing on elevated suicide risk and examining associations across sociodemographic, behavioral, psychosocial and masculinity-related domains. Considering these domains together may help identify priorities for gender-responsive prevention, while recognizing that individual-level measures capture only part of the wider social and institutional contexts shaping men's health. This study therefore aimed to examine associations between sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral factors, psychosocial factors and conformity to masculine norms and elevated suicide risk among men at a university in Ireland.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Study design and setting

This study used data from an anonymous cross-sectional online survey of men attending a multicampus technological university in Ireland. The wider survey examined mental health, suicide-related experiences, suicide literacy, stigma, help-seeking and conformity to masculine norms. An earlier publication reported analyses of suicide literacy, stigma and help-seeking attitudes (O'Donnell et al., 2025). The present study examined sociodemographic, behavioral, psychosocial and masculinityrelated factors associated with elevated suicide risk.

2.2 Participants and recruitment

Approximately 9,000 students were recorded as male in university enrolment figures at the time of the study. These administrative figures provided an approximate recruitment population rather than an exact count of students meeting the study's gender-based eligibility criteria.

Recruitment took place in November 2023 and coincided with campus activities marking International Men's Day. Invitations containing study information and a link to the Qualtrics survey were circulated through university email. Additional recruitment included posters with QR codes in communal spaces and promotion at campus health promotion stands. Students could access and complete the survey online at a time convenient to them. Participation was voluntary, and 512 students participated.

Recruitment across campuses and academic departments sought to broaden participation, although the resulting sample was self-selected.

Ethical approval was granted by the South East Technological University Carlow Ethics Committee (reference 380). Participants received study information and provided electronic informed consent before accessing the questionnaire. All participants were provided with information about university and community mental health support services on completing the survey. Participants who indicated suicidal thoughts or behaviors were also shown targeted messages directing them to appropriate support services.

3.1.8 Multivariable associations with elevated suicide risk

Hierarchical binary logistic regression examined associations between demographic, behavioral, psychosocial and masculine norm variables and scoring at or above the SBQ-R screening threshold (≥7), relative to scoring below the threshold (<7). The analysis included 305 participants with complete data across all model variables, of whom 122 (40.0%) scored at or above the threshold.

Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no evidence of problematic collinearity, with variance inflation factors ranging from 1.16 to 1.59 and tolerance values ranging from .63 to .86. The demographic block was not statistically significant overall (χ²(7) = 12.23, p = .093; Nagelkerke R² = .053). Adding behavioral variables significantly improved model fit (Δχ²(7) = 24.18, p = .001), increasing Nagelkerke R² to .152. The addition of psychosocial variables produced a further improvement (Δχ²(5) = 50.45, p < .001; Nagelkerke R² = .335). Adding the masculine norm subscales did not significantly improve model fit (Δχ²(6) = 3.22, p = .780), with Nagelkerke R² increasing to .346 (Table 3). The final model was statistically significant (χ²(25) = 90.08, p < .001). Higher loneliness (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.24, 95% CI 1.03-1.48) and hopelessness (aOR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.04-1.45) scores were associated with higher odds of scoring at or above the SBQ-R threshold, whereas higher resilience scores were associated with lower odds (aOR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.33-0.75). Participants identifying as heterosexual had lower adjusted odds than those not identifying as heterosexual (aOR = 0.44, 95% CI 0.21-0.93).

With drug use and heavy drug use included simultaneously, non-drug users had lower adjusted odds than drug users (aOR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.27-0.93). The additional contrast between heavy and non-heavy drug users was not statistically significant (aOR = 2.04, 95% CI 0.57-7.29). Financial stress, generalized trust and masculine norm subscales were not statistically significantly associated with the outcome after adjustment for the other variables in the final model (Table 4). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test provided no evidence of lack of fit (χ²(8) = 10.07, p = .261). At a probability cut-off of .50, the model correctly classified 77.0% of participants in the analysis sample, including 85.8% of those below and 63.9% of those at or above the screening threshold.

This study examined psychosocial factors and masculine norms associated with suicide risk among men at a university in Ireland. Among participants with available SBQ-R data, 42.5% scored at or above the screening threshold for elevated suicide risk. In the fully adjusted model, greater loneliness and hopelessness were associated with higher odds of scoring at or above this threshold, whereas greater resilience was associated with lower odds. Differences were also observed by heterosexual identification and drug-use status. Adding psychosocial factors substantially improved model fit beyond demographic and behavioural characteristics. Although several masculine norm subscales differed between participants below and at or above the threshold in unadjusted analyses, their addition did not significantly improve the multivariable model. These findings highlight the need to consider men's social experiences and psychological resources alongside gendered norms when examining suicide risk in university settings. They do not establish the direction of these relationships or imply that masculine norms are unimportant but highlight that the selected subscales did not demonstrate additional statistical contributions after adjustment for the other measured factors.

The association between loneliness and elevated suicide risk places the experience of connection at the center of understanding men's mental health within university settings. This finding is consistent with prospective evidence linking loneliness with suicidal ideation and behavior, and with theoretical accounts emphasizing the significance of belonging to suicidal distress (McClelland et al., 2020; Van Orden et al., 2010). Connection, however, involves more than the presence of social contacts.

Emotional and social loneliness represent distinguishable experiences among university students, highlighting why participation in a peer network may coexist with an absence of close, dependable support (Diehl et al., 2018). A gendered interpretation asks whether men can express uncertainty, acknowledge difficulty and seek care without anticipating a loss of acceptance or standing. Masculine expectations concerning emotional control and self-reliance can influence these negotiations, while distress may remain concealed within efforts to maintain an acceptable social identity (Cleary, 2012; Nordin et al., 2024; Seidler et al., 2016; Seidler et al., 2018). These dynamics also have implications for structural and systems-level change. Universities shape opportunities for connection through teaching arrangements, campus spaces, student participation and access to support. Addressing loneliness therefore requires coordinated action across academic, administrative and support functions to reduce barriers to participation and create environments in which vulnerability is met with recognition and care. This includes examining whose circumstances are accommodated by existing arrangements, particularly students whose commuting, employment or financial pressures limit their opportunities to develop dependable relationships (McGrath et al., 2026; Newton et al., 2016).

This interpretation also moves beyond the framing of men as inherently reluctant or unable to connect (Sharp et al., 2022). Shared activity, practical assistance and informal interaction can provide meaningful opportunities for care, reciprocity and health engagement (McGrath et al., 2022; Oliffe et al., 2020). Gender-transformative work within community settings illustrates how approaches grounded in familiar social settings can build on these resources while broadening opportunities for support and participation (McGrath et al., 2022). The issue is therefore whether men's relationships accommodate vulnerability and changing needs rather than conforming to a particular model of emotional intimacy. Research concerning mental health within and after men's intimate partnerships similarly draws attention to relationship dynamics, rather than relationship status alone, in understanding access to support (Oliffe et al., 2022). These perspectives position the quality, flexibility and perceived safety of relationships as central questions for gender-responsive prevention.

Within higher education, these relational processes intersect with belonging and sense of place. Recent Irish evidence associates university belonging and social connectedness with better mental wellbeing, reinforcing the importance of environments in which students feel recognized, valued and able to participate meaningfully (McGrath et al., 2026). The transition into university involves renegotiating relationships and support alongside new academic and personal expectations (Worsley et al., 2021).

For younger men, this also involves navigating expectations of independence, success and emotional openness, informed by experiences carried forward from boyhood and earlier social settings (Rice et al., 2021; Rice et al., 2018). These expectations may be contradictory where encouragement to communicate vulnerability can coexist with enduring expectations of self-sufficiency, economic provision and achievement. Breadwinner identities for instance, may remain culturally influential even where secure employment, affordable housing and financial independence are difficult to attain (Syrda, 2020). This can create intergenerational tensions around what becoming an adult man should involve, particularly when inherited markers of success are poorly aligned with contemporary opportunities to achieve them (Hughes et al., 2025; Mokhwelepa & Sumbane, 2025). From this perspective, uncertainty about identity and the future is situated within a potential mismatch between gendered expectations and the material conditions through which they can be realized. These conditions also shape opportunities for connection during university life. Financial pressures, paid employment and commuting can constrain the time and flexibility available to develop relationships, particularly where belonging depends on sustained participation beyond scheduled teaching (McGrath et al., 2026).

Changing expectations of masculinity may open possibilities for more supportive relationships, but these possibilities require environments in which vulnerability is accepted and participation is This is a provisional file, not the final typeset article practically attainable. The potential for vulnerability to deepen connection depends partly on how others respond and whether that response is experienced as understanding, validating and caring.

Earlier experiences of ridicule, dismissal or support may shape both men's willingness to disclose difficulty and their expectations of how disclosure will be received (Neilson & Maitland, 2025).

Qualitative evidence also identifies uncertainty about how much distress can be shared within male friendships, alongside experiences of support that fails to meet the needs being expressed [19].

Encouraging disclosure therefore needs to be accompanied by attention to peers' capacity to recognise and respond to vulnerability. For universities, fostering a sense of place consequently involves addressing both the relational culture of campus life and the material barriers that make connection less accessible to some students (Higher Education Authority, 2021).

These tensions also provide a context for understanding the association between hopelessness and elevated suicide risk. Hopelessness was associated with elevated suicide risk, consistent with evidence linking negative expectations about the future with suicidal ideation and behaviour (David Klonsky et al., 2012; O'Connor & Nock, 2014; Tsujii et al., 2020). This finding extends the discussion from present relationships to students' perceptions of what lies ahead. University is often understood as a pathway towards opportunity and independence, yet this period also involves uncertainty about identity, educational progression and future prospects (Arnett, 2000; Keshoofy et al., 2023).

Expectations of achievement and self-sufficiency may influence how some men interpret setbacks and whether they feel able to acknowledge difficulty (Cleary, 2012; Rice et al., 2021). Beyond a psychosocial resource, hope is therefore relevant in relation to the social and material conditions through which a viable future becomes imaginable. A public health response would connect support for distress with practical assistance and accessible pathways through academic, financial and personal difficulties (Higher Education Authority, 2021).

In addition, the inverse association between resilience and elevated suicide risk complements this emphasis on hope and support. Resilience is recognized as relevant to suicidal ideation and prevention, with perceived capacity to recover from stress offering one dimension of how people respond to adversity (Kim et al., 2020; Sher, 2019; Smith et al., 2008). Within men's health promotion, however, resilience should not become an additional expectation to withstand difficulty independently (Galdas, 2026). Such a framing risks reinforcing forms of self-reliance associated with poorer mental healthrelated outcomes and barriers to help-seeking (Galdas, 2026; Wong et al., 2017). A gender-responsive approach instead recognizes flexibility, interdependence and accepting assistance as compatible with resilience, while acknowledging that some sources of adversity lie beyond individual control. This connects work on coping resources with the development of supportive environments, avoiding an exclusive focus on helping students adapt to pressures that institutions may be able to reduce (Abulfaraj et al., 2024; Newton et al., 2016). Universities also operate within wider housing, employment and economic conditions that they cannot resolve alone. Their role nevertheless includes mitigating the effects of these pressures, advocating for structural responses and ensuring that students' difficulty in managing them is not interpreted as a personal failure of resilience.

Indeed, attention to the conditions that support resilience also requires recognizing that exposure to adversity and access to support are unequally distributed among men. Differences by sexual orientation reinforce this point, with lower adjusted odds of elevated suicide risk among participants identifying as heterosexual broadly consistent with evidence of inequalities in suicidality by sexual orientation (Ramchand et al., 2022). Minority stress theory locates these inequalities within socially produced pressures, including stigma, discrimination and anticipated rejection, rather than treating sexual orientation as an inherent vulnerability (Frost & Meyer, 2023). This provides a gendered lens through which to consider whose identities are affirmed and whose belonging may be conditional on conformity to prevailing expectations of masculinity and heterosexuality. As such, inclusive prevention should extend beyond designated services to everyday experiences of recognition, safety and participation.

The objective is an institutional environment in which men with diverse identities can access support and contribute to university life without concealment or pressure to conform ( O'Donnell et al., 2025).

Attention to the conditions of participation also raises questions about how students find connection within peer environments, including those where substance use forms part of social interaction (Stritzel, 2022).

Drug-use status was also associated with elevated suicide risk, with lower adjusted odds among nonusers than among participants reporting use even below the heavy-use threshold. This aligns with evidence linking substance use with suicidal thoughts and behaviors and supports integrating substance-use responses within student mental health provision (Giugovaz et al., 2024; Welsh et al., 2019). A gendered perspective places these behaviors within the wider contexts of coping, peer affiliation and expectations concerning risk-taking and emotional expression (Rice et al., 2021; WHO, 2018). This raises a potential tension where settings that provide social connection also involve exposure to harm. Prevention approaches should therefore combine accessible, non-judgmental support with varied opportunities for belonging that do not depend on substance use.

The findings concerning masculine norms reinforce the importance of distinguishing particular expectations from masculinity as a whole. Several subscales differed between the SBQ-R threshold groups in unadjusted analyses, but their addition did not significantly improve the fully adjusted model.

This finding sits alongside evidence of domain-specific relationships between masculine norms, mental health, suicidal ideation and help-seeking (Benakovic et al., 2025; Wong et al., 2017). Interpretation also requires attention to how masculinity is operationalized. The CMNI subscales assess individual conformity to specified norms and do not encompass the full range of masculine identities, practices or relationships which raises a broader conceptual issue of which dimensions of gender are made visible through standardized measures, and which remain outside their scope (Fisher et al., 2026). This distinction is consistent with a relational understanding of masculinities as configurations of practice shaped by gender relations, power and historical and social contexts, rather than fixed sets of individual traits (Connell & Messerschmidt, 2005). Masculine expectations may be reinforced, contested or reworked across relationships and settings, with different consequences for men's opportunities to express distress and access care. Standardised conformity scores therefore provide useful evidence about particular norms, while offering a partial account of how masculinities are enacted and negotiated across cultural contexts, generations and institutional environments. A contextual approach considers individual conformity alongside expectations operating within relationships and institutions, including how vulnerability is received and which forms of assistance become acceptable and accessible (Galdas, 2026; Nordin et al., 2024). For university suicide prevention, this directs attention towards responsive individual support and institutional conditions that enable diverse groups of men to experience belonging, sustain hope and obtain assistance. It brings gender-responsive practice into a settings-based public health approach, addressing students' immediate needs alongside the environments that shape their opportunities for wellbeing.

4.1 Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its examination of demographic, behavioral, psychosocial and masculine norm variables within a single analytical framework, contributing evidence on suicide risk among men in an Irish university setting. Several limitations should nevertheless inform interpretation. The crosssectional design prevents conclusions about temporal order or causality. Loneliness, hopelessness, resilience and substance use may precede, accompany or follow suicide-related distress. The findings cannot establish mediation or explain why the masculine norm subscales did not contribute significantly after adjustment. Similarly, scoring at or above the SBQ-R threshold represents a screening indicator rather than a clinical assessment of current suicide risk. The proportion exceeding this threshold should not be interpreted as the prevalence of current suicidal intent or the likelihood of subsequent suicide. Recruitment relied on voluntary participation at a single multi-campus university, creating potential for self-selection bias. Students experiencing distress or with a particular interest in mental health may have been more likely to participate. The findings therefore cannot be assumed to represent all men attending the university, students at other institutions or men outside higher education. Although the sample predominantly comprised younger adults, its wider age range also means that the findings should not be interpreted exclusively as reflecting young men's experiences.

The regression included 305 complete cases from 515 dataset records. Exclusion of participants with missing model data reduced precision and may have introduced selection bias if those included differed systematically from those excluded. Self-reported measures may be affected by recall and social desirability, particularly for sensitive experiences and behaviors. The CMNI subscales capture individual endorsement of particular norms rather than the full range of relational, cultural and institutional processes through which gender operates. Longitudinal and qualitative research would help clarify how gendered social conditions, relationships and psychological experiences interact over time.

This study sought to examine psychosocial factors and masculine norms associated with suicide risk among men in a university setting. Understanding these associations through a gendered lens requires attention to how expectations of masculinity interact with the relationships, institutions and material circumstances shaping men's lives. The conditions under which men feel valued, envisage a viable future and seek support are central to this understanding, alongside the inequalities that make these possibilities less accessible to some men. Gender-responsive prevention must therefore extend beyond encouraging men to disclose distress or cope more effectively to address how vulnerability is received, how belonging becomes possible and whether meaningful support follows. Universities have a role in shaping these conditions through everyday relationships, inclusive institutional practices and practical responses to the pressures students face. This places shared responsibility for connection, care and opportunity at the center of suicide prevention.
