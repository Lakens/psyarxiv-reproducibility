Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

• Participants with unusable full-condition recordings will be excluded before analysis.

data_exclusion_criteria Participant-level inclusion
A participant dataset will be included if the participant:
• Meets all inclusion criteria and no exclusion criteria at screening,
• Completes the full laboratory session (all four conditions),
• Provides usable physiological recordings for the primary signals needed for analysis (at minimum: respiration; and for cardiac outcomes: ECG).
Participant-level exclusion
A participant will be excluded from all confirmatory analyses if any of the following apply:
• Withdrawal of consent or incomplete session (did not complete all four conditions),
• Protocol violation that meaningfully compromises interpretation (e.g., removing sensors for a substantial portion of the task; repeated talking/movement despite reminders; failure to follow safety instructions),
• Missing or unusable respiration signal for one or more full conditions (because respiration is required for the primary entrainment outcomes and for computing bin-level and baseline–stimulation difference scores),
• For ECG-based outcomes specifically: unusable ECG signal for one or more full conditions (participants may still be retained for respiration-only outcomes if respiration is usable).
Participants who are found post hoc to meet an exclusion criterion (e.g., undisclosed relevant diagnosis, pregnancy) will be removed.
Segment/bin-level exclusion (within included participants)
Data will be excluded at the bin or breath/beat level (rather than excluding the entire participant) when artifacts are localized:
• 30-second bins with clear sensor dropouts, clipping, or gross motion artifacts will be marked invalid and excluded from bin-level analyses.
• Respiration (breath-level): breaths with failed detection (e.g., incomplete cycles) will be excluded from breath-derived metrics (RR, IER, breath duration variability).
• ECG (beat-level): ectopic beats and detection errors will be corrected using standard artifact correction; segments with excessive R-peak detection failure will be excluded.
• ETCO₂ (breath-level): breaths without a reliable expiratory plateau (or otherwise invalid end-tidal estimate) will be excluded from ETCO₂ averaging.
If, after bin/breath/beat exclusions, a condition has insufficient valid data to compute reliable segment means (e.g., fewer than 4 valid 30-second bins in baseline or stimulation), that condition will be treated as missing for that DV for that participant.
Outliers
Outlier handling will be applied at two levels:
1. Bin-level outliers (primary approach): within each participant and DV, 30-second bins with values > ±3 SD from that participant’s mean (for that DV within that condition/segment) will be flagged as outliers and excluded for bin-level analyses.
2. Aggregated outcomes (sensitivity approach): if condition-level Delta scores (stimulation − baseline) contain extreme values, analyses will be repeated with and without those observations to assess robustness. Both results will be reported if conclusions differ.
Missing data
• Mixed-effects models will use all available valid observations (i.e., they can accommodate some missing bins/conditions).
• Participants will not be excluded solely due to a small number of missing bins, provided segment-level summaries remain computable.
• Participant-level exclusions will be limited to cases where primary signals are unusable for a full condition or the session is incomplete.
All exclusions (participant-level and bin/breath/beat-level) will be documented, including counts and reasons.
missing_data_handling Analyses will be conducted on all available valid data. Linear mixed-effects models will use all non-missing observations (bins/segments) without imputing missing values, under the assumption that missingness is primarily due to technical artifacts (e.g., transient sensor dropouts) rather than the experimental manipulation.
No imputation is planned for physiological or questionnaire outcomes.
Handling rules:
• Bin-level missingness: Invalid 30-second bins (artifact/dropout) will be excluded; remaining bins will be used. Mixed models will naturally accommodate unequal numbers of bins.
• Breath/beat-level missingness: Invalid breaths (respiration/ETCO₂) and invalid beats (ECG) will be excluded during preprocessing; segment/bin summaries will be computed from the remaining valid samples.
• Condition-level missingness: If a participant has insufficient valid data to compute a baseline or stimulation mean for a given condition and DV (e.g., fewer than 4 valid 30-second bins in baseline or stimulation), that condition will be treated as missing for that DV for that participant.
• Participant-level missingness: Participants with unusable respiration data for an entire condition will be excluded from confirmatory analyses requiring respiration-derived measures; participants may still be retained for other outcomes if those signals are usable and the session is complete.
The amount and pattern of missing data (by signal, condition, and segment) will be reported, along with reasons (e.g., sensor dropout, plateau detection failure for ETCO₂).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Full text

Abstract

Auditory feedback may influence breathing without explicit instructions to regulate it, but its precise impact remains unclear. We examined responses to adaptive and nonadaptive breath-like sounds during passive listening. Thirty-nine young adults completed four counterbalanced conditions: nonrhythmic filtered noise (Static), rhythmic sounds progressively slowing or speeding relative to initial breathing rate (Slowing and Speeding respectively), and sound adapting continuously to respiratory frequency and phase with a prescribed phase delay relative to respiration (Adaptive). Each condition included 4 min of nominal silence followed by 4 min of stimulation. Primary outcomes were breathing rate slopes, auditory-respiratory phase locking as a measure of auditory-respiratory coordination, respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and subjective relaxation. Breathing-rate trajectories differed across conditions; Adaptive showed a more negative slope than Static, although average rates were similar. Phase locking was higher and more sustained during Adaptive than during the nonadaptive rhythmic conditions. Respiratory sinus arrhythmia trajectories differed, but planned contrasts did not survive multiplicity correction. A relaxation composite score did not differ by condition, but an item-level analysis indicated less physical relaxation after Adaptive than Static. Secondary outcomes provided no evidence of increased cardiorespiratory coupling or reduced upper-trapezius muscle activity. Interestingly, the negative breathing-rate trajectories remained significantly different in Adaptive compared to Static after retaining only the 25 respondents that did not notice the sound's adaptation to their breathing. These findings support adaptive auditory stimulation as a means of supporting auditory-respiratory coordination during uninstructed listening, with a modest and relative change in breathing rate. Clinical benefits and independence from intentional breathing control remain to be tested.

Introduction

Breathing is an accessible target for psychophysiological self-regulation because it is generated automatically while remaining sensitive to voluntary control and sensory context. Slow breathing and heart-rate-variability (HRV) biofeedback leverage this flexibility to modify cardiorespiratory activity. In resonance-frequency biofeedback, breathing near an individually determined frequency, typically approximately six breaths per minute, amplifies cardiovascular oscillations through interactions between respiratory and baroreflex processes (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014). Breathing interventions can also reduce self-reported stress and anxiety, although a meta-analysis of randomized trials identified methodological limitations and variation across studies that constrain general conclusions (Fincham et al., 2023). For applied psychophysiology, the challenge is to establish which forms of respiratory guidance change breathing, which changes accompany subjective benefit, and under what conditions those benefits occur. Slow paced breathing is not equivalent to relaxation. The resulting breathing pattern and the effort involved may both shape the experience. Previous work suggests that relaxation can vary with the inhalation-to-exhalation ratio (Van Diest et al., 2014), while following a prescribed rhythm can lead to hyperventilation in untrained subjects (Szulczewski, 2019) and require effort, particularly when it differs from spontaneous breathing (Fincham et al., 2023; Gholamrezaei et al., 2021; Stark et al., 2000). Breathing instructions may also influence attention and expectations alongside respiratory changes (Vlemincx & Cortez-Vázquez, 2024). This makes it worthwhile to explore guidance that supports respiratory adjustment without requiring continuous deliberate control. Sound provides a possible route to such guidance without imposing additional task demands. Musical rhythms can influence respiratory timing, with earlier work documenting changes in breath-period variability and in the relationships between breathing and perceived rhythm (Bernardi et al., 2017; Haas et al., 1986; Mollakazemi et al., 2019). The auditory signal also matters: previous research has shown how ecological breathing sounds reduce breath-duration variability more than artificial sounds with comparable temporal features (Murgia et al., 2016). These observations suggest that sound can provide temporal information relevant to respiratory timing. Sonification extends this approach by making sound contingent on physiological activity. In an earlier study, we developed tempo-and phase-aligned breathing sonification using participants' keyboard indications of inhalation and exhalation (Van Kerrebroeck & Maes, 2021). That procedure demonstrated a practical route to respiratory feedback, but reporting each breath using keyboard presses necessarily engaged participants with their breathing. Evidence for respiratory modulation without deliberate synchronization comes from Janson et al. (2013), who presented delayed versions of participants' breathing signals as auditory and visual feedback. Participants were instructed to breathe comfortably without consciously adapting to the signals; moderate delays slowed breathing in 11 of 24 participants, although responses varied and feedback induced irregular breathing in some individuals (Janson et al., 2013). In a complementary approach, (Leslie et al., 2019) presented ambient music with fixed, personalized, or continuously respiratory-driven amplitude modulation while participants performed an attention-demanding task. Breathing slowed across the music conditions, with the strongest effect for a tempo set below the individual's baseline rate. Together, these studies motivate to investigate the potential of personalized sound with continuous physiological sensing for respiratory regulation without an instruction to synchronize. Building on this work, a central question is whether continuous adaptation offers an advantage over a personalized auditory rhythm that changes independently of breathing. An open-loop rhythm may begin near a person's spontaneous breathing rate but become increasingly mismatched as its tempo changes. Continuous adaptation could preserve a temporal relationship as breathing fluctuates, while an added phase offset may encourage gradual changes in respiratory timing. This creates the possibility of guiding breathing through sustained coordination with sound. Evaluating this possibility requires examining both auditory-respiratory phase locking and changes in breathing rate: phase locking captures the coordination maintained by the feedback, while changes in breathing rate indicate whether participants' respiration also adjusts during exposure. The present experiment compared four auditory conditions during passive listening: nonrhythmic filtered noise (Static), breath-like sounds that progressively slowed or accelerated relative to initial breathing rate (Slowing and Speeding), and a breath-like sound that continuously adapted to respiratory frequency and phase with a configured phase delay (Adaptive). Participants received no instruction to regulate their breathing. Four preregistered hypotheses on primary outcomes addressed auditory-respiratory coordination and relaxation. Relative to Static, Slowing and Adaptive were expected to produce lower breathing rates slopes (H1), greater respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA; H3), and greater subjective relaxation (H4), with the opposite pattern expected during Speeding. Auditory-respiratory phase locking values (PLVs) were expected to be larger in Adaptive than Slowing, and larger during Slowing than Speeding (H2). Hypotheses on secondary outcomes predicted stronger cardiorespiratory phase locking during Slowing and Adaptive than Speeding (S1). They also predicted higher relative CO₂ levels (S2), lower uppertrapezius muscle activity (S3), and a lower hyperventilation composite score (S4) during Slowing and Adaptive than Static, with the opposite pattern during Speeding.

Together, these analyses examined how adaptive auditory stimulation shapes auditory-respiratory coordination and whether this is accompanied by physiological and subjective relaxation.

Methods

Participants

Forty-two adults were recruited through the university's experiment-participation platform and tested in the Health Psychology laboratory at KU Leuven. Three participants with cold-related obstructed breathing or excessive movement were excluded before statistical analysis. The final sample comprised 39 participants, aged 18-21 years (M = 18.59, SD = 0.88). 34 reported female and five male sex assigned at birth. All completed the four conditions. Six participants reported formal musical training (M = 6.0, SD = 2.37). Among the 39 participants, 15 performed physical exercise or sports 1-2 times per week, 13 exercised 3-4 times per week, four exercised at least five times per week, six never exercised, and one preferred not to say. The recruitment target of 40 allowed for 10% attrition and was based on a G*Power analysis for a four-condition within-participant design, assuming Cohen's f = .25, α = .05, and 80% power, informed by Van Diest et al. (2014). Eligibility required age 18-35 years and comprehension of written and spoken English. Exclusion criteria were recent psychotropic-medication use; cardiac or respiratory disorders, hearing impairment, or psychiatric disorders within the preceding two years; pregnancy; and breathing-exercise or mindfulness practice at least twice weekly during the preceding month. Participants avoided strenuous exercise and alcohol for at least 12 h before testing. They provided written informed consent and received course credits. The KU Leuven ethics committee approved the study (G-2025-10319).

Design

The within-participant design comprised Static, Slowing, Speeding, and Adaptive conditions, each presented once. Condition order was counterbalanced across participants using all 24 possible sequences. Each sequence was used once or twice across the 39 participants analyzed. Each condition comprised 4 min of nominal silence followed by 4 min of auditory stimulation. The study was preregistered at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/GCVQE. Primary outcomes were breathing rate slopes, auditory-respiratory PLV, RSA, and subjective relaxation. Secondary outcomes were cardiorespiratory PLV, % change in end-expiratory carbon dioxide levels, upper-trapezius muscle activity, and Nijmegen Questionnaire symptoms. Procedure Participants first completed demographic, baseline-state, and individual-difference questionnaires, followed by sensor attachment and signal checks (approximately 20 min). A 4-min familiarization with constant-tempo sound also included three approximately 5-s arm elevations, separated by approximately 10 s, to provide a functional EMG reference. Experimental EMG amplitudes were normalized to this recording (see Online Resource for further details). During the experimental trials, participants were instructed to sit quietly, remain still, and listen to the played back sounds. They could keep their eyes open or closed and received no instruction to regulate or synchronize their breathing. Each trial was followed by experience ratings (approximately 5 min) and a short break (approximately 2 min). Final questionnaires and debriefing followed the four trials. The visit duration was approximately 90 min.

Stimulus and materials

The stimulus was synthesized in Pure Data from filtered pink noise, with amplitude and spectral modulation producing a breath-like sound. It was delivered through Sennheiser HD 620S headphones at a reported level of 50 dB. Participants confirmed comfortable, low-to-medium loudness during familiarization. Static used the filtered noise without a rhythmic amplitude or filtered frequency envelope (see Online Resource for further details). Slowing and Speeding used the amplitude and spectral modulated sound that began at an estimate of the participant's spontaneous respiratory frequency at the end of the silent period and progressed linearly toward 80% and 120% of that initial frequency, respectively, over 4 min of the stimulation period. A 5-s audio gain ramp began at approximately 235 s, with full dynamic control beginning at 240 s. Online frequency estimates used a rolling 10-s respiratory window and were updated every 0.5 s. Adaptive used ongoing respiratory frequency and phase to control an auditory oscillator. The controller combined the rolling frequency estimate with a sinusoidal phase-error correction and a configured phase offset of -0.10 cycles (-36°), selected through pilot testing. This offset was intended to encourage slower breathing by positioning the auditory rhythm slightly behind the respiratory cycle. Respiratory activity continuously influenced the sound, and any participant response could alter the signal subsequently driving the controller. The audio waveform and audio phase was recorded alongside the physiological signals. Online estimation, signal processing, and controller details are provided in Online Resource, Section S1. Physiological signals were acquired with a BIOPAC MP160 at 2,000 Hz. A Vernier respiration belt connected to a gas-pressure sensor and positioned at the lower rib cage of participants recorded respiratory movements around the torso. Breathing rate and phase refer to quantities derived from these movements. Electrocardiography used a Vernier ECG Sensor with right-clavicle and left-lower rib electrode positions and a right-lower rib reference. Bipolar upper-trapezius electromyography (EMG) used pre-gelled Ag/AgCl electrodes aligned with the muscle fibres near the midpoint between C7 and the acromion, approximately 20 mm apart, with a reference over C7. An Arduino interface supplied the respiratory signal for online auditory control. Synchronization pulses linked its logs to BIOPAC recordings. Expired carbon dioxide was sampled continuously through a nasal cannula using a Vacumed 17515A capnograph. End-expiratory plateau values were summarized in 30-second bins and expressed as percentage change relative to the mean voltage during the final 2 min of the corresponding silence period. See the Online Resource for additional details. The primary subjective outcome was a composite score as the mean of three 1-7 ratings selected from the Smith Relaxation States Inventory (Smith, 2001): At Ease, Distant, and Physically Relaxed. Three additional items assessed stress-related experiences: Troublesome Thoughts, Discomfort, and Tight and Tense. The composite score was the primary measure of subjective relaxation; separate analyses of all six individual items were exploratory. The secondary symptom outcome summed six selected Nijmegen Questionnaire items: Heart Racing, Short of Breath, Dizzy Spells, Fingers Tingling, Unable to Breathe Deeply, and Tight Chest (Van Dixhoorn & Duivenvoorden, 1985). These items were selected to assess bodily symptoms relevant to the breathing manipulation while limiting the burden of repeated postcondition assessment. The resulting score was treated as a study-specific symptom index. Exploratory post-condition ratings assessed pleasure, arousal, and dominance (i.e. perceived control) of the sounds with the Self-Assessment Manikin (Bradley & Lang, 1994), and estimated time spent with closed eyes, mind wandering, attending to breathing, controlling breathing, or feeling sleepy. Individual-difference instruments and item-scoring details are described in Online Resource, Section S2.

Preprocessing

Respiration was processed using a custom cycle-detection pipeline and analytic-signal phase estimation (cf. (Harrison et al., 2021)). Breathing cycles were detected as trough-peak-trough sequences after lowpass filtering at 2 Hz and resampling to 100 Hz. Cycle duration was defined as the interval between consecutive troughs, and breathing rate was calculated as 60 divided by cycle duration in seconds. Cyclespecific rates were then averaged within each 30-s window. Respiratory phase was derived from the analytic signal of the filtered trace and aligned with logged auditory phase. Auditory-respiratory PLV was the magnitude of the mean unit vector of their phase differences, calculated within 30-s bins (Lachaux et al., 1999). PLV ranges from 0 (phase differences dispersed across the analysis interval) to 1 (a constant phase difference). Static had no defined auditory phase and was excluded from PLV analyses. ECG processing identified R peaks, rejected implausible or locally discrepant intervals, and interpolated invalid intervals. ECG-derived normal-to-normal (NN) intervals underwent artifact screening and correction before HRV computation (Laborde et al., 2017). Filtering, R-peak detection, artifact-screening criteria, and correction procedures are detailed in the Online Resource. RSA was calculated for each breath as the longest minus shortest corrected NN-interval within that respiratory cycle, then averaged within 30-s bins (Quigley et al., 2024). This measure captures respiratory modulation of cardiac timing; not a respiration-independent measure of vagal tone (Grossman & Taylor, 2007). Exploratory HRV measures included bin-level RMSSD, full-stimulation RMSSD, SDNN, and low-and high-frequency spectral power and are reported in the Online Resource. A secondary cardiorespiratory outcome quantified generalized n:m PLV , where n denotes cardiac cycles and m denotes respiratory cycles. In this analysis, m was fixed at 1 to assess coupling across several heartbeats per breath (Schäfer et al., 1998). A complementary full-segment analyses estimated magnitudesquared coherence between respiration and the interpolated NN-interval series. End-expiratory carbon dioxide values were extracted from plateaus and expressed as percentage voltage change relative to the final 2 min of the corresponding nominal silence period. Trapezius muscle activity was summarized as root-mean-square amplitude divided by a functional reference derived from the three arm elevations, similar to the procedure in (Vlemincx et al., 2010). Time-varying physiological outcomes were summarized in eight nonoverlapping 30-s stimulation bins. Physiological preprocessing used Python 3.14.2; statistical analyses used R 4.5.2. Detailed processing parameters, exploratory measures, and the analysis mapping are provided in Online Resource, Sections S1-S3.

Statistical analysis

The analysis distinguished hypotheses concerning primary outcomes (H1-H4), hypotheses concerning secondary outcomes (S1-S4), and exploratory analyses. Time-course 30s-bin models included condition, centered bin time, and their interaction, with an AR(1) residual structure within participant-condition series. Time was coded 0-7 and centered at 3.5. Condition-level contrasts compared model-estimated values at mid-stimulation, equal to the average across the eight bin positions. Slope contrasts compared rates of change per 30-s bin. Static was the reference except for auditory-respiratory PLV, for which Slowing was the reference. The planned random structure included participant intercepts and time slopes and participant-condition intercepts. Normalized EMG used participant intercepts only due to a singular model fit. Omnibus tests used marginal Wald F-tests and containment degrees of freedom of the nlme library in R. Estimated means and trends used the emmeans library in R. Adding quadratic time did not improve fit for any of the repeated-bin outcomes (likelihood-ratio p ≥ .154), so linear trajectories are reported. Confirmatory participant-level slope ANOVAs are reported in Online Resource. Post-condition ratings and full-stimulation physiological summaries were analyzed with condition as a fixed effect and participant intercepts. Self-report models used Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Condition contrasts were Holm-adjusted separately within outcome and model. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied to specified exploratory families. Unadjusted, Holm-adjusted, and Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted values are denoted p, pHolm, and pFDR, respectively. Confidence intervals are nominal 95% intervals. Correction families are detailed in Online Resource.

Departures from preregistration

The preregistered stimulation-minus-silence difference scores were not calculated because breathing rate varied significantly during nominal silence: the first 30-s window exceeded later windows by 0.84-1.18 breaths/min (all pHolm < .001). We instead analyzed stimulation-only time-course models followed by sensitivity analyses that adjusted slopes for outcome-specific means during the final 2 min of nominal silence. Adding AR(1) residuals also required containment rather than the preregistered Satterthwaite degrees of freedom for those models. The non-preregistered adaptivity awareness question and thoughtcontent summaries are explicitly exploratory.

Secondary outcomes

There was no evidence that Slowing or Adaptive increased cardiorespiratory phase locking relative to Speeding (both planned pHolm = .344). Neither normalized trapezius EMG nor the six-item hyperventilation score showed a condition effect (p = .401 and .694, respectively). Baseline-relative endexpiratory capnograph voltage showed a Condition × Time interaction, F(3, 1088) = 4.83, p = .002. The time trend was more negative in Speeding than Static, with a difference of -0.27 percentage points per 30-second bin, pHolm = .005. Average levels did not differ, and the corresponding slope-ANOVA contrast was not significant after correction (pHolm = .053). Full secondary results appear in Online Resource, Sections S5.

Exploratory experience ratings and awareness

Of eight exploratory experience-rating outcomes, only Dominance (i.e. perceived control) differed by condition after false-discovery-rate correction, F(3, 117) = 6.24, pFDR = .005. Dominance was lower in Slowing and Adaptive than Static (pHolm = .002 and pHolm < .001). Breath awareness, breath control, pleasure, arousal, sleepiness, eyes closed, and mind wandering showed no corrected condition effects. Of the 38 respondents, 25 (65.8%) reported not noticing that sound adapted to their breathing. Among those who answered "No," Adaptive retained higher auditory-respiratory PLV than Slowing and Speeding, and its breathing-rate slope was more negative than Static (pHolm = .043). Further exploratory analyses, including individual differences, respiratory variability, and HRV, are presented in Online Resource, Section S6.

Discussion

Adaptive breath-like sound sustained auditory-respiratory coordination during passive listening and was associated with a relative slowing of breathing compared with nonrhythmic sound. This change in breathing-rate was also present among participants who reported not having noticed that the sound adapted to their breathing. Participants also reported lower physical relaxation in the adaptive condition, raising the possibility that auditory guidance drew breathing away from a comfortable and spontaneous pattern established during the silent baseline. Alternatively, the more irregular auditory rhythm produced by continuous adaptation may have been less calming. Together, these findings suggest that adaptive sound can support respiratory adjustment without explicit recognition of the feedback or an immediate increase in relaxation. This distinction provides a basis for exploring when such guidance could benefit relaxation training and self-regulation.

Respiratory coordination and the role of adaptation

Continuous adaptation sustained auditory-respiratory coordination over time. Participants maintained stable phase locking in the Adaptive condition, whereas phase locking declined during both the Slowing and Speeding conditions. These open-loop rhythms began near each participant's spontaneous breathing rate but progressively moved away from it. Continuous adaptation may thus help preserve an effective temporal relationship by accommodating fluctuations in breathing as they occur. A progressively slowing or speeding and non-adaptive rhythm, in contrast, may become increasingly difficult to follow once its timing diverges from ongoing respiration. This provides a plausible explanation for why prescribing a slower auditory trajectory alone did not produce the expected respiratory response, while an adaptive stimulus sustained coupling and was associated with relative slowing. Adaptive stimulation was associated with a change in breathing behavior. The Adaptive-Static slope difference corresponded to approximately 1.27 breaths/min of relative divergence across stimulation, comprising an estimated decrease of 0.62 breaths/min in Adaptive and an increase of 0.66 breaths/min in Static. This contrast remained significant after adjustment for pre-stimulation breathing rate, suggesting that differences in initial breathing rate did not fully account for the observed divergence. As such, the combined pattern of stable phase locking and respiratory slowing supports entrainment as a plausible interpretation.

A dynamical systems account offers a possible explanation of this pattern. In oscillator models, synchronization depends on the mismatch between interacting rhythms, their coupling, and the tendency to return toward a preferred rate (Savinov et al., 2021). Models of auditory-motor synchronization further show how adaptation to external timing can coexist with a persistent attraction toward an individual's spontaneous tempo (Roman et al., 2023). Applied to respiration, this suggests that continuous tracking could keep the auditory rhythm within an effective synchronization range as breathing fluctuates. Within this maintained interaction, the configured phase offset might encourage small, repeated delays in respiratory timing that accumulate into longer breath cycles, similar to findings using delayed auditory and visual feedback of participants' own breathing (Janson et al., 2013). This mechanism would account for the combination of sustained phase locking and relative slowing in Adaptive, whereas a progressively diverging nonadaptive rhythm may eventually lose its influence. Adaptation may therefore support a gradual respiratory slowing by maintaining auditory-respiratory coordination with a phase offset intended to keep the auditory rhythm slightly behind breathing. A further prediction is that adaptation could enlarge the basin of attraction, allowing coordination to emerge from a wider set of initial states. Systematically varying initial phase, frequency mismatch, controller strength, and phase offset would help establish when coordination develops and whether it produces respiratory slowing. The findings extend evidence that auditory timing can shape respiration (Bernardi et al., 2017; Haas et al., 1986). Our earlier sonification study required participants to indicate respiratory phases manually (Van Kerrebroeck & Maes, 2021) while continuous sensing in this study allowed the interaction to develop during passive listening. (Leslie et al., 2019) similarly demonstrated respiratory slowing with personalized musical modulation while participants performed another task. Together, these approaches indicate that individualized auditory timing can influence breathing across different attentional settings. The present study adds evidence that real-time adaptation can sustain the temporal relationship throughout exposure, providing a practical foundation for respiratory guidance without an explicit synchronization task.

Implications for relaxation and biofeedback

Respiratory coordination and perceived relaxation followed different patterns. Adaptive sustained auditory-respiratory coordination and produced relative respiratory slowing but received lower Physically Relaxed ratings than Static. Exploratory ratings also indicated lower perceived control during Adaptive and Slowing. Several factors could explain this pattern. The modest respiratory slowing, with rates remaining near 16 breaths/min, may have been insufficient to produce a detectable relaxation benefit during four minutes of stimulation. Meanwhile, the more irregular auditory rhythm generated by continuous adaptation may have been less predictable and less calming. Lower perceived control also suggests that the experience of being guided deserves consideration alongside the physiological response.

The experience of respiratory guidance may depend on both its demands and its psychological context. (Stark et al., 2000) found that participants reported greater effort and lower accuracy when the prescribed breathing frequency deviated further from their spontaneous rate. Although their study involved deliberate pacing, it suggests that adjusting breathing to an external rhythm can impose demands that may offset relaxation benefits. Expectations may also contribute: experimentally enhancing expectations increased the subjective benefits of deep breathing (Szabo & Kocsis, 2017). Furthermore, (Fincham et al., 2023) found no additional psychological benefit of breathing at approximately 5.5 rather than 12 breaths/min under matched guidance, suggesting that breathing rate alone does not determine subjective outcomes. Here, participants listened without instructions to regulate their breathing, providing a different context from deliberate relaxation exercises. Future studies could vary auditory predictability and explicit breathing instructions while assessing effort, expectations, and perceived control to establish when respiratory guidance also promotes relaxation.

Respiratory adaptation may also occur without explicit recognition of the feedback. Of 38 respondents, 25 reported not noticing that the sound adapted to their breathing. In this subgroup, Adaptive retained higher phase locking than both nonadaptive rhythmic conditions and a more negative breathing-rate slope than Static. Recent research similarly suggests that respiratory timing can align with auditory rhythms without explicit instructions to synchronize breathing. (Andrews et al., 2025) observed mild auditory-respiratory entrainment during a rhythmic attention task, while (Muñoz-Caracuel et al., 2024) found respiratory entrainment during active auditory discrimination, although not during passive exposure. The present findings add evidence from passive listening, with respiratory adjustment also observed among participants who did not recognize the adaptive contingency. Together, these observations support the possibility that auditory guidance can influence respiratory timing without listeners explicitly recognizing how the sound responds to their breathing.

For biofeedback, these findings suggest that acquiring a new respiratory pattern and finding it comfortable may unfold on different timescales. In a study of instructed breathing at six breaths/min, (Szulczewski, 2019) found improved CO₂ regulation with practice and increases in calmness and relaxation at later assessments, but not the initial session. Although that task involved substantially slower and explicitly paced breathing, it demonstrates that immediate experience need not predict the experience after familiarization. Repeated exposure, gentler timing adjustments, and opportunities for the listener to influence the degree of guidance are therefore promising ways to test whether sustained coordination can become both effective and comfortable.

Potential applications

The silent baseline provides a basis for exploring how responses to adaptive sound may depend on the listener's initial physiological state. A respiratory influence was detectable after several minutes of quiet sitting, without first inducing stress or asking participants to change their breathing. A next step could be to test adaptive guidance during recovery from an experimentally elevated respiratory state. If a listener is breathing faster than their usual resting pattern, an individually tuned slowing influence may facilitate a return toward that pattern and be experienced more positively. Comparing rest and stress-recovery conditions would directly test this possibility while measuring both respiratory change and subjectively felt recovery. The aim would be to identify when and for whom a given degree of guidance is helpful, building on proposals to personalize breathing interventions (Vlemincx & Cortez-Vázquez, 2024). Another application could be to build on the continuous adaptation principle for respiratory support during recovery from critical illness, including weaning from mechanical ventilation. Music already has a clinical precedent in this setting: patient-directed listening reduced anxiety relative to usual care and reduced some measures of sedative exposure in mechanically ventilated patients (Chlan et al., 2013). Adaptive respiratory guidance could be investigated as a support for responsive patients during the transition to independent breathing. In such a setting, the objective would be comfortable, physiologically appropriate breathing, with targets determined by respiratory demand and clinical assessment rather than by slowing alone.

Limitations and future evaluation

The sample was young, predominantly female, and nonclinical, and participants experienced each condition only once, for 4 min. Further research should therefore examine broader populations, repeated exposure, and the potential for lasting improvements in self-regulation. Future evaluations should distinguish changes in breathing from benefits to the individual. Matched replay conditions and separate manipulations of controller components could clarify the role of adaptive feedback and the direction of influence between sound and breathing. Longer recordings and calibrated capnography would strengthen physiological interpretation, while measures of comfort, perceived agency, and intentional breathing control could establish how sustained coordination shapes the listening experience. The present findings support adaptive auditory stimulation as a promising approach to respiratory coordination without explicit synchronization instructions and provide a basis for evaluating its potential in clinical and self-regulation settings.

Conclusion

Adaptive breath-like sound sustained auditory-respiratory coordination and shifted breathing-rate trajectories toward relative slowing during uninstructed listening, including among participants who did not report recognizing the adaptive contingency. The combination supports spontaneous entrainment as a facilitatory account of the interaction. Its coexistence with lower physical relaxation makes the findings informative about how respiratory guidance is experienced, suggesting that adjustment of breathing and immediate subjective benefit depend on different aspects of the interaction. Continuous adaptation thus provides a basis for studying how sound can guide spontaneous respiration, and for developing personalized biofeedback that matches the listener's physiological state, comfort, and therapeutic goals.
