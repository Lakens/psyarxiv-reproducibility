Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Introduction

Generative artificial intelligencefoot_0 (genAI) can improve the quality and speed of knowledge work (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026; Grossmann et al., 2023). People also experience genAI as a personal resource: it lets them complete familiar tasks faster and take on tasks they could not have done alone, which strengthens their perceived competence and self-efficacy (Meyers et al., 2026b; Schrade et al., 2026). However, we posit that productivity benefits and individuals' positive experience are fundamentally challenged by AI's social costs that may scale to organizational, collective, or even societal problems. People who are suspected of using genAI are judged as lazier, less competent, and less caring (Kadoma et al., 2025; Meyers et al., 2026b; Reif et al., 2025). In the fog of uncertainty about changing social norms, people choosing to disclose genAI use are in fact sometimes trusted less (Schilke & Reimann, 2025). Everyday experience and research offer ample illustrations of such dynamics: colleagues annoyed by what they believe is pasted AI output in a shared document; reviewers and editors who feel that manuscripts with apparent traces of genAI are "synthetic" and void of meaning, wasting their time; managers who devalue reports they perceive as AI-generated (Niederhoffer et al., 2025); hiring managers who devalue applicants who report using genAI (Reif et al., 2025); and the public AI shaming of people whose work shows signs of genAI use (Sarkar, 2025).

We believe that social judgments of genAI use are a starting point for what we call the socially corrosive path of genAI use, both for individual humans and especially for collectives -teams, organizations, and institutions. When people use genAI themselves, they feel more competent but may also feel guilty or ashamed; when they perceive that colleagues have used genAI, they devalue the colleagues' work and doubt their care. Followed through to their behavioral consequences, these judgments lead to a decline in beneficial relational interdependence and an erosion of interpersonal trust. Ultimately, we posit that individual productivity gains may end in conflict and loss of cohesion rather than in better organizational or societal functioning (see also Baygi & Huysman, 2026). Specifically, we see two interlocking paths through which the corrosive path may unfold: i) a loss of relational interdependence, driven by substituting human team members with genAI and devaluing human work, thereby undermining collective intelligence and metaknowledge in teams and organizations; and ii) an erosion of collective trust, driven by uncertainty about the norms of genAI use that fuels concealment, suspicion, policing, and blame, thereby undermining people's willingness to collaborate with and help one another. The two paths reinforce each other: less interdependence leaves more room for uncertainty, and less trust leads to fewer interactions.

In this article, we caution that current genAI use, even if well-intentioned and objectively improving one's individual performance, will very likely contribute to this corrosive path for teams, organizations, and institutions, unless we actively counteract it. We outline social and collective dynamics through which the path unfolds, how technology design and productivity narratives amplify it, and what could be done to put us on a different path.

Social Judgments of GenAI Use

Meyers and colleagues (2026b) organize research on social judgments of genAI users along two dimensions, competence and warmth (Fiske et al., 2007; also described as agency and communion; Abele et al., 2021) and along two perspectives, that of the user and that of the observer. From the user's perspective, genAI use enhances perceived competence: users feel more productive, creative, motivated, and willing to explore tasks that appeared far beyond their capabilities before. This boost is not always calibrated, as people can perform better with genAI yet overestimate their performance (Fernandes et al., 2026). However, the competence gain appears to come at the cost of self-perceived warmth as users can feel inauthentic or dishonest when using genAI, up to feeling guilty or ashamed (Meyers et al., 2026b).

Social judgment is often studied from the perspectives of actors and observers (Jones & Nisbett, 1987; cf. Malle, 2006; see also the related distinction in moral psychology between agents and patients; Gray & Wegner, 2009). From the observer's perspective, others' genAI use lowers judgments on both competence and warmth dimensions. Leaders devalue subordinates who use genAI (Meyers et al., 2026a), and, as noted above, evaluators and audiences penalize people who use or disclose genAI (Reif et al., 2025; Schilke & Reimann, 2025). Observers read genAI use as a sign that the user invested little effort in the task (competence) and little care and respect in the relationship (warmth) (Meyers et al., 2026b).

As genAI increasingly moves toward agentic systems taking over even larger shares of tasks if not entire jobs, we predict even stronger attributions of low effort and little care, because fully delegated work leaves observers little reason to assume that a human remained engaged.

There is emerging evidence suggesting that these attributions shape social behavior: people help colleagues less when they interpret their genAI use as slacking off (Zhou et al., 2025) and are less willing to forgive when apologies were written with AI (Glikson & Asscher, 2023). Yet such downstream consequences have received little attention, and where they have been studied, research has focused on individuals and dyadic relationships. To our knowledge, little research on consequences of these judgments for groups, organizations, and societies exists. If we take the documented effects and possible downstream trajectories seriously and assume that they are linear (or even exponential), it becomes critical to spell out the downward spiral they may set in motion. We argue that the combination of competence and warmth judgments about oneself and others forms the social psychological foundation of the socially corrosive path. Technological affordances, contextual expectations, and the current uncertainty related to the absence of shared norms for genAI use, which leaves people with misaligned expectations about appropriate use, further increase the likelihood of entering this path.

The Socially Corrosive Path of Collective GenAI Use

Judgments of genAI use and their social consequences emerge in sender-receiver interactions, where both may use genAI, may anticipate judgments about others' perceptions of genAI use, and judge others for their genAI use. Integrating the self and other perspectives on competence and warmth within such interactions reveals two interlocking paths: a reduction of relational interdependence and an erosion of trust.

Reduction of Relational Interdependence

"With AI, I can do this myself." A Path to Substitution.

Higher perceived selfcompetence can be the starting point for a reduction of relational interdependence. This can have positive consequences: substitution can free people from problematic dependencies, for instance on abusive leaders (Schulz et al., 2026). It can also spare people the emotional effort of approaching difficult colleagues and the anticipated social costs of asking for help (Lee, 2002). Indeed, employees anticipate feeling more autonomous and less indebted when receiving help from AI rather than from a colleague (Zhai & Qu, 2027), which may especially relieve socially anxious individuals. Yet such relief can turn into dysfunctional avoidance behavior, as socially anxious individuals may also be more prone to problematic use of conversational AI (Hu et al., 2023). Overall, we argue that the benefits of interdependence and relatedness for learning, coordination, and well-being generally outweigh the benefits of substitution (Courtright et al., 2015; Ryan & Deci, 2000). GenAI substitution may also reduce help-seeking from colleagues. GenAI is a fast, always available, and socially costless source of effort and feedback. Asking colleagues takes longer, costs them time, and may signal one's lack of competence (Schulz et al., 2026; Zhai & Qu, 2027). Instead of waiting for a colleague's input, one can refine the work in quick iterations with genAI. Colleagues' help therefore has to be markedly better to outweigh the immediate return of genAI and is in turn held to a higher (often unrealistic) standard than before. This calculus holds even when objective gains are small, as people tend to overestimate their performance with genAI (Fernandes et al., 2026) and may in general see genAI as a resource (Schrade et al., 2026). Anticipatory effects may follow: why would others need my help if they can ask genAI, and why should I take up their time? Scaled up, substitution creates challenges for individual development, collective intelligence, and cohesion. For individuals, input from genAI differs fundamentally from input from other people. First, it is more homogeneous. GenAI use lifts the weakest outputs and levels differences between individuals (Doshi & Hauser, 2024), but it also makes ideas and language across people more similar and individual ideas less novel (Doshi & Hauser, 2024; Meincke et al., 2025; Sourati et al., 2026; Terwiesch et al., 2026). If every team member comes to a meeting with similar ideas, collaboration adds little (Burton et al., 2024).

Greater homogeneity of genAI output is especially detrimental for ill-defined tasksi.e., radically uncertain, often value-laden social problems where objectives are incommensurable, the probability distribution of outcomes is unknown, or the whole idea of a single "ground truth" is suspect (for reviews, see Grossmann & Johnson, 2025; Grossmann & Weststrate, 2026). Such tasks often require coordination of different standpoints instead of mere judgment of what is right or wrong. The out-of-distribution nature of such tasks creates a challenge for AI systems trained on fixed datasets -a challenge compounded genAI's tendency to underweight the tails of the distribution of possible scenario (Burton et al., 2024; Terwiesch et al., 2026). Without complex metacognitive coordination common to human teams (Johnson et al., 2026), genAI systems and insular human-AI hybrids will be at a disadvantage compared with human teams, whose intersubjective insights benefit from the diverse perspectives individuals bring to the table. In short, for ill-defined problems diversity in human teams is poised to provide an advantage in generated solutions compared to agentic AI or self-referential human-AI hybrids (Oppenheimer & Patterson, 2025). This advantage in turn depends on the social and metaknowledge insights of team members, and the less individuals engage in team-related exercises and the less they turn to each other for help and advice, the more difficult team-based coordination becomes.

The contrast of self-referential human-AI hybrids to human teams also includes a strong motivational component. In collegial exchange, something is always at stake, such as one's reputation or the relationship, whereas what comes from genAI can be dismissed without consequences (Lange et al., 2025). Individuals may thus only go as far as they feel comfortable, whereas exchange with another person, including social accountability effects, might have motivated them to go the extra mile, and, for instance, engage with a topic more deeply in order not to let colleagues down.

For collective intelligence, the more individuals approach genAI as their primary source of competence, the fewer opportunities remain to maintain relationships and metaknowledge in teams through exchange and chance encounters (Niederhoffer et al., 2026).

Insight often emerges from interactions among people who each contribute idiosyncratic knowledge and perspectives. Losing accidental exchange therefore also means losing serendipitous knowledge and the creative recombination of ideas from people with diverse backgrounds and contextual knowledge (Burton et al., 2024). Remote work offers a cautionary parallel: it puts a strain on collaboration networks (Yang et al., 2022), and remote teams are less likely to produce breakthrough ideas (Lin et al., 2023). GenAI use can further create a false sense of consensus or a majority view where none exists, reducing the perceived need to discuss controversial topics in teams and organizations (Burton et al., 2024). Furthermore, as genAI substitutes for colleagues, teams' transactive memory, that is, shared knowledge of who knows what and who is able and willing to contribute, may decay (Niederhoffer et al., 2026), hurting coordination and the scaling of individual knowledge into collective gains (DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus, 2010). Initial evidence is consistent with the path to substitution. In interviews, students reported that help-seeking is increasingly mediated by genAI: they ask genAI before asking peers or instructors, and when peers approach them with questions, they redirect them to genAI (Hou et al., 2025). Students described feeling isolated and demotivated as the social support of their learning communities eroded, which undermines not only knowledge sharing and collective competence building but also their sense of belonging (Hou et al., 2025).

Similarly, conceptual work warns that unmanaged genAI adoption can undermine expertise sharing and collegiality in organizations (Baygi & Huysman, 2026). The resulting "democratization of expertise" (Baygi & Huysman, 2026, p. 378) may lead non-experts to feel that they can do many things without others' help. Not approaching others, in turn, reduces opportunities to build trust and to maintain knowledge-sharing networks. As a result, organizational metaknowledge of whom to ask for what advice may decline (Niederhoffer et al., 2026).

Moreover, because genAI can be personalized explicitly and automatically, exchange with it may feel seamless and perfectly fitted to one's own work style, whereas others' ways of working and giving feedback may increasingly seem ill-fitting, alienating individuals in the team. There is first evidence that supports this concern: in a three-week study, people interacting with sycophantic AI reported lower satisfaction with their real-world social interactions (Ibrahim et al., 2026), and conversations with sycophantic AI made people less willing to take responsibility for and repair interpersonal conflicts (Cheng et al., 2026).

Finally, interacting with AI at work has been linked to loneliness (Tang et al., 2023).

Loneliness can motivate people to reach out to coworkers, but it is also associated with dysfunctional outcomes and may, at scale, feed a downward spiral of collective loneliness.

"With AI, I could have done this myself." A Path to Devaluation.

People who perceive themselves as more competent with genAI may hold what they receive from others to a higher bar. At the collective level, if everyone is expected to use genAI, expectations of speed and productivity rise (Ranganathan & Ye, 2026). Human work is then expected to at least match what genAI can deliver and to add what it cannot, such as authenticity or an idiosyncratic perspective (we return to these collective expectations below). If others' work falls short of this bar or takes long to arrive, people may conclude that they could have produced the same output with genAI themselves (Sarkar, 2025).

When this higher bar coincides with the devaluation of others' genAI use, it further undermines relational interdependence. When genAI use is suspected, observers infer little effort toward the task and little care toward them (Meyers et al., 2026b; Reif et al., 2025).

First studies support the existence of this path. Leaders devalue subordinates' work when they suspect genAI use (Meyers et al., 2026a). This implies that tasks delegated to employees who then use genAI come to be seen as low-value, low-effort work that leaders could have done equally well with genAI or an orchestrated team of AI agents themselves. Coworkers who interpret genAI use as slacking off see the user as less moral and help them less (Zhou et al., 2025). Possibly, what the colleague delivered fell short of expectations, leaving little reason to help in the future. People are even willing to pay workers less when they use genAI (Kim et al., 2026). Thus, while collective genAI use has raised the bar for what counts as good work, it seems to have lowered what we are willing to pay for work when we see others use genAI.

Devaluation may also lead people to reciprocate others' genAI use with their own, further contributing to the loss of relational interdependence.

The perceived quality of outputs likely moderates these reactions, but suspected genAI use seems to cap evaluations. Even high-quality output is unlikely to be fully credited once genAI use is suspected, because observers may never be convinced that enough effort went into the task or enough care into the relationship. From a signaling theory perspective (Connelly et al., 2011), genAI makes formerly costly signals of effort and care, such as polished writing, cheap to produce, so that they lose their informational value. Low-quality output or visible errors combined with suspected genAI use likely meet the harshest judgments (Niederhoffer et al., 2025). And as genAI use becomes the expected norm, even work produced without genAI may be judged against it, for instance as inefficient when it takes longer than expected.

Erosion of Trust

"Will others think I did this with AI?" A Path to Concealment.

If users perceive themselves as competent in their genAI use, they are likely motivated to protect this image to ensure that others see them as competent and caring. Users expect worse evaluations when they disclose genAI use, which lowers their willingness to disclose (Reif et al., 2025). This expectation seems accurate: disclosure reduces trust (Schilke & Reimann, 2025), which makes concealment a rational response. Making people aware of this penalty can also induce undisclosed "shadow use" of genAI (Dong et al., 2025). Freelancers tend to disclose genAI use only when asked. Conversely, clients feel less able to recognize genAI use and prefer proactive disclosure (Hwang et al., 2026). When directly compared, people also find secret genAI use less acceptable than open use (Purcell et al., 2026). Guilt and shame about genAI use add to the motives for concealment (Meyers et al., 2026b). Concealment can thus extend from non-disclosure or passive disclosure to making content "look human," for instance, by running genAI-generated text through "humanizing" tools that aim to reduce the likelihood of text being flagged as AI-generated. People may also try surface-level fixes such as avoiding em dashes or words that have become statistical markers of genAI use (Kobak et al., 2025), or deliberately insert imperfections, given that errors, particularly corrected ones, can make communicators appear more human and warm (Bluvstein et al., 2024).

"Did you use AI for this?" A Path to Suspicion.

People expect others to use genAI more than they do themselves and in ways they find less acceptable (Purcell et al., 2026).

With this broad assumption of others' (flawed) genAI use, it is likely that one common question regarding others' work is whether the other person has used genAI. This suspicion stems from uncertainty about others' genAI use (Hwang et al., 2026) and about their current and future behavior in collaboration. Is the other person competent and invested enough that I do not waste my time checking each line of their work for errors, and do they have expertise I can rely and build on? And does the other person care enough about me and our relationship to hand me something of high quality? Suspicion likely depends on perceived evidence of genAI use, the task context, and individual differences, for instance in knowledge of typical AI signatures.

"I need to know whether you did this with AI!" A Path to Policing.

Suspicion invites a search for evidence, and the longer it is maintained, the more likely one is to find what looks like evidence for genAI use. People are overconfident in their ability to detect AI-generated text (Fleckenstein et al., 2024) and rely on flawed heuristics (Jakesch et al., 2023). AI detectors are unreliable and biased against non-native speakers (Liang et al., 2023). Even actual traces of genAI use say little about a person's competence or care. Moreover, initial evidence suggests that human language may converge toward the style of large language models (Yakura et al., 2026), so that human writing may increasingly be misread as AIgenerated. Because non-native speakers rely more heavily on genAI and similar tools to correct their spelling and grammar, apparent evidence and policing of genAI use may disproportionately affect them, inviting questions about equity and discrimination.

"You have used AI!" A Path to Blame.

Suspicion and policing can end in covert blame, such as gossip, or overt blame, such as public allegations of genAI use (Gorichanaz, 2023; Sarkar, 2025). In this sense, every negative judgment of genAI use is a social sanction, and it is likely harsher the less transparent the other person's genAI use appears. Indeed, genAI use exposed by a third party erodes trust more than self-disclosure does (Schilke & Reimann, 2025).

Importantly, trust erosion does not require that blame is ever voiced. Suspicion and policing alone increase uncertainty about the other person's competence and care. Such uncertainty undermines the willingness to collaborate with, invest effort in responding to, and help the other person (Zhou et al., 2025). Once blame is voiced, it provokes reactions.

Accused individuals may defend their genAI use as adequate and legitimate, reject the allegations as false, conceal their genAI use more carefully, or avoid collaboration for fear of further suspicion and blame. Evidence from education illustrates these dynamics. Students accused of using genAI responded by denying the allegations, escalating them to higher authorities, and documenting their writing process; they reported damaged trust in their instructors and a more defensive approach to their work (Gorichanaz, 2023). Covert and overt blame thus further erode trust and likely reinforce the reduction of relational interdependence.

Reduced relational interdependence may further erode trust. Fewer interdependencies mean fewer occasions to learn why and how others use genAI. People judge themselves largely by their thoughts and intentions, but others by what they can observe (Pronin, 2008). Applied to genAI, people know their own reasons for using it but have little insight into others' reasons.

With less exchange, this asymmetry may encourage unfavorable inferences about others' use, reinforcing the self-other gap in expectations described above. If people also underestimate how widely their concerns about responsible genAI use are shared, these misperceptions may give rise to pluralistic ignorance (Prentice & Miller, 1996): individuals may privately favor thoughtful use while mistakenly believing that others are less concerned about responsible use than they actually are. When these concerns remain unvoiced, people lose opportunities to correct their misperceptions and establish shared expectations. Uncertainty about prevailing norms may then invite uncharitable assumptions, fueling suspicion, policing, and blame.

Amplifiers of the Socially Corrosive Path

The path we outline is only one possible trajectory of collective genAI use, perhaps even the worst-case scenario. Yet current technology design and the narratives and contextual demands surrounding genAI make entering this path considerably more likely.

Technology Design

Most genAI products are built around a dialogue between one user and the system.

Their underlying models are trained to produce responses that people rate favorably, rewarding responses that keep users satisfied and engaged (Sharma et al., 2024). GenAI tools keep the dialogue going through follow-up questions and extended sessions, and agentic setups let a single user orchestrate several AI agents at once (Allmendinger et al., 2026). Little in current designs prompts users to consider that now would be a good moment to involve a colleague. Organization-wide deployments could foster interpersonal exchange, but in practice they largely replicate the individual interface (Niederhoffer et al., 2026).

One consequence is that individuals produce more output, faster than they or others can verify (Bedard et al., 2026). Across contexts, output keeps growing while the systems in which individuals operate are strained by the need to review, verify, and quality-check it (Gartenberg et al., 2026). In a recent field experiment, individuals and teams working with genAI produced higher-quality solutions, which were more than twice as long as those developed by individuals without AI (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026).

Volume and speed are themselves cues of genAI use and may thus feed devaluation and suspicion. Moreover, decades of research on automation bias show that overreliance becomes more likely under high workload (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010), fueled by growing AI-assisted output. The penalty for the errors that go undetected due to overreliance can be severe: recipients of AI-generated "workslop," report seeing its senders as less capable, reliable, and trustworthy (Niederhoffer et al., 2025). Because verifying genAI output is seen as the sender's responsibility, receivers who believe that someone used genAI inadequately may simply stop reviewing their work or may delegate the task to their genAI agents. We suspect that this process contributes to the current crisis of scientific peer review, where growing submission numbers (Hanson et al., 2024) meet a declining willingness to review, especially when a manuscript is suspected to be largely genAI-generated.

Productivity Narratives and Contextual Demands

One prominent narrative in public and corporate discourse portrays AI as an inevitable, disruptive force in a global competition (Bareis & Katzenbach, 2022) that will raise productivity across the board (see Spencer, 2025, for a critique). Simply put, everyone can and must become more productive with genAI or will be left behind. Under this narrative, time saved with genAI is reinvested in more output: rather than reducing work, genAI use can intensify it, expanding the scope of jobs and blurring the boundaries of working time (Bedard et al., 2026; Ranganathan & Ye, 2026). The resulting pressure further increases the risk of overreliance (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

The narrative also turns the raised bar described above into an organizational norm. If genAI is assumed to replace human working hours, some tasks may soon no longer count as "human work" at all, and the willingness to pay for human effort declines (Demirci et al., 2025; Hui et al., 2024). Judging that a work output "could have been done by AI" thus becomes both an economic argument against paying for human effort and a devaluation of human competence (Sarkar, 2025). The narrative casts humans as slow and error-prone, and people may begin to ask themselves, "Who am I to do anything without genAI?" Even if they could improve an AI output, doing so would take time, so the AI output is deemed good enough. If everyone is expected to use genAI, the substitution calculus described above becomes the default as asking a colleague appears slow, and one's input appears dispensable.

At the same time, calls for human responsibility, verification, and oversight remain (Sterz et al., 2024), and every visible trace of genAI raises the question of whether such verification happened. Users are thus left with a dilemma: disclosure invites devaluation, and concealment invites suspicion, policing, and blame. For teams and organizations, concealment is likely the worse option. Exposure by others erodes trust more than self-disclosure does (Schilke & Reimann, 2025), and concealment deprives the collective of the exchange needed to develop shared norms of genAI use.

How to Leave the Socially Corrosive Path

The elephant in the room is whether the outlined effects will level out as genAI use becomes normal and legitimate (Meyers et al., 2026b). Waiting for this comes with clear risks. First, it is unclear whether normalization will occur. The evaluation penalty persisted when genAI use was described as common (Reif et al., 2025), and people penalized others' disclosed genAI use even when they used genAI themselves (Schilke & Reimann, 2025).

Expectations, attributions, and behaviors around genAI use may thus endure, or even intensify, as genAI becomes integrated into everyday work. Second, even if judgments of our own and others' genAI use shift, it is unclear how fast. In the meantime, the corrosive dynamics unfold. Instrumentally, the loss of relational interdependence, including metaknowledge of others in one's team and appreciation for their likely divergent perspectives, and the erosion of trust may offset genAI's productivity gains, increasing individual but harming collective productivity (Niederhoffer et al., 2026). Interpersonally, they risk conflict and a loss of cohesion that is hard to rebuild, as the effects of remote work on collaboration networks suggest (Yang et al., 2022). Collectively, these tendencies may fuel interpersonal acrimony and intolerance of those who think differently, with downstream consequences for civility.

Third, even if suspicion, policing, and blame stopped immediately, genAI tools would remain optimized for individual engagement rather than collective functioning. Whereas the erosion of trust might stop, the loss of relational interdependence would continue as individuals would still turn to genAI first, encounter colleagues' ideas less often, and lose the chance encounters through which teams learn collectively and generate new ideas.

Thus, we think steps should be taken now by organizations, teams, and societies to leave this socially corrosive path. Below, we outline ideas for these steps, hoping to stimulate a discussion about how to reach a more beneficial collective use of genAI.

Design genAI for collective functioning.

Technology can be designed to foster relatedness within groups (Faas et al., 2025). GenAI systems could support users' metacognitive awareness of when collaboration would be valuable, for instance by prompting them at decisive moments to consider who in their team might be best placed to review a draft or contribute a different perspective. Such prompts are all the more relevant because people working with genAI tend to misjudge their own performance (Fernandes et al., 2026). Beyond individual interactions, such support could help teams and organizations detect opportunities for learning, cooperation, and network building instead of defaulting to efficiency, strengthening rather than replacing their knowledge of who knows what. In short, genAI should be designed to support collective functioning rather than remain a tool for individual productivity that comes at a collective cost. Collaboration tools could also support handovers between colleagues, where many of the problematic social judgment effects arise. We imagine tools that efficiently help make explicit which parts were AI-generated and humanverified, and where the receiver's input, verification, and attention are especially needed.

Resist the genAI productivity trap.

We need a more realistic view of genAI's productivity promises and a more positive view of the value of human expertise. Speed is not wisdom -the latter classically depends on the metacognitive insight into when to use which tool to address the task at hand (Grossmann & Weststrate, 2026; Karlan & Allen, 2024).

Instead of expecting everyone to become substantially more productive, it is important to emphasize that not every minute saved must be reinvested in more output. Reinvesting every minute into more output deepens individual dependence on genAI and reduces relational interdependence without necessarily improving long-term organizational functioning or productivity.

Instead, time saved should go into at least two things. First, it should go into learning and maintaining one's competence with one key goal being to remain able to judge the quality of outputs of the AI (Hickman & Langer, 2026), for instance through periodic unassisted work. GenAI itself can support rather than undermine learning when it is explicitly designed and used to do so (Bastani et al., 2025). Second, time saved should go into deepening interpersonal exchange. Remote work offers an instructive parallel given that it brought individual benefits and risks for collaboration (Yang et al., 2022). Integrating remote work effectively means deliberately protecting time for in-person collaboration (Bloom et al., 2024). In the case of genAI, teams can likewise protect specific occasions for human exchange, for instance by generating ideas before consulting genAI or by reserving feedback on junior colleagues' work for human mentors. One may also reserve ill-defined tasks for human-centric teams, facilitated via structured team exercises promoting organizational metaknowledge and tolerance of divergent viewpoints (DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus, 2010; Edmondson & Bransby, 2023). Together, these shifts support an alternative narrative: highquality work with genAI requires human effort and collaboration, including time to verify outputs, to maintain the competencies needed to use genAI effectively, and to learn how to use it well collectively.

Make social norms of genAI use explicit.

Whereas building the technology and changing the narrative will take time, this countermeasure can be implemented immediately.

It starts with refraining from AI shaming -of others and of ourselves -which we do not expect to have any beneficial effects, and with explicit transparency, negotiating and explicating expectations about genAI use.

Within collaborations, making genAI use transparent can have immediate benefits.

Transparency shows others where genAI was used, invites them to reciprocate disclosure of their own use, and thus reduces uncertainty. It also invites discussion about where genAI use is seen as helpful or problematic and strengthens human control as well as individual and collective accountability. Arguably, disclosure carries risks including evaluation penalties (Schilke & Reimann, 2025). However, shared norms may address precisely this evaluation penalty and draw upon the shared individual preference for proactive disclosure (Hwang et al., 2026). Within teams, members can regularly exchange how they use genAI and agree in advance on how to hand over AI-supported work, complementing the technical design for effective handover described above: keeping outputs short instead of letting genAI's limitless output become limitless documents, and directing others' attention to where their input is valued and matters. At the organizational level, guidelines should go beyond liability, data protection, and individual responsibility and make social norms explicit: for which tasks genAI use is valued, that genAI use is disclosed by default, and how conflicts about genAI use are resolved. Initial evidence shows that AI-use policies attenuate leaders' negative reactions to subordinates' genAI use (Meyers et al., 2026a) and that better guidelines on how to disclose can make people more comfortable disclosing (He et al., 2026).

Finally, doubts about a colleague's competence or care, or the urge to gather evidence of their genAI use, are a cue to talk to that colleague directly rather than to continue down the path of policing and blame. Such a conversation differs from policing in its aim: it seeks to understand the other person's reasoning and to reduce shared uncertainty; it is open to being wrong about one's doubt and motivated to search for a collective solution rather than to collect evidence for a sanction. It is therefore best framed as an inquiry ("How did you approach this?") rather than an accusation ("Did you use AI for this?"). Such conversations can be uncomfortable, but they allow the other person to explain their reasoning and allow both to negotiate shared norms around genAI use. Without them, the erosion of trust is the likely outcome.

Conclusion

Rooted in research on social judgments of genAI use, we outline immediate and mounting risks of collective genAI use: a workplace in which people depend less on each Socially Corrosive genAI 20 other, trust each other less, and gain less collectively than genAI promises individually.

Although social judgments are our starting point, these risks extend to teams and organizations and to how knowledge accumulates and is shared within collectives. These risks are highest for ill-defined problems, for which no objectively right or wrong answer exists and whose navigation benefits from teams' diverse vantage points. The available evidence points toward the socially corrosive path. Further research can help trace the exact paths, provide evidence for the reinforcing dynamics we have outlined, including the role of uncertainty about norms and the extension to agentic AI, and identify and evaluate further ways out.

Scholars can benefit from looking beyond the study of judgments between individuals, toward the study of the behavioral consequences of genAI use in teams and organizations over time.

Practice, however, should not wait for this research, because the first problems are already here (Meyers et al., 2026b; Niederhoffer et al., 2026). Designing genAI for collective functioning can be set in motion now and raising awareness of the AI productivity trap and making norms of genAI use explicit can start today.
