Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

We found 10 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Contemporary changes in work have been characterized by intensified demands alongside greater expectations for employees to autonomously organize their work and pursue work-related goals (Mauno et al., 2023; Niazi et al., 2024). Meeting occupational demands involves regulatory processes such as maintaining attention, organizing and prioritizing tasks, managing time, and sustaining goal-directed behavior. These processes may be particularly relevant to attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), given documented difficulties in attention and executive functioning and their associations with occupational functioning (Chan & Langberg, 2024; Fuermaier et al., 2021; Turjeman-Levi et al., 2024). Although ADHD is clinically diagnosed categorically, its core symptoms of inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity are dimensionally distributed, with evidence suggesting continuity rather than a discrete boundary between affected and unaffected individuals (Arildskov et al., 2022; McLennan, 2016). This dimensional perspective is also compatible with the neurodiversity paradigm, which situates neurodevelopmental differences within broader variation in human neurocognitive functioning rather than treating diagnostic categories as necessarily reflecting qualitatively distinct populations (Doyle & McDowall, 2022; Koi, 2021). Accordingly, ADHD-related characteristics may be relevant to occupational functioning beyond clinically diagnosed populations. ADHD has been associated with occupational underattainment and impaired work functioning (Biederman et al., 2008; Brook et al., 2013), while functional impairment has also been documented at subthreshold symptom levels (Hong et al., 2014) and in relation to ADHD symptoms in community samples without established diagnoses (Fuermaier et al., 2021). Despite the occupational relevance of ADHD, workplace-specific research remains comparatively limited, particularly regarding how adults with ADHD-related characteristics manage occupational demands in everyday work contexts (Lauder et al., 2022).

Among adults with ADHD, research has identified diverse self-developed compensatory strategies involving attention, organization, adaptation, and external support (Canela et al., 2017; Kysow et al., 2017), while qualitative research among working adults with ADHD has described extending work into evenings and weekends in response to difficulties encountered during regular working hours (Oscarsson et al., 2022). Workaholism, procrastination, perfectionism, and taking on excessive numbers of tasks have likewise been discussed in relation to ADHD and increased work investment (Andreassen et al., 2016; Oscarsson et al., 2022). Importantly, such responses are not inherently self-endangering.

However, some may overlap with forms of self-endangering work behavior (SEWB) when maintaining occupational functioning involves additional investment of time, effort, or recovery resources. ADHD-related characteristics have likewise been associated with occupational strain, including greater stress and burnout (Erdal et al., 2025).

SEWB describes forms of work behavior through which employees attempt to maintain occupational functioning despite potential costs to health and recovery (Dettmers et al., 2016; Krause et al., 2015; Mustafić et al., 2023). It encompasses diverse strategies, including extending and intensifying work, working despite illness, sacrificing recovery, using substances to maintain performance or facilitate recovery, reducing work quality, and concealing performance difficulties (Dettmers et al., 2016). SEWB therefore provides a framework for examining not only whether employees experience difficulties at work, but also whether the strategies accompanying continued occupational functioning may themselves have implications for well-being.

A compensatory-regulation perspective provides a theoretical rationale for linking ADHD-related characteristics with SEWB. Difficulties with attention regulation, organization, prioritization, or time management may increase the regulatory demands involved in attaining occupational goals (Chan & Langberg, 2024; Turjeman-Levi et al., 2024). Hockey's (1997) compensatory control framework proposes that when demands threaten performance goals, individuals may maintain performance by mobilizing additional effort or reallocating resources, potentially at increased personal cost. Applied to ADHDrelated occupational difficulties, this framework suggests that maintaining work goals may under some circumstances involve additional investment of time, effort, or recovery resources and thereby overlap with particular forms of SEWB. This does not imply that SEWB among workers with elevated ADHD symptoms necessarily represents compensation for ADHD-related difficulties; rather, compensatory regulation provides a theoretical rationale for examining whether ADHD-related characteristics are associated with greater or different forms of SEWB. This question is relevant to occupational health because strategies that help maintain functioning in the short term may be associated with strain. Working longer and more intensively has predicted subsequent increases in time pressure and unfinished tasks (Baethge et al., 2026; Ganster et al., 2018), while SEWB has been associated with emotional exhaustion, fatigue, and psychosomatic complaints (Deci et al., 2016; Mustafić et al., 2023) and may partly account for associations between work demands and exhaustion (Knecht et al., 2017). At the same time, SEWB comprises conceptually distinct behaviours that may differ in their associations with occupational well-being. It also remains unclear whether these associations are comparable across workers with differing ADHD-related characteristics.

Perceived Stress. Perceived stress was assessed using the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10; Cohen et al., 1983). Items were rated on a five-point response scale ranging from 1 to 5. Positively worded items (Items 4, 5, 7, and 8) were reverse-scored so that higher scores consistently reflected greater perceived stress. A mean score was calculated when at least 8 of the 10 items were available. The scale showed α = .86, ω = .86 in the present sample.

Burnout. Burnout was assessed using the 22-item Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI; Maslach et al., 1996), comprising emotional exhaustion (9 items), depersonalization (5 items), and personal accomplishment (8 items). Items were rated on a seven-point frequency scale ranging from 1 to 7, with higher scores on emotional exhaustion and depersonalization indicating greater burnout symptoms. Personal accomplishment was reverse-scored for the analyses so that higher scores represented reduced personal accomplishment. A sample depersonalization item is, "I feel I treat some clients/colleagues as if they were impersonal objects." Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω and was α = .89, ω = .90 for emotional exhaustion, α = .80, ω = .80 for depersonalization and α = .79, ω = .79 for personal accomplishment.

Work engagement. Work engagement was assessed using the 9-item Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9; Schaufeli et al., 2006). Items were rated on a seven-point frequency scale, with higher scores indicating greater work engagement. A total mean score was calculated when at least seven of the nine items were available. A sample item is, "At my work, I feel bursting with energy." Internal consistency in the present sample was α = .95, ω = .95.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted using the final analytical sample of 471 participants. To address RQ1, differences in the ten SEWB dimensions between the ADHD and comparison groups were examined using Welch independent-samples t tests. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons across the ten SEWB dimensions using the Holm method, and Cohen's d was reported as an effect-size measure. Bivariate correlations and variance inflation factors (VIFs) were examined to assess multicollinearity among the SEWB dimensions.

An observed-variable path model was estimated in R using the lavaan package. ADHD group membership was modeled as a predictor of each of the ten SEWB dimensions. ADHD group membership and the ten SEWB dimensions were then simultaneously modeled as predictors of emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, reduced personal accomplishment, work engagement, and perceived stress. Age and sex were included as covariates. Age was meancentered, and sex was dummy-coded with female as the reference category. The model was estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR), with missing data handled using fullinformation maximum likelihood (FIML).

To address RQ2, latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using the two continuous ADHD symptom dimensions (inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity) and the ten SEWB dimensions as profile indicators. All 12 indicators were standardized prior to analysis. Candidate solutions containing one through six profiles were initially estimated in R using the tidyLPA package, with indicator variances constrained to equality across profiles and within-profile covariances fixed to zero. Solutions were evaluated based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size-adjusted BIC (SABIC), entropy, profile size, and substantive interpretability. Because the information criteria continued to improve as additional profiles were extracted, alternative solutions allowing indicator variances to vary across profiles while retaining zero within-profile covariances were examined.

As a robustness analysis, Gaussian finite-mixture models were additionally estimated using the mclust package, comparing equal-variance (EEI) and profile-specific diagonalvariance (VVI) covariance structures across one-through six-profile solutions. The final solution was selected based on statistical fit, classification precision, profile size, parsimony, and substantive distinctiveness. Posterior probabilities and classification uncertainty were examined to evaluate classification quality.

To address RQ3, the identified ADHD-SEWB profiles were compared on emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, reduced personal accomplishment, work engagement, and perceived stress using separate linear regression models. Profile membership was entered as a categorical predictor, with mean-centered age and sex included as covariates; sex was dummy-coded with female as the reference category. Analyses included participants with available data for the respective outcome. Because profile sizes were unequal, heteroskedasticity-consistent HC3 covariance estimates were used. Overall profile effects were evaluated using Type II robust tests implemented with the car package. Adjusted profile means were estimated using the emmeans package with HC3 covariance estimates from the sandwich package, and pairwise profile comparisons were Tukey-adjusted for the six comparisons among the four profiles within each outcome.

The code behind this analysis/simulation has been made publicly available at github repository and can be accessed at https://github.com/kerstin123456/SEWB_ADHD/tree/main.

Results

RQ1: ADHD, SEWB, and Burnout, Work Engagement, and Perceived Stress

Participants in the ADHD group reported significantly higher levels on all ten SEWB dimensions than participants in the comparison group, with all differences remaining significant after Holm correction (Supplementary Table S1). Effect sizes ranged from d = .20 for reduced exchange with colleagues to d = .78 for quality reduction.

Intercorrelations among the SEWB dimensions ranged from r = .10 to .57, and VIFs ranged from 1.12 to 1.82, indicating no problematic multicollinearity.

In the multivariable path model, individual SEWB dimensions showed differentiated associations with burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress (Table 2). Reduced exchange with colleagues was associated with greater emotional exhaustion (β = .25), depersonalization (β = .25), reduced personal accomplishment (β = .18), lower work engagement (β = -.17), and greater perceived stress (β = .13; all ps ≤ .004). Quality reduction was associated with greater emotional exhaustion (β = .15), depersonalization (β = .10), and perceived stress (β = .14), as well as lower work engagement (β = -.15; ps ≤ .047). In contrast, work intensification and working during leisure time/constant availability were associated with higher work engagement and lower reduced personal accomplishment. Thus, the SEWB dimensions did not show uniformly adverse cross-sectional associations with burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress. The model explained 13.1%-35.6% of the variance across the five well-being outcomes.

After accounting for the ten SEWB dimensions, age, and sex, ADHD group membership was associated with greater emotional exhaustion (β = .15, p = .001) and perceived stress (β = .31, p < .001), but not with depersonalization, reduced personal accomplishment, or work engagement (all ps ≥ .163).

Exploratory Analysis: ADHD Group Differences in SEWB

Omnibus interaction-block tests examined whether associations between the ten SEWB dimensions and burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress differed by ADHD group membership. Adding the ten ADHD group membership × SEWB interaction terms did not significantly improve prediction of emotional exhaustion (ΔR² = .014, p = .453), depersonalization (ΔR² = .019, p = .289), reduced personal accomplishment (ΔR² = .028, p = .163), or work engagement (ΔR² = .029, p = .126). For perceived stress, the interaction block improved prediction before correction, F(10, 438) = 2.21, p = .016, increasing explained variance from R² = .349 to .380 (ΔR² = .031); however, this effect did not remain significant after Holm correction across the five outcome-level tests (p adj = .082). Overall, there was little evidence that associations between individual SEWB dim ensions and burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress -being differed between the ADHD and comparison groups. Discussion

The present study examined how ADHD-related characteristics and self-endangering work behavior (SEWB) co-occur among workers and how they are associated with occupational well-being. Three findings are particularly relevant. First, participants meeting the study's ADHD criterion reported higher levels across all ten SEWB dimensions, although these associations were less consistent when ADHD was operationalized using self-reported diagnosis alone. Second, ADHD symptoms and SEWB occurred in heterogeneous configurations that did not simply reproduce categorical ADHD group membership. Third, individual SEWB dimensions and ADHD-SEWB profiles showed differentiated associations with burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress, with little evidence that associations between SEWB and occupational well-being differed by ADHD classification.

The higher levels of SEWB observed under the primary ADHD classification extend research on individual differences in self-endangering responses to work demands. From a compensatory-regulation perspective, one possible interpretation is that ADHD-related difficulties in attention regulation, organization, prioritization, or time management increase the regulatory demands involved in meeting occupational goals, making additional investment of time, effort, or recovery resources more likely. This interpretation is consistent with research documenting compensatory strategies among adults with ADHD symptoms (Kysow et al., 2017) and qualitative reports of working adults with ADHD extending work into evenings or weekends in response to difficulties encountered during regular working hours (Oscarsson et al., 2022). It may be particularly relevant to resource-intensive forms of SEWB such as recovery sacrifice, working during leisure time, work intensification, and skipping breaks. However, the present study did not assess why participants engaged in these behaviors. Compensation may not be the only process underlying additional work investment. Extended or intensified work could also reflect difficulty disengaging from highly engaging tasks or hyperfocus (Hupfeld et al., 2024), although evidence linking hyperfocus to burnout has so far also emerged outside occupational contexts (Pyszkowska et al., 2026). Greater SEWB among workers meeting the ADHD criterion therefore cannot be interpreted as evidence that these behaviors represent compensation for ADHD-related difficulties. Other processes, including difficulty disengaging from highly engaging tasks or characteristics of the work environment, may contribute to similar behavioral patterns.

At the same time, ADHD-related characteristics alone provide an incomplete account of SEWB. The latent profiles did not reproduce categorical ADHD group membership, and similar levels of ADHD symptoms co-occurred with markedly different patterns of SEWB.

Approximately average ADHD symptoms characterized both the average symptoms/relatively low SEWB profile and the smaller quality-compromising profile, despite pronounced differences in quality deficits and concealment of performance problems. Conversely, the elevated ADHD symptoms/broad SEWB profile combined elevated symptoms with broadly elevated self-endangering behavior. These findings complement the categorical group differences by showing that ADHD-related characteristics are associated with SEWB without determining a uniform pattern of self-endangering behavior. Other individual and work-related characteristics may therefore help explain why workers with similar ADHD symptom levels differ in how self-endangering behaviors are expressed.

The associations with occupational well-being further indicate that different forms of SEWB are not functionally equivalent. Reduced exchange with colleagues and quality reduction were associated with poorer outcomes across several indicators, and quality deficits showed a particularly strong association with depersonalization. In contrast, work intensification and working during leisure time/availability were concurrently associated with higher work engagement and lower reduced personal accomplishment. Given the cross-sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence that resource-intensive forms of SEWB improve occupational well-being. Rather, they indicate that self-endangering behavior can coexist with positive aspects of occupational functioning at a given point in time. This interpretation is consistent with longitudinal evidence showing that extending and intensifying work can contribute to subsequent work stressors despite potentially supporting immediate goal attainment (Baethge et al., 2026).

The person-centred findings reinforce this differentiated picture. The ADHD-SEWB profiles did not form a simple continuum from favourable to unfavourable occupational functioning.

The elevated ADHD symptoms/broad SEWB profile was characterized particularly by high perceived stress and emotional exhaustion, whereas the smaller quality-compromising profile showed high depersonalization and emotional exhaustion alongside comparatively high work engagement. The average symptoms/relatively low SEWB profile, in turn, showed intermediate levels on several strain indicators but comparatively low work engagement.

Thus, both the variable-and person-centred findings suggest that occupational strain, engagement, ADHD symptoms, and self-endangering behavior can occur in different combinations rather than forming a single continuum of occupational functioning. The distinctive pattern of the quality-compromising profile should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously given its small size and requires replication in independent samples. Finally, there was little evidence that associations between individual SEWB dimensions and occupational well-being differed by ADHD classification. None of the omnibus interaction tests remained significant after correction for multiple testing. Within the limits of the present sample and cross-sectional design, ADHD-related characteristics may therefore be more relevant to differences in the occurrence of self-endangering behaviors than to how those behaviors are associated with burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress. Larger samples are needed to determine whether smaller differences in these associations exist.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal ordering or causality among ADHD-related characteristics, SEWB, and occupational well-being. In particular, the positive concurrent associations of work intensification and leisure-time work with work engagement should not be interpreted as evidence of beneficial effects. Longitudinal and intensive repeated-measures studies are needed to examine how these processes unfold over time.

Second, ADHD classification was based on self-reported diagnosis and/or a positive ASRS-6 screen rather than clinical assessment. Moreover, associations between ADHD and individual SEWB dimensions were less consistent when ADHD was operationalized using self-reported diagnosis alone, indicating that conclusions about the breadth of these associations depend partly on ADHD operationalization. Future studies should distinguish clinically diagnosed, screen-positive, and comparison groups while also examining ADHD symptoms dimensionally.

Third, all focal constructs were self-reported, increasing the potential for commonmethod and reporting biases, particularly for potentially sensitive behaviours such as substance use, concealment of performance problems, and quality-compromising behaviour.

Future studies could complement self-reports with repeated assessments, objective indicators, or other-source measures.

Fourth, the sample was highly educated, with more than 90% reporting a university entrance qualification, and was recruited through university participant pools.

Generalizability to other educational, occupational, employment, and sociocultural contexts is therefore uncertain. The absence of information on several demographic characteristics further limits characterization of sample diversity. Replication in more diverse and occupationally representative samples is needed.

Fifth, the latent profiles are sample-dependent configurations rather than fixed worker types. In particular, the quality-compromising profile was small (n = 23; 4.9%) and requires replication before its distinctive pattern can be considered robust.

Finally, internal consistency was comparatively low for work intensification and skipping breaks, warranting caution when interpreting associations involving these dimensions and further examination of their measurement properties.

Implications for Psychologically Informed Management of Neurodivergent Work

Managers should avoid diagnosis-based assumptions, as ADHD-related characteristics were associated with heterogeneous patterns of SEWB. Rather than viewing SEWB or ADHD solely as discrete categories, managers should adopt a dimensional understanding of ADHD symptoms. Behaviors such as sacrificing recovery, working during leisure time, skipping breaks, or concealing difficulties may signal that employees are maintaining occupational functioning at personal cost. Managerial responses should therefore focus on the specific behavioral pattern and its work context and offer reasonable accommodation from individual needs rather than diagnosis.

Conclusion

The present study identifies ADHD-related characteristics as a relevant person-level correlate of SEWB. Participants meeting the study's primary ADHD criterion reported higher levels across all forms of SEWB, while person-centred analyses showed that ADHD symptoms and SEWB co-occurred in heterogeneous configurations rather than forming a uniform pattern. Individual forms of SEWB and ADHD-SEWB configurations were also differentially associated with occupational well-being, highlighting the importance of considering specific self-endangering behaviours rather than SEWB as a unitary pattern.

However, associations between ADHD and SEWB were less consistent when ADHD was defined by self-reported diagnosis alone, indicating that conclusions about the breadth of this association depend partly on how ADHD is operationalized. Taken together, the findings suggest that understanding self-endangering work behavior requires attention both to ADHDrelated characteristics and to heterogeneity in the ways self-endangering patterns of working are expressed. Beyond these average associations, ADHD-related characteristics and SEWB may occur in heterogeneous configurations across workers. Similar levels of ADHD symptoms may coexist with different patterns of SEWB, while similar levels of SEWB may occur alongside different levels of ADHD symptoms. Variable-centred analyses can establish average associations between ADHD-related characteristics, specific forms of SEWB, and occupational well-being, but cannot identify how these characteristics are configured within individuals. A person-centred approach can therefore complement these analyses by identifying distinct ADHD-SEWB configurations and examining whether they differ in occupational well-being. Accordingly, the present study combines variable-and person-centred approaches to examine associations between ADHD-related characteristics and specific forms of SEWB, identify ADHD-SEWB configurations, and examine their associations with occupational well-being. We address in this study the following research questions: RQ1. Is ADHD classification associated with different forms of self-endangering work behavior, and how are ADHD classification and individual SEWB dimensions associated with burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress? RQ2. What distinct configurations of ADHD symptoms and self-endangering work behavior can be identified among workers? RQ3. Do the identified ADHD-SEWB profiles differ in burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress? Exploratory analysis: Do associations between individual SEWB dimensions and burnout, work engagement, and perceived stress differ by ADHD classification?

Profile 1, elevated ADHD symptoms/broad SEWB (n = 134; 28.5%), was characterized by elevated inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity together with broadly elevated SEWB, particularly recovery sacrifice, presenteeism, work during leisure time/availability, work intensification, and skipping breaks. Profile 2, quality-compromising (n = 23; 4.9%), showed approximately average ADHD symptoms but markedly elevated quality deficits and concealment of performance problems. Profile 3, low symptoms/low SEWB (n = 103; 21.9%), was characterized by low ADHD symptoms and generally low SEWB. Profile 4, average symptoms/relatively low SEWB (n = 211; 44.8%), showed approximately average ADHD symptoms and generally low-to-average SEWB. The profiles did not correspond directly to the categorical ADHD and comparison groups. The proportion of participants belonging to the ADHD group was 67.9% in Profile 1, 30.4% in Profile 2, 3.9% in Profile 3, and 35.1% in Profile 4. Thus, the profiles reflected variation in the joint configuration of continuous ADHD symptoms and SEWB rather than reproducing the categorical ADHD grouping.
