How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Zhao and Plonsky's synthesis of task-engagement research uses a distribution of reported reliability coefficients to assess measurement quality and discuss minimum acceptability. Such judgments require more than a position in that distribution. As the authors note, the pooled coefficients don't all summarize the same measurement property, and even comparable coefficients have different consequences for different scientific targets. Using the synthesis as a worked case, this response connects the authors' cautions about coefficient choice to their rationale for a percentile benchmark. Under explicit classical-error models, reliability of .6 leaves a randomized raw mean contrast unbiased but reduces its effective sample size to 60% of the original. An uncorrected slope on a noisy predictor instead converges to 60% of the latent slope. Standardized differences and correlations require further distinctions. These familiar results show why a field percentile alone can't tell a researcher what a planned study can learn. A worked planning example shows how, under the stated model, reliability and sample size together set the precision of an experimental contrast. Reliability syntheses can better support such decisions by displaying comparable coefficients separately and connecting them to uncertainty about specified estimates and predictions.

[div-01]

1 What does a reliability benchmark justify?

A researcher needs to decide whether an engagement measure is adequate for a planned study. Knowing where its reliability coefficient falls among published values provides context. It doesn't yet say how much measurement error would change the study's conclusion. Assessing what a planned study can learn requires connecting measurement error to uncertainty about its intended estimates or predictions.

Zhao and Plonsky (2026) make the issue concrete. Their synthesis reports a median of .89 across 111 coefficients from 30 studies, and they take this to suggest that the measures are "overall, quite reliable" (p. 1). They stress that the primary purpose of such a synthesis "is not to simply establish an average level or threshold of reliability, but to examine the variation of reliability across samples and contexts" (p. 2). They also repeat a qualified recommendation from Plonsky and Derrick (2016): the 25th percentile for each type of reliability can serve as a general minimum benchmark, and results below it call for caution because "measurement error may attenuate observed effects" (Zhao & Plonsky, 2026, p. 7). Plonsky and Derrick (2016, p. 550) present such type-specific percentiles as an improvement on "arbitrary and binary acceptable/nonacceptable labels", and they ask that attenuation be "taken into consideration, whether in the form of psychometric corrections or simply in prose" (p. 548). Both points are right: standing among comparable studies says more than a fixed cutoff, and error can attenuate effects.

The missing step is why a particular percentile marks an acceptable consequence for the research question. Error can reduce precision, change a standardized effect, bias an uncorrected estimate of a latent relation, or impair prediction. A coefficient's rank among published values can prompt investigation, but it determines neither the presence nor the size of these consequences. Researchers need the quantity to be estimated -the estimand -and an account of how measurement enters its estimation.

Two questions follow. Do the coefficients being compared concern the same measurement property? If they do, what does that property imply for the intended inference? Zhao and Plonsky recognize several limits relevant to the first question: they distinguish reliability from validity, acknowledge sample dependence, and warn against interpreting differences among indices as differences in measurement quality (pp. 6, 10). Their inventory is useful, especially its finding that task-specific reliability is often unavailable. The response below applies those cautions to the synthesis's subgroup interpretations and then addresses the second question.

2 Do the coefficients support the comparison?

Earlier work co-authored by Plonsky provides a diagnostic for interpreting subgroup differences. Plonsky and Derrick (2016, p. 549) found higher reliability among instruments not reported as piloted and pointed to coefficient choice as an explanation: the groups used different mixtures of indices. Zhao and Plonsky (2024, p. 252) give a related caution: a benchmark based largely on alpha (an index of consistency among items) may not apply to omega (a reliability estimate derived from a factor model). These cautions call for checking what coefficients measure before treating their numerical values as evidence of measurement quality.

That check is consequential in the task-engagement synthesis. On pp. 9-10, Zhao and Plonsky (2026) describe higher medians for observation and interaction than for Likert-type scales, and connect this pattern to the gap between interrater and internal-consistency coefficients. They suggest that the pattern "likely reflects" differences between observable and latent engagement; self-report could introduce "additional sources of measurement error". But the note to their

3 What's being generalized?

Grouping by coefficient name is insufficient. Zhao and Plonsky's Table 4 reports alpha under both internal consistency (41 coefficients, median .83) and interrater/coder reliability (five, median .97). Repeating items and repeating coders vary different parts of the measurement procedure. Within one family of indices, variants may concern single or averaged ratings, consistency or absolute agreement, and fixed or sampled coders (Hallgren, 2012, p. 30).

For engagement, a learner's score on a particular task and that learner's average over a range of tasks are different targets. Variation across tasks distinguishes one task-specific target from another; sampling tasks contributes uncertainty about the cross-task average.

Generalizability theory makes the choice of target, and of the conditions allowed to vary, explicit. A generalizability study estimates variance components over a declared universe of admissible observations; a decision study uses them for a specified measurement procedure and universe of generalization (Brennan, 2001, pp. 5-9). Brennan (2001, p. 14) illustrates how two investigators can use the same components while generalizing over different conditions: one varies both prompts and raters, while the other holds the prompts fixed. Person-by-prompt variation then contributes differently to universe-score and error variance.

A variance-ratio reliability also depends on the population, because it can change with the spread of the measured quantity even when measurement-error variance stays constant (Hallgren, 2012, p. 24). The "reliability paradox" illustrates this dependence: tasks with robust within-person condition effects can leave too little variance between people for reliable individual differences (Hedge et al., 2018), a pattern taken up in L2 work on inhibitory control (Wu et al., 2025).

These are established measurement distinctions. The further question for a reliability synthesis is how its evidence supports a subsequent study using the score. A defensible score-level reliability estimate still needs to be connected to the mean contrast, association, prediction, or decision for which adequacy is being claimed.

4 What would measurement error change?

To examine the benchmark's attenuation rationale, suppose an engagement measure has reliability .6 under a classical model with independent, mean-zero error. In a balanced randomized experiment, error of this kind leaves the raw mean contrast unbiased. Its variance equals that of an error-free study with 60% of the learners. Increasing the sample can compensate for this loss of precision. The reliability coefficient alone doesn't determine how precisely the study can estimate the treatment contrast.

In a second design, engagement predicts learning and the target is the regression slope for latent engagement. Regressing learning on the noisy score with ordinary least squares (OLS) without correcting for measurement error yields a slope that converges to 60% of that target. More learners concentrate this estimator around the attenuated value. They don't recover the latent slope. These examples use the same numerical reliability under different population conditioning: within treatment arms in the experiment, and across the predictor population in the regression.

Table 2 distinguishes these results from standardized effects and correlations. A raw mean contrast that stays unbiased can still correspond to a smaller standardized difference because error enlarges the within-arm standard deviation (Karvelis & Diaconescu, 2025). Likewise, the attenuation factor for a correlation with one noisy variable is √ ρ, rather than the factor ρ for the unstandardized slope. If both variables have independent classical measurement errors, the population correlation is multiplied by √ ρ X ρ Y (Zhao & Plonsky, 2024, p. 254).

These are model consequences, not estimates from engagement research. A reported alpha, correlation, or agreement coefficient isn't automatically either model's reliability parameter. The standardized-difference and correlation rows concern population quantities, not exact expectations A known relevant reliability removes the model-implied attenuation by rescaling with the appropriate factor. An estimated reliability adds uncertainty and can introduce finite-sample bias; estimating the right parameter doesn't make a ratio unbiased. For the raw mean contrast, the cost is lost precision, which no rescaling of the same observations recovers.

Figure 1 compares each estimator's root mean squared error (RMSE) with its error-free counterpart. RMSE combines sampling variance and squared bias; a ratio above one indicates greater error for the latent target. In the experiment, the penalty is constant at 1/ √ ρ as sample size grows. For the predictor slope, absolute RMSE decreases toward a nonzero bias floor while error-free RMSE tends to zero, so the relative penalty grows. The underlying formulas and chosen effect sizes are in Appendix A.

Both curves in panel (b) start below one, so at small samples the noisy score gives the lower RMSE. That reflects the estimator as well as the measure. Attenuation shrinks the slope toward zero, which can reduce mean squared error (MSE) relative to unshrunk error-free regression. In this model, applying the same shrinkage to the error-free estimator gives lower MSE than using the noisy score (Appendix A.3).

Changing the target changes the interpretation again. In the Gaussian predictor model, the attenuated slope is the correct conditional-mean slope for predicting learning from the available noisy score. It's biased for the latent slope but not for that predictive coefficient. Prediction error still depends on measurement quality. An analysis needs to distinguish a relation involving latent engagement from a prediction using the score a future learner will provide.

Papi et al. (2026, pp. 4-6) report factor-analytic evidence and reliability for the three motivational self-system subscales, and compare motivational constructs as predictors of Intended Effort. One possible latent target is the difference between standardized partial coefficients for Ideal L2 Self and Integrativeness in a joint regression of latent Intended Effort on both latent constructs. This differs from their tested score-level contrasts of unstandardized coefficients. The predictors correlate at .53 (p. 5); error in either can bias both coefficients (Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016, p. 3). A joint measurement model could support sensitivity analysis, conditional on which error components the data distinguish and assumptions about the others.

Table 2's single-predictor factors don't settle that comparison. 5 How should adequacy be judged?

A field percentile alone can't establish adequacy for an intended inference. Plonsky and Derrick (2016, p. 548) qualify their benchmark, distinguish reliability types, and ask researchers to consider stakes. The Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing likewise treat "the minimum acceptable level for any index" as "a matter of professional judgment" and ask for reliability evidence "for the interpretation for each intended score use" (American Educational Research Association et al., 2014, pp. 41-42).

Even within a comparable class, a percentile changes when reporting practices or the distribution of studies changes. The error consequences for an unchanged study needn't change with it. Standing above the benchmark doesn't establish adequate precision, and standing below it signals the caution Plonsky and Derrick recommend without saying how much an inference is affected.

The practical alternative is to state the inference and examine plausible measurement processes. Gelman and Carlin (2014, pp. 641-643) orient design analysis toward what a study can learn about an effect's direction and magnitude, using assumptions informed by more than the observed estimate. Return to the balanced engagement experiment. Its target is the mean treatment contrast in the defined engagement score, expressed in units of its latent within-arm standard deviation.

The relevant reliability concerns variation among learners within treatment arms and error under the intended replication procedure. A pilot with parallel measurements of the same target could inform those components. Under the additive model, the covariance between replicates estimates the latent variance, and half the variance of their difference estimates the error variance (Appendix A). This interpretation requires a stable target and equal-variance errors independent of each other and of that target. Shared rater bias or genuine change would require a different model. Without suitable evidence, .6 and .9 are sensitivity scenarios, not estimated reliabilities or established bounds for engagement measures.

With latent within-arm variance set to one, the standard error is 4/(N ρ): about .183 at reliability .6 and .149 at .9 for 200 learners. Under a normal approximation, the corresponding 95% interval half-widths are about .36 and .29. If the estimated contrast were .2, either interval would include zero. Improving measurement narrows uncertainty without ensuring that this design can determine the direction of a contrast that small. Under the model, 300 learners at reliability .6 provide the same precision as 200 at .9.

These are planning approximations conditional on the stated variances; they don't propagate uncertainty in estimated reliability. Costs and feasibility would be needed to choose between improving measurement and recruiting more learners. The example shows what those choices change under the model, without prescribing an instrument or a sample size.

For a latent-predictor slope, a design analysis could show how estimates change across plausible measurement-error models and carry uncertainty about their parameters through to the estimated slope. Where repeated measurements are available, simulated data from the fitted model can be compared with observed patterns of disagreement across items, raters, or occasions (Gelman et al., 2020, p. 30). The analysis would clarify what the measurements support about the relation's direction and magnitude. Where a practical decision is involved, the consequences of acting under that uncertainty also need weighing.

A reliability synthesis can support these analyses by recording the scored object, replication design, population, coefficient variant, and intended use, together with study and sample identifiers. Comparable estimates can inform plausible measurement models and their uncertainty. Other estimates remain informative about measurement practice. Hierarchical modelling can accommodate dependence and variation when a defensible common target exists; it doesn't create that target.

Alongside medians and interquartile ranges, syntheses should display individual coefficients within groups sharing a coefficient variant and replication design. REGEMA, a reporting checklist for reliability generalization studies, already asks for separate meta-analyses "for each type of reliability coefficient" and for graphs of their distribution (Sánchez-Meca et al., 2021, items 8, 20). Within interrater reliability, Pearson correlations and agreement percentages also need separate treatment. Zhao and Plonsky report coefficient-specific medians and discuss the two indices separately (pp. 6, 10), but their pooled interrater row combines them (p. 8).

Separate dotplots, retaining study and sample membership and marking any coefficients removed as outliers (Zhao and Plonsky removed two, p. 4), would let readers inspect distributional shape, clustering, and the support for subgroup comparisons. A pooled distribution of unlike indices would retain the comparability problem.

Multiverse analysis can then show how conclusions vary across defensible analytic choices. Maie et al. (2024, p. 17) summarize across alternatives only when those alternatives target the same effect with comparable validity and precision. Scoring methods may differ in validity, so in their vocabularylearning example they report results for dictation-based, lenient, and strict scoring separately (pp. 8-10). A reliability synthesis could likewise examine outlier handling and the treatment of multiple coefficients within comparable groups.

Zhao and Plonsky already compare coefficient-, sample-, and study-level summaries (p. 6). Changing the unit or weighting can change the target distribution: a typical reported coefficient needn't represent a typical sample or study. A multiverse display would report them side by side rather than average them as interchangeable estimates. Choosing which coefficient to extract when a study reports several kinds also needs separate interpretation, because those coefficients aren't estimates of one quantity.

The reporting process also constrains what can be generalized. Zhao and Plonsky (2026, pp. 3, 5) required a reliability coefficient for inclusion; their flow diagram lists nonreporting as an exclusion reason for 57 records. They flag this among their limitations (p. 10), and Plonsky and Derrick (2016, p. 548) likewise allow that published estimates may give "an inflated view" while having "no evidence as to whether or not this is the case". The direction and size of any resulting bias remain unknown. Similarly, selection for statistical significance changes comparisons involving noisy effect estimates (Gelman, 2023; Loken & Gelman, 2017); the examples here are unconditional and make no selection claim.

Researchers choosing an engagement measure need to know what errors it introduces into their planned analysis. Zhao and Plonsky's inventory can help specify plausible measurement processes. Connecting those processes to the intended estimate or prediction would show what better measurement or more observations could achieve, and provide a basis for judging adequacy.
