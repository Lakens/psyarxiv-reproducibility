Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The common narrative around university students and generative AI is one of crisis. Underpinning this is an implicit assumption: if students have the capacity to cheat, they will. Our survey reveals two missing factors from that narrative: students have ethical principles, and students want to learn. While a minority of students might fit that implicit assumption of the crisis narrative, a similar proportion refuse to use any genAI tools at all. In between these are the majority: students using genAI tools in what they self-define as a 'supportive' way. These students, finding these tools unavoidable, worrying about being disadvantaged or simply finding them valuable, struggle to use them in a way that doesn't compromise their personal sense of authorship, fits with their environmental and social principles, and doesn't detract from their ability to learn. Students typically care greatly about these issues and are internally conflicted about this, feeling guilt when using these tools and personally trying to find that invisible line where support becomes cheating. They also use a range of rhetorical techniques to frame their personal usage and preserve their sense of authorship. We argue that we need to work with students, using approaches that enhance these feelings and encourage self-discipline, rather than primarily adopting a 'criminal' model of checks, detection and punishment.

Introduction

"AI can beat real university students, study suggests", (BBC, 2024), "Nearly all students say they now use AI" (Daily Mail, 2026), "Revealed: Thousands of UK university students caught cheating using AI" (The Guardian, 2025a), "Surge in students cheating with AI" (The Times, 2026), "Student cheating is becoming impossible to detect' (New York Times, 2026), "'An existential crisis': can universities survive ChatGPT?" (The Times, 2025). Mainstream media articles on university students' use of generative AI tools such as ChatGPT are currently rife. In combination, the existence of these tools that can score well on university assignments, the ubiquity of their use, higher education's inability to detect their use and the absence of any apparent countervailing force that could stop this gives the sense of a true crisis in higher education. However, this narrative rests upon an implicit assumption: that if the majority of students are given the opportunity to cheat, they will. Another related line of articles portrays students as using these tools as a 'buddy', relying on them continuously for things beyond the academic, such as wellbeing, nutrition, life advice and mental health support (e.g. The Guardian, 2025b).

Within the academic world, universities have indeed scrambled in the last few years to deal with the impact of these tools, such as ChatGPT, on education. The conversation has moved on from prevention, which is generally seen as impossible, to varying degrees of incorporation, with a common focus on teaching students to use these tools critically (Shibani et al., 2024). This involves either limiting their negative impact on students' learning or finding ways to use them positively, for example as dialogue tools to enhance learning, or having students criticize the output of these tools as a way of developing their own critical thinking skills (Mazari, 2025).

There has simultaneously been an explosion of research on how students are using these tools. However, little has focused on specifically challenging the implicit assumption in that crisis narrative, that the majority of students will cheat if given the opportunity. The clues to a counter-narrative to this are in the literature, dotted across a variety of work such as Ka et al. (2023), Johnston et al. (2024), Machkour and Abriane (2025), Oberg et al. (2026) and Rinaldy et al. (2023). This is a picture of students trapped in a situation not of their making or choosing, feeling either pulled or pushed towards using these tools, to varying extents and in various ways, and struggling to do this in line with their personal principles. This is often not an enjoyable experience and the image of the student gleefully strolling their way to a low-effort, low-learning degree is the exception, rather than the rule. This alternate vision of the student experience and their attitudes towards genAI was recently highlighted in an 'Inside Higher Education' article titled 'Students as conscientious objectors to Generative AI' (Fehr & Saul, 2026).

Generative AI is of course not the first opportunity students have had for drifting through a degree without doing the work. Contract cheating, where students have someone else, either someone they know, or someone paid from an 'essay mill' do their assignments for them, has always been a possibility, and near impossible to detect. However, studies estimate only a few percent of students have historically done this (Newton, 2018; Curtis & Clare, 2017; Bretag et al, 2019). There is a difference with the use of generative AI tools of course: contract cheating involves engaging with another human being and in the case of an essay mill is much more of a clear crossing of an ethical boundary. However, support from a family member or friend can cover a spectrum of levels of support and expose similar ethical grey areas. In an interesting study, Rundle et al (2023) looked specifically at why students don't engage in contract cheating, and students' personal morals and their desire to learn emerge as key reasons. These are the two things primarily missing from that crisis narrative: students have ethical principles, and students want to learn. They also of course want to get the best grade they can and can also be tempted into low effort and short-term, self-defeating choices as anyone can, but those latter factors compete with the former, which provide that countervailing force typically missing from the crisis narrative. These studies do also suggest that some students have a more Machiavellian / transactional approach to their degrees, but they seem to be the minority.

There is a tendency for older adults to stereotype younger people, and treat them as a homogeneous group (Devlin, 2006). However, if our experiences as university educators have taught us one thing it is that students are massively diverse in opinions, attitudes, goals, principles and personality (Geisler-Brenstein, 1996). In order to explore this diversity and how it relates to genAI-use, recent work (Bodei et al., 2026) first mapped out the various stages of a typical university assignment, from ideation, through sourcing and planning to drafting and reviewing. The authors then found a complex interaction between different student goals and the level of AI use across these different stages. Students who were focused primarily on maximizing the quality of their work vs maximizing their learning vs maximizing productivity all used these tools in differing ways across those five stages, with those focused on maximizing quality and their personal learning being much more cautious, selective and critical in their use. Machkour and Abriane (2025) noted that many students feel an 'internal conflict between cognitive autonomy and algorithmic assistance'. They found that students have fear, doubts, and resistance to using this technology: they are not all enthusiastic adopters. They are also concerned about their ability to learn and their sense of authorship being reduced by the use of genAI. With this in mind, the presence of tools like ChatGPT are not necessarily a positive for students, an option that they can just take or leave as they like. Like the modern presence of various stimulants, or online entertainment, their ease of access requires substantial willpower to continually resist which can be exhausting and frustrating for the modern student (Chou & Hsiao, 2000). This requirement for willpower is particularly taxing as genAI tools blur the lines between fair use and cheating more than previous ethically-dubious practises, leading some students to feel perpetually anxious about whether their level of use is acceptable (Malik et al., 2023). To some extent, as with familial support, this has also always been the case with plagiarism, where students often take time to learn what counts as copying vs research. There is a long history of some students overly relying on other work, or textbooks and rephrasing them just enough to bypass plagiarism detection while avoiding the deep 'thinking work' we would hope for them to do while producing e.g. an essay (Landau et al. 2002). A similar vague and invisible line exists between using AI tools in a supportive role vs allowing them to do the work for you. However, the interactive, back and forth nature of these tools, and the way they make helpful suggestions and encourage further use can make it easier to cross this line. Students seem aware of this tension however, and the majority show a strong desire to retain a sense of their personal authorship (Machkour and Abriane 2025).

The present study aims primarily to understand students' experience of these tools amidst a constantly shifting landscape in 2026. Most modern students have now experienced using these tools for several years at school and college, and are mature users (Fu et al., 2026), and so unlike in much early work, the novelty factor has now faded, students have well-worn workflows (Bodei et al., 2026) and are intimately aware of their quirks and limitations. We aim to understand how they think about these tools, their awareness and concern about them on a societal and environmental level, their feelings about their presence in academia, their attitudes, opinions and ethical stance with respect to them as well as their emotional response. To explore this, we conducted two surveys: one quantitative, and one qualitative with a cohort of year 1 BSc Psychology students at University College London. In the latter, we specifically asked students to respond to mainstream media narratives about the way they use genAI tools.

Method Participants

The quantitative survey was administered to a cohort of 120 year-1 BSc Psychology students at University College London and 94 took part in the survey. Both surveys were intentionally anonymous, requesting no demographics to maximise honesty. However, in terms of the cohort demographics: out of 120, 85.8% were registered as female and their average age was 19.2 (SD = 0.7), with a minimum of 18, and maximum of 22. The qualitative survey was administered to that same cohort, plus an additional cohort of 70 students on the similar 'BSc Psychology and language sciences', who share the class in which it was administered. Out of that combined cohort of 190, 108 took part in the qualitative survey. The combined cohort were 82.6% registered as female with an average age of 19.7 (SD = 1.4), a minimum of 18 and a maximum of 35 (this was an outlier, after this student the next oldest was 23). For the qualitative survey, eleven individuals did not give consent to their data being used for academic publishing, so were removed before analysis, leading to a final total sample of 97.

Procedure and materials

The quantitative survey was delivered in the first term of the first year of the students' degree in the 25/26 academic year during small group seminar sessions. Students were given a link to the Qualtrics survey, which they filled out individually. No demographics were collected due to a desire to maximise anonymity given that students may be concerned about admitting to AI practise to their university. This was overseen by the first author, as the head of year 1 for the programme, but was directly administered by a range of seminar tutors.

The quantitative survey involved a large range of questions on students' attitudes and beliefs towards generative AI tools. The full list can be found at the online database. We did not include all questions in this paper as some related to more local matters, such as awareness of local AI policy.

The qualitative survey was delivered in the following term (term two of the first year), in the same 25/26 academic year. This data was collected by the first author as part of a 'Qualitative and mixed methods' module. Students were all sent a link to the survey, were presented with the brief and consent forms and chose whether to take part.

In the qualitative survey students were presented with the following prompt and asked to type their response into an open text box.

Some media outlets have reported them being used as a 'constant buddy' --do you experience it like that, or more as a simple tool like a word processor? -How do you feel about their role as writing tools in academia? Analytical approaches

The first author conducted the primary analysis of the qualitative data using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022). Throughout the process my primary position probably leans towards one of defensiveness over my students with respect to many common narratives around their use of AI, and the disconnect between this and my interactions with them. However, it was never my aim to pretend that there aren't any students abusing these tools, and I try to recognise that variability and diversity throughout. The analysis was conducted with a simple tagging program, Workflowy, and can be seen at (anonymous link) https://workflowy.com/s/genai-analysis/vnFR66qtFdSdd91L. I began with familiarization by listening to all responses through a text to speech programme and it was clear even at this early stage that many students expressed a range of ethical concerns around generative AI, that there was a distinct lack of cynicism / Machiavellianism / instrumentalism and many felt torn and conflicted. Coding was then conducted in Workflowy, using the tagging function to code. All the tags used can be seen at the top of the linked document with working definitions developed throughout the process next to them. The authors' notes can also be seen in the same document. The final stages of theme development were integrated into the writing process, moving between the developing results write up and the Workflowy analysis. As I developed and refined my themes, this was aided and checked by rearranging the codes in the Workflowy document into the sub-headings that can be seen, which roughly map on to the final four themes. Over time a natural narrative developed, from general feelings, to the range of concerns students have, to then answering the puzzle of why they continue to use the tools.

In parallel to the bottom-up process of coding and theme consolidation, the second author conducted a discourse analysis (DA) of the survey responses informed by Michael Billig's (1996) rhetorical approach. DA is interested in the social functions of language, such as the use of rhetorical devices (metaphors, disclaimers, preamble, tangents, extreme-case formulations, etc.) to perform social functions (self-justification, performance of identity, policing of norms or boundaries, etc.). Billig's approach is purposely less formalised and more flexible methodologically than other DA methods, and it contributed two key insights to this analysis: (1) Thinking on social questions is argumentative, i.e., it involves taking a position on matters of controversy; consequently, self-reports on controversial matters perform rhetorical work, rather than being neutral descriptions of reality. (2) The building blocks of this rhetorical work are socially shared (common-sense) ideas, metaphors, imagery, etc., which can be often contradictory; i.e., society's common sense does not form a coherent worldview, but contains contrary themes (ideological dilemmas; Billig et al., 1988). The primary goal in the analysis was to identify what rhetorical resources participants drew on in their responses, how that was achieved in their speech, and what social function was served through them. Reading was iterative and comparative across the corpus: candidate observations were checked against the full set of transcripts for deviant or counter-cases, and analytic claims about a given rhetorical move were only retained where they held up against instances that seemed, on first pass, to run against them.

Results

The results section is primarily organised by the themes identified in the qualitative data but also provides bar charts from the quantitative survey at the start of themes to which they relate. We clustered our results under four themes: 'A tool, a tutor -but not a buddy', 'Societal concerns around genAI usage', 'Concerns around genAI usage in academia', and 'Factors driving genAI usage in students'. Following this, the second author provides an analysis of the way students use rhetoric to frame their use of these tools. In this first theme we look at students' general levels of genAI usage, the kinds of tasks they use it for, their awareness of limitations, and the importance to them of retaining their authorial voice.

1.1 Only a tool / just for support.

In the quantitative survey, students were asked a series of questions on their level of genAI usage, and the kinds of things they use it for. In the top left of Figure 1 we can see that very few (4.21%) use it every day, with 9.47% saying they never use it, and the vast majority using it in a more moderate manner. In the top right, the vast majority (81.05%) had used it to help in completing an assignment in some manner, while 11.58% had not. In the bottom graphs, we can see that genAI usage is spread across a range of academic activities, but is in fact highest for studying for exams, rather than for essay-writing (bottom left). Within the qualitative data, and in line with the top left graph in Figure 1, the vast majority of students rejected the idea of genAI tools as a 'buddy', in opposition to that particular mainstream media narrative, with most seeing them as "just a tool" (P37, P89). Some explicitly disclaimed any social use of genAI, for example P10: "I treat them more like practical helpers than emotional companions" and P27: "I don't necessarily see it as a 'buddy', but more like a useful tool instead". Others highlighted the instrumental uses, for example, P36: "a simple tool" and P47: "like an advanced writing aid".

P9: "I frequently use it to support me in my degree, but only as support: I always think and brainstorm an essay topic myself first, and then I discuss my ideas with the AI and it helps me find research."

1.2 Useful as a personal tutor

In the quantitative survey, students were asked about their experience of using genAI tools in academia, and the effect it has on their productivity, motivation, enjoyment, and work quality. In the top left of Figure 2, we can see that, while responses are spread, the majority of students felt that AI tools supported their learning of new concepts and the development of their skills. The majority also felt that it improved their motivation and enjoyment (top right), that it improves the quality of their work (bottom left) and increases their productivity (bottom right). We must also recognise the variability, as a substantial minority felt the opposite in all these cases.

Figure 2 A series of graphs depicting students' beliefs about genAI tools as an academic supportive tool.

The qualitative responses support these statistics and challenge a common media narrative that students will use genAI tools to just do their coursework for them. Many found more value in it as a learning aid, providing back and forth discussions to enhance learning, "similar to a tutor being accessible at any time." (P90). Uses included providing feedback on the grammar and structure of essays, tightening arguments, sense-checking ideas, and explaining concepts. One student described having extended discussions of lecture content that they were not confident enough to have in person:

P86: "When studying, AI helps me understand concepts discussed in class more clearly by rephrasing ideas, breaking down complex material, and answering follow-up questions that I might feel hesitant to ask in person. This makes learning feel more interactive and personalised, rather than passive."

Some found a sense of calm, feeling held and supported by the AI tool: P40: "I feel relaxed and happy whenever using them, like everything becomes easy and organized, I feel less anxious about organizing a note on my own or come up with a plan myself."

1.3 Awareness of limitations

The quantitative survey asked the students a series of questions about their awareness of the limitations of genAI tools. In Figure 3 we can see, in the top left graph, the vast majority of students agreeing that these tools are limited in their ability to handle complex tasks. In the top right, again the vast majority agree these tools can generate out of context or inappropriate output, and in the bottom left, that the output can be factually inaccurate, and in the bottom right, can be biased and unfair.

Figure 3 Responses to four questions on the students' beliefs about the limitations of genAI tools.

This awareness of limitations was echoed strongly in the qualitative data, where the students made clear that these tools were "… useful for small writing things but it's not that great when it comes to longer writing about more complex topics." (P20). Students noted that they sometimes "...makes up papers or references or quotes from papers." (P36), that "...it does hallucinate..." (P92) and "…they often overlook details and their accuracy is not always the best, they need to be constantly supervised and check by the human eye." (P55).

Some students recognised the cognitive limitations of AI, that "...it's not pretty good at critical thinking" (P94), that "the insights they give are very surface level…" (P83) and they are "not that good at understanding human behaviour" (P99). They also felt it would make faulty arguments that wouldn't stand up in an academic setting: P92: "For example, it may find a study relating to concepts adjacent to what you are writing about but then draw causal links to help your argument where it wouldn't be acceptable to do so in an academic context."

For some students, AI writing was perceived as "not that good" (P67), and "not very high quality and often uses a lot of repetition." (P73). One student said they knew "deep down that I could do it better myself" (P95), demonstrating a trade-off between effort and quality. However, a different dynamic was in play for other students, perhaps where English wasn't their first language, as in that case some felt their own "writing isn't as good as AI-generated writing" (P14). AI usage for such students is therefore likely harder to resist, as it produces higher quality for lower effort than the student alone.

1.4 Authorship

In the quantitative survey, students were asked a series of questions about the importance of a sense of authorship to them, how much they thought that using genAI affected that feeling of authorship, and their level of trust in genAI in their academic writing. In the top left graph of Figure 4, we can see that the majority of students stated this sense of authorship as very, or extremely important, with none saying it was not at all important. In the top right, we can see a slim majority of students feeling that using genAI doesn't affect their sense of their own authorship. In the bottom left we see mixed results, where some feel it enhances their personal writing style and voice, while others feel it hinders it. In the bottom right, we see that overall trust in AI for academic writing is generally low, to moderate.

Figure 4 Four graphs depicting student responses to a range of questions about the extent to which authorship is important to them, and how their use of AI affects that.

In the qualitative data, students showed considerable concern that their sense of authorship remain intact, and we see that the effect of genAI on their sense of authorship depends very much on how they use it. P32 felt that "The good balance for me is using it minimally, and critically, then ensuring the final work is demonstrably mine." while P44 asked "…if you use an AI tool to generate your idea, structure your essay, summarise/explain all you sources, etc. then is it really your work anymore?" Overall many students emphasised the tool's supportive role and their desire to feel that their work is their own:

P35: "I'm aware of ethical boundaries and want to make sure my work is always genuinely my own. Overall, I see generative AI as a useful tool, as long as it's used critically and not blindly trusted."

Theme 2. Societal concerns around genAI usage

While the quantitative survey did not ask the students about societal issues, the qualitative survey did, and the students showed a high degree of awareness of the societal debates around AI use, and considerable concern over its impact.

2.1 Social impact

In general, many students expressed strong disliking for these tools, which is an under-represented position in most media narratives. P17 said that "I think people abuse this tool ... I don't like AI, when it suppresses its role of tool and becomes creator.", while P38 expressed a strong position: "Overall, I think genAI is a plague and needs to die asap before we become completely complacent."

P71: "I think to great extent this is the result of our pervasive culture of capitalism which idolises efficiency and productivity above quality and the process."

Interestingly, several individuals expressed concern at older generations, "who can oftentimes not tell the difference between real content and AI generated ones. This can have consequences such as fraud or spreading propaganda" (P77) and concern for "...how creative industries are dying out as a result of AI." (P19).

Highlighting the diversity of student perspectives, others expressed only positive sentiments, mostly about its value as a tool or convenience:

P107: "I feel like generative AI is beneficial in daily life. We can ask about anything we're unsure and it will provide the answer really fast and efficiently. It's like a pro version of Google."

2.2 Environmental impact

Our students showed a high degree of awareness of the environmental costs of using AI, including the requirement of water for cooling, as well as carbon production. For some, such as P19, this was a reason to avoid them entirely ("The damaging effects AI has on our already vulnerable environment is also a reason I stay away from it."), and others, such as P23 to curb their use: "...trying to send less chats because it consumes so much energy". They also frequently expressed guilt over this when using these tools.

P9: "Additionally, I don't like how damaging constant use of GenAI is for the environment -after having gotten so used to it as an efficient search engine, it's hard to imagine going back to always only googling, but the environmental concern makes me feel guilty."

2.3 How to live with AI

Some students expressed a belief that we need to find a way to preserve human thinking in an age of AI. P34 felt we should "...focus on how to adapt the human race to this new creation, train their minds to a newer world, so that this worry of losing their human ability to even think does not happen.". P36 felt that AI tools cannot "replace humans in terms of critical analysis and evaluation" and P44 believed that "initial idea generation should be left up to humans and I think that evaluating and discussing things should also be left up to humans."

Several students also expressed more of an ethical stance that AI shouldn't replace humans specifically in academia. P53 felt that "Investigation of sources, research, and writing about a subject should come from the human mind ... If we don't have to think for ourselves, how necessary is this field as a whole?" and P103 agreed that "It's important that critical thinking, originality and personal understanding remain central to academic work, rather than being replaced by AI systems and AIgenerated responses."

Theme 3. Concerns around genAI usage in academia

The quantitative survey did not probe deeply into students' concerns around AI usage in academia, however it did ask an overall question about their ethical standpoint on its use in academia, depicted in Figure 5. We can see that, while there is a broad spread of responses, the overall trend is towards being unfavourable. As we will see these strong ethical principles were echoed in the qualitative data.

3.1 Cognitive debt

In the qualitative data, our students showed a consistent general awareness of the wider conversation over the effect of AI on cognitive skills and also articulated personal concerns about the effect they were experiencing themselves. In some cases, as with P38, this felt self-defeating as "I would learn much faster by trying and failing than by using this shortcut.". P36 also expressed concerns on their impact: "I feel more stupid when I use it too much -like it gets harder to think clearly the more I use it, and I don't like feeling that kind of brain fog". P92 also felt that it could lock them into an idea that might be worse and reduce their creativity: "...if I use AI to brainstorm ideas for my essay... I am not as innovative in my ideas. " The way in which these tools limit personal exploration of ideas was echoed by P101: "I have found that if I put an essay topic etc in just to brainstorm/get ideas, it limits the way I explore the topic -I will align to whatever chatGPT puts out instead of really fully thinking through the topic myself." Other students were concerned about the long-term impact:

P95: "In terms of my using it, I do find that it has replaced my creative brain to a point which actually upsets me sometimes. I find it so much harder now to read an essay title or a prompt and actually come up with all of my points to discuss without any AI. I don't use it in the actual writing stage but that initial planning stage I now struggle without it. I'm not even sure when it changed because I used to be completely capable of doing it without the help."

3.2 The loss of learning opportunity

Our students showed considerable concern about lost learning opportunities from the use of genAI e.g. to support them in coursework. P35 felt that "… using it to produce whole assignments takes away from the point of developing academic skills." and P56 asked "why pay for a degree if you're not going to actually do it?" P76 felt that use of AI for the "substantive problem solving process… bypasses the learning itself." and P41 said they "would never use AI … otherwise why am I here." P17 was similarly absolute: "My view is simple. If I signed up to do this degree, then I need to learn, not use a tool to do the learning and work for me. It's unfair on myself..."

P42: "I personally do not tend to rely on them too much during my studies, to ensure I gain all the skills and knowledge available to me. It might be more convenient and quicker way of doing things -but that does not mean the long-term outcome and benefits gained from education stay the same!"

However, some saw it as commensurate with educational purposes as they felt it helped them learn, which perhaps connects to many perceiving it as a useful tutor:

P64: "I don't find it problematic to use them on my degree, as the final goal of doing a degree is to learn skills and knowledge and if AI plays a role in helping me to better acquire those skills and knowledge I should use it."

For students who saw their degree as transactional (where the grade was just the aim) the pressure to use was greater (or at least there was less of a countervailing force):

P71: "I also think this is why it is so tempting and addicting for students to lean on genAI (fairly or unfairly) in their assignments: a university degree feels to me no longer like it is about learning, and more so that it is a qualifying stepping stone towards future employment."

3.3 Ethical stance / refusal to use

In line with Figure 5, many students expressed a very strong ethical stance against AI, with many wishing it didn't exist, believing it shouldn't be used in education and could somehow be blocked (although most recognised this is impossible). P102 said that they "...do not use tools such as chatgpt or gemini under any circumstances … they prevent critical thinking skills and nurture a dependance on the tool itself.

" Similarly, P53 said that "... I don't tend to use AI other than when it's explicitly advised, purely out of principle. I like the satisfaction of knowing that my work is my own." and P73 agreed: "I never use AI generatively in my degree, as it would be against my personal moral code...". P19: "I don't use genAI tools frequently, maybe once or twice a month. This is partially due to my own pride (I like to do things on my own terms), and because I really value accurate feedback (so if my writing is poor, constructive criticism from teachers etc. can make me better)."

Theme 4. Factors driving AI usage in students

Given the concerns highlighted in themes 2 and 3, and the substantial anguish students experience, we might wonder why many students continue to use these tools (although as has also been highlighted in theme 1, 'use' can vary massively from minor to major support). The quantitative survey did not ask questions specifically about why students used genAI, however they volunteered a lot of opinions on this in the qualitative survey. This revealed that there are powerful forces pushing usage, even despite all the concerns.

4.1 Unavoidable / need to use in future

Students often felt powerless, seeing AI adoption in academia and in their future careers as unavoidable. P21 said that "Even though I would have felt better if I had not used Chat GPT at all, in this day and age it is inevitable." P92 explained that this inevitability is because: "...realistically, we and our coworkers will be utilizing it in actual jobs".

P95: "I think it's hard to completely avoid the tools in academia now because they've become so widespread. In theory I wish we could go back to a time without them and remove it completely but that obviously can't happen. So if they continue to be used in the working world then I think all we can do is teach people to incorporate it in the right ways in academia so they can go on doing the same."

4.2 Disadvantaged if not used

Due to the unavoidability and widespread use, many students felt they would be disadvantaged if they didn't use genAI, and had a suspicion that academic standards were changing to allow for the use of AI. P56 felt that "If I use them on my degree, it feels like I am keeping up with everyone else." and P92 felt that "I sometimes fear that not utilising it would put me at a disadvantage." P51 regretted this seeming inevitability: "I also would rather not use it but I know that everybody else does so I don't want to be behind or doing work that everyone else is having AI do for them." P46 captured this ambivalence: "I have negative feelings towards genAI...it is hard not to feel like you are falling behind if you do not use it in any capacity, even for the understanding of terminology."

P43: "I personally use it because I feel as though I would be at a disadvantage, majority if not all students use gen ai, meaning resources and ideas are found much quicker and easier… in secondary school, I would feel cheated, friends who had been absent and unwilling and unmotivated to work would use gen ai, often completely writing their coursework would get much better grades than individuals such as myself who tried hard to use their own experiences and notes from teachers, or even when writing personal statements."

4.3 Temptation / too easy

One important factor to consider is the sheer size of the gulf in effort between the hours and days a student might need to e.g. craft a powerful argument in an essay themselves and the moments it takes to ask genAI to do it (even if this produces lower quality output). This provides a colossal temptation which even a strong ethical stance can struggle to surmount (although as mentioned, some still do). P73 said that "… it feels impossible to resist the temptation to use it." and P49 said "... I have to admit that sometimes I may rely on these tools too much, probably due to my laziness and dependence."

P95: "So generally I think using the tools honestly frustrates me because not only is the quality of it not particularly good, I know deep down that I could do it better myself but those skills just haven't been used in the same way as they used to be so I find myself struggling more and using AI as a crutch in certain situations."

4.4 Time pressure

The above becomes all the more powerful as students face the pressure of multiple deadlines in a short space of time, and if they have left assignments too late. P93 admitted that, despite not liking genAI generally, "...it has been very useful in certain occasions, particularly when I don't have a lot of time to complete an essay or other time-consuming assignment." P61 also captured this ambivalence: P61: "… there have been times where

I've been rushing to complete something by a deadline and have almost relied on AI to help me do it more efficiently by giving me ideas, summarising literature, and making what I've written sound more academic. When I submit my work I don't feel as accomplished as I feel like it's not really my work. It also makes me feel less intelligent because I then think that I never would've been able to come up with these clever ideas. I feel that I won't be as proud of myself getting my results for coursework because I know that I have used AI significantly to help me."

4.5 Attached / addictive -habit creeps up

Many students expressed a desire to reduce their usage but having grown attached or addicted to these tools found it hard to stop. Once that gulf in effort had been experienced, it was hard to go back, as P54 indicated: "It is very tempting to cross the line when using AI, so if you do not have a strong moral compass people will use it to claim the work as their own…" P56 agreed that "There is a delicate balance between using it for efficiency and being lazy."

Other students, such as P34 highlighted the way that more low-level use can creep up: P34: "... I do find myself using AI innocently and often, which can of course adapt my mind to become dependent on it". As P15 noted, early exploration can become a habit: "... they keep using it, even after they've figured things out." Others noted how this can turn into a well-developed dependence: P59: "...now, whenever I have an important assignment, I ask generative AI to approve of my ideas and proofread my work in the end. I am sometimes afraid to proceed without having asked those AI tools."

Rhetoric and the dilemmas of AI use

Students were not split into "pro-AI" and "anti-AI" camps -most expressed mixed feelings and drew on two contrary repertoires simultaneously: efficiency and authenticity / earned achievement. Each repertoire could be used flexibly to justify either the pro-or anti-genAI stance.

The Efficiency repertoire drew on values of capitalist productivity, technological progress, and streamlining: "it's the future [...] everyone else is using it" (P99), "everybody else does so I don't want to be behind" (P51). Some argued that withholding AI use is in some way setting them up to fail on the job market (with frequent references of "keeping up" and "falling behind"), which constructs academia as an extension of the workplace. Others emphasised the productivity gains from using genAI ("It also helps me to write essays much quicker" (P3); "resources and ideas are found much quicker and easier" (P43); "I will admit it's a quicker way to do a literature review, etc." (P81)), which transposes the values of the workplace and the production line: coursework reimagined as industrial output amenable to AI-automation so the student can produce more content per unit of time. Such responses often translated the abstract value of "fairness" into "overcoming disadvantage": it would be unfair for me to not use genAI, the implicit argument goes, because it would put me at a disadvantage. Most of the time, a straightforward acceptance of efficiency norms was used to justify genAI use; however, this repertoire could be combined with a moral critique to delegitimise genAI, as P7 did: "Nowadays, everyone wants to be technological and modern and trendy. AI is making people lazy".

The repertoire of Authenticity or Earned Achievement drew on more humanist values of selfexpression and integrity. Here the value of "fairness" could be translated into "maintaining integrity" or "not cheating". However, this did not automatically lead to a rejection of genAI, as many respondents used this set of values to distinguish between legitimate and illegitimate uses. The notion of "authorship" was ubiquitous in achieving this goal: if one can claim to have maintained one's sense of authorship, one has not cheated or done anything unethical.

The challenge for this rhetorical strategy is that "authorship" itself is poorly defined and is open to renegotiation. Here a variety of social representations of genAI became useful -we saw these in Theme 1.1 and 1.2, "the tool" and "the tutor". Understanding genAI as a tool boosts one's claims to authorship: mere tools have no agency (a text processor cannot be credited as the author of a novel), so authorship by default goes to the user of the tool. But genAI is clearly more than "just a tool", as it is in principle capable of making creative decisions about a text, often beyond the understanding of the user who entered the prompt. The metaphor of "the tutor" is therefore potentially destabilising because it carries the implication that genAI could be more than just a tool -and could therefore strip its user of their legitimising "authorship".

To resolve this quandary, some students fixated on their role in shaping the final product (e.g., an essay submission), rather than the process that leads up to it. The intellectual work that goes into producing an essay -searching for literature, reading primary sources, annotating and synthesising, forming conclusions and checking them against counter-arguments -gets deprioritised; the focus shifts onto the end product, the written work at the point of submission. For this subset of students, as long as they evaluate the product (P35: "as long as it's used critically and not blindly trusted") -that is, as long as they retain a veto over the final draft -they can claim to have retained "authorship" over it:

P29: "They're useful for getting started or refining work, but they don' t replace my own thinking.

I still see my role as the one making decisions about what to include, what to argue, and what to submit."

Two additional justificatory strategies were also repeatedly used in the dataset without mapping onto the repertoires outlined above.

First, by acknowledging the negatives of genAI, the student can demonstrate their credentials as a morally aware genAI-user. Expressions of concern over the societal impacts of genAI use (Themes 2.1 and 2.2) can generate a kind of moral currency that is independent of the individual's behaviour (i.e., being a moral person is evidenced by one's concern for the environment and is therefore not contingent upon one's own genAI use). A similar function could be served by strategically generalising and particularising (Billig, 1985) genAI use to draw boundaries of legitimacy: other people who "abuse" or "over-rely" on AI were often amalgamated into a mass whose use of genAI is in some way illegitimate; the example below includes an extreme case formulation ("rely on it for everything") that further emphasises the illegitimacy:

P9: "it worries me to see how prevalent the use of AI has become and seeing people around me, even my parents, rely on it for everything. Before even giving two seconds to think about a topic themselves, they ask ChatGPT to do it for them. This concerns be because I feel we are in ways losing our ability to independently reason."

The generalisation is combined with a particularisation carving out an exception for themselves, their own use rendered benign through the contrast with the extreme case formulation (Pomeranz, 1986). P9 explicitly states that this framing helps them manage potential guilt:

P9: "I always think and brainstorm an essay topic myself first [...] This makes me feel less guilty or anxious about using it because I know I'm essentially receiving the same support I would if I were to discuss my ideas with a teacher, classmate, etc."

A second strategy was the drawing of a boundary between legitimate and illegitimate use that separates the technical aspects of writing (word choice, grammar, structure) from "thinking" as a higher cognitive activity (argumentation, evaluation, creativity). The boundary, however, tended to be unstable -"brainstorming" and "summarising", for example, were often cited as legitimate use cases for genAI, when the former involves generating ideas and the latter shapes interpretation. Both can be seen as creative acts that have the potential of subverting the claim to authorship. In other words, although some genAI users might appeal to "authorship" as a straightforward source of legitimacy, the concept itself is open to negotiation and demands justification in turn.

Discussion

Across four themes we have established a general picture of our students' relationship with generative AI tools. The first and most important thing to note is that students are highly diverse in their attitudes, goals, opinions and experiences which has also been emphasised by work like Bodei et al. (2026). It is all too common to lump students together (Devlin, 2006) but they are as varied as humanity at large. At one end of the spectrum, some refuse to use genAI at all (Farinosi & Melchior, 2025), despite all the pressures and potential benefits to their grade and workload; at the other some are fully immersed and find it hard to imagine doing academic work without one anymore (Lu et al., 2024). However, the majority are using it to varying levels of support, while having to decide for themselves where to set the limits and how to justify them (Machkour and Abriane 2025).

One notable pattern, in opposition to some mainstream narratives, is that very few individuals, in the quantitative or qualitative data, reported using the tool as a constant 'buddy', with use generally seeming to be as and when needed. This is in opposition to some work (e.g. Durgungoz & Kharrufa, 2026; Zheldibayeva, 2025), however those studies examined either students studying a second language, or studying a topic in a second language, where it's easy to imagine the need for nearcontinual use. The majority of our students reported seeing genAI as 'just a tool', and for 'support'. None reported using it just to write their essays for them (although, while the study was fully anonymous, with no demographics collected, it's still possible this is under-reported). There was a mixture of reasons for this, generally in line with the findings of Machkour and Abriane (2025). First, there was overall a strong desire among students to feel like their work was their own, and to preserve their authorial voice. Due to the 'supportive' way most students were using these tools, many didn't feel like this was compromised by using genAI, as they felt like the driving force behind the ideas was still them.

Alternately, many students found it helpful in something like a tutor / teaching assistant role (Kasneci et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2025), providing them with an easy way of testing themselves for exams and revising key ideas. This may be why many (but not all) thought it actually improved their ability to learn new concepts and skills (Zhu et al., 2025). Many also reported it increasing their motivation and enjoyment of the process (Hmoud et al., 2024), with the qualitative data hinting that genAI use helped avoid the initial block phase of academic work as well as the more monotonous work such as literature reviewing or organising reference sections (Cummings et al., 2024). There is room for critically exploring the sources of this enjoyment further: individual respondents indicated that part of it could be the result of the cognitive closure provided by the immediateness of AI-generated answers (even if they are also unreliable), or the opportunities it offers to replace interpersonal communication in learning. There are good pedagogical reasons for resisting both of these trends, as the ability to delay the gratification of closure and to collaborate with others are arguably essential academic skills (Bembenutty & Karabenick., 1998; Hassanien, 2007; Hou et al., 2025).

Students' use of genAI was tempered by a range of concerns, from its poor performance (e.g., poor argumentation and outright fabrications; Chan et al., 2023) and environmental impact (Dungo et al., 2025) to it undermining the "purpose of education" and diminishing their own ability to think critically and creatively (Cash et al., 2026; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). They reported different strategies to mitigate these concerns: from limiting its use to a (self-defined) supportive role or minimising the number of prompts, to the choice made by around 10% of our sample not to use it at all.

Despite all these concerns, many students still rely on genAI for a variety of tasks. Some described external pressures: that they would be expected to use these tools well in their jobs, that some of the skills seen as important pre-genAI were now obsolete, and that they would be disadvantaged if they didn't use them, as almost everyone else was (Machkour and Abriane 2025). Another important factor was the huge gulf in effort between doing certain tasks for themselves vs asking genAI, and the compounding effects of time pressures, such as when leaving coursework too late (Fu et al., 2026).

The lack of a clear line between 'supportive' and 'illegitimate' usage produced a ratchet effect: usage tends to creep up (Tang et al., 2025) and, once developed, the habit of using genAI is very hard to kick. Some also found it hard to submit work without 'checking' with an AI to see if it could be improved (Hou et al., 2025).

Navigating the dilemmas of genAI

Our students' responses showed them navigating dilemmas between their guilt and worry on the one side, and the huge incentive structures pushing genAI use on the other. We saw a variety of rhetorical strategies at play that allowed students to frame personal engagement with the software in a way that elides the ethical pitfalls. We see this in the two widespread but competing metaphors for genAI (or, in Serge Moscovici's (2008; 1984) terms, two social representations that anchor the new, poorly understood phenomenon of genAI into familiar images): "the tool" and "the tutor". They serve different purposes in the context in which they are deployed: the "tool" has no agency of its own, so the student can deny having surrendered authorial control to it. In contrast, the "tutor" metaphor emphasizes the legitimacy of the software in an academic context, placing it in a supportive role that aids learning. But inscribing it into the language of pedagogical relationships ("tutor") also contains a tacit admission that genAI is much more than a word processor or a "mere tool", creating a delicate rhetorical balancing act.

Many minimised the ethical implications of genAI use by relying on an ambiguous notion of "authorship" that understands coursework as a finished written product, rather than as encompassing the invisible work of reviewing literature, planning, thinking, and drafting. Having the prerogative to decide whether they are happy with the LLM's output therefore guarantees for the student that they are "thinking critically" about the coursework, which preserves their "authorship". This framing also relies on a particular notion of "critical thought" which is extraneous to academic writing. This separation is also enacted structurally in the division of labour between the genAI text predictor, which does the writing, and the student, who is relegated to the role of reading and evaluating its output.

In their responses, students appeared to be reasoning through a broader societal dilemma about the ambivalent role of higher education in a capitalist society and the degree to which it should be subordinated to preparing cadres for the workforce. As students reasoned about their own genAI use, they mobilised capitalist values (competition and efficiency) against academic ones (scholarship and integrity) as a reflection of the broader societal dilemma about the purpose of higher education: should it serve as a job training programme and satisfy the demands of the labour market, or should it be governed by the internal standards of scholarship?

Strengths and limitations

Qualitative studies in this domain have typically used face to face interviews to elicit qualitative data. There are pros and cons compared to our method of using an open text box survey. While with our method there is no ability to follow up on comments or interrogate, it does enhance anonymity and allows students to think and reflect before they respond without the pressure of a face-to-face interaction. This has produced some beautiful, insightful and thoughtful comments from our students.

Importantly our students may not be representative of other groups, in several ways. They are BSc Psychology / Language sciences students, who are 80-85% female. They are also at a high-entryrequirement university, and so our students are disproportionately high achieving, self-critical and prone to shame, anxiety, perfectionism and imposter syndrome (Benoit, 2025). A-priori, this could go either way with respect to AI use. One might imagine that the pressure to achieve high grades could make our students more transactional. However, it doesn't seem this is the case. Students' frequently express guilt and shame and this seems to be directed more at something unobservable but which they themselves know: did they really do the work, and did they learn anything? However, if some other student populations lean more towards a transactional attitude towards their degree, perhaps if they don't have the same levels of self-criticism, or if those personal standards are focused more purely on externals, such as grades, the current findings might apply less. We would need convincing that this applies to the majority of students in any degree however. Students increasingly question the market value of a degree (Tomlinson, 2018). They have to pay to do this, and if that doesn't translate into a substantially higher wage, then the value of a degree must primarily be in a desire for selfimprovement and personal pride from achievement.

Conclusion

Overall, the responses from our students belie the implicit assumption behind the crisis narrative: that if given the opportunity to cheat, the majority of students will simply take it. While a subset of students refuse to use genAI at all, the majority use it in different capacities, all the while understanding this as a contentious act that requires self-monitoring and justification. Students report valuing the originality of their work, and by extension their self-perception as ethical academics-intraining; their use of genAI poses dilemmas that each student must resolve for themselves by mobilising different moral values and different representations of the nature of genAI.

This creates an opportunity to strengthen the hand of that ethical self against the temptations of quick results and easy closure. Universities increasingly recognise we cannot stop genAI usage,or define exactly what counts as fair use (Hu, 2026). We are therefore in a position where we must largely leave this up to the student. The first author has argued in other work that we need more ethical training as part of our degree programs (Dewitt et al., 2025) to combat issues like the replication crisis, and we would argue this is the case here too. We must build their intrinsic motivation to curb their use of these tools, rather than focus primarily on extrinsic carrots and sticks.

A step in this direction could be to reconceptualise the goal of academic work. As academics, we could emphasise the process over its product (i.e., the academic essay, which genAI has divorced from the need to read, plan, or revise); we could place greater value on going down unfamiliar paths in the dense literature written on a subject, even if the student's search for a reference to support their preformed conclusion occasionally ends up frustrated.

More broadly, we could foster an alternative view of what constitutes "thinking" that goes beyond the role of an auditor signing off on the LLM's procedurally combined strings of words. Discursive psychologists have argued that critical thinking is an essentially dialogical, rhetorical process (Billig, 1996; Potter & Wetherell 1987; Hermans, 2025): that social reality is open to multiple competing and contested discursive constructions, which can serve different rhetorical purposes. Truly critical thinking therefore relies on the ability to interrogate their own discursive choices and, as such, cannot be done outside of a dialogical activity such as writing or talking, even if the dialogue is internal to oneself. To this end, students should be invited to think of academic writing and thinking as essentially the same process.

This situation is ripe for social norms-based interventions (Chung & Rimal, 2016), as students are stuck in a situation of pluralistic ignorance over each other's use, and likely over-estimate it.

Interventions which encourage long-term thinking e.g. about how the student will feel once they complete their degree, over short term e.g. a focus on just getting the next assignment in (Chang et al., 2026) are also a likely fruitful avenue. A benefit of these kinds of non-AI-specific approaches are that they also apply to other forms of cheating, which still exist among the maelstrom of generative AI concern, such as contract cheating and plagiarism.
