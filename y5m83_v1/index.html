Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Time is foundational to social life, yet Social Representations Theory has often treated it as the background against which representations change, rather than as a dimension of meaning-making in its own right. This theoretical review distinguishes processual dynamics (how representations emerge, stabilize, circulate, and transform) from temporality: how past, present, and future are socially and symbolically constituted. From here, it examines structural, dialogical, sociodynamic, and genetic approaches in order to trace how these traditions have engaged with the past through narratives, memories, and histories, and with the future through projects, expectations, and anticipations. The review shows that all four traditions contain temporal assumptions, but that the field has developed fuller accounts of how the past organizes the present than of how possible futures orient collective life. Drawing together conceptual resources already present within the theory, we propose a re-temporalization organized around four components: temporal contents, representational processes, projects, and projections. This agenda treats time as symbolically constructed, experientially lived, normatively encoded, and politically distributed. It enables research to examine how groups negotiate temporal pace, direction, reversibility, and legitimacy, and how unequal capacities to define the past and the future shape representational life. Re-temporalizing SRT therefore deepens the theory's concern with common-sense knowledge by linking continuity and transformation to the politics of memory and anticipation.

1. Introduction

In Émile Durkheim's (1915) formulation, time constitutes a paradigmatic collective representation: an abstract and impersonal framework that transcends individual consciousness and organizes collective existence. Temporal categories, in this view, are socially produced yet relatively stable, embedded in ritual and institutional life. The shift from collective to social representations proposed by Serge Moscovici (1961) fundamentally alters this picture. Representations are no longer unified and stable social facts. Even the most consensually shared -what Moscovici (1988) termed hegemonic representations-remain historically situated and open to contestation. They circulate across differentiated public spheres, rather than standing above them (Jovchelovitch, 2007). This shift has important implications for how time is understood within representational processes. If collective representations are tied to enduring structures, social representations are embedded in communicative dynamics. They are continuously reworked through media, institutional debate, and everyday interaction. In this perspective, representations are not fixed entities but processes in transformation (Marková, 2008).

Yet, while some approaches within Social Representations Theory (SRT) have developed explicit temporal sensibilities, the status of time remains unevenly theorized. This limitation is not unique to SRT. Across social and psychological theories, time is often treated implicitly as a background dimension against which social processes unfold (Martins, 1974; Nowotny, 1992). As Prosser (2025, p. 2) notes, social psychology still captures "a snapshot of human life that is in motion", leaving how time itself becomes meaningful relatively unexplored. However, a growing body of research shows that meaning-making processes concerning the past and the future are central to present social life (Michaelian & Sutton, 2017) and culturally embedded (Sircova et al., 2014; Stolarski et al., 2018). Together, these developments highlight the need to clarify how time is conceptualized and mobilized within SRT.

In this article, we address this need by distinguishing between two analytically related but non-equivalent dimensions. On the one hand, processual dynamics refer to how representations emerge, stabilize, and transform. On the other, temporality concerns how time itself is socially and symbolically constituted within the everyday sphere. While SRT has extensively developed the former, the latter often remains implicit in empirical research. As a result, time frequently appears as a background condition within which representational processes unfold, rather than as a dimension or object of representation in its own right.

Building on this distinction, we will first examine how time is addressed across different approaches to SRT (structural, genetic, sociodynamic, dialogical), asking to what extent it is treated as a background condition or explicitly addressed as a dimension or object of representation. Then, we will focus on how empirical research engages with the past (through narratives, memories, and histories) and the future (through projects, expectations, and anticipations), exploring how these engagements reveal different temporal openings within the theory. This mapping serves a twofold objective: first, to clarify how time has been conceptualized within SRT; and second, to specify what is gained by treating temporality as a constitutive dimension of meaning-making. These two movements converge in the discussion, which advances a re-temporalization of SRT by outlining future research directions grounded in a theoretical definition of temporality that is more fully integrated into the theory's own conceptual resources.

2. Revisiting Time Across Theoretical Traditions in Social Representations

The following sections examine how different traditions within SRT address time. The distinction between structural, sociodynamic, genetic, and dialogical approaches is adopted here as a heuristic framework, rather than as a strict taxonomy of authors or schools, as it reflects a consolidated way of organizing theoretical orientations within the theory (e.g., Sammut et al., 2015; Lo Monaco & Rateau, 2025).

Importantly, many scholars initially associated with one approach have progressively expanded their theoretical scope, particularly to engage more directly with issues of history, development, and temporality. We treat these expansions as evidence of SRT's internal pluralism rather than departures from it.

Accordingly, the aim is to examine how time is variously taken for granted, implicitly structured, or explicitly problematized across different approaches to SRT. In doing so, we distinguish between processual dynamics -well established within SRT-and the more limited ways in which time itself becomes an object of representation or a dimension of meaning-making.

2.1 The Structural Approach

The structural approach (Abric, 1987 (Abric, , 1994) ) embeds a distinctive orientation toward continuity, in which representational stability takes precedence over transformation. By distinguishing between a central core of historically sedimented meanings and a peripheral zone of adaptive elements (Moliner & Abric, 2015), it frames social knowledge as a system that endures across generations while selectively absorbing novelty. This view aligns with broader cognitive perspectives emphasizing the economization of resources and the pursuit of coherence and cognitive consonance (Festinger, 1957; Heider, 1946). Consistent with this orientation, social representations have been described as possessing "some stability over time… between conscious cognitions and the longue durée of collective mentalities" (Bauer, 2015, p. 79).

Within this framework, time is primarily a dimension against which continuity and change are described. Change is conceived as gradual and accumulative, proceeding through peripheral adjustments that, as far as possible, preserve the integrity of the core (Wagner et al., 1996). Although subsequent refinements have acknowledged phases of emergence, stabilization, and transformation (see for instance Wachelke, 2011 , or Moliner, 2016), these developments largely extend the analysis of processual dynamics without explicitly problematizing time; transformation remains predominantly reactive, typically triggered by contextual disruptions that pressure the system to recalibrate (Flament, 1994). Indeed, the core is defined primarily by its resistance to change. It comprises a limited set of beliefs that remain stable and around which strong group consensus is organized (Abric, 1994). Core elements simultaneously perform meaning-making, ordering, and stabilizing functions: any alteration at this level therefore implies a potential reconfiguration of the representation as a whole and, by extension, a disturbance of the social bonds it sustains (Moliner, 2016). Peripheral elements, by contrast, operate as conditional buffers. They absorb contextual variability and shield the core from direct confrontation with disconfirming experience; they are historically situated, draw on biographical experience, and remain negotiable among social groups (Flament, 1994; Wagner et al., 1996).

The peripheral system is also closely tied to behavioural schemas that orient conduct in concrete situations, thereby mediating between representational meaning and situated practice.

Representations and practices engage in an ongoing interaction, and contradictions in action become the primary trigger of representational adjustment (Quenza, 2005). Flament (1994) further argues that representational change hinges on whether new practices are seen as reversible, legitimate, and justified, leading to different degrees of core restructuring. While, these formulations introduce temporal distinctions (e.g., reversibility, anticipation), they remain anchored in the regulation of change rather than in an explicit theorization of temporality as such.

In this context, the notion of the zone muette (Abric, 2005) acquires particular significance.

Traditionally defined as those cognitions available to individuals but left unexpressed under ordinary conditions (Guimelli & Deschamps, 2000), this "silent zone" emerges because articulating such contents could challenge moral or normative values upheld within the group (Chokier & Moliner, 2006). These unspoken elements embody a form of inertia, reflecting meanings sedimented in the history of the group. At the same time, the silent zone constitutes a latent site of change: when sociosymbolic conditions shift, dormant elements may resurface, unsettling the apparent stability of the representation. Continuity thus appears as an ongoing negotiation between stabilization and transformation, in which what is silenced remains available for reactivation.

A related opening is identified in what Abric (2005) termed the zone des éléments contrastés.

Often detected in Free-Association studies, this domain includes contents mentioned by relatively few participants (low frequency) but attributed high subjective importance (low rank). These contrastive elements express divergent or minority viewpoints within the group and may signal the presence of subgroups organized around alternative value configurations. While marginal, they introduce a limited opening within the otherwise conservative architecture of the model: transformation may emerge from the periphery when minority positions accumulate symbolic weight or become more publicly legitimate.

However, recent empirical work comparing social representations across cohorts indicate that stability in central elements may coexist with variation in peripheral meanings, pointing to gradual differentiation over time (Fasanelli et al., 2020; Moreira et al., 2023). These findings introduce a diachronic perspective in which different generations may organize and prioritize meanings differently around the same object, enabling the study of change and persistence as jointly observable.

Taken together, the structural approach recognizes the relevance of context, practices, and latent zones of potential transformation, yet it ultimately conceives social representations as systems oriented toward the preservation of established meanings. Change is primarily reactive rather than anticipatory. In this sense, time is not explicitly theorized but functions as an implicit condition of continuity, within which stability is maintained and transformation is regulated. The approach therefore offers a detailed account of processual dynamics -how representations stabilize, adjust, and occasionally restructure-while leaving largely unexamined the question of temporality properly speaking: how time itself is socially and symbolically constituted within the everyday sphere.

2.2 Dialogical Approaches

The dialogical approach (Marková, 2000 (Marková, , 2003 (Marková, , 2023) ) offers one of the most explicit engagements with time within SRT. It conceptualizes representations as ongoing dialogical processes unfolding across past, present, and anticipated future positions. Representations are never formed in isolation; they emerge through exchanges with real, remembered, or imagined others (Marková, 2003; Jodelet, 2014). In this sense, processes of representing are intrinsically temporal, as they carry traces of prior discourses while orienting toward possible responses.

Dialogical subjectivity is inherently multi-positional and multi-voiced. The Self is constituted through the continuous interplay between internalized and external perspectives, including those of concrete interlocutors, social groups, institutions, and imagined audiences (Marková, 2008). Time, from this standpoint, refers to the lived organization of experience, in which past trajectories, present engagements, and future orientations coexist within a polyphony of voices. The self is thus always engaged in dialogue with an "inner alter" that reflects socially and symbolically mediated perspectives (Marková, 2003). This dialogical engagement is not only backward-looking, drawing on shared histories, or present-focused, negotiating current positions, but also anticipatory, insofar as it unfolds within relations that orient participants toward possible futures of interaction and understanding (Marková, 2023).

Imagination plays a central role within this dynamic (Marková, 2018). From a dialogical perspective, imagination is not an isolated mental faculty but is intrinsically intertwined with communication, meaning-making, and memory. In this sense, imagination emerges through the dynamic relations between Self and Others, across temporal horizons of past, present, and future. Despite this strong emphasis on temporality, dialogical approaches have been subject to internal critiques regarding their treatment of stability, power, and constraint. Consequently, the presence of multiple voices does not guarantee equal recognition or influence over future-oriented meaning-making.

Rather, dialogue may reproduce dominant representations by constraining how alternative voices are positioned and received.

Recent dialogical extensions further foreground time through the analysis of imagined futures. Pedersen (2021) shows how dialogical impasses can emerge when competing social representations project incompatible futures. In such cases, breakdowns in dialogue are not reducible to past misunderstandings but to conflicts over which futures are considered viable or legitimate. At the same time, these future orientations are unevenly distributed across social positions, as different actors have unequal capacity to define and sustain particular trajectories. This highlights how time can become a dimension of representational struggle, rather than merely a background for processual dynamics.

Taken together, the dialogical approach makes temporality more explicit within SRT by conceptualizing representations as polyphonic processes in which past, present, and future are dynamically articulated. At the same time, while dialogical approaches theorize the negotiation of meanings in considerable detail, they pay less attention to how understandings of the past and the future themselves become contested objects of representation. The distinction between dynamics and temporality remains, even here, only partially drawn.

2.3 Sociodynamic approaches

The sociodynamic approach (Doise, 1980; Doise et al., 1999; Clémence et al., 2014) conceptualizes social representations as structured by social differentiation and positioning rather than assuming uniform consensus. Its central proposition is that representational diversity emerges from the articulation of distinct but interdependent levels of analysis: intraindividual, interindividual/situational, positional, and ideological (Doise, 1980; see also Doise & Valentim, 2015, for a recent extension of the model). These levels are not sequential but mutually conditioning: meaning-making is simultaneously organized through interaction, shaped by social positions, and regulated by ideological and normative frameworks.

From the perspective adopted in this review, the sociodynamic approach does not explicitly theorize temporality; rather it offers a differentiated account of how time is implicitly structured across levels of analysis, without theorizing it as a dimension of representation in its own right.

At the intraindividual level, the focus lies on how individuals organize their perceptions and evaluations of the social environment through cognitive and motivational processes shaped by prior socialization (Doise, 1980). Time remains implicit and is primarily reflected in processes of consolidation and reorganization of cognitive structures, rather than being treated as an object of representation.

At the interindividual and situational level, processual dynamics are the central analytical concern. Doise (1980) characterizes these dynamics as spiral rather than strictly linear, suggesting that interactional tensions may intensify, transform, or reconfigure across sequences of exchange.

This perspective is consistent with the socio-psychological tradition of cognitive development developed by Doise and Palmonari (2010), where representations are produced within interactional sequences in which positions are negotiated, stabilized, or destabilized. Meanings are therefore produced within ongoing dynamics of coordination and conflict between groups; accordingly, anchoring and objectification are treated as socially positioned operations, governed by normative and ideological principles (Clémence, 2001; Clémence et al., 2014).

At the positional level, representational differences reflect structured social affiliations -such as class, profession, institutional role, or political orientation-which are historically constituted and relatively durable. Here, time is tied to the persistence and transformation of social positions across trajectories: representational patterns endure insofar as the structures that sustain them persist, and change when these structures are reconfigured (Doise, 1980).

At the ideological level, continuity is maintained through normative and value systems that regulate the interpretation of social positions and conflicts. Doise (1980) describes this level as providing metasystems that structure the dynamics occurring at other levels. These frameworks display relative continuity across episodes of contestation, while also allowing limited reconfigurations in the interpretation of social arrangements. In this sense, the ideological level introduces a limited opening toward anticipatory dimensions, as political and normative frameworks often imply projections about desirable or undesirable collective futures.

Crucially, these levels are articulated rather than independent. Transformation occurs when articulations between levels are reconfigured: for instance, when shifts in social positioning alter interactional dynamics, or when ideological principles are challenged. Time in the sociodynamic approach is therefore multilevel and conjunctural: it is structured differently across levels and emerges through their articulation..

Taken together, the sociodynamic approach offers a differentiated account of how time is structured across levels of social organization and conflict. However, it only partially opens toward temporality as an explicit object of representation. What remains untheorized is how actors across these levels represent time itself: as urgent or deferred, as progressive or cyclical, as a resource distributed unequally across social positions. This gap is not incidental; it reflects the approach's constitutive focus on the synchronic organization of difference rather than on the symbolic meanings through which groups orient themselves toward past and future.

2.4 Genetic approaches

The sociogenetic approach (Duveen & Lloyd, 1990; Duveen & de Rosa, 1992) situates representational change within the organization of time across social life. It understands social knowledge as continuously produced through the interplay between individual development, social interaction, and collective meaning making. Representations are therefore examined across three intersecting processes which articulate situated interaction, individual development, and broader historical transformation rather than forming a simple sequence of temporal scales (Psaltis, 2015a).

Studying these processes requires attention to how representations are constructed in interaction, appropriated across the life course, and circulated and transformed at the societal level.

Methodologically, this orientation has been associated with the combination of longitudinal designs, interactional analyses, and documentary, policy, or media analysis to examine change across these scales (Flick et al., 2015; Negura et al., 2021).

Microgenesis refers to the situated construction of representations in interaction. It captures the processes through which social identities and the representations that sustain them are elaborated and negotiated in real time (Duveen & Lloyd, 1990). The focus here is on the present in the making: representations are co-constructed, stabilized, or revised through discourse.

Microgenesis functions as the immediate condition of both ontogenesis and sociogenesis, insofar as broader representational fields are reproduced -or occasionally transformed-through situated encounters (Duveen & Lloyd, 1990; Psaltis, 2015a). While interaction often reproduces taken-for-granted meanings, it can also generate ruptures in which implicit assumptions become explicit and open to reworking (Psaltis, 2015b). Wagner et al. (1998), for example, showed how representations of the public sphere in Brazil emerged through everyday exchanges among citizens, politicians, and media actors, linking civic identity to competing moral evaluations of the nation.

Ontogenesis brings time to the level of the life course, tracing how individuals appropriate and transform shared meanings through experience and socialization. Ontogenetic processes describe how people grow into already existing representational fields while also potentially reshaping them (Flick et al., 2015). Biographical time thus constitutes the site where broader sociogenetic transformations become psychologically real. A classical line of research has therefore focused on developmental studies examining how social representations are constructed, stabilized, and transformed through the articulation of microgenetic interaction, ontogenetic development, and sociogenetic structures. Within this tradition, development is understood not as the internal maturation of isolated cognitive structures, but as a socially mediated process in which identities, power asymmetries, and forms of communication shape both learning and representational change (Duveen & Lloyd, 1990; Psaltis, 2015b).

Research in Cyprus illustrates this dynamic particularly clearly. Decades of ethnocentric history education embedded opposing national narratives so deeply that they structured memory, political expectations, and attitudes toward coexistence among both Greek Cypriots and Turkish

Cypriots (Makriyianni & Psaltis, 2007). Following the opening of checkpoints in 2003, cross-group contact reduced prejudice in both communities, but in ways that differed depending on participants' biographical starting points (Yucel & Psaltis, 2020). The same historical turning point was therefore taken up differently across individual life trajectories.

Finally, sociogenesis concerns how representational fields are shaped by collective, geopolitical, and historical transformations (Duveen & de Rosa, 1992; Sibley et al., 2008). It focuses on how representations circulate within societies, interact with institutional discourses, and are reconfigured across historical contexts (Flick et al., 2015; Carugati & Selleri, 2011). Sociogenesis is therefore not a distant macro layer but an ongoing historical process that becomes visible in situated meaning making (see for example Negura, 2021).

Building on Duveen's sociogenetic framework, subsequent work has explicitly tracked representational change across multiple scales, proposing "genetic social psychology" as a continuation and expansion of this legacy across developmental and cultural research (Psaltis, 2015a (Psaltis, , 2015b;; Psaltis & Wagoner, 2026; Zapiti & Psaltis, 2012). The core premise of this framework is that all objects of knowledge are pre-signified by social representations and ideologies that pre-exist individual subjects and condition their knowledge potential (Castorina, 2017). From this perspective, time is organized across interrelated scales: meanings circulate between the immediacy of social encounters, the biographical trajectories of individuals, and broader historical transformations. Over time, scholars working within the genetic tradition have increasingly incorporated dialogical themes to better account for how actors navigate present positions, emerging tensions, and possible futures (Gillespie, 2006; Gillespie & Cornish, 2010; Psaltis & Wagoner, 2026; Zittoun, 2019). These developments extend the genetic framework making more explicit the relational and prospective dimensions of representational processes, particularly in contexts marked by social conflict.

Beyond Schools of Thought: SRT through Past and Future The four approaches outlined above offer distinct ways in which SRT has engaged with time: structural continuity and reactive change, dialogical articulation of past, present, and future, sociodynamic differentiation across levels of social organization, and genetic articulation across developmental scales. However, several research strands cut across these approaches, substantially extending how time is addressed within the theory. Drawing on adjacent traditions -most notably memory studies, narrative psychology, and cultural psychology-this literature examines how the past is narratively constructed and how future orientations are anchored in collective meaning-making.

The following sections trace these developments, beginning with SRT's engagement with the past and showing how it already implies a future orientation.

3.1 The Past: Narratives, Memories, Histories

Work on the past within SRT extends and reinterprets its core mechanisms of anchoring and objectification, foregrounding how representations draw on prior meanings while being reworked in relation to present concerns. Within this field, three interrelated strands -memory, narrative, and history-offer complementary ways of examining how time is made meaningful in social knowledge.

Some perspectives emphasize continuity and endurance, while others foreground rupture, contestation, and the moral tensions involved in representing the past.

Drawing on Bartlett's (1932) social theory of memory, Moscovici (1988) noted that "very ancient beliefs lurk in the shadows" and continue to shape representational life (p. 244). Indeed, the foundational proposition that "the purpose of all representations is to make something unfamiliar, or unfamiliarity itself, familiar" (Moscovici, 2001, p. 37) resonates with Bartlett's (1932) observation that "the familiar is readily accepted; the unfamiliar may hold us" (p. 19). The backward-oriented dimension of anchoring has therefore been implicit in SRT from its inception. Moscovici (1988) further emphasized that meanings shift across generations: what appears unfamiliar in one moment may later become taken for granted. Representational processes thus negotiate continuously between novelty and inherited cultural resources.

Building on these foundations, empirical SRT studies have examined both the persistence and transformation of memory. Sammut et al. (2012) traced how historically rooted representations of national belonging structure identity negotiation among Maltese migrants. Kane (2012) showed how medieval gender representations persist even when detached from their original institutional contexts.

Geka and Dargentas (2010) demonstrated how representations of cremation evolved while remaining anchored in traditional burial practices. Extending this line of work, recent research on social representations of colonialism in Belgium shows that the content of collective memory is not only a residue of the past but an active structuring force in the present: representing colonialism in terms of exploitation is associated with more positive intergroup attitudes and support for reparations, whereas framing it in terms of development predicts more negative attitudes and higher levels of modern racism (Lastrego et al., 2023). These studies highlight the dual role of memory: as a relatively stable product of prior meaning-making and as an ongoing process through which societies reinterpret and selectively reorganize the past (Ryan & Hewer, 2016; Wertsch & Roediger, 2008).

Research on collective remembering shows that representations of the past are shaped by moral positioning. Leone and Sarrica (2012) demonstrate how narrating in-group violence involves tensions between identity protection and moral accountability, while Sarrica et al. (2016) show how family-level reminiscing about environmental change connects local experiences of loss with broader collective meanings. Remembering is thus a situated process of negotiation in which silences, omissions, and reframings shape what can be publicly acknowledged and transmitted.

Yet the past is not only recalled; it is narrated. Narrative approaches show that remembering involves structuring events into meaningful sequences. As Bruner (2011) argues, narrative constitutes a mode of thought that organizes experience through plots connecting beginnings, developments, and outcomes. Within SRT, narratives function as interpretative frameworks through which collective experiences become intelligible and socially shareable (Jovchelovitch, 2012; László, 2013). They sustain identity and continuity, but they also reveal instability, as stories are reinterpreted across generations and contested in public arenas. Cultural psychology further emphasizes how institutional and social contexts shape these narrative constructions (Barreiro, 2020; Lyra et al., 2020). Narratives may therefore embed moral justifications within historically resonant frames. For instance, Nicholson's (2017) analysis of Israeli and Palestinian narratives illustrates how competing accounts of the past continue to structure present conflict.

When narrative analysis intersects with the study of history, the structuring of time becomes especially visible. Liu and colleagues show how historical representations function as "charters" that legitimize present institutions and orient collective trajectories (Liu et al., 1999; Liu & László, 2007; Sibley et al., 2008; Hilton et al., 2017; Choi & Liu, 2024; Liu & Khan, 2021). Individuals organize personal and national histories through patterns of progress, stability, or decline, which become particularly salient in moments of disruption (Yamashiro et al., 2023). Such continuity supports what Sani et al. (2007) term perceived temporal continuity: the sense that collective identity is anchored in a shared trajectory that helps groups manage change (Jetten & Hutchison, 2011). Historical representations thus integrate memory and narrative processes, functioning as resources for identity, legitimacy, and moral positioning (see also : Gries et al., 2009; Tileagă, 2009). Power relations are intrinsic to these processes: dominant narratives achieve relative stability when alternative accounts are marginalized or silenced (Brown, 2006; Leone & Sarrica, 2017; Ryan & Hewer, 2016).

Spatial mediations extend the representational work of memory. Monuments, memorials, and commemorative sites materialize historical narratives in public space, stabilizing dominant accounts or inviting critical reflection on past destruction and future responsibility (Brescó de Luna et al., 2023; Zittoun, 2004). The Berlin Wall, for example, functions as a symbol of past division: it simultaneously serves as a reference point for present and future political positioning (Villas Bôas, 2021).

Recent developments within and around SRT further emphasize remembering as an ongoing activity oriented toward action. Wagoner (2018) conceptualizes memory as a reconstructive process through which actors reinterpret the past in light of present concerns. Similarly, de Saint-Laurent and Obradović (2018) propose shifting attention from collective memory as content to social thinking about the collective past as an interactional process. Representations of history are thus continuously negotiated and reworked across changing social positions.

Across these contributions, the past emerges not as a fixed repository but as a dynamic resource mobilized in relation to present concerns. Remembering stabilizes identity and continuity, but it also opens space for reinterpretation and contestation. In this sense, engagement with the past already implies a future orientation: if representations draw on prior meanings to make sense of the present, they also contribute to shaping how possible trajectories are imagined and negotiated.

3.2 The Future: Projects, Expectations, Anticipations

If memory and history ground SRT in continuity, research on the future foregrounds its projective and imaginative dimensions. Rather than treating anticipation as a derivative of remembering, this body of work shows that future orientations are central to representational life.

SRT is particularly useful here because it treats anticipations not as individual attitudes, but as socially shared meanings anchored in collective practices and identities. Making this contribution explicit requires, however, a sustained distinction between processual dynamics -how representations change over time-and temporality as a lived, symbolically constituted horizon of expectation.

The theoretical foundations of this distinction can be located within SRT itself. Bauer and Gaskell's (1999) Toblerone model embeds anticipation as a temporal axis, connecting memory and project within a single representational structure. Representations extend backward toward shared pasts and forward toward envisioned outcomes, rendering time constitutive of sense-making rather than merely its background. Their subsequent Wind Rose model ( 2008) further demonstrates how multiple groups may sustain competing projects toward the same social object -technological innovation, for instance-each projecting distinct and potentially incompatible futures. Through this pragmatic lens, time becomes "substantivized" (Martins, 1974): representations are teleological, oriented toward action, justification, and coordination (Sammut et al., 2012). Wallace and Batel This social anchoring of anticipation is further evident in research on joint projects and intergroup relations: Buhagiar and Sammut (2023) show that Arab and Maltese communities in Malta hold systematically different -yet partially overlapping-representations of integration as a shared project, and that reconciliation depends precisely on identifying those overlaps. Future-oriented imagination is thus structured by the semiotic resources available to each group, making it both a site of conflict and a potential locus of convergence.

Cultural psychology approaches complement this view by conceptualizing social representations as symbolic resources through which individuals and groups navigate uncertainty.

Valsiner (1999, 2001, 2003) situates representations alongside language, narratives, images, rituals, and artifacts as semiotic tools that enable actors to project alternatives beyond the immediate present.

Building on this perspective, Zittoun and Gillespie (2018) conceptualize imagination as a looping process in which individuals temporarily decouple from the present, mobilize symbolic resources to explore distal spheres of experience, and return transformed -a mechanism that is both individually enacted and socially scaffolded. Empirical illustrations of this process show how symbolic resources are mobilized across contexts: Gillespie (2006) demonstrates how tourists construct idealized selves through imaginative engagement with culturally available meanings, while Zittoun (2019) shows how shared narratives enable communities to project possible futures for their landscapes.

Within this broader framework, Brescó de Luna (2018) introduces the notion of prolepsis to account for a more explicitly temporal mechanism: imagined futures are brought into the present by shaping how the past is narratively reconstructed, thereby orienting collective action. Relatedly, Jovchelovitch and Hawlina (2018) show that utopian thinking functions as a collective semiotic guidance system capable of mobilizing transformative agency, while also warning that fixed blueprints risk monological closure. From an SRT perspective, this suggests that the critical issue is not only how representations change, but how temporal horizons remain open, contested, and plural.

Across applied domains, these dynamics take on normative and political dimensions. In environmental contexts, temporal framing shapes moral engagement: representing distant risks as far away delays collective responsibility (Lee & Barnett, 2022; Uzelgun & Castro, 2014; Fischer et al., 2018). In education and professional trajectories, anticipated selves structure learning, motivation, and career planning (Soidet et al., 2024; Cohen Scali, 2003; Cohen Scali et al., 2022; Vera Gajardo et al., 2018; Falgares et al., 2017). In health and body image research, future self-representations reveal how normative ideals are internalized, contested, or reworked (Aim et al., 2020; Brigido et al., 2011; Silva & Cohen, 2025). What unifies these domains is the insight that future imaginaries are not politically neutral: they distribute responsibility, agency, and possibility unevenly across social positions. Indeed, in colonial and environmental contexts, competing visions of progress reveal whose futures are prioritized and whose are marginalized (Cabecinhas & Brasil, 2019; Normann, 2021; Negura et al., 2020; Castro, 2014).

Despite these advances, research on the future within SRT remains more fragmented than the study of the past. Anticipation is still frequently treated as an adjunct to anchoring or as an applied theme rather than as a representational process in its own right.

A more fully temporalized SRT would instead theorize anticipation as projectual temporality: a generative and contested capacity through which communities imagine, negotiate, and legitimize what could or should be. In this sense, temporality should be understood not only as dialogical, but also as teleological and pragmatic, binding social representations to collective agency -and SRT as a framework uniquely equipped to explore it. Additionally, narrative processes become particularly relevant here. While representations of the past and present can draw on relatively stabilized reference points that carry argumentative force, future-oriented representations lack such empirical grounding and must be rendered intelligible through symbolic and narrative forms (Brescó de Luna, 2018; Jovchelovitch & Hawlina, 2018). Anticipation therefore relies on culturally available narrative resources that organize what can be imagined, justified, and coordinated within a given representational field. Narratives do not simply describe possible futures; they structure them by linking imagined endpoints with meaningful trajectories, thereby enabling actors to orient themselves toward what is not yet realized. In this sense, the capacity of a future-oriented representation to circulate and gain legitimacy depends not only on its normative appeal, but also on its narrative coherence and cultural resonance (Pedersen, 2021; Wallace & Batel, 2024).

Accordingly, more coherent and symbolically familiar narratives are more easily shared, negotiated, and stabilized across social positions, shaping the competition between alternative representations of what the future could or should be.

4. Discussion: Re-temporalizing Social Representations

Questions of dynamics have always been central to Social Representations Theory, which has long attended to how representations emerge, stabilize, circulate, and transform. From Moscovici's foundational concepts of anchoring and objectification (1961, 1988) to Marková's dialogical reconceptualization of meaning-making (2003), the theory has developed a rich vocabulary for analysing processes of change and continuity. Yet, dynamics should not be conflated with temporality: where the former describes movement and stability within time, the latter concerns how time itself is socially and symbolically constituted within the everyday sphere.

Across its diverse schools and empirical traditions, the theory embeds distinct conceptions of time, often implicitly. Together, these perspectives illuminate how societies bind knowledge to time, yet it is primarily the genetic and dialogical traditions of SRT that treat temporality as an explicit concern. As the review in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 demonstrates, research remains considerably more robust in explaining how the past structures the present than in accounting for how alternative futures orient collective life. The future, when it appears, is often treated as a derivative of memory or as an implicit extension of anchoring processes, reflecting what Hartog (2003) identified as the presentist bias of modern social thought. This imbalance is mirrored empirically: while memory, narrative, and history constitute consolidated research fields within SRT, future-oriented studies remain fragmented across applied domains such as education, climate change, or health. Underlying this asymmetry is a deeper epistemic hierarchy, in which the known is systematically privileged over the possible.

Notwithstanding this tendency, several strands within SRT already provide the conceptual resources for a more fully temporalized theory. Contributions such as Cinnirella's (1998) work on possible social identities, Bauer and Gaskell's (1999, 2008) projectual models, Buhagiar and Sammut's (2020) analysis of joint projects, or Wallace and Batel's (2024) regimes of future orientation demonstrate that SRT can theorise anticipation, projection, and purposive orientation. Flament's (1994) distinction between reversible and irreversible change further suggests that actors' representations of an object are shaped not only by its current state but by their projections of whether transformation can be undone.

Yet these contributions remain dispersed, functioning more as thematic incursions than as components of an integrated research programme on temporality.

To integrate them coherently, SRT must move beyond treating time as a passive backdrop and instead conceptualize temporality as constitutive of how representations are formed, contested, and transformed. A conception of temporality grounded in the theory's own conceptual resources would therefore include how time is symbolically constructed (symbolic dimension), lived and experienced (experiential dimension), encoded through moral obligations and responsibilities (normative dimension), and distributed unequally across social positions (political dimension). What SRT can contribute to the study of these dimensions is a framework linking temporality to the social production of common sense: that is, to how societies collectively construct, negotiate, stabilize, and contest representations of the past, present, and future through communication, conflict, and everyday meaning-making.

Building on the present review, we argue that developing this linkage within SRT requires attention to four core components through which temporality becomes empirically tractable: (1) contents, namely the representations through which groups define past, present, and future; (2) representational processes, through which temporal contents (and time itself) are anchored, objectified, and contested; (3) projects, understood as the collective orientation through which social groups assign direction, purpose, and value to represented objects; and (4) projections, referring to the imaginative extension of representations into possible futures or their retrospective reconfiguration in light of anticipated ends.

Temporal contents are present in SRT but remains underdeveloped. As Ramos (1994) demonstrated in his lexicometric analysis, temporal categories are not arbitrary but structured lexically and semantically within shared vocabularies. As a working definition, representations of time may be understood as the systems of values, ideas, and practices (Moscovici, 1988) through which social groups make sense of past, present, and future. These contents are stabilised in language (e.g. "running out of time," "timeless tradition"), embedded in everyday metaphors ("time is money," "time flies"), and enacted through social practices such as scheduling, sequencing, and ritual timing.

In this sense, temporal contents function as symbolic resources through which groups navigate uncertainty, sustain collective identities, and orient themselves toward past and future.

Crucially, however, groups do not merely represent objects in time; they also represent time itself: its pace, direction, and perceived fairness. Temporal categories such as urgency, delay, progress, or irreversibility encode moral judgements and political claims. Discourses of urgency in climate politics, for instance, frame responsibility and justify intervention, while bureaucratic regimes of waiting transform duration into a mode of subjection. Affective orientations such as hope, stagnation, or inevitability operate as chronotopic configurations (Marková & Novaes, 2020), aligning collective emotions with particular temporal orders. Temporality is thus inherently normative and political, shaping who may act, when, and with what legitimacy.

These first two facets of temporality thus contribute to the project dimension, defined as the collective and pragmatic orientation assigned to objects of representations..

Finally, we suggest adding a fourth aspect -projection-to capture the narrative and imaginative extension of representations through which groups imagine possible futures, and therefore alternative visions of past and present. Narratives are inherently teleological, endowing past events with meaning in light of anticipated futures (Bruner, 2011; Ricoeur, 1984). Imagined futures may retroactively reshape the past, reconfiguring what is remembered, forgotten, or silenced (Brescò de Luna, 2018).

In line with SRT scholarship on absences and silences (Barreiro & Castorina, 2016; Chokier & Moliner, 2006; Guimelli & Deschamps, 2000), attention to imagination can illuminate the moral and affective shifts that occur as societies move between inherited pasts and envisaged futures. For example, research on memory and history already shows that counter-histories and reparative narratives emerge when new futures demand new pasts (Leone & Sarrica, 2017; Liu & Hilton, 2005; Lienen & Cohrs, 2021; Tileagă, 2009).

Each strand of SRT contains the seeds for this temporal reorientation. Structural approaches provide a robust framework for understanding continuity but tend to treat change as reactive.

Extending them temporally entails focusing on representational tempo: the pace at which meanings migrate from periphery to core. Peripheral elements may function as temporal incubators for alternative futures, circulating at the margins before gaining centrality. Similarly, zones of challenge may reveal minority memories and emergent projects.

Sociodynamic approaches are particularly well suited to analysing the politics of anticipation.

Social groups possess unequal temporal agency: the power to define desirable futures, to set the pace of change, or to defer transformation. This can be conceptualised as a form of modal power: the capacity to shape what others perceive as possible, plausible, or inevitable. A re-temporalized sociodynamic approach could thus examine conflicts over tempo (acceleration versus delay), trajectory (growth versus degrowth), and closure (open versus foreclosed futures), revealing how status and power structure participation in imagining collective futures.

Genetic approaches already conceptualise temporality as developmental. Extending them toward projection would allow analysis of how anticipated futures interact across scales: how shortterm discursive innovations may, through repetition and institutionalisation, reshape personal meaning systems and eventually sediment into collective representations. At the individual level, research on time perspective (Fieulaine & Apostolidis, 2015; Lewin, 1943; Stolarski et al., 2018) offers a compatible vocabulary for examining how shared temporal orientations anchor and reproduce collective representations, providing a bridge between micro-and macro-temporal dynamics.

Finally, dialogical approaches situate temporality within ongoing interaction but often underplay hierarchies of voice and resonance. Integrating dialogical analysis with a sensitivity to power enables questions such as: who speaks for the past, and who claims authority over the future?

In public debates on sustainability, some actors invoke ancestors, others appeal to "future generations" positioning themselves within moral chronologies of debt, inheritance, and responsibility. Combining dialogical SRT with discourse and deliberative analysis (Hajer, 2006; Wodak, 2016) could reveal how this temporal polyphony is stratified, that is, how some futures gain legitimacy while others remain inaudible. Cultural domains such as fiction, cinema, and online subcultures are particularly fertile sites for such analysis, functioning as representational incubators where possible futures are affectively rehearsed before they materialise (Zittoun & Gillespie, 2018).

By tracing how temporalities are anchored, objectified, and projected through narratives, practices, and artefacts -and by interrogating how power, memory, imagination, and identity condition which futures become thinkable, which pasts authoritative, and which presents appear "out of time"-SRT can move beyond its presentist bias and develop a genuinely substantive theory of temporality.

Such a re-temporalisation would remain faithful to SRT's core concern with meaning-making while recognising that meaning itself is always made in and through time.

5. Conclusion

Taken together, the review demonstrates that SRT is already, at its core, a theory deeply concerned with time, albeit one that has rarely made its temporal commitments explicit or distinguished them from the study of processual dynamics.

We mapped the temporal logics embedded within SRT, showing how each of its main approaches contains an implicit opening toward time. Across its diverse developments, a persistent tendency remains: to treat time as a neutral container rather than a constitutive dimension of representational processes. While the field abounds with studies that touch on temporal aspects, and while each major approach offers its own vocabulary for doing so, representations are examined primarily for how they preserve continuity with the past rather than how they open pathways to the future.

Yet, across these configurations, a gap persists within SRT between research on how representations emerge, stabilize, and transform and research on how time itself is socially and symbolically constituted within the everyday sphere. Much of SRT's intellectual architecture is oriented toward the former. While important developments -including the pragmatic turn initiated by Bauer and Gaskell (1999, 2008), as well as more recent work on imagination, anticipation, and creativity-have begun to address this limitation, the temporal dimensions of representational life remain underdeveloped. By recognizing temporality as a constitutive dimension of meaning-making, SRT can move beyond the past-present dyad and engage more fully with the politics of anticipation.

This reorientation also demands attention to the symbolic meanings of time itself: its tempo, directionality, reversibility, and moral legitimacy. Temporal categories such as speed, delay, progress and decline are not neutral descriptors but moral and political signifiers that regulate what kinds of futures are imaginable and for whom. The "urgency" of energy transition (Uzelgun & Castro, 2014) or the "waiting" imposed on migrants (Näre et al., 2022) illustrate how temporality becomes a medium of governance and inequality. In this sense, representations do not merely evolve in time; they actively construct temporal regimes and literacies through which social meanings are experienced, negotiated, and contested.

Building on these insights, we claim for a temporalized approach to social representations; one that treats time not as backdrop but as symbolically mediated, politically charged, and affectively experienced. Crucially, this agenda requires developing analyses of contents, processual dynamics, projects and projection.

We are not the first to advance a temporally attentive social psychology. Recent work within and adjacent to SRT has increasingly foregrounded anticipation, imagination, and creativity as constitutive dimensions of meaning-making (Glăveanu & Tanggaard, 2014; Power et al., 2023; see also the recently published special issue edited by Prosser et al., 2025). For instance, building on the dialogical approach, Glăveanu and de Saint Laurent (2025) have proposed a future-oriented framework for studying horizons of possibility as embedded in social interaction and mediated by symbolic resources. Such contributions demonstrate that SRT already possesses the conceptual apparatus to study temporality both as a content and as a generative and pragmatic process, even if these insights have yet to be fully integrated into our collective understanding of what lies within -and at the margins of-a social representations perspective.

Ultimately, our call to substantiate time within SRT is not a departure from its foundational concerns but a deepening of them. Temporality must be theorized as plural, situated, and imaginative -that is, capable of linking memory and anticipation, sedimentation and rupture, continuity and transformation. This plural temporality is not linear but ecological: a living network of durations through which groups orient themselves in symbolic space. To study this ecology is to engage with the moral architectures of collective life: who is allowed to move fast, who must wait, who claims the past, and who can speak for the future.
