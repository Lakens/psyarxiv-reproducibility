How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Within the second language (L2) acquisition field, there is a large divide between generative/formal approaches and variationist approaches. In this study, we attempt to unite these approaches by implementing a sociosyntactic approach which adopts aspects of both formal and variationist research. Specifically, we examine the acquisition of verbal agreement with collective nouns by French and German learners of L2 English. On the formal side, we investigate how universal restrictions on Agree, i.e., c-command, constrain the availability of plural agreement. On the variationist side, we explore how differences in exposure to American vs. British English affect the acceptance of plural agreement. We find that plural agreement is largely rejected by both L2 groups. Still, the German learners display sensitivity to the universal constraints on Agree. While the French learners do not, their behavior also did not violate these constraints. Furthermore, while we find an effect of dialect exposure, it was the opposite of what was predicted: those learners exposed to more American English rated plural agreement higher. We ultimately argue that future research should continue to adopt the sociosyntactic approach to gain a fuller understanding of the L2 acquisition process.

Introduction

Within the field of second language (L2) acquisition, there are two broad research paradigms: (1) generative/formal approaches (Slabakova, 2026; White, 2020; Rothman and Slabakova, 2018; and citations therein) and (2) variationist approaches (Gudmestad and Kanwit, 2025; Bayley and Escalante, 2022; and citations therein). While both approaches seek to address questions central to L2 learners' linguistic competence, there is little (joint) work between the two traditions, as they are often viewed as complementary, and sometimes even in opposition. This division is due to the questions that each addresses. The generative approach to L2 acquisition has largely investigated the role of innate language-specific mechanisms in additional language acquisition. Still, within this field there is a growing body of work that addresses the role of extra-linguistic factors, such as frequency, in interaction with these linguistic mechanisms (e.g., Smeets, 2024; Slabakova, 2015; Gonzalez, 2023; Rankin and Wagner, 2025; and many others). Variationist approaches, on the other hand, largely address contexts of language use, particularly in relation to sociolinguistic variables, such as age, gender, and region (e.g., Geeslin and Gudmestad, 2010, 2016; Regan and Terry, 2025). However, this subfield has generally not addressed the role of a language faculty in additional language acquisition. A similar divide is found in the general linguistics literature. However, in theoretical linguistics work, there have been significant attempts to unite the two approaches, with theoretical discussion devoted to questions of syntactic variation (e.g. Cornips and Corrigan, 2005; Adger and Trousdale, 2007; Duncan and Robinson, 2025). This has given rise to the field of sociosyntax, which is situated at the intersection of generative syntax and variationist sociolinguistics. That is, this framework seeks to address both what the set of possible utterances are and the restrictions on them, in alignment with the generative tradition, as well as the factors that influence the use and frequency of occurrence of those utterances, in alignment with the variationist tradition.

In this study, we apply the sociosyntactic framework to L2 acquisition. Specifically, we examine how a universal syntactic mechanism, Agree, constrains the occurrence of variability between semantic and syntactic verbal agreement with collective nouns in L2 English. In conjunction with this more generative/formal investigation, we explore how differences in the frequency of this variation in agreement across dialects, as well as differences in exposure to those dialects by L2 learners, leads to differences in the acceptance of the variant.

Literature Review L2 Theoretical Background

We characterize research on variability in L2 acquisition in generative and variationist SLA as reflecting differences in focus and emphasis, rather than necessarily a fundamental divide. Applying sociosyntactic thinking, we incorporate elements of each into consideration of the acquisition of the linguistic variable of agreement with collective nouns in English.

Generative SLA has a long tradition of research on variability, even if it has typically adopted different terminology. The focus has been on syntactic 'optionality' and research has investigated the extent to which operations which give rise to optionality are subject to similar constraints in L1 and L2 speakers, and the extent to which L1 influence is at play in the occurrence of this optionality (e.g., Unsworth, 2004; Hopp, 2005; etc.). A central focus has been word order and the interfaces of syntax with semantics and pragmatics, with an influential proposal being that optionality at the end-state of very advanced L2 acquisition is the result of the interface of syntax with pragmatics (e.g., Sorace, 2011). For example, pronominal subjects in Italian may be null or post-verbal, in addition to overt pre-verbal expression. The felicity of use of null/post-verbal subjects is regulated by discourse-pragmatic factors such as topic and focus. Italian contrasts with English, which does not syntactically license null or post-verbal subjects, where the distinction between the two languages is traditionally viewed as opposite settings of a null-subject parameter. Studies have shown that even advanced L1 English-L2 Italian speakers evidence optionality in their production and judgement of null/post-verbal subjects in Italian (e.g., Belletti et al., 2007). Nevertheless, Sorace (2011) argues that this optionality is not a syntactic issue. Rather, the learners have acquired the grammar of null subjects; however, knowing when to felicitously use them in context remains a persistent challenge.

Extra-linguistic issues regarding exposure, frequency, and input, in interaction with linguistic constraints, are seen as central to explaining these learning outcomes. To illustrate, Slabakova (2015) tests focus fronting, topicalization and clitic left dislocation (CLLD) in L2 Spanish and L2 English. While these interface properties are all regulated by discourseappropriateness, the key difference between them is construction frequency interacting with transfer: focus fronting is available in both languages, CLLD is restricted to Spanish but is frequent in speech across all varieties, and topicalization is available in English but exceedingly rare. While learners of Spanish show robust knowledge of CLLD, learners of English even at near-native levels do not show knowledge of topicalization. The learnability issue is not simply integrating information at the syntax-pragmatics interface, but rather the interplay of input frequency, L1 transfer (and UG).

Variationist SLA, on the other hand, puts extra-linguistic variables center-stage and investigates L2 sociolinguistic development in terms of probabilistic, socially meaningful patterns of use (see Geeslin, 2018; Regan and Terry, 2025). The focus is on language production, unlike generative SLA's aim to abstract away from performance. However, in line with the variationist tradition, variable usage is not assumed to be a poor reflection of underlying competence, but rather indicative of the "orderly heterogeneity" of language use in context (Weinreich et al., 1968). Studies of L2 variation show how this orderly heterogeneity plays out in learners' performance. Pertinent illustration comes from studies of subject expression in L2 Spanish for which the acquisition of null subjects is also investigated (e.g., Geeslin and Gudmestad, 2010, 2016). This variationist work surveys the full range of ways in which native and non-native speakers of Spanish can express subjects according to linguistic constraints such as person, number, discourse status, and so on. This variable production is then also analyzed against a range of sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, time spent abroad, and identity. This examination delivers sociolinguistic findings such as the fact that subject-expression among native speakers of Spanish is affected by speaker gender, but not among L2 speakers (Geeslin and Gudmestad, 2010). Additionally, from a linguistic perspective, L1 and L2 speakers produce similar rates of null and overt pronouns in first person contexts, but their rates differ significantly for second person pronouns, where L1 speakers use more personal pronouns than L2 learners (Geeslin and Gudmestad, 2016).

Despite differences in underlying theoretical assumptions and methodological choices, we can identify a certain overlap in concerns of generative and variationist SLA, especially as regards questions of linguistic exposure and experience in the development of an L2. In our study, we take a sociosyntactic perspective by focusing on formal learnability considerations, considering issues of sociolinguistic variation in the input available to learners. We treat verbal agreement with collective nouns in English as a linguistic variable, i.e. "alternative ways of 'saying the same thing'" (Labov, 1972: 94). As will be discussed in the following section, this variation is constrained by linguistic and extralinguistic factors. The resulting picture forms a learnability issue for learners of English whose L1s have different superficial agreement patterns.

Considering the (socio)linguistic variability in the input, we investigate learner preferences for semantic vs. syntactic agreement patterns, as conditioned by dialect exposure.

Agreement with Collective Nouns English

Collective nouns in English display variability in verbal agreement, allowing the occurrence of both singular and plural morphology (1; Quirk et al., 1985; Levin, 2001; Huddleston and Pullum, 2002; Hristov, 2013; among many others). This is in opposition to other types of nouns, including general singular (2) and plural (3) nouns, which do not display such variability. This variability in agreement morphology with collective nouns is linked to the choice between semantic and syntactic agreement. Semantically, collective nouns are plural because they refer to groups of individuals or objects. Syntactically, however, collective nouns are singular. Therefore, when semantic agreement occurs, plural morphology appears, but when syntactic agreement occurs, singular morphology surfaces. Other nouns do not have a mismatch between semantics and syntax, so variability never arises.

(1) The committee {was / were} considering her fate.

(2) A man {was / *were} considering her fate.

(3) The members of the committee {*was / were} considering her fate.

While agreement variability with collective nouns is observed across the major English dialects (Levin, 2001 (Levin, , 2006;; Bauer, 1988; Corbett, 2000), there is variation in the occurrence of semantic agreement between these dialects. For example, Levin (2001) observes that plural morphology is produced much more frequently in British English than in American English. Levin finds that plural morphology is produced about 23 percent of the time in British English, but it is only produced in about 3 percent of cases in American English. However, there were additional effects of modality (written vs. spoken) and register (formal vs. informal). Furthermore, even among the collective nouns there is variation in the frequency of production of plural morphology. Levin, for example, finds that nouns referring to decision-making bodies (e.g., committee) tend to be used more with singular morphology, and more quantificational nouns tend to be used more with plural morphology (e.g., majority). Still, there are collective nouns that were more clearly variable which did not belong to either type (e.g., family). In addition to these factors, the use of singular and plural morphology with collective nouns is likely affected by other sociolinguistic variables as well.

There are, however, some syntactic restrictions on this variability. Specifically, while both semantic and syntactic agreement may occur when collective nouns are pre-verbal (1), when collective nouns occur in existential constructions, plural agreement becomes impossible (4; Elbourne, 1999; Munn, 1999; Smith, 2017; den Dikken, 2026). This restriction on plural agreement cannot be due to a general restriction on plural morphology in existential constructions, or agreement with expletive there, because English does display plural agreement with syntactically plural nouns in existential clauses (5). 1 This contrast means that the observed restriction is specifically for semantic agreement, leaving only syntactic agreement, and therefore singular morphology, available for collective nouns.

Several analyses have been proposed to account for the observations with collective nouns above, as well as other structural and interpretive differences between syntactic and semantic agreement with collective nouns (den Dikken, 2026; Smith, 2017; and references therein). Here we will follow Smith's (2017) analysis, though the choice of analysis does not affect the motivation or interpretation of the results in this paper. Under this analysis, collective nouns carry two features: an uninterpretable singular feature and an interpretable plural feature. For evidence that nouns may carry non-matching features for the same construct, comparisons are drawn with pluralia tantum nouns (e.g., scissors), which are argued to carry an uninterpretable plural feature and an interpretable singular feature, and cases of gender mismatch, as with German Mädchen ('girl'), which carries an uninterpretable neuter feature and an interpretable feminine feature.

Importantly, Smith shows that Agree can target either the interpretable or the uninterpretable feature, though there are some restrictions on this optionality. Semantic agreement occurs when the interpretable feature is accessed, and syntactic agreement when the uninterpretable feature is accessed. Leaving aside some of the details of how this achieved, it is argued that "[semantic agreement] requires the controller to c-command the target at LF, but [syntactic agreement] does not" (Smith, 2017: 827). Thus, while syntactic agreement can look up or down in the structure for a feature to target, semantic agreement can only look upwards. As collective nouns c-command the verb when they are pre-verbal subjects, both types of agreement can occur.

The lack of semantic agreement in existential sentences (4) falls out from this analysis. If one adopts an analysis of there-existentials in which the subject stays low throughout the derivation and there is either base-generated in TP or moves there after merging lower in the structure (Bošković, 1997; Witkós, 2004; Bjorkman and Zeijlstra, 2014; den Dikken, 1995; cf. Chomsky, 1995; Lasnik, 1995; Bobalijk, 2002), the subject never c-commands the verb (either on the surface or at LF), so semantic agreement is impossible. However, in English, semantic agreement with V2 constructions in which the subject is post-verbal (6-7) are possible because the subject c-commands the verb at LF due to reconstruction (see Bayer, 2010; Bayer and Freitag, 2020; Sportiche, 2006), though it does not in the surface word order.

Still, there is both cross-linguistic and language-internal variation in the targets that can display semantic agreement. Corbett (1978) captures this variation with the agreement hierarchy in (8). Under this hierarchy, if an agreement target further to the left allows semantic agreement in a given language, then targets further to the right will as well.

That is, one needs to determine how some languages license semantic agreement with predicates, but others do not, even if they allow it with other targets. While Smith (2017) acknowledges that languages vary in this way, he does not provide a mechanism to account for this difference. We also do not attempt to produce such a mechanism and simply draw attention to this variation.

L2 Acquisition of Semantic Agreement with English Collective Nouns

While a few studies have examined the use and/or acceptance of semantic agreement with collective nouns by L2 English learners (e.g., Dziemianko, 2008; Rickman, 2018), these studies only address whether semantic agreement is possible in the interlanguage grammar, not the syntactic conditions on its use or dialect exposure effects. Turning to the current study, L1 French and German learners of English are expected to display different acquisition paths. At the initial state, due to (full) transfer (Schwartz and Sprouse, 1996), the L1 French learners are expected to accept semantic agreement with (at least some) collective nouns, but the L1 German learners are expected to reject this agreement. However, both groups should behave the same regarding the rejection of semantic agreement in existential clauses, due to universal restrictions on Agree, despite the lack of evidence from L1 influence and the lack of negative evidence in the input regarding the impossibility of semantic agreement in existential clauses (i.e., encountering syntactic agreement does not rule out the possibility of semantic agreement, and this specific property is not taught), assuming full access to domain-specific linguistic mechanisms (Schwartz and Sprouse, 1996). Additionally, both groups' acceptance of semantic agreement with collective nouns will likely be affected by their sociolinguistic experience. Differing rates of exposure to specific English dialects will provide different amounts of positive evidence for grammar restructuring. For the L1 French learners, this adjustment would consist of lowering acceptance for those exposed to more American English or increasing acceptance for those exposed to more British English. For the L1 German learners, exposure to both dialects should prompt increased acceptance of semantic agreement, though this adjustment will likely be more readily facilitated for those exposed to British English more.

Method

In this study we address the acquisition of semantic agreement with collective nouns by L1 French and German learners of L2 English. We address the following research questions: 1.

Do L2 English learners accept semantic agreement with collective nouns? We expect a proficiency effect. As proficiency increases, the L1 German learners will begin to accept semantic agreement, but the L1 French learners should accept it from the initial state. Because of the higher frequency of semantic agreement with collective nouns in British English, those with greater (relative) exposure to British English will begin to accept semantic agreement earlier than those with greater exposure to American English.

2. Are L2 English learners sensitive to the syntactic restrictions on semantic agreement? As the syntactic restriction is a universal constraint on Agree, we predict learners will be sensitive to this restriction from the earliest stages of acquisition, even though their L1s do not provide evidence for its application in the tested context. It is important to test L1 English speakers' sensitivity to this restriction as well to create a baseline to compare the L2 learners' behavior with because the application of this restriction has not been empirically tested previously.

3. Does learner knowledge of agreement align with their sociolinguistic experience in the rates of exposure to different varieties? We predict that with increasing proficiency, learners will converge on dialect-specific acceptance patterns. As English learners are often exposed to both American and British dialects, the effect will depend on the dialect they are primarily exposed to; that is, the relative proportion of exposure should influence the speed of convergence. For example, those exposed to a higher proportion of British English will accept semantic agreement more than those exposed to a higher proportion of American English, and this should begin at lower proficiency levels. Again, it is important to test L1 English speakers' judgements of semantic agreement as well to determine whether they align with dialectal differences in production frequency.

Materials

We conducted an acceptability judgement task (AJT) testing acceptance of verbal agreement with collective nouns in two conditions: structure (SV, VS_wh, VS_there) and agreement (syntactic, semantic). Here, SV refers to sentences in which the collective noun is pre-verbal. In VS_wh items, the collective noun is post-verbal in wh-questions, but still located in the TP, where it c-commands the verb at LF. In VS_there items, the collective noun is post-verbal due to being part of an existential clause. These conditions were fully crossed. Responses were rated on a scale of 1-4, where 4 indicated 'Completely Acceptable' and 1 'Completely Unacceptable'. Labels were always presented with the response scale. An example test item is provided in Figure 1. We selected 10 collective nouns for use in test items. These words were chosen with four goals: variation in the amount of semantic agreement, a distinction between American and British English in the percentage of semantic agreement, likelihood of familiarity by L2 learners, and sufficient frequency in the input. Measures of variation in the amount of semantic agreement were collected from Levin's (2001 Levin's ( , 2006) ) searches of The Independent and The New York Times. Metrics from written sources were chosen because our task was only presented in written form. We determined likelihood of familiarity based on the English Vocabulary Profile, which provides evidence for which proficiency level specific words begin to be used by learners productively, and we set our cut-off at B2 (Common European Framework of Reference for Languages). Finally, frequency of the collective nouns was collected from the SUBTLEX-US corpus (Brysbaert and New, 2009), which was created using subtitles from American television shows and movies. This corpus was chosen because spoken word frequency has been shown to be a better predictor of word recognition accuracy than written frequency (Brysbaert and New, 2009). Discussion

In this study we examined the acquisition of variability in syntactic and semantic agreement with collective nouns in English by L1 French and German learners. In doing so, we addressed the role of syntactic environment (i.e., licit vs. illicit) in the acceptance of the two types of agreement. Additionally, we examined the effect of differences in the relative frequency of semantic agreement compared to syntactic agreement in American and British English, in combination with learners' exposure to those dialects, in the acceptance of semantic agreement. In analyzing the effects of both formal (i.e., generative) and sociolinguistic (i.e., variationist) aspects of semantic agreement, we adopted a sociosyntactic approach to L2 acquisition.

Returning to our research questions, we first asked whether the L2 learners accept semantic agreement with collective nouns. Contrary to our predictions, the learners did not tend to accept semantic agreement; that is, there is little evidence for the acceptance of semantic agreement by either L1 group at any proficiency level. Additionally, the learners' acceptance of semantic agreement tends to decrease as their proficiency in English increases, regardless of L1 background. This general lack of acceptance by both groups, while not surprising for the L1 German group at lower proficiency levels, is surprising for the L1 French group who could potentially transfer semantic agreement with predicates from their L1. It is possible that such transfer was not more evident because of the nouns chosen in this experiment. While French speakers produce semantic agreement with more quantificational collective nouns (see, e.g., Tristram, 2014), we are unaware of data on the occurrence of semantic agreement with less quantificational nouns like team and family, which were the type primarily used in this study. A future study should examine the granularity of transfer by examining the acceptance of semantic agreement with quantificational vs. non-quantificational nouns by L1 French/L2 English learners. Though our study was not designed to differentiate these collective noun types, preliminary positive evidence is found with the SV structure, such that the average rating of semantic agreement for quantificational nouns was higher than for non-quantificational nouns among the L1 French learners. Notably, a similar difference was not found among the L1 German learners, suggesting that the patterns observed for the French can be viewed as evidence for the relexification approach to transfer (Sprouse, 2006). That is, individual lexical items are transferred with their associated features (however construed) and the learner maps those features to a lexical item in the input.

Our second research question addressed L1 English speakers' and L2 English learners' sensitivity to syntactic restrictions on semantic agreement. Both groups of L1 English speakers display sensitivity to the restriction on semantic agreement. For the British English speakers, this sensitivity is very clear. For the American English speakers, the effect is less robust because there was no difference in their ratings between VS_wh and VS_there sentences. However, this lack of robustness is likely due to floor effects. The L1 German learners also display some sensitivity to the restriction on semantic agreement because they rate VS_there sentences lower than SV sentences. As with the American English speakers, however, they did not differentiate the VS_wh and VS_there contexts. Again, though, this lack of difference is likely a floor effect. Finally, the L1 French learners did not show any evidence of sensitivity to the syntactic restriction as none of the contexts produced statistically different ratings, though they also did not violate the restriction because they tended to reject all semantic agreement. So, again, there were floor effects. These findings are consistent with the prediction that semantic agreement in contexts where the subject does not c-command the verb will be rejected both initially and at all proficiency levels by the L2 acquirers because it is a universal constraint on possible grammars (e.g., Smith, 2017). This knowledge is available in the L2 despite the lack of evidence available from their L1 grammars, which either never display semantic agreement with collective nouns (German) or only have invariant agreement morphology in existential clauses (French).

Our final research question asked whether there were effects of English dialect (exposure) on the acceptance/rating of semantic agreement. There are clear differences in the acceptance patterns by L1 speakers of the two English dialects. The British English speakers did not make a distinction between syntactic and semantic agreement in licit syntactic contexts in their ratings, strongly accepting both types of agreement. The American English speakers, however, rated syntactic agreement higher in both licit syntactic contexts. Additionally, while the two groups never differed in their ratings for syntactic agreement, the British English speakers provided higher ratings than the American English speakers for semantic agreement in both licit syntactic contexts. The rating patterns correspond to the previously observed frequency differences between American and British English in the frequency of production of semantic agreement with collective nouns: British English speakers both produce semantic agreement more, and rate it higher, than American English speakers.

We also found effects of dialect exposure among the L2 learners. However, contrary to our predictions, the more a learner reported exposure to American English (compared to British English), the more likely they were to accept semantic agreement (in the SV syntactic context for all learners, and generally for L1 German speakers. This finding is surprising because the frequency of occurrence of semantic agreement in British English is much higher than in American English. Additionally, the L1 English speakers' judgement behavior mirrors the general frequency data. It is possible that the dialect differences observed for the frequency of semantic agreement in L1 corpora do not match the input that the L2 learners are exposed to; however, it would be odd to find that L2-directed input from American English speakers would contain more evidence of semantic agreement than L2-directed input from British English speakers. Given this, the unexpected direction of dialect exposure effects should be addressed in future studies with other dialect-specific properties. It is also possible that while relative exposure points towards influence from one dialect, cumulative exposure would paint another picture. That is, while many participants were primarily exposed to American English at the time of testing, the majority were educated in British English. Potentially, this early input had a more significant impact on the acquisition process than current exposure. Research on other L2s, principally Spanish, shows that dialect exposure, even in an immersion setting during study abroad does not reliably result in acquisition of sociolinguistically constrained grammatical variables (e.g., Linford, 2016; papers in Zahler et al., 2023). The nature of the linguistic variable and individual differences such as identity and attitudes also play a role. This is even more challenging in the L2 English context, where the status of English means that input to individual L2 speakers likely comes from a range of both L1 and L2 varieties. For formal approaches to language acquisition, this raises some questions about the nature of the "target" grammar.

Clearly, future research should continue to explore methods of accounting for variation in exposure and how this relates to L2 acquisition outcomes even within a formalist framework.

This study is a first attempt to adopt a sociosyntactic framework in L2 acquisition, marrying questions about formal learnability to issues of sociolinguistic patterns in the input. Future studies should expand on this endeavor by examining further linguistic properties where sociolinguistically relevant variation and universal mechanisms interact. While here we examined variation in frequency across dialects/regions, one could also explore other sociolinguistic variables, such as age, gender, prestige, and L2-self.

Furthermore, in this study we only examined effects of learners' relative exposure to the written form of different dialects on the acceptance of semantic agreement. Future studies could also consider other measures of exposure, such as exposure to spoken dialects and calculations of total input, rather than relative measures. One could also explore other potential predictors that are otherwise related to dialect exposure, such as motivation to sound/write like a particular dialect speaker, the dialect that was taught in formal lessons, identity and attitudes toward a dialect, the role of close contacts who are speakers of particular dialects, and immersion experience within specific dialect areas (e.g., Gudmestad and Kanwit, 2025; and citations therein). Additionally, one could create and/or search a corpus of L2-directed input to determine how the frequency of semantic agreement differs from L1-directed speech/writing. Research on wider variation beyond American and British English, such as in outer-circle varieties (e.g., Hundt, 2006), suggests that the available input is messier, and leads to more diverse acquisition outcomes, than what has been considered within the L2 literature so far. By marrying sociolinguistic analysis of variation in target languages with formal morphosyntactic analysis, we can come to a fuller understanding of the nature of L2 grammatical knowledge.
