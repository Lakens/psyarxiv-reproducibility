Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. Abstract

Cross-cultural research has examined whether parents differ in how they value curiosity as a developmental characteristic, but it remains unclear whether they understand the concept in the same way. Because early curiosity must be inferred from behavior, concrete behavioral judgments offer a direct test of this question. Parents in Germany, China, and Japan were randomly assigned to judge age-appropriate behaviors from a published infant-toddler or earlychildhood curiosity measure, rating each either as an expression of curiosity or as socially desirable. The 689 parents (142 German, 425 Chinese, and 122 Japanese; ethnicity not explicitly collected) reported on children aged 5 to 71 months (344 girls, 345 boys) between July 2025 and March 2026. Candidate curiosity items clearly outperformed non-curiosity fillers in every country (differences of 1.19 to 2.84 points on a 7-point scale), and behavior orderings were strongly similar across countries (Spearman ρ = .67 to .88). Ratings were near-identical for individual items such as poking and probing, with country differences in curiosity attribution occurring much less compared to desirability differences (for 4 versus 19 items). These findings suggest that cultures may differ less in what curiosity looks like than in which behavioral expressions of curiosity are welcomed and reinforced.

Curiosity helps young children learn, but adults cannot see curiosity directly-they must infer it from what children do, such as exploring objects, asking questions, or trying new actions.

Recognizing versus valuing curiosity: Cross-country similarities and differences in parental judgments of early curious behaviors

Curiosity is often treated as an important driver of children's learning, but it is not obvious what curiosity looks like in early childhood. Even within psychological research, curiosity has been described in different ways, including interest in novelty, information seeking, uncertainty reduction, exploratory behavior, question asking, and engagement with the environment (Jirout et al., 2024; Loewenstein, 1994). These theoretical distinctions are useful, but they also highlight a more basic question for developmental research. When a young child looks closely at an unfamiliar object, takes objects out of a container, follows another person's actions, asks repeated questions, or tries out a new activity, do adults see the same underlying construct? In early childhood, curiosity cannot usually be assessed through children's explicit self-reports. It has to be inferred from behavior, and parents are among the people who make these inferences most often.

Parents' interpretations matter because they are likely to shape how curiosity is supported in everyday life. A behavior that is recognized as curiosity may be noticed, scaffolded, and encouraged, whereas the same behavior may be redirected if it is seen as disruptive, risky, or socially inappropriate. At the same time, recognising a behavior as curious is not the same as wanting to see more of it. A child who takes apart an object, investigates someone else's belongings, or asks many questions may be trying to learn something, but parents may differ in whether they view such behavior as desirable and worth encouraging. Thus, parents' judgments of early curiosity involve at least two related but separable questions: whether a behavior is understood as an expression of curiosity, and whether that behavior is evaluated positively.

Most cross-cultural research on curiosity has not directly separated these questions.

Existing studies have examined whether parents regard curiosity as an important child characteristic, often alongside broader developmental qualities such as independence, selfconfidence, creativity, or approaches to learning (Chuang & Su, 2009; Jose et al., 2000). Other work has investigated parenting goals and practices that may support exploration, autonomy, learning, or social engagement (Bornstein et al., 1992; Keller et al., 2006; Lansford et al., 2021).

These studies show that curiosity-related values and socialization goals can be studied across cultural contexts, but they do not tell us whether parents identify the same concrete behaviors as curiosity in the first place.

A related line of work has developed and validated caregiver-report questionnaires of early curiosity across different language and cultural contexts (Altmann, et al., 2025; Altmann, de Boer, et al., 2026; Eiteljoerge & Altmann, 2026; Lee et al., 2023; Passaquindici et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2024). These studies are important because they show that individual differences in curiosity can be measured meaningfully in more than one context. At the same time, questionnaire validation starts from an already specified operationalization of the construct. It can show whether a set of items functions psychometrically, but it does not by itself answer whether parents across contexts intuitively understand those behaviors as curiosity, nor whether they would want to encourage them in daily life.

Distinguishing these judgments is developmentally important. If parents across contexts recognize similar behaviors as curiosity, this would suggest some shared understanding of how early curiosity is expressed, even before children can explain their own motives. If parents differ mainly in whether they welcome those behaviors, cultural variation in curiosity may emerge less from recognising different forms of curiosity and more from how particular expressions are encouraged, tolerated, or redirected over time. Conversely, if parents differ in which behaviors they recognize as curiosity at all, then cross-cultural differences may begin already at the level of interpreting children's actions. In either case, parental judgments provide a plausible link between cultural beliefs and the everyday behavioral forms through which children's curiosity develops.

Germany, China, and Japan provide an informative comparison because they differ in socialization goals and parenting practices that may be relevant to children's exploratory behavior, while also allowing us to move beyond a simple East-West contrast. German middleclass parenting is often described as relatively autonomy-oriented, with an emphasis on children's independent exploration, verbal engagement, and self-expression (Keller et al., 2006; Keller & Demuth, 2006). In such a context, behaviors that signal self-initiated exploration or active information seeking may be especially likely to be encouraged, although this does not imply that all forms of exploration are equally welcomed.

Chinese parenting has often been discussed in relation to Confucian traditions of learning, effort, family obligation, and respect for adult guidance. Yet, contemporary urban Chinese parenting cannot be reduced to authoritarian control or collectivism, and recent work shows both warmth and high involvement alongside stronger academic and training goals than are typically emphasized in Western European samples (Chao, 2001; Dong et al., 2023; He et al., 2021; Keller et al., 2006). These patterns make it plausible that parents may value curiosity when it is linked to learning and focused knowledge acquisition, while evaluating some behaviors differently when they appear to challenge adult authority, disrupt routines, or conflict with social expectations.

Japanese parenting is also often described as interdependence-oriented, but it differs from Chinese parenting in ways that are relevant for the present study. Japanese socialization has been linked to empathy, group harmony, observational learning, and children's gradual internalization of self-control, with caregivers often supporting children through indirect guidance and close attunement rather than overt instruction alone (Bornstein et al., 1992; Holloway & Nagase, 2014; Rothbaum et al., 2000a Rothbaum et al., , 2000b)). These features make it plausible that behaviors involving careful observation, social attunement, and context-sensitive exploration may be interpreted and evaluated differently from more disruptive or assertive forms of curiosity.

At the same time, none of these contexts should be treated as homogeneous, and broad cultural models cannot determine how individual parents will judge particular behaviors. This caution is especially important because some of the cross-cultural parenting studies most relevant to the present comparison were conducted several decades ago, whereas contemporary families in all three countries are shaped by education, urbanization, changing gender roles, and globalized ideas about child development. Within-country variation in parenting values and attitudes can also be substantial (Lansford et al., 2021). The present study therefore does not use Germany, China, and Japan to represent fixed cultural types or to derive precise country-level predictions from earlier cultural models. Instead, these samples provide a way to examine three more specific questions: (i) whether parents recognize the same concrete behaviors as expressions of curiosity, (ii) whether they differ in how positively they evaluate those behaviors, and (iii) whether any emerging differences are broad or concentrated in particular items.

To examine these questions, we asked parents to children from 5 months to 5 years of age to judge concrete, age-appropriate behaviors drawn from two validated caregiver-report measures of early curiosity: the Infant and Toddler Curiosity Questionnaire (ITCQ; Altmann, et al., 2025) and the Early Childhood Curiosity Questionnaire (ECCQ; Altmann, de Boer, et al., 2026). These questionnaires were well suited to the present purpose because they include diverse behaviors spanning sensory, investigative, and interactive expressions of early curiosity. We included both, final questionnaire items and items that had been excluded during scale development, because both sets originated as candidate curiosity behaviors. We also included filler items that were intentionally designed not to measure curiosity but instead covered behaviors more closely related to temperament, prosociality, routine, and neutral play.

Parents were randomly assigned to one of two judgment conditions: In the curiosity condition, parents indicated their agreement with the statement that the specific behavior was an expression of curiosity, whereas in the desirability condition, they indicated their agreement with the statement that it was socially desirable. This design allowed us to compare two judgments that are often implicitly combined: whether a behavior is recognized as curiosity-related, and whether that behavior is evaluated positively. The analyses addressed the research questions as follows: First, we examined whether candidate curiosity behaviors, including final and excluded questionnaire items, were rated more highly than intentionally non-curiosity filler behaviors.

Second, we tested whether parents in Germany, China, and Japan differed in their curiosity and desirability ratings. Given cross-cultural differences in socialization goals and expectations about children's behavior, we expected differences to be especially likely for desirability, whereas agreement in curiosity recognition remained an open question. Third, we examined whether cross-country differences were broad or concentrated in particular behaviors, especially if they received diverging ratings on either dimension across countries.

Materials

The Infant and Toddler Curiosity Questionnaire assesses behavioral tendencies from 5 to 24 months and contributed 23 final items, 11 items excluded during scale development, and 16 filler items (Altmann, et al., 2025). The Early Childhood Curiosity Questionnaire targets children from 2 to 5 years and contributed 25 final items, 14 excluded items, and the same 16 filler items (Altmann, de Boer, et al., 2026). The analyzed item profiles therefore comprised 50 infant and toddler items and 55 preschool items. Candidate items that were ultimately excluded from a scale had nevertheless been generated as possible curiosity behaviors; their exclusion during measure development could reflect psychometric rather than conceptual reasons. The filler items instead sampled a heterogeneous set of behaviors related to temperament, prosociality, routine, and neutral play and served as a reference set that was not intended to form a unified psychological construct. They were expected to receive low curiosity ratings while potentially retaining variable desirability ratings, providing a discriminant benchmark against which curiosity-related items could be compared.

The shared questionnaire introduction described exploratory tendencies and individual preferences for interacting with the environment without naming curiosity, so that participants were not primed with the construct label before the condition-specific rating prompt. Conditionspecific instructions then asked participants to judge either the extent to which they agreed that the respective behavior expressed curiosity or the extent to which they agreed that it was desirable. Parents responded on a 7-point agreement scale from 1 (do not agree at all) to 7 (fully agree). Reverse-worded items were rescored so that higher values consistently represented ratings of positively stated behaviors: SR, Ex2, and Ex10 in the infant and toddler questionnaire, and N3, N5, Ex10, and Ex13 in the preschool questionnaire.

Items were adapted from English into German, Chinese, and Japanese using forward and backward translation. The original authors reviewed the back-translations for conceptual equivalence, and discrepancies were resolved through iterative revision. German translations were based on prior validation work where possible (Altmann, de Boer, et al., 2026; Eiteljoerge & Altmann, 2026). The complete item wording in all three languages, together with item codes and category labels, is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted separately for two age groups. Primary item-set analyses used linear mixed-effects models with response as the outcome and the full country by condition by item-group interaction as the focal fixed effect. The models additionally included condition by child-sex and condition by maternal education interactions and standardized child age. Crossed random intercepts for participant and item accounted for the repeated ratings contributed by each participant and for systematic differences among items. Models were estimated by maximum likelihood, with Satterthwaite-approximated degrees of freedom for inference (Satterthwaite, 1946), as implemented in lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). Planned item-group contrasts compared final with excluded items, final with filler items, and excluded with filler items within country and condition. Pairwise country contrasts were estimated within each condition and item group. Spearman correlations between country-specific item means assessed similarity in the rank ordering of behaviors.

For the item-level analyses, a separate linear model was fitted to each item. These models included country, condition, their interaction, condition by child sex, condition by education, and standardized child age. Because each participant contributed only one response to a given item, participant random effects were not required at this stage. HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent covariance estimates were used to reduce sensitivity to unequal residual variances and unequal country sample sizes (MacKinnon & White, 1985; Long & Ervin, 2000). Estimated marginal means and pairwise country contrasts were obtained separately for Curiosity and Desirability.

For each item and country, a desirability-versus-curiosity contrast was defined as the adjusted

Desirability mean minus the adjusted Curiosity mean. The two conditions were kept betweensubjects by design to avoid parents' rating of one dimension biasing their rating of the other.

Country differences in these contrasts are equivalent to item-specific country by condition interactions. Because different participants completed the two conditions, these quantities describe between-group differences and should not be interpreted as within-parent ambivalence.

Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction (FDR; Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) was applied separately to prespecified families of related tests. Planned item-group contrasts and item-set country contrasts were corrected within questionnaire. Item-profile correlations were corrected within questionnaire and condition across the three country pairs. Item-level country contrasts were corrected within questionnaire and condition across all items and country pairs. Within-country desirability-versus-curiosity contrasts and pairwise country differences in those contrasts were each corrected within questionnaire. Child-sex contrasts were corrected within questionnaire and condition, as were education contrasts against the undergraduate or bachelor reference group.

A difference of 0.50 response-scale points was defined a priori as the threshold for practical importance. A country difference was classified as meaningful when at least one pairwise estimate reached both an absolute difference of 0.50 points and the FDR criterion of p < .05. For descriptive classification of cross-country similarity, all three pairwise 95% confidence intervals had to fall entirely within -0.50 to 0.50. This deliberately conservative confidenceinterval criterion was used as a practical-similarity rule rather than as a formal equivalence test.

Results meeting neither criterion were treated as inconclusive, and nonsignificant differences alone were not interpreted as similarity.

Item-level curiosity and desirability differences

Nearly all final items were recognized as curiosity-related across samples. Of the 144 final item-by-country adjusted Curiosity means, 143 had 95% confidence intervals entirely above the neutral midpoint of 4. The exception was ECCQ item Ia4 (seeking help from others), in Japan (adjusted mean = 4.66, 95% CI [3.97, 5.35]). Country differences therefore usually concerned the strength of curiosity attribution rather than whether a behavior was judged to express curiosity at all.

Meaningful country differences in Curiosity occurred for 2 of 23 infant and toddler final items and 2 of 25 preschool final items. In the ITCQ, German ratings exceeded Chinese ratings for Iv1 (taking items out of containers) by 0.75 points (95% CI [0.24, 1.26], p = .049). German ratings for S2 (inspecting a variety of objects) exceeded both Chinese (difference = 0.58, p = .011) and Japanese ratings (difference = 0.74, p = .019). In the ECCQ, Chinese ratings exceeded Japanese ratings for Iv3 (inspecting all angles and sides) by 1.02 points, p = .035, and for N4 (seeking new experiences) by 0.84 points, p = .045.

In contrast, infant item S4 (poking and probing) showed the most closely aligned adjusted Curiosity means of 6.15 in Germany, 6.16 in China, and 6.12 in Japan.

Country differences in Desirability were more frequent, occurring for 5 of 23 final ITCQ items and 14 of 25 final ECCQ items. Overall, the larger differences often involved lower ratings in the German sample and higher ratings in the Chinese sample, although this was not a uniform country ordering across items. For example, Chinese Desirability ratings for infant item Iv3 (peeking into a bag) exceeded Japanese ratings by 1.11 points, p = .007, whereas Japanese ratings for S1 (showing interest in properties of objects) exceeded both Chinese ratings by 0.59 points and German ratings by 0.97 points (both p < .05).

Among ECCQ items, some of the largest differences involved Iv4 (taking items out of containers) and N6 (pointing in new environments). For both items, adjusted Desirability ratings were lowest in Germany (Iv4: Germany = 3.91, Japan = 5.09, China = 5.75; N6: Germany = 4.86, Japan = 6.27, China = 6.38).

Participant characteristics

Child age was not associated with ratings in either questionnaire (ITCQ: b = -0.017, 95% CI [-0.068, 0.034], p = .507; ECCQ: b = -0.020, 95% CI [-0.067, 0.026], p = .393). The estimated difference in ECCQ Curiosity ratings between boys and girls was small but did not survive false discovery rate correction (difference = -0.09, 95% CI [-0.22, 0.04], p = .189). One education contrast survived correction: in the ITCQ Desirability condition, the upper-secondaryor-below category made higher ratings than the undergraduate or bachelor reference category (difference = 0.58, 95% CI [0.12, 1.05], p = .044). The remaining education contrasts were not significant after correction.

Discussion

Across Germany, China, and Japan, parents showed considerable agreement about which concrete behaviors expressed early curiosity, while country differences were more common in judgments of desirability. Meaningful Curiosity differences involved two final items in each questionnaire, compared with five infant and toddler (ITCQ) and fourteen preschool (ECCQ) items for Desirability. Several behaviors also differed across countries in the extent to which their Desirability ratings stood above or below their Curiosity ratings. The distinction between recognizing and valuing curiosity therefore mattered empirically: an action could be identified as curiosity-related without receiving equally positive evaluation across settings.

This pattern was not assumed at the outset. Existing validation studies establish that caregiver-report curiosity measures can function across several national and language contexts, but they do not show that parents necessarily identify the same individual behaviors as manifestations of curiosity. The present item profiles provide initial evidence that many of these concrete behaviors were interpreted similarly across the three samples, with strong rank-order correlations and very few practically meaningful Curiosity differences among final items. At the same time, this agreement should not be interpreted as evidence that early curiosity is culturally universal in its behavioral expression. The questionnaires necessarily sample a finite set of behaviors generated within an existing measurement program, and locally generated behaviors may reveal expressions that are not represented here. Nevertheless, each age group included at least 50 diverse behaviors and situations, thereby offering a meaningful depth into the construct.

The more frequent Desirability differences are compatible with accounts in which socialization goals shape how caregivers respond to children's behavior (Keller et al., 2006), but the observed country pattern does not support a simple contrast between permissive Western and restrictive East Asian parenting. German parents often provided the lower Desirability ratings, and item-level differences varied in direction. In another behavioral domain, Runge and Soellner (2024) similarly linked socialization goals to differences in parents' evaluations of identical problem-behavior vignettes. Such findings make the meaning of particular actions more informative than a general ranking of cultures by how much they support curiosity. The present study did, however, not measure such socialization goals, safety concerns, privacy expectations, or situational norms that parents may have brought to individual items, so these explanations remain hypotheses for future work.

The difference between the two age-targeted questionnaires also raises a developmental possibility that deserves direct testing. Desirability differences and country differences in the desirability-versus-curiosity contrasts were more frequent in the preschool questionnaire than in the infant and toddler questionnaire. As children become capable of a wider range of self- directed, verbal, and socially consequential forms of exploration, there may be more opportunities for caregivers to encourage some expressions while redirecting others.

Repeated responses could, in principle, shape which forms of curiosity children increasingly express. The present design, however, cannot demonstrate such an age-related trajectory because the questionnaires contain different items and were completed by different samples. Longitudinal work that follows comparable behaviors across development is needed before stronger developmental conclusions can be drawn. The distinction between recognition and desirability is also relevant to questionnaire interpretation. Curiosity measures do not invariably reproduce identical structures across samples; for example, Kaneko (2022) did not recover distinct interest-and deprivation-type factors in Swedish preschoolers, whereas Tang et al. (2024) supported this distinction in young Chinese children. These separate studies do not isolate culture as the explanation, but they illustrate why successful translation is not sufficient to establish equivalent measurement.

Likewise, similar item rankings in the present study do not demonstrate measurement invariance (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). An item may be widely recognized as curiosity-related yet describe behavior that is differently encouraged, permitted, or reported across settings. If future validations identify non-invariant loadings or response thresholds, item-level information about perceived curiosity and desirability may help generate hypotheses about the source of that misfit, alongside alternatives such as translation or response-style differences.

Limitations & future directions

Several limitations constrain interpretation. The self-selected samples were drawn from particular recruitment contexts in Göttingen, Hangzhou, and Osaka, and cannot represent the internal diversity of Germany, China, or Japan. Furthermore, ethnicity was not measured in a harmonized way across sites, while education differed markedly between samples, with the German model sample in particular containing a high proportion of postgraduate respondents.

The country samples and randomized condition cells were also unequal in size. The mixedeffects models accommodate unbalanced data and the item-level models used HC3 robust covariance estimates, so that no downsampling was required; nevertheless, smaller cells provide less precise estimates and reduce power to detect modest differences. Cultural response styles may contribute to absolute rating differences (Lamm & Keller, 2007), and the neutral questionnaire introduction cannot eliminate evaluative response tendencies. Finally, different parents rated Curiosity and Desirability, so the contrast analyses describe group-level patterns rather than discrepancies within individual parents. Even though this was deliberate to avoid cross-contamination of either evaluation dimension, future work should relate these to each other within participants. Conclusion To our knowledge, few studies have directly separated parents' recognition of concrete curiosity-related behaviors from their evaluation of whether those same behaviors are desirable across cultural contexts. The present findings suggest substantial shared recognition within the behavior sets examined here, alongside more selective variation in how those behaviors are valued. Associations between parental responsiveness and children's epistemic curiosity (Iwasaki et al., 2023) make caregiver responses a plausible developmental pathway, but the current data do not establish such an effect. Future work combining locally generated behavioral examples, contextualized vignettes, qualitative accounts, and longitudinal observation could test when parental evaluations translate into differential encouragement or inhibition of children's exploration. In this way, distinguishing what adults recognize as curiosity from what they wish children to do may help clarify how curiosity is expressed across development and sociocultural settings.

Method Participants

The final analytic sample comprised 689 parents from Germany (n = 142), China (n = 425), and Japan (n = 122; see Table 1 for details by country, questionnaire, and condition).

Children were between 5 and 71 months old (M = 31.17, SD = 17.92); 344 were girls and 345 were boys. The proportion of respondents with a Bachelor's degree, equivalent qualification, or higher was 79.6% in Germany, 84.9% in China, and 73.0% in Japan. In China, respondents were 21 to 40 years old, in Japan they were 25 to 45 years old, whereas this information was not collected for the German sample. Furthermore, 98.6% of German children were reported as monolingual while the Chinese and Japanese samples were reportedly homogeneously monolingual.

Families in Germany were recruited through the developmental participant database at the University of Göttingen between December 2025 and March 2026. Families in China were recruited through Hangzhou Normal University between July and September 2025. Families in Japan were recruited through collaborating universities in Osaka between August and September 2025. Participants received a small thank-you for completing the survey: a printable storybook in Germany, online credits worth RMB 60 in China, and an Amazon gift card worth JPY 600 in Japan. Ethical approval was obtained at all participating sites before data collection, and parents provided informed consent before supplying any information.
