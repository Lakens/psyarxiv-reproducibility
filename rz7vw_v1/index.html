How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

In an online field experiment (N = 589 rental listings), we replicated Study 2 of Mazziotta, Zerr, and Rohmann (2015) on ethnicity, sexual orientation, and their intersection as sources of rental discrimination in Germany. Extending the design, listings were sampled from cities with low versus high vote shares for the radical-right AfD. Ten years after the original, a Turkish heterosexual couple again received fewer replies (59% vs. 73%) and fewer invitations to viewings (39% vs. 58%) than a German heterosexual couple (ω = .15 and .19).

As in the original, we found no significant discrimination based on sexual orientation and no evidence that holding both minority statuses reduced discrimination. Ethnic discrimination did not differ significantly between low-and high-AfD-share cities.

Introduction

Finding a home in Germany has rarely been as difficult as it is today and it is markedly more difficult for some than for others. In a representative survey, 35% of housing seekers with a migration background reported racist discrimination in the preceding decade, more than twice the overall rate (Antidiskriminierungsstelle des Bundes, 2020). Germany's National Discrimination and Racism Monitor paints the same picture with behavioral data: in a nationwide correspondence experiment, fictitious applicants with German-sounding names had a 22% probability of being invited to a viewing, compared to 16% for applicants with names from the Middle East, North Africa, and Turkey (DeZIM-Institut, 2025). Housing access shapes health, social networks, and economic opportunity, and such discrimination has been illegal in Germany since 2006.

Correspondence tests, in which fictitious applicants differing only in signaled group membership inquire about real listings, are regarded as the gold standard for measuring such discrimination (Auspurg et al., 2019; Flage, 2018). Meta-analytically, ethnic discrimination appears in nearly all such experiments, although its estimated magnitude has declined over the decades (Auspurg et al., 2019); robust ethnic penalties have also been documented in Germany (Auspurg et al., 2017).

The Original Study

In two online field experiments (N = 336 and N = 456) by Mazziotta, Zerr, and Rohmann (2015), a German heterosexual, a German gay male, a Turkish heterosexual, or a Turkish gay male couple inquired about rental offerings. Two competing predictions exist for doubly stigmatized applicants. The double-jeopardy account holds that stigmas accumulate, producing the most discrimination for those belonging to two minority groups (Purdie-Vaughns & Eibach, 2008). Conversely, stereotype-blending accounts suggest that the stereotype of gay men as warm and harmless could neutralize the threat-related stereotype attached to Turkish men, so that gay Turkish applicants would face less discrimination than heterosexual Turkish applicants, by analogy with US findings on Black gay men (Pedulla, 2014; Remedios et al., 2011), at the possible cost of intersectional invisibility (Purdie-Vaughns & Eibach, 2008). Empirically, both original studies found ethnic discrimination (Study 1: ω = .31 and .32; Study 2: ω = .14 for replies and .11 for invitations, the latter not statistically significant), no significant discrimination based on sexual orientation (ω ≤ .05), and no support for either intersectional prediction: the Turkish gay male couple did not differ significantly from the Turkish heterosexual couple.

The Present Study

A decade later, it is an open question whether this pattern still holds. Much has changed since the original data collection in 2013/2014: same-sex marriage was legalized in 2017; anti-immigrant sentiment has become far more politically mobilized, most visibly in the electoral rise of the radical-right party Alternative für Deutschland (AfD); and the housing market itself has tightened considerably. Survey data give no indication, however, that negative attitudes toward Turkish immigrants have become rarer: social distance toward Turkish neighbors in the German General Social Survey (ALLBUS) was unchanged between 1996 and 2016 (Lux & Gülzau, 2022), and in the biennial Mitte studies the share of respondents endorsing manifestly xenophobic statements fluctuated without a downward trend between 2014 (7.3%) and 2024/25 (7.6%; Zick et al., 2025). The stability of attitudes toward Turks documented by Mazziotta et al. (2015) thus appears to have persisted. We therefore conducted a close replication (Brandt et al., 2014) of Study 2 of Mazziotta et al. (2015), following their design and analysis approach while updating the materials.

Updating the materials also serves a methodological purpose: because names signal social class as well as ethnicity (Gaddis, 2017), and also age cohort, a result that holds across different stimulus samples carries more evidential weight than one tied to a single set (Wells & Windschitl, 1999). We additionally extended the original design. Discrimination against sexual minorities appears context-sensitive: in the German labor market, it was detected in Munich but not in Berlin (Weichselbaumer, 2015). Analogous regional variation is plausible for ethnic discrimination. We therefore sampled listings from cities with comparatively low versus high AfD vote shares, using vote share as a proxy for the regional prevalence of antiimmigrant and anti-LGBT attitudes, and tested whether this political context moderates discrimination.

Following the original study, we predicted fewer replies and invitations to viewings for the Turkish than for the German heterosexual couple (Hypothesis 1) and for the German gay male than for the German heterosexual couple (Hypothesis 2), and, as in the original intersectionality rationale, less discrimination against the Turkish gay male than against the Turkish heterosexual couple (Hypothesis 3). As an extension, we predicted stronger discrimination in high-than in low-AfD-share cities (Hypothesis 4).

Method Design and Sampling

Between December 6, 2024, and January 2, 2025, we sampled rental listings posted by private landlords on Germany's largest online rental platform (immobilienscout24.de, as in the original study). Listings were sampled in four cities/regions with a low AfD vote share in the 2024 European election (Aachen, Bielefeld, Braunschweig, Mainz/Wiesbaden; 6.2-10.9%) and five cities with a high AfD vote share (Chemnitz, Dresden, Gelsenkirchen, Halle/Saale, Magdeburg; 20.7-28.2%; national average: 15.9%). As in the original, we excluded listings targeting specific groups, with fewer than two or more than five rooms, or smaller than 50 m²; additionally, listings larger than 200 m², renting above €1,500 per month, more than 20 km from the city center, and--deviating from the original--those posted by companies or restricted to the platform's paid "MieterPlus" tier, which made up the majority of listings in our screening. The final sample consisted of N = 589 listings (low AfD share: n = 290; high AfD share: n = 299), each randomly assigned to one condition of the 2 (ethnicity: German vs. Turkish) × 2 (sexual orientation: heterosexual vs. gay male) between-subjects design. An a priori power analysis for a single chi-square test (α = .05, power = .95, ω = .18) indicated a required N of 402; we targeted 600 inquiries in total. Because each hypothesis compares two of the four conditions, however, the realized n of about 299 per comparison yields a power of .88 for ω = .18.

Materials and Procedure

Each landlord received one standardized German-language inquiry (full text in the supplementary material at https://osf.io/j9n7e/overview?view_only=3e1a1363bd6d4161af84e623c02472d5) in which the sender expressed interest in the apartment, described the couple as 36 years old, employed (teaching and IT), non-smoking, and without children or pets, and asked for a viewing.

Unlike the original inquiry, ours stated occupations and permanent employment; because status information can reduce statistical discrimination (Auspurg et al., 2017), this works against finding ethnic discrimination. Ethnicity was manipulated through common German versus Turkish first and family names (German heterosexual: Noah and Emma Zimmermann; German gay male: Noah and Paul Zimmermann; Turkish heterosexual: Mehmet and Zeynep Yilmaz; Turkish gay male: Mehmet and Yusuf Yilmaz). The original study had chosen cohort-typical names and pretested the Turkish first names for gender clarity (Christian, Stefanie, and Stefan Schneider; Mehmet, Fatma, and Hasan Yilmaz). We retained Mehmet Yilmaz and otherwise took first names from current popularity rankings and family names from frequency lists; all names are unambiguously gendered but were not pretested. Sexual orientation was manipulated by referring to "meine Partnerin" (female partner) versus "mein Partner" (male partner) in the text and by the two signature names, so that, as in the original, the cue rested on two gendered signals. We replaced the original, somewhat dated Lebensgefährte/Lebensgefährtin with Partner/Partnerin; both terms unambiguously communicate the partner's gender in German. As in the original study, we coded (a) whether the landlord replied within one week (reply to inquiry) and (b) whether the reply contained an invitation to a viewing or a request to schedule one (invitation to viewing). Ambiguous replies were coded by group consensus and invitations were declined promptly.

Deviations, Exclusions, and Analysis Plan

Hypotheses, design, materials, exclusion criteria, sample size, and the chi-square tests followed a written a priori study protocol; its translation and all deviations, with rationale, are in the supplement at https://osf.io/j9n7e/overview?view_only=3e1a1363bd6d4161af84e623c02472d5. Twelve replies addressed a gay male couple as "Herr und Frau," suggesting that the orientation manipulation was not registered. Because such failures are detectable only among repliers, excluding these cases would selectively remove positive responses from the gay-male conditions and bias the orientation estimates. We therefore report the full sample as the primary analysis and the reduced sample (N = 577) as a sensitivity analysis (not part of the protocol). Following the original study, Hypotheses 1-3 were tested with six chi-square tests, which we Holm-corrected within this family of six; the protocol had specified correction across all planned tests (see supplement). Because comparing the significance of separate subgroup tests is not a valid test of an interaction (Gelman & Stern, 2006), we additionally fitted logistic regressions. Hypotheses 1-3 were re-estimated as contrasts in a model with ethnicity × orientation and context as predictors, which also yields the ethnicity × orientation interaction (the direct test of the intersectionality hypothesis) and the context main effect; Hypothesis 4 was tested by a likelihood-ratio test of this model against the full ethnicity × orientation × context model. As a sensitivity analysis, Hypotheses 1-3 were re-estimated with city fixed effects. Full estimates are in Table S6. Data and analysis code are available at https://osf.io/j9n7e/overview?view_only=3e1a1363bd6d4161af84e623c02472d5.

Results

Overall, 370 inquiries (63%) received a reply and 268 (46%) an invitation to a viewing. Table 1 shows frequencies by condition and political context. Supporting Hypothesis 1, the Turkish heterosexual couple received fewer replies (59%) than the German heterosexual couple (73%), χ²(1, N = 299) = 6.83, p = .009, ω = .15, and fewer invitations to viewings (39% vs. 58%), χ²(1, N = 299) = 10.94, p < .001, ω = .19; both effects remained significant after Holm correction (p = .045 and p = .006; across the protocol's larger test family, only the invitation effect survives correction, see supplement). Both effect sizes exceed the protocol's replication criterion (ω ≥ .10).

Contrary to Hypothesis 2, the German gay male couple did not differ significantly from the German heterosexual couple on replies (63% vs. 73%), χ²(1, N = 300) = 3.42, p = .064, ω = .11, or invitations (48% vs. 58%), χ²(1, N = 300) = 3.35, p = .067, ω = .11. Contrary to Hypothesis 3, the Turkish gay male and the Turkish heterosexual couple did not differ on replies (55% vs. 59%), χ²(1, N = 289) = 0.39, p = .530, ω = .04, or invitations (36% vs. 39%), χ²(1, N = 289) = 0.22, p = .639, ω = .03. Model-based contrasts led to the same conclusions (Hypothesis 1: OR = 0.52 and 0.45; Hypothesis 2: OR = 0.62 and 0.64; Hypothesis 3: OR = 0.86 and 0.88, for replies and invitations, respectively; 95% confidence intervals in Table S6), and the ethnicity × orientation interaction was nonsignificant, χ²(1, N = 589) = 0.85, p = .355, and χ²(1, N = 589) = 0.93, p = .335 (interaction ORs = 1.38 and 1.39). With city fixed effects, these estimates were virtually unchanged (Table S6). As in the original's exploratory comparison, the Turkish gay male couple also tended to fare worse than the German gay male couple (replies: p = .164; invitations: p = .052). Regarding Hypothesis 4, the joint likelihoodratio tests of all political-context interaction terms were nonsignificant for replies, χ²(3, N = 589) = 2.42, p = .490, and invitations, χ²(3, N = 589) = 2.37, p = .499. Unexpectedly, response rates were higher in high-AfD-share cities (reply: OR = 1.44 [1.03, 2.03]; invitation: OR = 1.77 [1.27, 2.47]).

In the sensitivity analysis excluding the twelve misaddressed replies, all conclusions were unchanged; the orientation effects reached nominal but not Holm-corrected significance (ps = .028), which should be interpreted cautiously given the asymmetric nature of the exclusion.

Discussion

Persistent Ethnic Discrimination

A decade later, the central findings of Mazziotta et al. (2015) replicated closely. A Turkish couple was substantially disadvantaged on the German rental market on both indicators, with effect sizes (ω = .15/.19) at least as large as in the original Study 2 (ω = .14/.11). This result does not fit the meta-analytic trend of declining ethnic discrimination in housing markets (Auspurg et al., 2019), although a single study cannot overturn an average trend and our modified materials preclude a direct estimate of change over time. It converges, however, with the independent, contemporaneous correspondence experiment of the National Discrimination and Racism Monitor, which likewise found substantially fewer invitations for applicants with non-German names (DeZIM-Institut, 2025).

Descriptively, the gap was larger for invitations than for replies. Because invitations are nested within replies, this pattern is consistent with, but does not demonstrate, screening at a later stage; testing research has located the strongest disadvantage at the later stages of the allocation process (Antidiskriminierungsstelle des Bundes, 2015).

Sexual Orientation and Intersectionality

As in both original studies, we found no significant discrimination based on sexual orientation. The descriptive gap was nevertheless larger than in the original (ω = .11 vs. .04-.05), and the relative risks for German gay versus heterosexual couples (0.86 and 0.82) resemble the meta-analytic risk ratio of about 0.90 for gay male couples (Flage, 2021). The 95% confidence interval for the reply difference between German gay and heterosexual couples ranged from -20 to +1 percentage points; the null result is therefore not evidence of absence. Both the original study and ours signaled sexual orientation subtly (a gendered partner noun and a first name) to preserve ecological validity. Such a cue may be read through landlords' default heteronormative schema and go unnoticed, all the more so when first names are unfamiliar. Twelve landlords (eight in the German, four in the Turkish gaymale condition) addressed a gay male couple as "Herr und Frau," so the cue was missed at least by them and plausibly by others; eleven of these replies contained an invitation. This trade-off makes our orientation estimates (and probably the original ones) conservative; a stronger or verified manipulation is warranted in future work. As in the original, holding both minority statuses provided no detectable protective effect: the Turkish gay male couple did not differ significantly from the Turkish heterosexual couple and tended to fare worse than the German gay male couple. Neither double jeopardy nor beneficial stereotype blending is clearly supported; descriptively, ethnicity mattered more than sexual orientation on this market, although our data cannot establish this firmly (in contrast, Schwegman, 2019, found an additional penalty for Black gay male couples in the US).

Regional Political Context

Our extension yielded no statistically significant evidence that ethnic or orientationbased discrimination depended on regional political context. Three caveats apply. First, context was confounded with market conditions: the high-AfD-share cities showed markedly higher response rates, plausibly because their housing markets are less tight and landlords with many inquiries can discriminate at little cost. Second, context varied between only nine unequally sampled cities, so any other city characteristic is confounded with it (the Hypothesis 1-3 estimates, by contrast, were robust to city fixed effects). Third, aggregate vote shares are a coarse proxy for the attitudes of the specific landlords contacted, and they refer to the city proper whereas listings within 20 km may extend into surrounding municipalities; neighborhood-level indicators would provide a stronger test.

Limitations and Future Directions

Beyond the changed stimuli and the added status information, limitations include the data-collection window over the December holidays, which may have lowered response rates; the restriction to private, non-subscription listings on a single platform; a single name set per condition; the new German first names, which are popular today but were uncommon in the applicants' birth cohort; and the possibility that Turkish names signal lower socioeconomic status in addition to ethnicity (Auspurg et al., 2019). Future research should examine the later, decisive stages of the allocation process (viewing to contract), where survey and testing evidence locates the strongest disadvantage (Antidiskriminierungsstelle des Bundes, 2015; DeZIM-Institut, 2025).

Conclusion

This replication shows that the core result of Mazziotta et al. (2015) is robust across a decade, different cities, different names, and a modified operationalization of sexual orientation: prospective tenants with Turkish names remain markedly disadvantaged on the German rental market, and we found no evidence that this depended on regional political climate. The policy implication of the original article has, if anything, gained urgency.
