Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Descriptive norms measure the perceived prevalence of each emotion within participants’ groups (e.g., “How many people in your group feel empathy (compassion) for the suffering of outgroup members in the context of the conflict.?”). Multiple regression for mediation analysis will be used to assess direct and indirect effects of emotion norms (independent variable) on support for conflict-related policies (dependent variable) via personal emotions (mediator). Separate models will be run for each emotion and emotion norm, as well as a single model with all emotions and emotion norms to assess their relative effects. H4: Misperceptions of norms and policy support
Multiple regression will test whether misperceptions of norms (calculated as the difference between perceived and actual norms) predict support for conflict-related policies (dependent variable). Control variables such as countries, socio-demographics and personal emotions will be included.

H5: Moderation by group identification
Moderation analysis using multiple regression will assess the interaction between emotion norms and group identification to predict support for conflict-related policies. The interaction term between emotion norms and group identification will test whether the relationship between emotion norms and policy support varies depending on the level of group identification. Socio-demographics and personal emotions will be included as control variables.

Separate models will be run for each emotion norm within each reference group. In addition, combined models including emotion norms from all three reference groups (e.g., national hate norm, political hate norm, and local hate norm) will be estimated to evaluate their relative influence.

In H1 to H6:
Subgroup analyses will be conducted for each country and for distinct conflict groups (where applicable), such as Protestants and Catholics in Northern Ireland. Separate models will be run to estimate the relationships between variables across different groups and countries.

H7: Cross-country differences

To test H7, we aim to assess whether the strength of the association between perceived emotion norms and support for conflict-related policies varies systematically by country-level conflict status (ongoing vs. post-conflict). Given our cross-national sample includes 13 countries, we will use two complementary strategies to analyse this relationship while accounting for the nested data structure and the small number of level-2 units.

1. Two-Step Estimation Approach

We will adopt a two-step estimation procedure as our primary strategy, given its transparency and its appropriateness for settings with a small number of countries and a large number of respondents per country:

Step 1: For each country, we will estimate the individual-level association between perceived emotion norms and policy support using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. These models will control for relevant individual-level covariates.

Step 2: We will extract the estimated regression coefficients for the effect of perceived emotion norms on policy support from each country-level model. These coefficients will then be used as dependent variables in a country-level linear regression, with the binary indicator of conflict stage (0 = post-conflict, 1 = ongoing conflict) as the independent variable.

This will allow us to test whether the association between emotion norms and policy support is significantly stronger in ongoing conflict countries than in post-conflict countries, as predicted in H6.

We acknowledge that, due to the limited number of countries (N = 13), this second-stage analysis will have limited statistical power, and results will be interpreted cautiously.

2. Multilevel Modeling

As a robustness check, we will also explore multilevel models (hierarchical linear models) that include random intercepts for countries, and random slopes for the emotion norms predictor. In these models, we will test for cross-level interactions between perceived emotion norms (level-1 predictor) and country-level conflict stage (level-2 moderator). Specifically, a significant cross-level interaction between perceived emotion norms and conflict stage would support H6, indicating that the strength of the association varies by national context.

We are aware of concerns about the stability of random effect estimates with only 13 countries. Following Gelman & Hill (2007), we will not dismiss multilevel models a priori but will interpret them with caution and focus on them as supplementary to the two-step approach.

H8: Competitive victimhood and outgroup responsibility
Multiple regression will be used to test whether competitive victimhood beliefs and higher attribution of responsibility to the outgroup predict greater support for conflict-promoting policies. Control variables will include socio-demographics, country, and personal emotions.

H9: Inclusive victimhood and ingroup responsibility
Multiple regression will assess whether inclusive victimhood beliefs and greater recognition of ingroup responsibility predict higher support for conflict-resolution policies. Socio-demographics, country, and personal emotions will be included as controls.

H10: Historical lesson framing
Separate regressions will test whether framing group victimization as a lesson to avoid harm predicts support for reconciliation policies, and whether framing it as a justification for self-defense predicts support for conflict-promoting policies. Control variables will include socio-demographics, emotions, and victimhood beliefs.

H11: Interaction between victimhood beliefs and historical lesson framing
Moderation analysis using regression will test interactions between inclusive victimhood and “avoid harm” framing, and between competitive victimhood and “self-defense” framing, in predicting policy support. Controls will include socio-demographics, emotions, and country fixed effects.

For H8 to H11, we will also conduct subgroup analyses by country and conflict group (where applicable) to explore whether effects are consistent across contexts.
research_questions H1: Perceived emotion norms will be positively associated with corresponding personal emotions. For example, individuals who perceive stronger group norms of empathy will express higher personal levels of empathy.

H2: Perceived emotion norms will be positively associated with support for relevant conflict-related policies. Specifically, stronger perceived norms of constructive intergroup emotions (e.g., empathy) will be associated with greater support for conflict-moderating policies, whereas stronger perceived norms of negative intergroup emotions (e.g., hate) will be associated with greater support for conflict-promoting policies.

H3: The effect of perceived emotion norms on support for conflict-related policies will be mediated by personal emotions.

H3.1: Perceived emotion norms will have a direct effect on support for conflict-related policies, even when controlling for corresponding personal emotions. That is, perceived norms will have an additional direct (un-mediated, or mediated otherwise) on policy support.

H4: Group identification will moderate the relationship between perceived emotion norms and policy support. The association between emotion norms and policy support will be stronger among individuals with higher levels of group identification compared to those with lower identification.

H5: Misperceptions of emotion norms will be associated with support for conflict-related policies. Specifically, individuals who underestimate the prevalence of positive emotion norms (e.g., empathy) within their group will show lower support for conflict-moderating policies, whereas individuals who overestimate the prevalence of negative emotion norms (e.g., hate) will show greater support for conflict-promoting policies.
We expect that individuals will often misperceive the prevalence of group emotions, though the direction and magnitude of these misperceptions are likely to vary. We will explore whether these misperceptions depend on the specific emotion, individual characteristics, and country-level context.

H6: Importance of Reference Groups
Social norms are most influential when they are linked to reference groups - groups to which people belong to and care about (Sherif & Sherif, 1964). In conflict settings, research often focuses on norms within conflict-defined groups (e.g., ethnic or national groups), as violent conflict tends to heighten identification with these groups, making them chronically salient lenses through which individuals interpret the conflict and related issues (Elcheroth & Reicher, 2017). However, other reference groups - such as political groups (Harel, Maoz, & Halperin, 2020) or local community groups (e.g., neighbors, friends; Matthes et al., 2018) - can also play a key role.
In a pilot study conducted in Israel (Penić, Mostovoy, & Halperin, 2024), we found that perceived emotion norms within one’s political group were more predictive of policy support than those within the broader national group. This suggests that the relative influence of reference groups may vary by context.
In this study, we will assess the influence of perceived emotion norms across three types of reference groups:
1. Conflict-defined group (e.g., ethnic or national group central to the conflict),
2. Political group (e.g., based on ideological alignment such as left vs. right),
3. Local community group (e.g., village, town, or neighborhood).
Hypotheses:
• H6.1: Perceived emotion norms in the conflict-defined group will be positively associated with personal emotions and support for conflict-related policies, in line with the mediation and direct effect patterns described in H1–H5.
• H6.2: Perceived emotion norms in the political group and local community group will also show positive associations with personal emotions and policy support, consistent with the relationships outlined in H1–H5. However, the relative influence of these groups is expected to vary across reference groups and countries.
Specifically, in Israel, we expect political group norms to be the most influential predictor of personal emotions and policy support. In other countries, we do not formulate specific predictions regarding which reference group will be most influential, and will examine these differences in an exploratory manner.

H7: The relationship between emotion norms and policy support will differ across national contexts. Specifically, the association will be stronger in countries experiencing ongoing conflict compared to countries in a post-conflict phase.

H8: Embracing a competitive victimhood belief, in addition to higher attribution of responsibility to the outgroup, will predict support for conflict-promoting policies.

H9: Embracing an inclusive victimhood belief, in addition to higher recognition of the ingroup responsibility, will predict support for conflict-resolution policies.

H10: Those who perceive the history of their group’s victimization as a lesson to avoid perpetuating harm to others will show greater support for reconciliation-oriented policies. Conversely, those who interpret their group’s history as a justification for self-defense, including violence if necessary, will exhibit higher support for conflict-promoting policies.

H11: The relationship between victimhood narratives (inclusive vs. competitive) and policy support (reconciliation vs. conflict) will be moderated by perceptions of historical lessons, such that inclusive victimhood combined with a commitment to avoid harm will amplify support for reconciliation-related policies. In contrast, competitive victimhood combined with a focus on self-defense will increase support for conflict-related policies.

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Collective victimization can have different political consequences. Experiences of war, persecution, occupation, and intergroup violence may contribute to defensive or hostile orientations toward other groups, but they may also foster recognition of shared suffering and support for more constructive intergroup relations (Vollhardt & Bilali, 2015; Vollhardt, Cohrs, et al., 2021). Research on collective victimhood distinguishes the experience of harm from the beliefs through which group members interpret that harm (Bar-Tal et al., 2009; Noor et al., 2017). Some beliefs compare an ingroup's suffering with that of an adversary or other groups; others connect the ingroup's experience to the victimization of others (Noor et al., 2017; Vollhardt, 2015). Recognizing victims across group boundaries can involve acknowledging comparable suffering or recognizing their shared humanity despite differences in the extent of harm. These understandings of collective suffering may carry distinct implications for reconciliation, intergroup equality, and aggression.

Within this literature, two competitive beliefs concern suffering relative to a particular conflict opponent or relative to groups worldwide. Two inclusive beliefs make a different distinction: perceiving parity in the suffering of the ingroup and its adversary versus recognizing the shared human standing of victims even where their experiences differ. The latter, which we call human-level inclusive victimhood, acknowledges victimization across group boundaries independently of judgments about the relative magnitude of suffering. The present study examines these four victimhood beliefs and their associations with three conflict-related outcomes: support for reconciliation policies, support for intergroup-equality policies, and aggression endorsement or justification.

Examining these beliefs separately advances our understanding of variation within competitive and inclusive victimhood. A central contribution is the distinction between comparative and human-level inclusive victimhood and the examination of their respective associations with conflict-related policy support and aggression. A second contribution concerns variation across conflict contexts. Drawing on survey data from 14 ethnonational groups in 10 conflict-affected settings, we investigate differences in the endorsement and political associations of victimhood beliefs across groups and explore the relevance of conflict stage and relative group power. Together, these contributions extend research on collective victimhood by examining how the content of beliefs about suffering and the contexts in which they are held relate to conflict-related responses.

Collective victimhood as an interpretation of group suffering

Collective victimhood refers to a shared understanding of harm as unjust, undeserved, and consequential for the group. In intractable conflicts, this understanding can become embedded in collective memory and the broader ethos of conflict, shaping how group members interpret their past and present circumstances (Bar-Tal et al., 2009). Individuals may participate in collective narratives of victimization without having personally experienced the events invoked by those narratives, and similar histories of harm may be interpreted through different comparative frameworks (Noor et al., 2017).

The social and political meanings attributed to suffering are central to these interpretations. Bilali and Vollhardt (2019) show that groups involved in collective violence may disagree about who was victimized, who was responsible, perpetrators' intentions, the severity of harm, and the historical period considered relevant. Their account distinguishes motivations to protect the group and obtain acknowledgment from motivations to defend the ingroup's moral image. Research on the Lebanese Civil War further illustrates these dynamics. Across two studies involving Lebanese Christians and Muslims, Zahreddine, Licata, et al. (2026) found that religious identification was associated with more ingroup-favoring interpretations of victimhood and responsibility, particularly when participants were reminded of their group's past victimization. At the same time, participants acknowledged outgroup suffering, illustrating how recognition of another group's victimhood can coexist with defensive interpretations of the conflict. These perspectives highlight how interpretations of collective victimization are embedded in broader understandings of group identity, responsibility, and conflict history. They also suggest that the political associations of victimhood beliefs may differ according to how the ingroup's suffering is understood in relation to that of others.

Competitive victimhood: comparisons with the adversary and the wider world

Competitive victimhood concerns the belief that the ingroup has suffered more than another group (Noor et al., 2012). We distinguish two competitive beliefs. Conflict-specific competitive victimhood concerns the judgment that the ingroup suffered more harm than the focal adversary during the conflict. Global competitive victimhood concerns the broader claim that no group anywhere has experienced as much victimization as the ingroup. These formulations differ in their comparison referents and in the scope of the claims they make (Vollhardt, Cohrs, et al., 2021; Vollhardt, Szabó, et al., 2021). Noor et al. (2012) conceptualize competitive victimhood as an intergroup competition that can obstruct forgiveness and peaceful conflict resolution. Their account connects competition over victim status to broader intergroup rivalry and, drawing on the needs-based model (Shnabel & Nadler, 2008), proposes that such competition may serve motivations to restore power or moral acceptance. Recognition of suffering can therefore become a resource through which groups seek acknowledgment and defend their position in the conflict (Young & Sullivan, 2016).

Both competitive beliefs may accompany aggression by placing victimhood within competition over legitimacy, recognition, and moral standing. However, the comparison referent may shape the political relevance of these claims. Conflict-specific competitive victimhood is embedded in a particular adversarial relationship and may be especially relevant to judgments about the opponent's responsibility, legitimacy, and entitlement to reconciliation or equal treatment. Global competitive victimhood extends beyond the immediate conflict, locating the ingroup within a broader hierarchy of suffering and potentially reinforcing claims of exceptional victimization. Their political associations may therefore differ across groups and conflict settings (Noor et al., 2017; Vollhardt, Szabó, et al., 2021).

Previous research has highlighted the relevance of comparison referents in collective victimhood. In a multigroup study of Armenian Americans, Jewish Americans, Hungarians, and Poles, Vollhardt, Cohrs, et al. (2021) examined exclusive comparative victim beliefs using global or regional victim-group comparisons and linked these beliefs to hostile intergroup outcomes.

Building on this research, the present study examines the distinct associations of conflict-specific and global competitive victimhood alongside two forms of inclusive victimhood across multiple conflict contexts.

Inclusive victimhood: perceived parity and shared humanity

Inclusive victimhood connects the ingroup's suffering to that of others, extending recognition of victimization across group boundaries (Vollhardt, 2015). Research has generally associated inclusive victimhood with constructive intergroup attitudes, including prosocial orientations, willingness for contact, and support for peace (Noor et al., 2015; Voca et al., 2023; Vollhardt & Bilali, 2015). A recent meta-analysis of 745 estimates from 103 articles similarly found that inclusive victimhood beliefs tended to be associated with more constructive intergroup outcomes, whereas non-inclusive beliefs were generally associated with conflictenhancing attitudes (Kljajić et al., 2026).

Previous research has distinguished inclusive victimhood according to the scope and interpretation of shared suffering. Rupar et al. (2022) differentiated general inclusion, involving victim groups worldwide, from conflict-specific inclusion involving opposing groups. Cohrs et al. (2015) further distinguished universal from selective interpretations of shared suffering, showing that their implications for forgiveness differed. The present study examines a complementary distinction: whether recognizing an adversary's victimhood requires perceiving equivalent suffering or instead involves acknowledging victims' shared human standing despite differences in victimization.

Parity-based conflict-specific inclusive victimhood involves the judgment that the ingroup suffered as much harm as its focal adversary during the conflict. Human-level inclusive victimhood, by contrast, involves recognizing that a victim is a victim at the basic human level, regardless of group membership or differences in the extent of harm. This recognition remains situated within the relationship between conflict opponents, distinguishing it from broader identification with all humanity (McFarland et al., 2012). This distinction is particularly relevant in conflicts characterized by asymmetrical suffering or contested historical narratives. Individuals may acknowledge an adversary's victimization without considering the groups' suffering equivalent. Human-level inclusion accommodates such recognition, whereas parity-based inclusion rests on a comparative judgment about the magnitude of harm. These different understandings of shared victimhood may have distinct political implications, particularly for support for reconciliation and intergroup equality.

Conflict context and group differences

Victimhood beliefs emerge within specific histories of violence, distributions of power, interpretations of responsibility, and public narratives of conflict (Bilali & Vollhardt, 2019).

Their endorsement and associations with conflict-related outcomes may consequently vary across ethnonational groups and conflict settings. The same belief may carry different political meanings depending on the groups involved, their experiences of victimization, and the role of collective suffering in contemporary intergroup relations. Examining group-specific patterns alongside pooled associations can reveal both recurring relationships and differences in how victimhood beliefs relate to conflict-related responses.

Previous research illustrates the importance of these contextual meanings. In Northern Ireland, Cohrs et al. (2015) showed that the relationship between inclusive victimhood and forgiveness depended on how similarities in suffering were understood. Rupar et al. (2022) likewise documented differences in the associations between inclusive victimhood and willingness for different forms of intergroup contact in Bosnian and Basque samples. Beyond individual-level beliefs, research on Croatia and Kosovo has shown how narratives of national victimization, liberation, and victory remain closely connected to state legitimacy and political identity after the end of armed violence (Boduszyński & Pavlaković, 2019). These findings illustrate how the meaning and political relevance of collective suffering are embedded in particular historical and political contexts.

Two contextual characteristics may be especially relevant to variation in the associations between victimhood beliefs and conflict-related outcomes. First, conflict stage may shape the salience and interpretation of collective victimization. Ongoing conflicts involve immediate insecurity, mobilization, and perceived threat, potentially intensifying competition over victimhood and distinctions between victims and perpetrators. Post-conflict settings may provide different opportunities for coexistence and the reinterpretation of collective narratives, while memories of violence and disputes over responsibility can continue to shape intergroup relations.

Such differences may also shape the political associations of victimhood beliefs.

Second, relative group power may influence the meanings attached to victimhood claims and their political implications. Members of relatively disadvantaged groups may invoke collective suffering in demands for acknowledgment, protection, and equal treatment, whereas members of relatively advantaged groups may draw on victimhood narratives to affirm their group's moral standing or defend existing arrangements (Kahalon et al., 2019). These dynamics are particularly relevant to comparative victimhood beliefs, as claims about relative suffering are embedded in relationships characterized by different distributions of power, experiences of violence, and access to recognition. Group positions may also change over time, making the distinction between power during the focal conflict and power at the time of the survey theoretically relevant.

The comparative design allows us to address two related questions about contextual variation. The first concerns between-group heterogeneity: the extent to which associations between victimhood beliefs and conflict-related outcomes are similar across groups or differ in magnitude and direction. The second concerns contextual influences on this heterogeneity, particularly whether conflict stage and dyad-specific relative power are associated with differences in these relationships. By combining group-specific analyses with exploratory contextual comparisons, we examine how the political associations of collective victimhood vary across conflict-affected societies and identify patterns that may inform the interpretation of these differences.

Domains of conflict-related policy support

The political implications of victimhood beliefs may differ across the outcomes being considered. We examine three domains: reconciliation-policy support, intergroup-equality-policy support, and aggression endorsement or justification. Reconciliation concerns support for peaceful coexistence, dialogue, negotiation, or renewed intergroup relations. Intergroup equality concerns support for policies promoting equal treatment, representation, or opportunities between groups, including where such policies may challenge established group advantages.

Aggression concerns the endorsement or justification of violent or aggressive action against an outgroup in connection with conflict goals, including aggression-related policies where these are represented in the measures.

Although related, these outcomes involve different political judgments. Support for improved relations with an adversary does not necessarily imply endorsement of policies that change the distribution of rights or opportunities between groups. Similarly, rejecting aggression does not necessarily entail support for either reconciliation or equality. Because competitive and inclusive victimhood beliefs differ in their comparison referents and the forms of recognition they express, their associations may also vary across these political domains. Distinguishing the outcomes therefore allows us to examine how different interpretations of collective suffering relate to specific responses to intergroup conflict.

The present study and hypotheses

The present study examines associations between four collective victimhood beliefs and three conflict-related outcomes among 12,499 respondents from 14 ethnonational groups across VICTIMHOOD AND POLICY SUPPORT 11 10 conflict-affected settings. The four victimhood beliefs are entered simultaneously in the primary models, allowing us to estimate their distinct associations while accounting for their overlap.

Drawing on previous research linking competitive victimhood to conflict-enhancing responses and inclusive victimhood to constructive intergroup attitudes (Adelman et al., 2016; Kljajić et al., 2026; Noor et al., 2012; Vollhardt & Bilali, 2015), we formulated two directional hypotheses, preregistered as part of the broader project (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/JAEMS).

H1.

Both conflict-specific and global competitive victimhood will be positively associated with aggression endorsement or justification.

H2.

Both parity-based conflict-specific and human-level inclusive victimhood will be positively associated with (a) reconciliation-policy support and (b) intergroup-equality-policy support.

Beyond these hypotheses, we examine differences in the endorsement and political associations of the four victimhood beliefs, with particular attention to the distinction between parity-based and human-level inclusive victimhood. Formal coefficient comparisons assess differences in their associations across the three political domains. Group-specific analyses further examine the consistency and heterogeneity of these relationships across ethnonational groups, while exploratory contextual analyses investigate the relevance of conflict stage and dyad-specific relative power.

Method Participants and Procedure

The study used survey data from the Emotion Norms Project (Penić et al., 2026; Zahreddine, Penić, et al., 2028). A common questionnaire was translated, pretested, and adapted by researchers familiar with each setting's language and conflict context. Adaptations concerned group labels, descriptions of the conflict, political reference groups, and policy examples.

Data were collected between February 2025 and January 2026, primarily through online survey panels. In southern Thailand, data were collected through face-to-face interviews, predominantly administered on tablets or mobile devices. Recruitment sought demographic diversity and used quota matching where feasible; samples were not drawn using a common probability-sampling design. The study received approval from the University of Geneva Institutional Review Board (CUREG-20231002-323-2), and all participants provided informed consent. Additional fieldwork information is available in the project methodology report (Zahreddine, Penić, et al., 2026).

Sample Selection and Data Preparation

The analyses included ethnonational groups with usable data on all four focal victimhood indicators. The Ethiopian and Colombian samples were excluded because the complete set of valid indicators was unavailable. Respondents were included if they passed at least one of two attention checks and their completion time was not below the country-specific fifth percentile.

The retained sample comprised 12,499 respondents from 14 ethnonational groups in 10 conflictaffected societies (Table S1). Model-specific sample sizes varied with missing data in the analysis variables (Table S2).

Measures

Unless otherwise indicated, the victimhood, outcome, and identification items used sixpoint response scales from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). Questionnaire templates, response formats, and setting-specific adaptations are documented in the project codebook (Zahreddine, Penić, et al., 2028).

Collective Victimhood Beliefs

Four single-item indicators distinguished the comparison referent and content of victimhood beliefs. Conflict-specific competitive victimhood was measured with "During the conflict, [ingroup] suffered more harm than [outgroup]." Global competitive victimhood was measured with "No other group in the world has been victimized as much as [ingroup]." Paritybased conflict-specific inclusive victimhood was measured with "During the conflict, [ingroup] have suffered harm as much as [outgroup]." Human-level inclusive victimhood was measured with "Despite essential differences between the victimization of [ingroup] and [outgroup] at the basic human level, a victim is a victim, here or there." All four indicators were analyzed separately and included simultaneously in the focal regression models.

Conflict-Related Outcomes

Three outcomes were examined: reconciliation-policy support, intergroup-equality-policy support, and aggression endorsement or justification. Reconciliation was assessed with a general item, "I would support programs that promote living in peace and coexistence with [outgroup] members (or between ingroup and outgroup) as neighbors and friends", supplemented where available by a setting-specific item concerning dialogue, negotiations, or reconciliation initiatives. Intergroup equality was assessed with "I would support policies that promote equality between the groups in housing, education and jobs", supplemented where available by an item concerning locally relevant equal treatment, resource distribution, or representation.

Aggression was assessed with a general item concerning the acceptability of violence against the outgroup in pursuit of ingroup conflict goals and, where administered, a settingspecific item on aggressive action or policy. In ongoing-conflict settings, the general item was "Physical and violent actions against the members of the outgroup are justified in order to promote our goals in the conflict". In post-conflict settings, the general item referred to the justification of violence during the past conflict, whereas the setting-specific item referred to aggression in a possible renewed conflict. The outcome therefore captures aggression endorsement or justification, with differences in temporal framing across settings.

Outcome scores were calculated as the mean of available general and setting-specific items; a single available item constituted the score when no second item was retained. For intergroup equality in both Thai groups, only the general item was used because its association with the setting-specific item was negligible or negative. The setting-specific aggression item was not administered in Turkey or either Indian group. Accordingly, outcomes were based on one or two items depending on the setting. Item correlations and reliability estimates are reported in Table S18.

Individual-Level Covariates

The primary models adjusted for age, gender, and ingroup identification. Identification was scored as the mean of two items where available (pooled item correlation r = .607; Spearman-Brown reliability = .755; group-specific estimates in Table S19). Gender was represented by a female-versus-male indicator; respondents with other or missing gender responses were excluded from models containing this covariate. Additional models adjusted for political orientation or affiliation. Seven groups used a common seven-point left-right measure, whereas the other seven used setting-specific political-party or affiliation variables.

Contextual Classifications

For exploratory comparisons, conflict stage was classified using the UCDP/PRIO Armed Conflict Dataset (version 25.1; Davies et al., 2025; Pettersson, 2025). Settings with a recorded state-based armed conflict in 2024 were categorized as active-conflict settings; settings with an earlier conflict record but none in 2024 were categorized as post-conflict settings. This classification yielded seven ethnonational groups in each category.

Relative group power was coded with reference to the opposing group in each focal conflict. For domestic ethnopolitical relationships, classification drew primarily on institutional and executive access recorded in the Ethnic Power Relations framework (Vogt et al., 2015).

Where these indicators did not characterize the relevant dyad or wartime asymmetry, conflictspecific historical, military, or interstate-capability evidence was used. Power during the focal conflict was the primary classification (lower vs. higher); survey-period power was examined separately, excluding dyads coded as having parity. The resulting categories reflect dyad-specific judgments based on partly different dimensions of power. Coding decisions and sources are reported in Tables S41-S42.

Two additional exploratory setting-level indicators were derived from the UCDP/PRIO Armed Conflict Dataset (version 25.1, 1946 -2024; Davies et al., 2025; Pettersson, 2025). Conflict records were matched using country and, where applicable, conflict-territory identifiers.

These indicators captured setting-level conflict histories rather than the duration of violence specifically involving the focal ethnonational groups and were constant across respondents within each setting.

Analytic Strategy

Primary Pooled Models

Separate linear mixed-effects regressions were fitted for reconciliation, intergroup equality, and aggression. Each model entered the four victimhood indicators simultaneously and adjusted for age, gender, and ingroup identification, with a random intercept for ethnonational group (Model 1). The four victimhood indicators, three outcomes, and continuous covariates were z-standardized in the retained sample before complete-case selection for each model.

Models were estimated by maximum likelihood using lme4 and lmerTest. Fixed-effect tests used Satterthwaite-adjusted degrees of freedom, with two-sided α = .05; Holm-adjusted tests of the preregistered focal hypotheses are reported in Table S20.

Group-Specific Models and Coefficient Comparisons

To describe variation across ethnonational groups, Model 1 was also estimated separately within each group using ordinary least squares regression with HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. Model 2 added political orientation or affiliation. Separate pooled specifications were estimated for the groups with a common ideological scale (Model 2A) and those with setting-specific political-affiliation variables (Model 2B). Formal within-model contrasts compared the coefficients for conflict-specific and global competitive victimhood, and for parity-based and human-level inclusive victimhood. Between-group variation in the Model 1 slopes was summarized using Cochran's Q and descriptive I² statistics (Table S34); because some groups share conflict settings, the resulting statistics were interpreted descriptively rather than as tests of independent contextual effects.

Exploratory Contextual Moderation and Robustness Checks

Exploratory moderation analyses examined whether the within-group association of each victimhood indicator with each outcome differed by the 2024 conflict-stage classification, conflict-period relative power, or survey-period relative power. One focal victimhood-by-context interaction was estimated per model. Victimhood variables retained their pooled-sample standardized units and were decomposed into individual deviations from their ethnonationalgroup means and the corresponding group means. Models included all four group-centered victimhood indicators, the focal indicator's group mean, age, gender, and ingroup identification, together with a group random intercept and an uncorrelated random slope for the focal groupcentered indicator. Each interaction therefore represented a contextual difference in a withingroup association (Tables S35-S37).

For these 36 moderation models, model-based estimates were supplemented by CR2 cluster-robust standard errors and Satterthwaite-adjusted tests, with clustering at the conflictsetting level. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, we report setting-clustered CR2 95% confidence intervals and unadjusted p values. Holm-adjusted p values across the four victimhood-belief interactions within each outcome and contextual classification are provided as a sensitivity analysis for multiple testing (Table S43). The analyses included 10 independent settings, or eight in the survey-period power sensitivity classification.

Two additional analyses evaluated the robustness and contextual consistency of the results. First, the pooled Model 1 regressions were re-estimated with uncorrelated random slopes for all four victimhood indicators (Tables S22 and S39). Second, group-specific Model 1 HC3 coefficients and their sampling variances were entered into two-stage random-effects metaregressions estimated by restricted maximum likelihood, with setting-clustered CR2 standard errors and Satterthwaite tests (Table S40). The latter summarize contextual variation in groupspecific estimates, complementing the individual-level moderation models. Contextual comparisons were exploratory and based on a limited number of independent settings.

As an additional exploratory analysis, we examined cumulative conflict years and peace years separately as setting-level moderators. For each of the three outcomes and four victimhood beliefs, one focal victimhood-by-duration interaction was fitted per model (24 models in total). Models retained all four group-mean-centered victimhood indicators, the focal indicator's group mean, age, gender, ingroup identification, a group random intercept, and an uncorrelated random slope for the focal indicator. Each duration measure was standardized across the ten distinct settings, with settings weighted equally. We used setting-clustered CR2 standard errors and Satterthwaite-adjusted tests; Holm adjustments across the four interactions within each moderator-outcome combination and across all 24 duration tests were examined as multiplicity sensitivity analyses (Table S44). Regional fatalities and violent-event counts were not included in these setting-level analyses.

The group-specific equality coefficients for human-level inclusion ranged from β = .276 to .388 across post-conflict groups. Among active-conflict groups, they were smaller among Thai Buddhists (β = .039), Malay Muslims (β = .177), and Ukrainians (β = .177), alongside stronger positive associations elsewhere (Figure 3; Table S38). The complementary two-stage metaregression yielded a similar conflict-stage difference for equality, Δβ = -.132, CR2 SE = .034, p = .005; Holm-adjusted p = .022 (Table S40). Two competitive-victimhood contrasts for aggression reached nominal significance in the two-stage analyses (p = .027 and p = .034), but neither remained significant after Holm adjustment.

Relative Group Power

Conflict-period relative power moderated the association between conflict-specific competitive victimhood and equality-policy support. The within-group association was more negative among higher-than lower-power groups (high minus low: Δβ = -.200, CR2 95% CI [-.310, -.091], p = .003; Holm-adjusted p = .013; Table S43). The estimated conditional slopes were β = .093 among lower-power groups and β = -.107 among higher-power groups.

Descriptive group-specific estimates further illustrate this contrast. Among groups classified as relatively lower-power during the focal conflict, conflict-specific competitive victimhood was positively associated with equality-policy support among Bosniaks in Bosnia (β = .125) and Albanians in Kosovo (β = .114). Among relatively higher-power groups, the association was negative among Turks (β = -.312), Hindu Indians (β = -.181), and Jewish Israelis (β = -.109). However, Thai Buddhists, also classified as higher-power, showed a positive association (β = .235), illustrating variation within the power categories (Figure 3; Tables S24 and S41).

Two other interactions reached nominal significance: conflict-specific competitive victimhood with reconciliation, Δβ = -.180, 95% CI [-.348, -.012], p = .039, and global competitive victimhood with equality, Δβ = -.124, 95% CI [-.246, -.003], p = .046. Neither remained significant after Holm adjustment (adjusted p = .156 and p = .139, respectively). No interaction involving survey-period power reached the nominal significance threshold (Table S43).

The two-stage meta-regression likewise indicated a more negative conflict-specific competitive victimhood-equality association in higher-than lower-power groups, Δβ = -.147, CR2 SE = .049, p = .020; the Holm-adjusted p value was .079 (Table S40). Thus, the individuallevel interaction remained statistically significant after multiplicity adjustment, whereas the corresponding two-stage estimate did not.

Conflict Duration

Across 24 separately estimated exploratory duration-moderation models, none of the 12 interactions involving cumulative conflict years reached the nominal .05 threshold with settingclustered CR2 inference. For peace years, the within-group association between human-level inclusive victimhood and intergroup-equality-policy support was more positive at higher values of the contextual indicator (Δβ = .053, CR2 95% CI [.021, .085], p = .005; Holm-adjusted p = .021 within the four-interaction family). The interaction did not remain significant after Holm adjustment across all 24 duration tests (adjusted p = .127), and no other peace-years interaction reached nominal significance (Table S44). Peace years equaled zero in all five active-conflict settings and was positive in all five post-conflict settings, so this contrast cannot separate differences in conflict stage from elapsed time since conflict cessation.

Discussion

The present study examined how four collective victimhood beliefs relate to reconciliation, intergroup equality, and aggression-related policy support across 14 ethnonational groups in 10 conflict-affected societies. Both conflict-specific and global competitive victimhood were positively associated with aggression-related policy support, whereas both forms of inclusive victimhood were positively associated with reconciliation and equality. Beyond these preregistered associations, the findings highlight differences within each family of beliefs.

Human-level inclusive victimhood showed stronger positive associations with reconciliation and equality and a more negative association with aggression than parity-based inclusion. Global competitive victimhood also showed more consistent associations across groups than conflictspecific competitive victimhood. These patterns suggest that the political relevance of collective victimhood depends not only on whether a belief is competitive or inclusive, but also on what kind of comparison or recognition it expresses.

Differentiating Inclusive and Competitive Victimhood

Inclusive Victimhood: Parity in Suffering and Shared Humanity

The clearest theoretical distinction concerns two ways of recognizing victimization across group boundaries. Parity-based inclusive victimhood entails a judgment that the ingroup and its conflict opponent suffered comparably. Human-level inclusive victimhood instead affirms victims' shared human standing while explicitly allowing differences in their experiences of victimization. Recognizing another group's suffering in this way need not entail agreement about the relative magnitude of harm. This distinction is particularly relevant where accounts of victimization are asymmetric or contested: acknowledgment of an adversary's suffering can be conceptually separated from claims that the two groups suffered equally.

The empirical patterns corresponded to this distinction. Human-level inclusive victimhood was more strongly associated with reconciliation and equality policy support than parity-based inclusion, and its association with aggression-related policy support was negative across all 14 groups. Parity-based inclusion showed smaller positive pooled associations with reconciliation and equality, greater variation across groups, and no pooled association with aggression. These findings extend research linking inclusive victim consciousness to constructive intergroup orientations (Vollhardt & Bilali, 2015) by distinguishing recognition of another group's victimhood from a judgment of equivalent suffering. They also complement work on different referents and interpretations of inclusive victimhood (Cohrs et al., 2015; Rupar et al., 2022).

The distinction has implications for the political outcomes examined here. Support for peaceful coexistence does not necessarily entail support for equal treatment, opportunities, or representation. Yet human-level inclusion was associated with both reconciliation and equality, suggesting that recognizing victims across group boundaries may be relevant not only to intergroup relations but also to judgments about how groups should be treated politically. Its negative association with aggression further distinguishes it from parity-based inclusion. One possible interpretation is that acknowledging common human standing provides a basis for political consideration of an adversary without requiring agreement about whose suffering was greater. This interpretation is related to work on identification with all humanity and equal concern for ingroup and outgroup members (McFarland et al., 2012), although the belief examined here is specifically anchored in experiences of victimization rather than humanity as a general identity.

Competitive Victimhood: The Scope of Comparison

The two competitive beliefs also showed distinct patterns. Global competitive victimhood, expressed as a claim that no other group has suffered as much as the ingroup, was positively associated with aggression-related policy support and negatively associated with reconciliation and equality in most groups. This pattern is consistent with research connecting competitive and non-inclusive victimhood to conflict-enhancing intergroup orientations (Noor et al., 2012; Kljajić et al., 2026). A claim of exceptional suffering that extends beyond the immediate adversarial relationship may accompany a broader understanding of the ingroup as uniquely entitled to recognition or protection, with implications for judgments about accommodation and equality.

Conflict-specific competitive victimhood also showed positive pooled associations with aggression, but its associations with reconciliation and equality varied more substantially across groups, including differences in direction. Comparisons with a particular opponent may be especially sensitive to how the parties interpret the focal conflict, assign responsibility, and situate their respective experiences within collective narratives (Bilali & Vollhardt, 2019). In contrast, the global item expresses an especially strong claim of exceptional suffering. The two indicators thus differ in wording and claim strength as well as comparison referent, which is relevant when interpreting their contrasting patterns. Together, the findings support examining the substantive content of competitive beliefs rather than treating competitive victimhood as a uniform orientation.

Cross-Group Variation and Conflict Context

The associations between victimhood beliefs and policy support varied in magnitude across ethnonational groups, even when their directions were similar. This heterogeneity is compatible with the view that beliefs about collective suffering acquire meaning within particular histories of violence, political relationships, and narratives of responsibility (Bilali & Vollhardt, 2019). The group profiles illustrate this contextual variation. Ukrainian respondents reported relatively high conflict-specific competitive victimhood and low parity-based inclusion during the full-scale war with Russia, a setting in which comparisons with the adversary take place amid ongoing violence. Wartime narratives and judgments about territorial concessions provide relevant background for interpreting such comparisons (Bachleitner, 2025). Relatively high competitive and low parity-based inclusion also appeared among Kosovo Albanians, Bosniaks in Bosnia, and Croats, suggesting that adversarial comparisons can remain salient after armed conflict has ended. Historical narratives of victimization, victory, and responsibility remain relevant to political identities in these settings (Boduszyński & Pavlaković, 2019; Demirel, 2023).

Southern Thailand offered a different descriptive pattern: both Malay Muslim and Thai Buddhist samples had relatively high group means on conflict-specific competitive and inclusive victimhood. The region's history of ethno-religious conflict and intercommunal coexistence provides a context in which acknowledgment of ingroup and outgroup suffering may both be salient (Askew, 2009; Mohamad, 2018). These aggregate patterns suggest that relatively high levels of competitive and inclusive beliefs can be observed in the same populations; they do not establish that individual respondents endorsed both. Across the examples, differences in sampling and measurement also limit direct comparisons of group levels. Their principal value is to illustrate variation in how the beliefs are expressed across settings.

Exploratory contextual analyses offered more specific, though necessarily limited, evidence about this variation. The association between human-level inclusive victimhood and equality-policy support was weaker in settings classified as active-conflict than in those classified as post-conflict, a difference also observed in the complementary two-stage analysis.

One possibility is that immediate security concerns and adversarial political demands affect how recognition of another group's suffering relates to support for equality-oriented policies. The corresponding moderation of reconciliation was not statistically reliable, however, and the active-conflict category included diverse patterns. The findings therefore point to a potentially relevant distinction between recognizing shared humanity and supporting equality under different conflict conditions, rather than to a uniform contextual effect.

Relative group power was also relevant to one exploratory association. Conflict-specific competitive victimhood was more negatively associated with equality-policy support among groups classified as relatively higher-power during the focal conflict than among lower-power groups. This contrast survived multiplicity adjustment in the individual-level moderation analysis; the two-stage estimate pointed in the same direction but did not survive that adjustment.

The pattern is compatible with the possibility that claims of greater ingroup suffering carry different political implications when they are expressed from positions of relative advantage or disadvantage, particularly for policies concerning intergroup equality. The evidence is exploratory, and power was classified using context-specific indicators that were not fully equivalent across conflicts. Other contextual comparisons offered less consistent evidence, leaving the sources of much between-group heterogeneity to be examined in future research.

Additional analyses of conflict duration yielded no clear moderation by cumulative conflict years. The more positive human-level inclusion-equality association at higher values of peace years was consistent with the conflict-stage contrast, but the two findings are not independent: peace years was zero in all active-conflict settings. The analyses therefore cannot establish that elapsed time since conflict cessation, rather than other differences between settings, accounts for the association. These exploratory patterns merit examination in a larger sample of conflict contexts.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

A strength of this study is its examination of distinct victimhood beliefs and political outcomes across multiple conflict-affected populations within a common analytic framework.

Considering reconciliation, equality, and aggression-related policy support separately revealed differences that would be obscured by a single index of constructive or conflict-enhancing attitudes. The combination of pooled and group-specific analyses also made it possible to identify recurring associations while examining how their magnitudes varied across groups.

Several limitations qualify these contributions. The cross-sectional design does not establish whether victimhood beliefs shape policy support, whether political orientations inform beliefs about suffering, or whether both reflect other psychological and contextual processes. Longitudinal and experimental studies could investigate changes in these relationships and test the effects of victimhood framings on political judgments. In addition, each victimhood belief was represented by a single item. The indicators differ not only in comparison referent but also in wording, the strength of their claims, and moral content. In particular, the human-level item combines recognition of victimization with an explicit appeal to shared humanity. Matched multi-item measures would help determine which of these features account for the observed differences in associations.

The conflict-related outcomes were conceptually harmonized but not identically composed in every setting. Some relied on one item and others on two; the aggression items also differed in temporal framing between active-and post-conflict settings. Data were collected predominantly through nonprobability online panels, whereas southern Thailand used face-toface interviews. Together with the absence of established measurement invariance, these features constrain interpretations of cross-group mean differences and population-level generalization.

Moreover, the study measured expressed policy support and aggression endorsement or justification, not observed political behavior. Future research could assess whether the distinctions extend to actual cooperation, collective action, voting, or responses to specific institutional reforms.

Finally, the contextual analyses drew on 14 ethnonational groups in 10 conflict settings, limiting precision at the level where conflict stage and relative power were classified. The power coding combined evidence about institutional access, wartime advantage, and interstate capabilities according to the focal dyad. Studies involving more conflict settings, independently evaluated classifications, and alternative indicators of power would allow stronger tests of contextual explanations. Such work would be especially informative if it examined how perceived asymmetries in suffering and responsibility intersect with structural power in shaping support for equality and reconciliation.

Conclusion

Collective victimhood beliefs were associated with distinct patterns of support for reconciliation, intergroup equality, and aggression-related policies across conflict-affected societies. The central contribution is the distinction between perceiving parity in the suffering of opposing groups and recognizing victims' shared human standing despite differences in their experiences. The latter showed stronger and more consistent associations with reconciliation and equality, alongside a negative association with aggression-related policy support. Within competitive victimhood, the scope and content of suffering comparisons also mattered.

Understanding these distinctions offers a more differentiated account of how interpretations of collective harm relate to political responses in conflict-affected societies.

Cumulative conflict years was defined as the number of distinct calendar years between 1946 and 2024 in which at least one state-based armed conflict was recorded for the setting, counting each year only once regardless of the number of recorded conflicts. Peace years was calculated as the number of years elapsed since the most recent recorded conflict year (2024 minus the last conflict year), with a value of zero assigned to settings experiencing recorded conflict in 2024.
