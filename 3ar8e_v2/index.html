Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Ethics Approval Check

Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, and it was present:

Before and after this sleep or wake delay, participants were presented with the scene images while psychophysiological arousal (SCR and HRD) and subjective emotional responses were recorded.

Fifty-two healthy young adults (9 male, 43 female, mean ± SD age = 19.5 ± 1.6 years) were recruited via the University of York’s online participant recruitment system and posters displayed on campus.

Participants were fluent English speakers and reported a regular sleep schedule (typically rising by 8 a.m. and obtaining at least 6 hours of sleep per night).

In line with pre-registered criteria, two participants were excluded because they obtained less than 30 min of sleep during the nap period, and one additional participant was excluded due to failure to follow task instructions.

Participants were shown sample negative images before providing consent.

Participants completed an experimental protocol designed to examine the influences of memory suppression and sleep-associated memory processing on emotional responses to previously learned scenes (see Figure 1).

Next, participants completed a Think/No-Think task where they were shown face reminder cues inside a green or red frame and were instructed to either recall (Think) or suppress (No-Think) the associated scene image, respectively.

After the delay, participants completed a final affect evaluation task.

Each pair was presented for 6 s and participants were instructed to form a vivid mental connection between the face and scene.

After this initial study round, participants completed two test rounds in which they were shown each face in isolation and were asked to recall the associated scene within 4 s.

After each trial, participants were shown the correct face-scene pair to reinforce their knowledge.

Participants were instructed to focus on the scene throughout the presentation window.

Participants were presented with the face cues from 32 of the learned face-scene pairs and instructed either to retrieve or suppress the associated scene, which was not shown.

Participants were shown each face in isolation for 3 s.

For green-framed faces, participants were instructed to retrieve and hold the associated scene image in mind for the entire time that the face was on the screen.

For red-framed faces, participants were instructed to prevent the associated scene image from entering awareness, without looking away from the face.

Participants were instructed to press the spacebar upon seeing a digital counter, which appeared at random intervals between 1 to 4 sec.

To confirm that participants were successfully engaging in memory suppression during the TNT task, we examined the change in intrusions (i.e., unsuccessful suppression attempts, collapsing across “briefly” or “often” responses on No-Think trials) across trial blocks.

Indeed, Arpaci et al. (2025) found that participants had reduced subjective valence and arousal after a nap, and Gujar et al. (2011) found reduced ratings of fearful and angry expressions.

research_questions There are a number of potential outcomes, which are each outlined below. Our planned analyses will allow us to test each of these hypotheses:

Independent operations: Memory suppression in wakefulness and memory consolidation in sleep reduce the affective tone of emotional memories independently. In this case, only non-suppressed memories should become less emotionally arousing over sleep (because memory suppression has already reduced the affective tone of suppressed memories).
Combined operations: Memory suppression in wakefulness and memory consolidation in sleep reduce the affective tone of emotional memories in an additive manner. In this case, both suppressed and non-suppressed memories should become less emotionally arousing over sleep.
Targeted operations: Memory consolidation in sleep reduces the affective tone of emotional memories that have been weakened via suppression in prior wakefulness. In this case, only suppressed memories should become less emotionally arousing over sleep.

study_design_overview There will be three main assessment points: (1) pre-suppression, (2) post-suppression, and (3) post-delay. At each assessment point, participants will view emotionally negative and neutral images while their psychophysiological responses are monitored (skin conductance responses [SCR] and heart rate deceleration [HRD]). They will also provide subjective arousal ratings for each image.

Preliminary analysis: First, we will confirm that suppressing emotional memories in wakefulness reduces the affective charge of those memories (see below for details of the suppression paradigm). For each participant, we will calculate the difference in mean emotional arousal between the pre-suppression and post-suppression assessment points (separately for each image and arousal measure described above). We will apply these affect change scores to 2 X 2 repeated measures ANOVAs, with the factors Image (Suppressed, Non-Suppressed [Baseline]) and Emotion (Negative, Neutral). We predict that an interaction will emerge, with post-hoc tests indicating that emotional affect is reduced for suppressed negative images relative to non-suppressed negative images. No such difference is expected to emerge between suppressed and non-suppressed neutral images.

Confirmatory analyses: Next, we will test the hypotheses outlined above. For each participant, we will calculate the difference in mean emotional arousal between the post-suppression and post-delay assessment points (separately for each image and arousal measure described above). We will apply the affect change scores for the emotionally negative images to 2 X 2 mixed ANOVAs, with the within-subjects factors Image (Suppressed, Non-Suppressed [Baseline]) and the between-subjects factor Delay (Nap, Wake). A different outcome is predicted by each of the hypotheses described above:

Hypothesis 1 (Independent Operations): Memory suppression in wakefulness and memory consolidation in sleep reduce the affective tone of emotional memories independently.

An interaction will emerge, with post-hoc tests indicating that emotional affect is reduced for non-suppressed negative images relative to suppressed negative images in the nap group, but not in the wake group.

Hypothesis 2 (Combined Operations): Memory suppression in wakefulness and memory consolidation in sleep reduce the affective tone of emotional memories in an additive manner.

A main effect of Delay will emerge, indicating that emotional affect is reduced for both suppressed and non-suppressed negative images in the nap group but not in the wake group.

Hypothesis 3 (Targeted Operations): Memory consolidation in sleep reduces the affective tone of emotional memories that have been weakened via suppression in prior wakefulness.

An interaction will emerge, with post-hoc tests indicating that emotional affect is reduced for suppressed negative images relative to non-suppressed negative images in the nap group, but not in the wake group.

Note that, under the targeted operations hypothesis, sleep-associated consolidation processes may reduce the affective tone of suppressed negative memories, even if the impact of wakeful suppression is not immediately evident prior to sleep. Hence, the prediction for hypothesis 3 holds irrespective of whether a significant interaction emerges in the preliminary analysis described above.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

The ability to retrieve memories of adverse events without fully reinstating their associated emotional charge is a hallmark of an adaptive memory system. This decoupling of emotional reactivity from episodic memory has been linked to two key processes: memory suppression during wakefulness and memory consolidation during sleep. Yet, the combined influences of wakeful suppression and sleep-associated memory processing on emotional affect during retrieval are unknown. We sought to address this gap in the current study. Participants learned to associate images of faces with emotionally negative and neutral scenes and then either suppressed or retrieved the scenes when presented with the faces as reminder cues. They then took a nap monitored with polysomnography (nap group) or remained awake for an equivalent period of time in the laboratory (wake group). Before and after the delay, emotional responses to the negative and neutral scenes were evaluated using self-report, skin conductance responses (SCRs) and heart rate deceleration (HRD) to index changes in emotional affect that were attributable to memory suppression and/or sleep-associated consolidation. Memory suppression had no impact on self-reported emotion, SCRs or HRD in either the nap or wake group. However, subjective emotional responses decreased significantly more after sleep than wake. These findings support a role for sleep-associated consolidation in reducing the affective salience of emotional memories.

Introduction

When recalling adverse events from our past, we often feel only a small fraction of the emotional charge that was present during the experience itself (Gibbons et al., 2011). This decoupling of emotional reactivity from our autobiographical histories is central to an adaptive memory system and ensures that we can retain important information without being consumed by negative emotions. Hence, failure to downregulate the emotional tone of unpleasant memories may contribute to perseverative thoughts and heighten the risk of affective disorders (Engen & Anderson, 2018).

The adaptive decoupling of emotions from memory has been linked to memory suppression; that is, intentionally stopping retrieval processes to avert thinking about a prior experience (Gagnepain et al., 2017). Evidence for such affective suppression has come from recent work using the Think/No-Think paradigm, which mimics scenarios where people suppress unwanted memories that are triggered by reminders (Anderson & Green, 2001). Here, participants learn cue-target pairs (e.g., a face associated with a scene image) and are later instructed to retrieve (Think) or to suppress (No-Think) the scene images when presented with face cues in isolation. Studies using this paradigm have shown that suppression reduces both the accessibility and affective charge of emotionally negative memories, as indicated by poorer recall and reduced self-reported negative emotion relative to a set of baseline images that were neither suppressed nor retrieved (Anderson & Green, 2001; Gagnepain et al., 2017; Harrington et al., 2021). The weakening of memory and emotional affect through suppression is thought to be orchestrated by the prefrontal cortex, which inhibits memory retrieval and emotional arousal via top-down inhibitory projections to the hippocampus and amygdala, respectively (Benoit et al., 2015; Gagnepain et al., 2017). Memory suppression mechanisms have been implicated in the trajectory of mental health conditions that are characterised by emotion dysregulation and intrusive thoughts (Catarino et al., 2015; Mary et al., 2020). For example, the disruptive effects of suppression on retrieval are reduced in individuals with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) relative to controls, consistent with a breakdown of top-down inhibitory control processes (Catarino et al., 2015). Likewise, among individuals exposed to serious psychological trauma, those with a PTSD diagnosis exhibit a breakdown of prefrontal regulatory activity during suppression, as compared to those without a PTSD diagnosis and non-trauma-exposed controls (Mary et al., 2020). More recent work has even demonstrated that memory suppression training delivered online leads to durable mental health benefits, with participants reporting less anxiety, negative affect and depression over extended intervals (Mamat & Anderson, 2023).

Sleep-associated memory processing constitutes another pathway through which emotional affect is thought to be decoupled from prior experience (Walker & van der Helm, 2009). While it has long been established that sleep supports the consolidation of newly formed memories (Lutz et al., 2025), recent perspectives suggest that sleep may have different effects on the mnemonic strength and emotional charge of aversive memories. A particularly influential theoretical account in this domain is the Sleep to Forget, Sleep to Remember (SFSR) framework, which proposes that sleep actively reduces the emotional tone of recent and unpleasant experiences while simultaneously strengthening the central episodic components (Walker & van der Helm, 2009). Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is considered central to this process due to its reduced noradrenergic profile, meaning memories can be reactivated at a time when arousal levels are low.

Support for the SFSR model comes from a growing body of evidence (van der Helm et al., 2011; Gujar et al., 2011; Hutchison et al., 2021). For example, van der Helm et al. (2011) found that a night of sleep (relative to a day of wakefulness) reduced self-reported feelings of negativity towards emotionally salient stimuli. This overnight decrease in subjective responses was accompanied by reduced amygdala reactivity to the same emotional images, with the extent of these changes linked to REM sleep physiology. More recently, Hutchison et al. (2021) found that selectively reactivating emotionally negative images with auditory cues during REM sleep, but not slow-wave sleep, decreased self-reported emotional affect when viewing those images again, consistent with a putative affective decoupling in emotional memory.

It is important to note, however, that some studies have observed sleep-associated increases in emotional reactivity during re-exposure to aversive imagery (Baran et al., 2012; Ashton et al., 2019; Jones & Spencer, 2019), suggesting that the consequences of sleep for emotional memory consolidation are not uniform. Moreover, the effects of sleep may vary not only in direction, but also across different measures of emotional reactivity (Lipinska et al., 2022). Studies assessing psychophysiological arousal, such as skin conductance responses (SCRs) and heart rate deceleration (HRD), have shown that sleep-associated changes in self-reported emotional affect are not always accompanied by corresponding changes in autonomic reactivity, or vice versa (Pace-Schott et al., 2011; Bolinger et al., 2018; Ashton et al., 2019), highlighting the importance of assessing multiple domains of emotional responding.

Extant evidence raises several hypotheses about the way in which wakeful suppression and sleep-associated processing may jointly influence the affective charge of aversive memories.

Recent work suggests that weakly encoded memories are more sensitive to sleep than strongly encoded memories, and thus receive an amplified benefit of overnight memory processing (Cairney et al., 2016; Denis et al., 2021; Schechtman et al., 2021). Our first hypothesis is that memories that are weakened through suppression during wakefulness may undergo targeted consolidation during later sleep, leading to an accumulated effect of emotional downregulation. Alternatively, memories which have already been processed during wakefulness may be less susceptible to further downregulation, with sleep prioritising non-suppressed memories. Our second hypothesis is that suppression would reduce emotional arousal for the targeted memories, but any additional emotional downregulation during sleep would occur only for non-suppressed memories. Another possibility is that sleep may operate unilaterally across suppressed and non-suppressed memories, reducing the affective charge of all adverse experiences. Our third hypothesis is that suppression and sleep have additive effects, and sleep downregulates emotional arousal for both suppressed and non-suppressed memories. We tested these pre-registered hypotheses 1 in the current study. Participants learned face-scene pairs in which the scenes were emotionally negative or neutral. They suppressed a subset of these images (using a Think/No-Think protocol) before taking a nap monitored with polysomnography or remaining awake for an equivalent period of time in the laboratory. Before and after this sleep or wake delay, participants were presented with the scene images while psychophysiological arousal (SCR and HRD) and subjective emotional responses were recorded. By examining the changes in psychophysiological and subjective affect across the delay for suppressed and non-suppressed images, we could determine whether the relative strength of emotional memories influences the affective consequences of sleep-associated memory processing.

Methods

Participants

Fifty-two healthy young adults (9 male, 43 female, mean ± SD age = 19.5 ± 1.6 years) were recruited via the University of York's online participant recruitment system and posters displayed on campus. Exclusion criteria included any history of serious head injury or neurological, psychiatric, or sleep disorder, current or past employment in emergency services, the armed forces, healthcare, or any existing role involving alternating shift patterns. Participants were fluent English speakers and reported a regular sleep schedule (typically rising by 8 a.m. and obtaining at least 6 hours of sleep per night). Following standard procedures in our laboratory (Cairney et al., 2018; Guttesen et al., 2024; Petzka et al., 2023), they were instructed to refrain from consuming alcohol and caffeine for 48 and 24 hours before the study, respectively. In line with pre-registered criteria, two participants were excluded because they obtained less than 30 min of sleep during the nap period, and one additional participant was excluded due to failure to follow task instructions. Our sample comprised N=49 participants (nap group: N=23, 5 males, 18 females, 19.41 ± 1.34 years, wake group: N=26, 4 males, 22 females, 19.72 ± 1.93 years). Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Department of Psychology at the University of York. Participants were shown sample negative images before providing consent. They were compensated with either a £30 Amazon voucher or BSc Psychology experimental participation credit.

Procedure

Participants completed an experimental protocol designed to examine the influences of memory suppression and sleep-associated memory processing on emotional responses to previously learned scenes (see Figure 1).

Fig 1. Schematic of experimental procedure. Participants first completed a learning phase, where they formed associations between neutral faces and neutral or negative scenes. They then completed an affect evaluation task where the scenes were presented in isolation and subjective ratings, skin conductance responses (SCRs) and electrocardiography (ECG; for heart rate deceleration analyses) were recorded. Next, participants completed a Think/No-Think task where they were shown face reminder cues inside a green or red frame and were instructed to either recall (Think) or suppress (No-Think) the associated scene image, respectively. They completed another affect evaluation task before taking a 95-min nap with polysomnography (nap group) or remaining awake and watching nature documentaries (wake group). After the delay, participants completed a final affect evaluation task.

Learning

First, participants studied 48 face-scene pairs. Each pair was presented for 6 s and participants were instructed to form a vivid mental connection between the face and scene. After this initial study round, participants completed two test rounds in which they were shown each face in isolation and were asked to recall the associated scene within 4 s. Participants indicated whether or not they could bring the associated scene to mind and, if so, were prompted to identify the correct scene from three options (the correct scene and two foils drawn from other face-scene pairs). Once a scene was correctly identified, it was excluded from subsequent test trials within that round; this process continued until all images had been correctly identified. After each trial, participants were shown the correct face-scene pair to reinforce their knowledge.

Affect Evaluation Participants then completed the first Affect Evaluation task. The task included 48 scene images from face-scene pairs, 12 scenes used for practice, and eight additional filler scenes, resulting in 68 scenes in total. Scenes were viewed one after another in isolation (i.e, without the accompanying face) and each one appeared once for 6.5 s. Participants were instructed to focus on the scene throughout the presentation window. The screen then went blank for 2 s, after which participants rated how the image made them feel using a 9-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("completely unhappy, annoyed, unsatisfied, melancholic, despaired, or bored") to 9 ("completely happy, pleased, satisfied, contented, and hopeful"). They were instructed to make this response as quickly as possible (trial time out = 15 s). After each rating, a 5-second blank screen appeared and this was followed by a fixation cross of 0.5 s, 1 s, 1.5 s, or 2 s in duration. SCRs and ECG were monitored throughout the affect evaluation phase.

Think/No-Think (TNT) Phase Memory suppression was evaluated using the TNT task. Participants were presented with the face cues from 32 of the learned face-scene pairs and instructed either to retrieve or suppress the associated scene, which was not shown. The remaining 16 pairs were not re-presented during this phase and served as baseline items (see data analysis). Participants were shown each face in isolation for 3 s. Critically, the faces were framed in either green (Think condition) or red (No-Think condition). For green-framed faces, participants were instructed to retrieve and hold the associated scene image in mind for the entire time that the face was on the screen. For red-framed faces, participants were instructed to prevent the associated scene image from entering awareness, without looking away from the face. They were told to actively block the image, pushing it out of mind if it did enter conscious awareness, and not replace it with another thought or image. After each trial, participants reported how much they thought about the associated scene image using a 3-point scale: "never," "briefly," or "often." For Think trials, "often' responses indicated successful retrieval of the associated scene. For No-Think trials, "never" indicated successful memory suppression, whereas "briefly" and "often" responses indicated failed suppression attempts (referred to as intrusions). The TNT phase comprised five blocks, each with 64 trials (16 Think: 8 negative and 8 neutral, 16 No-Think: 8 negative and 8 neutral), with each face presented twice per block (10 repetitions per face in total). Stimuli were presented in pseudorandom order, with each face cue appearing twice and the two presentations of the same face separated by at least three trials. Short breaks (~1 min) were provided between blocks to minimise fatigue. The TNT phase lasted ~40 min in total.

Stimuli

The stimuli comprised 48 scene images (24 negative and 24 neutral) selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS; Lang, Bradley, & Cuthbert, 2005). Forty-eight emotionally neutral face images served as reminder cues in the TNT task. The faces had neutral expressions, with half depicting men and half depicting women. Each scene was paired with a face, and these pairs were fixed. For each participant, the face-scene pairs were randomly assigned to one of three conditions (Think, No-Think, or Baseline) resulting in 16 pairs per condition. Twelve additional pairs (6 negative, 6 neutral) were used as practice trials. The affect evaluation task also included 8 filler scenes, resulting in 68 scenes in total. Condition assignments were counterbalanced across participants using three predefined sets.

Alertness Measurements

A 5-min psychomotor vigilance task was administered at three timepoints: 1) before the learning phase, 2) after the TNT task, and 3) after sleep or wake delay. Participants were instructed to press the spacebar upon seeing a digital counter, which appeared at random intervals between 1 to 4 sec. Vigilance was assessed using median Reaction Time (RT).

The Stanford Sleepiness Scale (Hoddes et al., 1973) was administered at the beginning and at the end of the experiment to measure sleepiness. Participants rated their current state on a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 was "Feeling active, vital, alert, or wide awake" and 7 was "No longer fighting sleep, sleep onset soon; having dream-like thoughts".

Equipment

Behavioural Tasks The Learning Phase and TNT task was programmed and run using Presentation version 24.0 (Neurobehavioral Systems, Albany, CA), and the Affect Evaluation Task was delivered using E-Prime 3.0 (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA). All tasks were presented on a flat-screen monitor, and responses were collected via a standard keyboard.

Psychophysiological Recording Psychophysiological data were recorded using a BIOPAC MP36R system and processed with AcqKnowledge 5.0 software. Stimulus onset was synchronised with physiological recording using square pulse outputs transmitted from E-Prime to the BIOPAC MP36R system via an STP35A interface, allowing temporal alignment between stimulus presentation and psychophysiological responses. Electrodermal activity was recorded with two BIOPAC EL507 electrodes. They were attached to the index and middle fingers of the non-dominant hand. Data were sampled at 2000 Hz and then downsampled to 62.5 Hz. A median smoothing filter (63-sample window) was applied to remove transient spikes. A 1 Hz low-pass FIR filter was applied to reduce high-frequency noise. Smoothing baseline removal method with baseline estimation window of 5 s was applied to the tonic EDA waveform to construct a phasic EDA waveform. SCRs were detected by event-related analysis in AcqKnowledge. They were identified as an increase in electrical conductance ≥ 0.01 µS within 1-6 seconds after stimulus onset. SCR frequency was calculated as the percentage of trials that met this threshold. HRD was derived from ECG. Two BIOPAC EL503 electrodes were placed in a standard Lead II configuration (right clavicle and lower left rib). ECG was sampled at 2000 Hz and converted to interbeat intervals using AcqKnowledge. For each trial, heart rate (in beats per minute, BPM) was segmented into five consecutive one-second time bins covering 1 s before stimulus onset to 4 s after onset. The pre-stimulus bin served as a baseline. HRD was calculated as the maximum decrease in heart rate relative to baseline across the post-stimulus interval. Polysomnography Naps were recorded with an Embla NDx Polysomnography system using RemLogic 3.4 software. EEG data were monitored using electrodes attached to two scalp locations (C3 and C4). They were referenced to the contralateral mastoid (A1 or A2). Eye movements were monitored using left and right electrooculography electrodes, and muscle tone was monitored using three electromyography electrodes located on the chin. A ground electrode was placed on the forehead. Electrode locations on the scalp and face were cleaned with NuPrep exfoliating agent. Then, gold-plated electrodes were applied using EC2 electrode cream. Data were sampled at 256 Hz, and electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ. The acquired data were scored in 30 s epochs as wakefulness, N1, N2, slow-wave sleep (SWS) and REM sleep using AASM criteria (Iber et al., 2007).

Data Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 31). Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared (η²) for ANOVA models.

Intrusions Consistent with previous studies, "briefly," or "often" responses on the TNT task were treated as "intrusions" (i.e., where a scene entered awareness despite the participant's efforts to suppress it; Benoit et al., 2015 , Nardo & Anderson, 2024). A reduction in intrusions across repeated suppression attempts implies that participants became increasingly successful at preventing scenes from entering awareness. For this purpose, we calculated the percentage of No-think trials which were rated as "briefly" or "often", separately for each TNT block. A repeated measures ANOVA with Block (1-5) as a within-subject factor was run to analyse the change in intrusions across blocks.

Affective Change Scores Emotional responses to scenes during the affect evaluation tasks were assessed using subjective affect ratings and psychophysiology (SCR and HRD). Mean responses were calculated for each measure, separately for each participant, session and scene type (negative or neutral; suppressed or baseline). To assess changes in emotional affect arising from memory suppression, we subtracted mean responses in the pre-suppression affect evaluation task from mean responses in the post-suppression affect evaluation task, for each scene type (referred to hereafter as TNT-related change). To assess changes in emotional affect that emerged across the sleep or wake delay, we subtracted mean responses in the post-suppression affect evaluation task from mean responses in the post-delay affect evaluation task (referred to hereafter as delay-related change). For both change intervals, the direction of affective change was interpreted separately for each measure: positive change scores reflected more positive valence ratings, negative change scores reflected reduced SCRs, and positive HRD change scores reflected lower cardiac deceleration. To account for individual differences in emotional reactivity, changes in subjective ratings were proportionalised by dividing them by responses at the earliest assessment point (e.g., TNT-related change scores were divided by affect ratings in the pre-suppression task) (Harrington et al., 2021). Changes in SCR and HRD were not proportionalised because their values were sometimes at zero, rendering proportional change undefined (Harrington et al., 2021).

Effect of Suppression on Affective Responses

To examine the immediate effect of suppression on emotional reactivity, TNT-related change scores were applied to a 2 (TNT condition: Suppressed, Baseline) × 2 (Valence: Negative, Neutral) mixed ANOVA, separately for subjective affect ratings, SCRs, and HRD.

Interaction Between Suppression and Sleep To test the joint influences of suppression and sleep on emotional reactivity, delay-related change scores were applied to a 2 (TNT condition: Suppressed, Baseline) × 2 (Group: Nap, Wake) mixed ANOVA, separately for subjective affect ratings, SCRs, and HRD. As pre-registered, these analyses focused on negative scenes.

Results

To confirm that participants were successfully engaging in memory suppression during the TNT task, we examined the change in intrusions (i.e., unsuccessful suppression attempts, collapsing across "briefly" or "often" responses on No-Think trials) across trial blocks. Because suppression is thought to reduce accessibility of the underlying representation, rendering memories less intrusive, intrusions should decrease with successive suppression attempts (Levy & Anderson, 2012; Gagnepain et al., 2017; Harrington et al., 2021). Indeed, intrusions decreased significantly across TNT blocks (F(4, 188) = 23.14, p < .001, η² = .33), indicating that participants became more successful at preventing unwanted scenes from entering awareness (see the Supplementary Material for further details of this analysisfoot_1 ).

We next examined whether suppression also induced a change in emotional affect. TNT-related change scores were applied to a 2 (TNT condition: Suppressed, Baseline) × 2 (Valence: Negative, Neutral) ANOVA. For subjective ratings, we further excluded N = 2 participants due to missing data (session file overwritten). There was no main effect of TNT condition, (F(1, 46) = 0.429, p = .516, η² = .009), nor a TNT condition × Valence interaction, (F(1, 46) = 0.541, p = .466, η² = .012). A significant main effect of Valence (F(1, 46) = 7.076, p = .011, η² = .133) indicated that changes in emotional responses were more positive for negative images than neutral images.

For SCRs, we further excluded N = 3 participants due to poor EDA signal (no SCRs observed during initial exposure). There was no main effect of TNT condition (F(1, 45) = 1.931, p = .171, η² = .041), or Valence (F(1, 45) = 0.379, p = .541, η² = .008). However, a significant TNT condition x Valence interaction was observed (F(1, 45) = 6.075, p = .018, η² = .119). Follow-up paired-samples t-tests indicated that arousal responses for suppressed neutral images increased to a greater extent than those for baseline neutral images (t(45) = 2.807, p = .007, d = 0.414). SCR change scores for baseline and suppressed negative images did not differ significantly (t(46) = 0.311, p = .756, d = 0.046). This interaction showed an unexpected increase in SCRs for suppressed neutral images, but no effect for negative images, which were the main focus of this study. For HRD, we further excluded N = 9 participants due to poor ECG signal (movement artifacts affected heartbeat detection). There was no main effect of Suppression, (F(1, 39) = 0.101, p = .752, η² = .003), no main effect of Valence (F(1, 39) = 0.135, p = .716, η² = .003) and no interaction (F(1, 39) = 1.587, p = .215, η² = .039).

We also tested whether vigilance and sleepiness differed across the conditions and experimental sessions. Vigilance was assessed via median response times (RTs) in the psychomotor vigilance task (PVT). We excluded N=1 participant whose median RT (850.73 ms) was >3 SD above the group mean (300.88 ms). A 3 (Session: Pre-Suppression, Post-Suppression, Post-Delay) × 2 (Group: Nap, Wake) mixed ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Session (F(2, 82) = 12.35, p < .001, η² = .232). Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons demonstrated that RT was slower at post-TNT compared to pre-TNT (p < .001), and faster at post-delay compared to post-TNT (p < .001). RT at post-delay did not differ from RT at pre-TNT (p = 1.000). There was no main effect of Group (F(1, 41) = 0.089, p = .768, η² = .002), and no interaction between Group and Session (F(2, 82) = 0.519, p = .597, η² = .012).

Sleepiness was assessed via scores on the Stanford Sleepiness Scale using a 2 (Session: Pre-Suppression, Post-Delay) × 2 (Group: Nap, Wake) mixed ANOVA. There was no main effect of Session, (F(1, 46) = 2.632, p = .112, η² = .054) or Group, (F(1, 46) = 3.293, p = .076, η² = .067). However, a significant Session x Group interaction (F(1, 46) = 6.016, p = .018, η² = .116) emerged, with Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons showing that sleepiness increased significantly between sessions in the wake group but not in the nap group (p = 0.009).

Discussion

Prior work has shown that both suppression and sleep might affect the emotional intensity of episodic memories (Gagnepain et al., 2017; Harrington et al., 2021; van der Helm et al., 2011; Hutchison et al., 2021). Research to date has not yet determined whether these processes interact to shape emotional memory processing. We addressed this gap in the present study. Three hypotheses were considered. First, given evidence that sleep can preferentially process weaker memories (Cairney et al., 2016; Denis et al., 2021; Schechtman et al., 2021), sleep may produce a greater attenuation in emotional responses to memories that had been previously weakened by suppression. Second, sleep may target those memories that have not been suppressed before, because suppression may already reduce the emotional salience of suppressed memories. Third, sleep may equally reduce responses to both suppressed and non-suppressed memories, affecting memories regardless of prior suppression.

Our results provide partial support for the third possibility. Participants who napped showed a greater reduction in negative responses to emotionally aversive images than participants who remained awake. There was no evidence that prior suppression amplified or limited the subsequent effect of sleep-associated consolidation processes. These results suggest that sleep influenced the affective charge of emotional memories in a generalised manner, regardless of whether they had been previously suppressed. However, this effect was limited to subjective ratings and was not observed in SCRs and HRD. Memory suppression partly operated as expected, with intrusions decreasing across TNT blocks as participants became increasingly successful at preventing the associated memories from entering awareness. This is consistent with previous findings that suppression reduces intrusions (Benoit et al., 2015; Gagnepain et al., 2017; Harrington et al., 2021). However, memory suppression itself did not attenuate emotional responses to negative scenes. This finding is contrary to previous studies (Gagnepain et al., 2017; Harrington et al., 2021). Gagnepain et al. (2017) demonstrated that retrieval suppression can engage prefrontal control over hippocampal regions involved in memory retrieval and amygdala regions involved in emotional reactivity. Their findings suggest parallel modulation of memory and emotional processing, rather than emotional change being a consequence of reduced retrieval. In the present study, the only suppression effect was associated with increase in SCRs to neutral scenes, but no significant effect emerged for negative scenes, which were downregulated by sleep. Future studies may consider whether more suppression trials result in a suppression-associated attenuation.

Turning to the effect of sleep, reduced subjective negativity after sleep relative to wake is consistent with the SFSR framework, which suggests that sleep reduces the emotional tone of negative memories (Walker & van der Helm, 2009). Interestingly, while a recent meta-analysis found that generally sleep was associated with increased subjective arousal, subgroup analyses revealed that nap studies were associated with emotional downregulation (Lipinska et al., 2022). Indeed, Arpaci et al. (2025) found that participants had reduced subjective valence and arousal after a nap, and Gujar et al. (2011) found reduced ratings of fearful and angry expressions. Thus, our data add to the growing evidence of nap-associated emotional attenuation.

Although the meta-analysis by Lipinska et al. (2022) suggested that subjective valence decreased after nap, but arousal increased after sleep in general, differences in sleep duration do not fully explain why the direction of the effects differs. Mixed findings have been reported for both nap and full-night paradigms (Davidson & Pace-Schott, 2021). For example, contrary to our findings, preserved negative subjective valence after a nap was found in Jones & Spencer (2019). For full-night paradigms, van der Helm et al. (2011) found that overnight sleep was associated with a reduction in both subjective arousal and amygdala reactivity. By contrast, Baran et al. (2012) demonstrated that negative valence and arousal were preserved after sleep. This inconsistency may also be due to differences in sleep architecture. (Lipinska et al., 2022). Previous literature suggests that REM sleep plays an important role in emotional memory processing and may modulate emotional responses to memories (Walker & van der Helm, 2009). However, the relationship between REM sleep and emotional reactivity has been inconsistent as well. Some findings support SFSR hypothesis demonstrating the attenuating role of REM sleep. For example, van der Helm et al. (2011) found that REM was associated with reduced emotional responses, and Hutchison et al. (2021) demonstrated that targeted reactivation of emotional memories during REM reduced subsequent arousal. In contrast, other studies support the opposite pattern. Baran et al. (2012) found that more REM sleep was associated with preservation of emotional responses, and Pace-Schott et al. (2011) demonstrated that occurrence of REM sleep was associated with less SCR habituation. Our exploratory analyses on REM sleep and changes in emotional responses (see Supplementary Materials) did not reveal any significant association. However, the present study was limited in its ability to test REM sleep effects; half of the participants did not reach REM sleep at all or had only a limited amount of REM sleep (< 10 min). Future studies with overnight sleep designs may provide more REM-rich sleep and allow testing of the role of REM more robustly.

Another factor that may contribute to variability across studies is the measure of emotional reactivity. In our study, the reduction in subjective negative affect after sleep was not accompanied by physiological change. The meta-analysis by Lipinska et al. (2022) found no effects of sleep on psychophysiological responses, but a significant reduction in subjective negativity following nap. Individual studies have also reported inconsistent results across different types of measurements. For example, while Ashton et al. (2019) found that sleep preserved HRD responses to negative images but had no significant effects on subjective responses and SCRs. Pace-Schott et al. (2011) observed greater reduction in SCRs across a nap, but no significant effects for subjective arousal or valence. This misalignment may potentially be explained by the fact that subjective ratings, SCRs and HRD may index different aspects of emotional reactivity. Subjective ratings reflect conscious evaluation of the emotional content of the scenes, whereas SCRs and HRD assess immediate autonomic responses. Furthermore, SCRs are associated with sympathetic arousal (Critchley, 2002), while HRD is linked to parasympathetic reactivity (Bradley et al., 2012). Thus, psychophysiological responses may not consistently change in parallel with subjective responses or with one another.

The aim of this study was to examine whether memory suppression and sleep-associated processing interact to regulate emotional responses to aversive memories. This experiment did not detect any evidence for this interaction. Although sleep was associated with a reduction in subjective negativity, this effect did not depend on whether memories had previously been suppressed. Prior suppression neither increased nor prevented sleep-related downregulation. Sleep appeared to reduce emotional responses in a more general manner, across both suppressed and non-suppressed memories. Thus, these findings suggest that sleep-associated emotional downregulation operated independently of prior suppression.
