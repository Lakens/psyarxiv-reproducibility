To improve Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

An Individual-Level Paradigm for Research on Deception Cues: An Urgent Proposal

How can you tell when someone is lying? This question has fascinated humans for millennia and empirical psychologists for over a century (Denault et al., 2022). Central to psychological research on the topic is the development and assessment of causal theories to explain what cues-behavioral indicators-can be used to distinguish lies from truths.

However, despite the many proposed theories and the extensive empirical work on deception cues, we are still far from a satisfying answering to the fundamental question of how to tell when someone is lying. The reason is that all theories of deception detection assume causal explanations at the level of the individual, yet almost all research on deception cues compares the behavior when lying or telling the truth at the group level.

At first glance, this preference for group-level research may seem inconsequential.

However, translating group-level findings to individual-level explanations is not as straightforward as one might think. Unless two conditions are met, described by the mathematical theorems of ergodicity, one cannot extrapolate findings on the group level to the individual level (Molenaar, 2004; Molenaar & Campbell, 2009). We will show that these conditions are not met for deception cues. Consequently, deception researchers' near exclusive use of group-level methodology says little about the underlying theories they are purporting to test. Rather, to appropriately test theories of deception cues, a wholly different, individuallevel, research paradigm is required.

Our aim with this article is to sketch a way forward to establish this new individuallevel approach to deception cue research. We begin by providing a non-technical description of the ergodic theorems and explain the limitations of using group-level research to test causal theories in psychology. We then discuss the implications of this for deception research and explain why the field needs an individual-level research agenda. Finally, we offer tractable steps towards this goal by outlining an individual level research agenda to deception cue research.

Several of the issues we raise regarding the problem of using group-level methodologies in deception research apply to many areas of psychology. For discussions on the broader implications of group-level methodologies for psychological research see Molenaar (2004), Molenaar and Campbell (2009), and Speelman and McGann (2026). Here we zero in on deception research, using it as a case study to illustrate the broader implications discussed elsewhere. In doing so, we aim to show the limitations of group-level research and the opportunities of complementing group-level research with individual-level methodologies. We focus on deception cue research because we believe group-level methodologies are particularly problematic in this field of enquiry. First, deception cues are likely highly sensitive to context (Markowitz & Hancock, 2019) and individual differences (e.g., Lane et al, 2014), complicating how group-level findings should be interpreted on the individual level. Second, decades of group-level research has failed to provide compelling tests of the underlying theoretical mechanisms posited as the causes of deception cues. It is, therefore, unsurprising that there is considerable disagreement between deception researchers on the validity of deception theories (Luke et al., 2025). In this paper, we argue that to move the field forward, a change of tack is required, and encourage researchers to adopt an individual-level approach to research on deception cues.

Background Ergodicity and its Consequences for Psychological Research

Research conducted at the group level focuses on interindividual variation. That is, differences between individuals. However, most psychological processes and causal theories are at the level of the individual, focusing on intraindividual variation-how individuals vary over time. In Figure 1, this distinction is schematically demonstrated using a Cattell Box (Cattell, 1952). The blue vertical slice represents a typical group-level study, where several individuals are measured on one occasion. The horizontal red slice represents an individuallevel study, where the same individual is measured on several occasions. A model or a process is said to be ergodic when one can apply quantitative conclusions on the group level to the individual level, or vice versa.

For a process to be ergodic, two conditions must be met (Molenaar, 2004; Molenaar & Campbell, 2009): Condition 1. Homogeneity across individuals: Ergodicity requires that each subject in the population must follow the same statistical model. As an example, consider a test of cognitive ability designed to measure a single latent factor of intelligence, in addition to two subfactors, verbal and numerical intelligence. To extrapolate from the group to the individual, this hierarchical factor structure and loadings must be identical for each subject in a population.

If, for some individuals, the factor structure of their test responses differs from the group-level structure, Condition 1 would not be met.

Condition 2. Stationarity across occasions:

Ergodicity requires that statistical models should be stationary across occasions; they should not vary over time or across settings.

Developmental processes, learning, and habituation are examples of psychological processes that vary over time and therefore would not fulfill Condition 2. Consider for instance how one can get used to a bad smell. How well your rating will match a group's average rating for how bad a smell is will depend on how long you have been exposed to it. Such habituation processes are non-ergodic: Different models are required to describe an individual's experience and the group average.

Schematic distinction between group-level and individual-level research on deception cues

When both Condition 1 and 2 are met, a process is ergodic, and one can directly draw inferences about the individual-level from findings at the group-level (and vice versa). An example of psychological processes that are reasonably ergodic are the gestalt principles of perception (Wertheimer, 1938). These principles are relatively stable across individuals and across time (though even the gestalt principles show some maturation effects; Furth & Mendez, 1963).

However, when one or both conditions are not met, a process is non-ergodic. In such situations, one cannot make valid inferences to the individual-level from research conducted at the group-level. This limitation has important consequences for the testing of psychological theories. If the theory to be tested functions at the level of the individual, and if the underlying process is non-ergodic, then group-level research is an inappropriate way to examine the proposed causal-mechanisms of the theory (Speelman & McGann, 2026). The error of conducting group-level research in such contexts is all too common in psychology. Speelman and McGann (2026) explain how this ultimately concerns issues around group aggregation. The logic behind group-level research is that by averaging across large samples, thereby accounting for random error and individual differences, researchers can get better estimates of the underlying psychological processes. However, this logic only holds if all individuals being tested follow the same underlying psychological process. If individuals in a group follow qualitatively different processes, aggregation becomes highly problematic and may say little or nothing about how the process works on an individual level. Mehoudar et al.'s (2014) study on the eye tracking patterns of facial processing provides a concrete example. On a group-level Mehoudar et al. replicated the well-known triangular shape pattern of facial processing, where an increase in fixations is shown for the areas around the eyes and nose (Althoff & Cohen, 1999 ; Yarbus, 1967). However, on the individual-level, people showed a diverse set of unique, yet stable, scanning patterns, that were largely inconsistent with the triangular shape observed when aggregating the data. This strongly indicates that these individuals were using qualitatively different strategies for the task of facial processing. This crucial finding could not have been discovered when using grouplevel data (Speelman and McGann, 2026). Examining theory and data in the deception literature, it is clear that deception research has also committed the error of conducting grouplevel research to test individual-level processes.

Implications for Deception Research

All causal theories of deception cues (that is, theories about how and why cues to deception should manifest) function at the level of the individual. Consider the popular cognitive load theory, which holds that lying is more cognitively demanding than truth telling, and that this can result in reliable deception cues such as delayed response times (Sporer, 2016).

Since cognitive load is a psychological process that occurs at the level of the individual, the causal explanation for this theory must also be at the individual level. The same applies to all deception theories, regardless of whether they focus on emotion (Ekman et al., 1988), arousal (de Turck & Miller, 1985), self-presentation (DePaulo et al., 2003), or any other mechanism.

The underpinning psychological processes of all such theories occur at the individual level.

On the other hand, the methodological paradigm that has dominated research on deception detection compares the behaviors of people telling the truth or lying at the group level. In a typical study, hypotheses (e.g., lies will be shorter than true statements) are derived from some explanatory mechanism at the individual level (e.g., fabricating is more cognitively demanding than remembering). The hypotheses are then tested using a between-group design where participants are asked to either lie or tell the truth about a certain event or topic (e.g., what they did at the weekend). Their behavior or statement is then coded on some cue of interest (e.g., statement length). The average degree to which this cue manifests when providing honest compared to deceptive statements is then compared.

As noted above, an implicit assumption of this group-level approach is that all participants follow the same underlying psychological process (Speelman & McGann, 2026).

In our example, this would mean that all people tested find remembering easier than fabrication (and at an identical magnitude) and that ease of production can be accurately measured by statement length. However, if some people follow a qualitatively different process (e.g., if some people find fabricating easier than remembering or if people find fabrication difficult to varying extents), any observed difference in group means will not be indicative of individual level patterns. A similar problem occurs if the process changes on different occasions or in different settings relevant to the hypothesis being tested (e.g., under some circumstances, fabrication is easier than remembering, and under other circumstances, the opposite is true). Thus, if either issue occurs, a between-subjects comparison such as this will not provide a test of the individual-level explanations underpinning the hypotheses of the study 1 . This problem should not be understated: If one posits a substantive hypothesis about individual-level mechanisms, one cannot test this hypothesis with group-level data unless the assumption of ergodicity holds.

Again, for group-level research to say something meaningful about individual-level processes, the two aforementioned conditions for ergodicity must be met. In the following section we show how untenable this claim is for deception cues.

Deception Cues Are Non-Ergodic

Many (if not most) psychological processes would fail to meet both conditions required for ergodicity. Deception cues are no exception. The potential influence of temporal effects and individual differences on deception cues is simply too great for them to be ergodic. To illustrate this, consider the deception cue statement detail-one of the most studied deception cues (DePaulo et al., 2003; Vrij et al., 2017). In this context, detail refers to how much granular information a person includes in their verbal statement. The statistical model typically used to characterize this cue (and most cues in the literature) is simply a (standardized) mean difference in detail between truthful and deceptive messages. Past research often finds that deceptive statements are less detailed than truthful statements at the group level (Amado et al., 2016; DePaulo et al., 2003). It is also useful to view mean differences through the framework of the general linear model. In this framework, such a mean difference is the slope in a regression model for a predictor representing veracity. Implied by the model is an intercept representing the mean level of detail (for truths, lies, or collapsed across both depending on how the veracity indicator is coded). Ergodicity requires that the slope and intercept of this model apply homogeneously across people and stationarily across occasions.

Thus, if we hypothesize that detail as a cue to deception is ergodic, the following would need to be true: All individuals give statements that are less detailed when lying, deviating from the same baseline with the same average magnitude (Condition 1) each time they tell a lie (Condition 2). This hypothesis of ergodicity seems implausible on its face, both on the basis of theoretical reasoning about the likely psychological processes involved and on the basis of data. For example, ergodicity would implicitly assume that all people are equally (un)able to generate sufficient compelling detail when they fabricate a story, but people likely vary widely in this capacity (Nahari & Pazuelo, 2015; Nahari & Vrij, 2014).

Beyond reasoning about the plausibility of ergodicity, we can examine data from past studies to empirically assess whether the requirements are met. We stress that these analyses are not meant as a criticism of the studies from which the data has been taken or the researchers who have done the good work of collecting these data. Rather, they are simply used to further examine our claim that deception cues are non-ergodic. In all cases except Luke (2015), the analyses reported here do not address the same statistical hypotheses examined in the original studies. In sum, existing data provide compelling evidence that group-level models of the deception cues statement length and detail do not apply homogeneously across individuals and are not stationary across occasions. We can think of no compelling arguments that other deception cues would not also fail to meet the two conditions of ergodicity. Here, we have examined a limited set of the large body of datasets generated by deception researchers over the last century. However, in every dataset we have looked at that has been suitable to test the assumptions of ergodicity, the assumptions have been clearly violated.

The Need for an Individual-Level Approach

We have shown that deception cues are almost certainly non-ergodic. Thus, the grouplevel conclusions from the previous century of deception cue research cannot be trusted to apply at the individual level. To draw meaningful conclusions about deception cues and their underlying psychological processes, an individual-level research agenda must be adopted.

As an example, consider the common finding that many cues appear to have a nearzero association with deception at a group level (Luke, 2019). However, the analyses reported above suggest that the diagnostic value of a single cue may vary considerably across people and occasions. The aggregate finding that a given cue is weakly associated with deception may be a poor description of many individuals: for some people the cue may be positively related to deception, for some people negatively, while others may show no relation at all.

Additionally, the relationships between cues may systematically vary across individuals (e.g., the same basic mental process might manifest with different behavioral indicators for different people). Group-level analyses typically reported in deception research simply cannot uncover such insights.

To draw more meaningful conclusions, a repeated measures design with appropriate analyses is required. Straightforward pervasiveness analyses (Speelman & McGann, 2026) show the value of adopting an individual-level approach. These results suggest that a single explanatory mechanism is insufficient since those who provide longer statements when lying cannot be accounted for by the proposed theoretical explanation claiming that remembering is easier than fabrication. In many cases a plurality of theoretical explanations may be required to best capture the pattern of deception cues at the individual level.

Further contributions to theory can come in the form of identifying general trends or "laws" at the individual level. There is a common misconception that only group-level research can uncover generalizable laws of behavior. However, through detailed research of individuals over time it is also possible to establish individual level laws, unlikely to be uncovered through group-level analysis (Robinson, 2011). Famous examples of attempts to establish individuallevel laws in the history of psychology include Ebbinghaus' forgetting curve (Ebbinghaus, 1913; Radvansky et al., 2022; Rubin & Wenzel, 1996) and the principles of operant conditioning (Murphy & Lupfer, 2014; Staddon & Cerutti, 2003).

Operant conditioning provides an apt example of the potential insights that may be realized from an individual-level research agenda on deception cues. According to operant conditioning, a positive reinforcer is a desired stimulus that, when presented after a behavior, will strengthen the behavior (Staddon & Cerutti, 2003). However, since people's preferences vary, what counts as a positive reinforcer will also vary between people. For some people chocolate may be a positive reinforcer, for others, cigarettes. Hence, although the general law is the same for all individuals, how it manifests at the individual level can look extremely different.

What if something similar occurs with deception cues? Consider again the claim that lying is more cognitively demanding than truth telling (Blandón-Gitlin et al., 2014; Zuckerman et al., 1981; but see Neequaye, 2022). Now consider the possibility that indicators of mental effort may vary between people. When experiencing mental effort, some people may rub their chin; others may close their eyes. If these individual differences are not taken into account, an analysis at the group level may result in near-zero associations between cues and deception, even if the general law that 'lying is more cognitively demanding than truth telling' is true.

Alternatively, group-level analyses may misleadingly suggest an association between some cues and deception on average, even if these relationships are complicated by person-level variation. For example, imagine a situation where a cue has a strong relationship with deception for a small minority, but the same cue has no relationship with deception for the majority (as in Figure 5). This pattern would produce a group-level effect indicating that, on average, the cue in question is a valid indicator of deception.

In a court case, judgments of whether a witness is lying do not concern witnesses in general, but one specific witness. A customs officer questioning a suspected smuggler is not concerned about smugglers in general, but the specific person they are questioning.

An employer suspicious over claims in a CV, is not interested in CVs in general, but the specific CV they are assessing. Non-ergodicity raises concerns about practical recommendations derived from group-level research on topics such as using checklists of verbal and nonverbal behavior to create composite scores of the likelihood of deception (Evans et al., 2013) or the use of the level of detail of a statement as an indicator of credibility (Verschuere et al., 2023). Variance across individuals and occasions renders such recommendations potentially misleading, for at least some people and situations. An individual-level research agenda will allow us to map the variances in patterns of deception cues across people and occasions. This will increase our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of deception detection tools. By identifying when, for whom, and why deception cues are valid, individual-level research can develop deception detection techniques that are more applicable to the individual case.

An Individual-Level Research Agenda

To provide a clearer sense of what exactly we are suggesting, it will be useful to sketch the contours of a possible individual-level research program, to provide some concrete examples of data collection paradigms, and to describe approaches to data analysis. Some of what follows is common among all fields seeking to study phenomena at an individual level (e.g., the need to take multiple measures from individuals), but the unique features of the deception cue research require specific considerations. Luke (2019) has argued that one particularly methodologically problematic feature of the deception cue literature is that virtually any behavior that differs between truthful and deceptive messages in any direction and with any magnitude can be framed as theoretically interesting, and if using recorded material, researchers are able to code an unlimited number of behaviors from the raw data.

Consequently, in contrast to other research areas, deception cue studies often feature exceptionally broad researcher degrees of freedom (Simmons et al, 2011). This flexibility may have interfered with the development of cumulative science and contributed to a literature characterized by frequently inconsistent and theoretically inconclusive results (Luke et al, 2025). This problem will persist even in an individual-focused paradigm, unless research is structured to address it. For this reason and others, we recommend an approach that encourages (if not requires) clear theoretical specification, to make hypotheses about mechanisms and cues explicit, without hampering the possibility of exploration (see, e.g., Guest & Martin, 2021).

A Model Program of Individual-Level Deception Cue Research

How could an individual-level program of research on cues to deception proceed? We propose four stages for such a program, focused on theory development and appraisal: 1.

Generation of a theoretical model of an underlying mechanism or mechanisms.

2. Identification of potential observable manifestations of the mechanism (i.e., potential deception cues related to the mechanism).

3. Empirical characterization of the individual variability in the identified cues as a function of the hypothesized mechanism 4. Empirical characterization of the effect of deception on the hypothesized cues These stages should be understood as a rough outline, rather than a strict and linearly sequential process, but we have arranged the stages such that they build on each other and rely on information collected in previous stages. Each stage also provides possibilities to make discoveries at each stage that falsify or set boundaries for the hypotheses under investigation.

Accordingly, the process must be iterative, with revisions to hypotheses necessitating revisitation to earlier stages. If individual-level cues to deception exist (and we believe they probably do), the proposed process will be capable of providing a theoretically and empirically grounded description of them.

As a first step, a researcher should develop a theoretical account of a psychological (and/or physiological) mechanism thought to be causally related to deception. That is, they should generate a causal model of psychological processes hypothesized to differ between the act of lying and telling the truth. Several such models already exist (e.g., models that assert that lying is more cognitively demanding than telling the truth) but have, in our view, not been empirically assessed in a framework capable of actually testing them. At this stage, it is important that researchers specify their theoretical models as explicitly and clearly as possible so that the hypothesis testing in subsequent stages can be oriented toward rigorously appraising Second, equipped with a theoretical account about a mechanism, the researcher can proceed to identify potential observable manifestations of the mechanism-that is, potential cues to deception related to the mechanism. Such identification can proceed in a variety of ways, and in many cases, there is likely to be previous research from which potential cues can be derived. Continuing with the theoretical claim that lying is more cognitively demanding than truth telling, researchers could, for example, use basic cognitive literature to identify measurable physiological and behavioral signs of heightened cognitive activity.

Third, the researcher should empirically characterize the individual variability in the identified cues as a function of the hypothesized mechanism. At this stage, it is not necessary to study the cues in relation to deception. Rather, the goal is to study the individual variability in the sensitivity and validity of the identified cues, across many people and under a variety of circumstances. For example, if blink rate is identified in the second stage as an observable manifestation of cognitive load, the third stage can assess individual variability in blink rate to more direct manipulations of cognitive load. This stage is primarily descriptive and exploratory, but it plays an important role in developing the foundation for informative hypothesis testing. Basic empirical description of individual variability is invaluable for assessing the plausibility of deception-related hypotheses, as well as the practical demands of studying a given cue. If it is found, for example, that the rate of blinking is related to cognitive load but that there is an enormous amount of individual variability in the extent to which cognitive activity affects the rate of blinking, these facts bear on the plausibility that and the generality with which deception might affect the rate of blinking. As part of this process of characterizing individual variability, it is important to search for potential homogeneous subsets of individuals who may, for example, show diverging patterns of behavioral manifestations of the psychological mechanism under investigation (e.g., subgroups of people who reliably manifest cognitive load with different patterns of behavior).

Fourth and finally, the researcher should proceed to empirically characterize the effect of deception on the hypothesized cues, making special effort to sample across any subgroups identified at the third stage. In contrast to earlier parts of the process, this stage is primarily aimed at hypothesis testing (though it does not preclude exploration). Assessing the effects of deception with consideration to possible subgroups is crucial for correctly identifying for whom the hypothesis holds and in what ways (e.g., if different subgroups manifest different cues). Importantly, at this stage (and all empirical stages of the research program), it is essential to take an approach to data collection that is suitable for individual-level analysis.

Empirical Approach

The basic approach for collecting data for individual-level research is a time series or repeated measures design, where the same individuals are repeatedly measured over a specified period, possibly under varying conditions (Barlow et al., 2009; Gregson, 1983; Molenaar & Campbell, 2009). Taking several measures from the same person enables a direct assessment of intraindividual changes, as well as interindividual profile differences. Though not common in deception cue research, this research design has a long history in psychology, in the domains of memory (Murre & Dros, 2015; Rubin & Wenzel, 1996), behaviorism (Carr & Durand, 1985; Morse & Skinner, 1958) and neuropsychology (Astafiev et al., 2004; Gryga et al., 2012; Olivo et al., 2022), among others. We draw on these well-established research areas to inform our proposal for an individual-level approach to deception research.

Below we outline two experimental procedures to examine deception cues using a repeated measures design. In the program outline above, these procedures are relevant to the fourth stage of the process, wherein a researcher may want to experimentally manipulate the veracity of messages to assess the effects of deception. These suggested procedures are, of course, not intended to cover all possible approaches to individual-level deception research, but rather, are meant to serve as jumping-off points for a new paradigm and to illustrate the basic design features that might be desirable in laboratory research on deception. The first approach is derived from the cue-word paradigm common in research on autobiographical memories (Crovitz & Schiffman, 1974; Harris et al., 2015). This highly controlled laboratorybased approach allows for greater reliability in measurement. However, this greater experimental control reduces the realism of the setting. To complement the cue-word paradigm, we also describe a design with a more interactive social context, since researchers have claimed that it is in the interaction of a dialogue that valid deception cues emerge (Burgoon & Buller, 1996). This more interactive setup is based on the speed-dating paradigm that has previously been used in deception research (Falkiewicz et al., 2015). These procedures represent two of many possible repeated measures designs to study cues to deceit. These are certainly not meant as the final word, but rather as an introduction to the general methodological shift we propose and to inspire researchers to consider procedures that might be useful for collecting large amounts of repeated measures data.

Cue-Word Paradigm

In a typical cue-word study, each participant performs several trials. In each trial participants are presented with a prompt-a word or phrase-and are required to briefly describe the first memory they recall in response to the prompt. Each trial uses a different word, and participants will often perform 30+ trials (Barzykowski & Staugaard, 2016; Crovitz & Schiffman, 1974). This basic setup can be repurposed for a deception context by adding a within-subjects manipulation of veracity, resulting in honest trials and deceptive trials. For honest trials, participants will honestly describe the autobiographical memories elicited by the prompts, as they would in a typical memory study. For deceptive trials, participants will be required to lie by describing an event that they have not experienced. This paradigm means that researchers can quite quickly and easily collect many true and false statements from the same participants. This design is also highly flexible. Depending on the goal of the research, the prompts can be changed to full sentences or phrased as questions. The design is also scalable, both in terms of number of participants and the number of statements from each participant.

To properly examine intraindividual variation, people should be assessed a large number of times.

New problems of course come with a repeated measures design, including fatigue and training effects. Fatigue can be mitigated by having sessions spread over days or weeks. As for training effects, we do not necessarily see this as a problem. Rather, we see it as another opportunity to gain new insights into deception cues. If, for instance, it is the case that deception cues weaken after practice with a task, surely this is of utmost interest for both theoreticians and practitioners.

Speed-Dating Paradigm

The second paradigm we propose is based on the speed-dating paradigm, introduced by Falkiewicz et al. (Falkiewicz et al., 2015). Participants are divided into pairs. Pairs of participants will play a party game designed for strangers to become more acquainted with each other. The game consists of cards, each of which contains one discussion topic concerning different personal information (e.g., music preferences). Participants are to discuss each topic together for approximately one minute. These one-minute discussions represent a trial. For each trial, participants are randomly assigned to either discuss the topic honestly or deceitfully.

Crucially, for each topic, participants will be blind to whether their partner is telling the truth or lying. As with the cue-word paradigm, this design is both flexible and scalable. The discussion topics can easily be changed to suit the research goal. For instance, if a researcher is interested in the interplay between emotion and deception, topics designed to elicit certain emotions, such as shame or embarrassment, could be used. More complicated designs with confederates would also be possible, opening possibilities for investigating other research questions. In terms of scalability, it is straightforward to increase the number of participants and the number of trials.

Analytic Approach

Past research on deception cues has used analytic techniques that have attempted to account for individual differences in different ways. For example, researchers have examined how individual differences, such as personality, may moderate the diagnosticity of deception cues (Lee et al., 2008; Riggio & Friedman, 1983; Turi et al., 2022) or have used within-subjects analyses comparing individuals' behaviors when lying to their 'baseline' behavior when telling the truth (Bogaard et al., 2023; Verigin et al., 2021; Vrij, 2016). Although these analyses give an impression of an individual-level approach, they are still conducted at the group-level.

Ultimately, these analyses still focus on between-group differences and interindividual variation. They do not, in any meaningful way, examine intraindividual variation required for a truly individual-level approach. In fact, the lack of several measurements for each participant in most studies on deception cues means that an analysis of intraindividual variation is not possible at all. Even in studies in which the same participants are assessed several times (as is the case in the data we examined above), conventional statistical approaches still do not provide insights on the individual level.

A methodological shift to an individual-level research agenda must move beyond the statistical techniques typically used. One highly useful approach is based on network modeling (Fried & Cramer, 2017). Network modeling is a flexible analytic approach that can be used to examine individual-level data, including data from panel designs and time-series designs (Epskamp, 2020). One analytic technique that may be useful for deception researchers is Gaussian graphical modeling (GGM), which attempts to model the covariance structure of a set of variables (similar to structural equation modeling) as a sparse network of partial correlations.

An individual-level model can be constructed for each person, which provides measures of the association between each cue with deception, as well as with other cues.

Because edges in a GGM are partial correlations (accounting for shared variance of the variables in the model), observed associations between deception and a given cue are more plausibly direct causal relationships, compared to examining ordinary correlations. For example, imagine a person for whom deception causes an increase in speech rate, which in turn increases speech errors. In such a situation, there would be an apparent correlation between deception and speech errors, but this correlation would be mitigated or eliminated controlling for speech rate. GGMs provide an efficient tool for implementing such statistical control.

In addition to being examined in themselves, individual network models can be combined to create an average group-level model, and a cross-sectional model across all messages can also be estimated (directly analogous to the method in group-level research).

Furthermore, the variability across individuals for each relationship can be modeled. This variability model provides information about how much individuals differ, for example, in the extent to which different behaviors correlate with deception. Using a variety of methods, clusters of interrelated cues can be identified (Blanken et al., 2018), both at the individual-and group-levels. Examination of such clusters, as well as variability across individuals, can provide previously unavailable insight into the structure of deceptive behavior across people.

For instance, if we were to observe that many people feature one or more clusters of behavior that correlates with deception, even if the specific content of those clusters varies between people, we may be able to infer a general principle about the manner in which deception tends to influence behavior. Network modeling is not the only quantitative approach one can take in order to examine phenomena on the individual level, but its flexibility, wide applicability to relevant situations in the deception literature, and the availability of software make it an appealing option.

A Worked Example of a Network Model of Deception Cues

Here we provide a worked example of a Network modelling approach of deception cues, using data from Loy et al. (2018). In the study, 24 people each told 24 truths and 24 lies in an interactive context (total 1149 valid messages). Each message was coded for several cues to deception. Here, we examine 14 verbal and nonverbal cues from this dataset: filled pauses, silent pauses, substitutions, prolongations, repairs, repetitions, head movements, hand movements, body movements, shoulder movements, mouth movements, eyebrow movements, smiles, and gaze. We used a network modeling approach to analyze the data. Specifically, for each individual, we fit a Gaussian graphical model (GGM; Epskamp et al., 2018) using the psychonetrics package (Epskamp, 2021) for R (R Core Team, 2023). For each person, we found an optimal model by iteratively pruning and adding edges with a liberal alpha of .10, to minimize the Bayesian information criterion. These personalized network models represent each individual's (within-subject) partial correlations between the measured behaviors and deception. We also fit a GGM using the same approach across all messages by all individuals.

This cross-sectional network is akin to typical group-level deception research, as it represents the group-level partial correlations across messages. Additionally, we estimated a network using person mean centered cue variables, to create a network representing the acrossindividual average (or average within-subjects effects). One might expect the across-individual average network to be equivalent to the cross-sectional network, but under many circumstances the cross-sectional correlation and the average within-subjects correlation are not equal or even in the same direction (Hamaker, 2023). Finally, we calculated the variance in the withinsubjects correlations for each possible edge in the full network, to depict the degree of variability across individuals. These data are displayed in Figure 6 and Figure 7 as network graphs created using the qgraph package (Epskamp et al., 2012).

Concluding Remarks

It is by now almost a truism in the field that cues to deception are weak and unreliable.

Some have gone so far as to call research on deception cues a "blind alley" not worthy of future research (Brennen & Magnussen, 2020). This conclusion is too sweeping. The issue is not with research on deception cues, but rather the methodology and paradigm used to study them. The typical group-level analysis only tells half the story (and indeed, this story is not favorable for offering practical recommendations). What is needed to tell the other half is a methodological shift to an individual-level research agenda. Although theoretical and meta-research discussions of deception acknowledge the difficulties in drawing conclusions about the individual from group averages (e.g., Brennen & Magnussen, 2022; Nahari, 2023), these discussions stop short of suggesting the overhaul of the methodological approach necessary to address this problem. Our suggested shift to an individual-level research agenda would not amount to an amendment or extension of the approach that has dominated research on deception cues, but rather, it is a fundamentally different take on the topic. One that we believe is required in order to progress research and theory in the field.

An individual-level approach will enable us to arrive at novel theoretical understandings of deception, from the bottom up. We will be able to examine how, for example, deception may induce idiosyncratic changes in behavior in different cues (or clusters of cues) between individual people. Understanding such potential differences can help provide crucial new insights into the psychological processes involved in human deception and create a science of people as individuals, rather than of averages across people.
