Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .07 Overt victimization trended toward statistical significance (p = .07), with greater experiences of overt victimization corresponding to higher reports of social anxiety.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Adolescence is a developmental period characterized by increased interest in peer relationships and social inclusion. Rejection sensitivity (RS) -the tendency to anticipate, perceive, and experience heightened emotional responses to rejection -is common in adolescence and linked to internalizing symptoms. However, RS is understudied in autistic adolescents despite their social communication differences, increased risk of peer rejection, and online lived experiences of heightened rejection sensitivity. It is also unknown how RS impacts different mental health symptoms and whether it might contribute to the heightened prevalence of anxiety and depression in autistic youth. In the present study, 106 autistic adolescents (14-17 years old) completed surveys of RS, internalizing symptoms, autistic traits, self-esteem, and peer victimization. Results showed that virtually all autistic adolescents experienced a degree of RS, with higher rates among females and those with higher autistic traits. Hierarchical regression models indicated that Anxious RS predicted social anxiety symptoms above and beyond traditional risk and protective factors, but neither Anxious nor Angry RS predicted depression or generalized anxiety. Findings have implications for risk stratification models and point to potential intervention targets; yet more research is needed to determine the directionality of relationships with rejection sensitivity.

Introduction

Adolescence is a particularly vulnerable period for the development of internalizing disorders (e.g., depression and anxiety) as youth experience biological and social changes and begin focusing on peer relationships (Allen & Loeb, 2015; Blakemore, 2008; Collishaw & Sellers, 2020). Social experiences are important for adolescents' development, as positive experiences (e.g., peer acceptance) are known to support well-being (Arslan, 2018; Guyer et al., 2014; Shah et al., 2024) while negative experiences (e.g., rejection) contribute to negative psychological outcomes (Arslan, 2021; Orben et al., 2020; Skogen et al., 2023). Autistic youth are far more likely to develop internalizing disorders than their non-autistic peers (Hudson et al., 2019; Kent & Simonoff, 2017), and negative social experiences may contribute to this disparity.

Autism is characterized, in part, by differences in social communication that are associated with difficulties in peer interactions and forming relationships (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Müller et al., 2008; Petrina et al., 2014). Though some prior work has theorized that autistic youth have universally low levels of social motivation (Chevallier et al., 2012), growing evidence suggests that autistic youth are socially motivated and desire connectedness with others at similar levels to their non-autistic peers (Cresswell et al., 2019). Given the importance of social acceptance to autistic youth, social rejection may have a particularly negative impact on their mental health (Lynn Mulvey et al., 2017; Minihan et al., 2023). This is concerning, since autistic youth are also at a greater risk of experiencing peer victimization and social rejection (Feldman et al., 2022; Sreckovic et al., 2014), with peer victimization and low self-esteem established as robust risk factors for internalizing disorders in this population (Daghustani et al., 2025; Storch et al., 2012; Ung et al., 2016). Rejection sensitivity -the tendency to anxiously anticipate, readily perceive, and have heightened emotional reactions to rejection (Downey & Feldman, 1996) -is a robust risk factor for internalizing disorders in non-autistic youth (Gurbuz et al., 2024; London et al., 2007; McDonald et al., 2010) that remains relatively unexplored in autism. Interestingly, rejection sensitivity is a common topic of discussion amongst autistic communities online, where it is described as heightened and distressing (Van Asselt et al., 2025), but these lived experiences have not been addressed empirically. The present study aimed to address this gap and examine the lived experiences of autistic youth by: (1) characterizing rejection sensitivity in a community sample of autistic adolescents, and (2) exploring rejection sensitivity as a potential predictor of depression, generalized anxiety, and social anxiety symptoms in this sample.

Rejection Sensitivity

Proposed by Downey & Feldman (1996), rejection sensitivity (RS) is defined as the tendency to readily anticipate, perceive, and have strong emotional responses to perceived rejection. In the face of more ambiguous social situations and cues, individuals with higher RS may expect to be rejected or assume rejection occurred, resulting in negative emotional and behavioral responses. Internalizing responses to rejection, such as nervousness, are known as anxious RS, while externalizing responses, such as irritability, are known as angry RS (Downey et al., 1998). Initial research on rejection sensitivity focused heavily on anxious RS, but later expanded to include angry RS to capture externalizing behavioral responses to rejection (Downey et al., 1998). Downey & Feldman (1996) proposed a cognitive-affective model of RS in which individuals develop rejection fears and take preventative actions (e.g., becoming watchful for cues, evading situations where rejection could occur) to reduce chances of rejection. Then, when an individual feels rejected, this prompts behavioral overreactions (e.g., panic, sadness, or anger) that then elicit additional negative consequences or continued rejection from observers, further contributing to experiences of rejection. The RS model describes a negative feedback loop, with new rejection experiences contributing to additional learned fears of rejection, effectively increasing sensitivity.

As the model implies, heightened RS is associated with several internalizing psychological disorders such as depression, anxiety, borderline personality disorder, and body dysmorphic disorder (Gao et al., 2017). When looking specifically at depression, several studies established a bidirectional relationship with RS. For example, Beeson et al. (2020) found that depression can precede RS, as well as predict future depression during later adolescence, highlighting a negative feedback loop between RS and depression. Another study by Chango et al. (2012) found that heightened RS strengthened the effect of relational stressors (e.g., emotional abuse, low peer support) on depression symptoms in late adolescents, emphasizing its role in the diathesis-stress model of depression. Heightened RS has also been linked to various anxiety disorders and symptoms, particularly social anxiety. In a longitudinal study of youth transitioning into adolescence, Zimmer-Gembeck and colleagues (2021) found that RS was a unique predictor of social anxiety symptoms, and that these symptoms specifically increased around age 14. Middle adolescence (i.e., ages 14-17) may be a particularly important developmental window as both anxious and angry RS contribute to the development and maintenance of social anxiety in this period (McDonald et al., 2010).

Though RS and internalizing symptoms are linked, less is known about how this relationship occurs (if at all) for autistic adolescents, despite their higher risks of both internalizing symptoms and social rejection. Some work in autistic adults showed that adults with social anxiety were more likely to anticipate rejection, a key characteristic of rejection sensitivity (Gurbuz et al., 2024); however, it is unclear whether these constructs function in the opposite direction, wherein rejection sensitivity might also impact mental health. Some qualitative investigations have also been completed in small populations of autistic adults, such as by Van Asselt et al. (2025), which documents these lived experiences as "overwhelming" and "exhausting." A more recent review highlighted that the evidence base on mental health and risk/protective factors in autistic people is sparse, mixed, and lacking examination of rejection sensitivity (Van Asselt et al., 2026). The authors largely suggest that rejection sensitivity in autistic people may contribute to negative mental health outcomes, like depression and anxiety.

Adolescence, Social Relationships, and Autism

Adolescence is a significant developmental period characterized by social transitions towards peer relationships and acceptance (Allen & Loeb, 2015; Blakemore, 2008). As peer relationships become increasingly significant to youth, social experiences become an important contributor to adolescent well-being and psychological outcomes. For example, experiences of peer acceptance and inclusion have been shown to shape and support emotional functioning (Arslan, 2018; Guyer et al., 2014; Shah et al., 2024), while negative social experiences are linked to negative psychological outcomes, such as anxiety and depression (Sequeira et al., 2025; Skogen et al., 2023). As adolescents' desire for peer acceptance is pronounced in this period, they are particularly vulnerable to social rejection experiences and the subsequent impacts of accumulating negative social experiences (Lynn Mulvey et al., 2017; Minihan et al., 2023).

Therefore, exploring differences in how adolescents perceive, anticipate, and respond to social rejection may be key to predicting internalizing disorders.

Although social rejection can be distressing for adolescents, autistic adolescents may be disproportionally affected by social rejection. Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by differences in social communication, including in social reciprocity, social interactions, and in forming and maintaining relationships (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). These characteristics are accompanied by repetitive behaviors (e.g., repetitive motor movements) and sensory sensitivities (i.e., negative responses to environmental stimuli) that appear early in development. Given these differences, unfortunately, autistic adolescents experience greater peer rejection and victimization relative to their non-autistic peers, often in response to social miscommunications (Daghustani et al., 2025; Sreckovic et al., 2014). A review by Cresswell et al. (2019) demonstrated that autistic teenagers largely want friendships, value social experiences, and wish to fit in with others, yet often become isolated by their peers, contributing to poorer mental health outcomes. Taken together, autistic adolescents value social belonging and peer acceptance, yet they face both unique barriers to peer acceptance and an elevated risk of rejection, which may make them particularly susceptible to the negative mental health consequences of rejection experiences.

Internalizing Disorders, Risk Factors, and Autism

Depression and anxiety disorders are some of the most common disorders among autistic youth (Hossain et al., 2020; Van Steensel et al., 2011), with higher prevalence rates than their non-autistic peers (Hudson et al., 2019; Van Steensel et al., 2011). Existing research has explored various risk and protective factors for internalizing disorders in autistic youth, such as peer victimization and self-esteem (Chou et al., 2020). Different types of victimization, namely relational victimization (i.e., social exclusion, verbal bullying) and overt victimization (i.e., physical bullying), occur more frequently for autistic youth than their non-autistic peers (Feldman et al., 2022; Sreckovic et al., 2014) and have strong links with internalizing disorders (Adams et al., 2014). Though peer victimization is strongly associated with high rejection sensitivity in non-autistic youth, this association has received surprisingly little attention in autistic youth, despite frequent victimization (Gao et al., 2021; Kellij et al., 2024; Sreckovic et al., 2014; Zimmer-Gembeck et al., 2014).

Conversely, self-esteem may serve as a protective factor against social rejection experiences (Li et al., 2012); however, this potential effect remains largely unexplored in autism even though autistic youth tend to have lower self-esteem than non-autistic youth (McCauley et al., 2019; Van Der Cruijsen & Boyer, 2021). Li and colleagues (2012) found that non-autistic youth with low self-esteem were more hypervigilant to rejection cues, which further underscores the need to understand this potential association in autistic youth. In other words, autistic youth may have similar risk and protective factors for internalizing disorders as non-autistic youth, but rejection sensitivity may be a salient risk factor contributing to higher prevalence rates of internalizing disorders among autistic youth.

In addition to established risk and protective factors for internalizing disorders, individual differences in autistic traits may help characterize variability in rejection sensitivity and internalizing symptoms. Autistic traits are different attributes associated with autism (e.g., repetitive behaviors, social camouflaging, and sensory sensitivities) which provide valuable insight into heterogeneous presentations of autism and the importance of tailored clinical approaches for autistic youth (Cruz Puerto & Sandín Vázquez, 2024). In the general population, autistic traits have been associated with increased social rejection-related pain and, in turn, more severe depression and anxiety symptoms (Lin et al., 2022). Therefore, it is important to consider the heterogeneity of autistic traits and identify if certain subgroups of autistic adolescents are disproportionately experiencing heightened RS.

The Present Study

The present study seeks to extend research on RS to autistic adolescents, better understand the lived experiences of autistic teens, and identify if RS presents a unique vulnerability to mental health problems in this population. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) characterize RS in a community sample of autistic adolescents, and (2) examine RS as a predictor of internalizing symptoms. Given the well-documented lived experiences of autistic people facing heightened RS, it was hypothesized that RS would be prevalent in autistic adolescents and would contribute significantly to depression, generalized anxiety, and social anxiety.

Methods

Participants

The sample included 106 autistic adolescents without intellectual disability. Participants ranged in age from 14 to 17 years old (M = 15.3) and were 68% male. The sample was mostly White (59%) and approximately half identified as Hispanic/Latino (48.5%). All participants were diagnosed with autism through a prior evaluation or an evaluation provided through the study; The present study is a secondary data analysis of cross-sectional data collected from adolescent self-report at the baseline timepoint. At baseline, adolescents completed several surveys assessing their RS profile, internalizing symptoms, autistic traits, peer victimization experiences, and self-esteem. Families received monetary compensation and a free psychological assessment report (if desired) for their participation.

Measures Rejection Sensitivity

Adolescent rejection sensitivity (RS) was measured using the Children's Rejection Sensitivity Questionnaire (CRSQ; Downey et al., 1998). The measure presented adolescents with 12 social scenarios with ambiguous social rejection cues and instructed participants to imagine themselves in each situation described. For example, one item asks, "Imagine you're back in your classroom‚ and everyone is splitting up into groups to work on a special project together.

You sit there and watch lots of other kids getting picked. As you wait‚ you wonder if the kids will want you for their group." Participants then responded to three questions for each of the 12 social situations: (1) how nervous they would be in this situation (i.e., anxious RS), (2) how mad they would be in this situation (i.e., angry RS), and ( 3) to what extent they expect the rejection to happen (i.e., expectancy). Participants numerically rated how nervous and mad they would feel in each situation on a 6-point scale [e.g., 'not nervous/not mad' (=1) to 'very nervous/very mad' (=6)]. Adolescents rated their expectancy of being rejected on a scale of 'no' (=1) to 'yes' (=6).

Due to the wording of certain scenarios, expectancy scores on items 2 and 12 were reversecoded. Anxious and Angry RS scores were computed by multiplying the anxiety score with the expectancy score, and the anger score with the expectancy score, respectively. This process was repeated for each scenario and then averaged across the Anxious items and Angry items; a total score was not produced and instead, two separate scores of Anxious and Angry RS. Possible scores ranged from 1 to 36 in both the anxious and angry rejection sensitivity domains, with higher scores indicating greater RS. Internal consistency was acceptable for both anxious RS (α = .86) and angry RS (α = .82).

Of note, the research team created a set of visual aids to ease the administration of the CRSQ and to facilitate comprehension for participants. Visual aids were developed for the 12 social situations and for each of the nervous, mad, and expectancy rating scales (see Supplemental Materials). The visual aids were presented to each participant with the verbal instructions for each scenario and participants were invited to provide their ratings verbally or by pointing.

Internalizing Symptoms

Internalizing symptoms were measured using the Revised Children's Anxiety and Depression Rating Scale-47 (RCADS-47; Chorpita et al., 2000), a self-report questionnaire that measures total internalizing symptoms as well as several types of internalizing symptoms, including separation anxiety, social anxiety, generalized anxiety, panic, obsessive-compulsive traits, and depression. In the present study, the RCADS depression, generalized anxiety, and social anxiety subscales were used given their potential association with RS. Items were rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale, ranging from "Never" to "Always", with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Internal consistency was acceptable across subscales (α = .85-.89).

Autistic Traits

Autistic traits were measured using the Comprehensive Autistic Trait Inventory (CATI; English et al., 2021), a 42-item self-report questionnaire that measures total autistic traits, as well as social interaction, communication, social camouflage, cognitive rigidity, repetitive behavior, and sensory sensitivity subdomains. Adolescents rated their agreement with various statements, using a 5-point scale from "definitely disagree" (=1) to "definitely agree" (=5), with possible scores ranging from 42 to 210. Higher scores indicate higher prevalence of autistic traits. Internal consistency was acceptable (α = .88).

Self-Esteem

Self-esteem was measured using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (Rosenberg, 1965), a self-report questionnaire. Adolescents rated their agreement with 10 statements about themselves on a 4-point scale, with answer choices ranging from "strongly agree" to "strongly disagree."

Higher scores suggest more positive self-esteem while lower scores indicate more negative selfesteem. Internal consistency was acceptable (α = .93).

Peer Victimization

Peer victimization experiences were measured using the Overt and Relational Victimization in Adolescents (ORAA), a self-report questionnaire of the frequency of various forms of peer victimization. Response options for each item range from "Never" to "A few times a week." The ORAA differentiates between overt victimization (i.e., physical bullying rated on 4 items) and relational victimization (i.e., social exclusion rated on 5 items). Scores for overt and relational victimization were summed across items, with possible scores ranging from 4 (overt) and 5 (relational), to 20 (overt) and 25 (relational).

Prior research shows autistic adolescents are at a greater risk of peer rejection and internalizing disorders compared to non-autistic adolescents (Chandler et al., 2016; Gurbuz et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2019; Sreckovic et al., 2014); however, there is limited research on the experience of RS in this population, despite discussions of lived experiences of heightened RS within autistic communities (Van Asselt et al., 2025). To our knowledge, the present study is the first to identify correlates of RS and examine cross-sectional associations with internalizing symptoms in a sample of autistic adolescents. Our findings not only emphasize the importance of RS on mental health outcomes in this population, but also empirically support the lived experiences of autistic people.

Virtually all autistic adolescents experienced a degree of RS. On average, autistic adolescents reported greater anxious RS than angry RS, suggesting that they are more likely to anxiously anticipate and expect rejection in ambiguous situations than respond angrily. One possible explanation of greater anxious RS in this population could be that internalizing disorders (i.e., anxiety disorders) are one of the most common mental health conditions experienced by autistic people (Hossain et al., 2020; Postorino et al., 2023; Van Steensel et al., 2011); longitudinal studies are needed to understand to what extent RS may contribute to internalizing disorders, and vice versa, in this population. Taken together, elevated rates of RS and internalizing behavioral reactions in this population highlights the importance of rejection sensitivity as a point of treatment intervention. Sex assigned at birth was significantly associated with both types of RS, with autistic females experiencing greater anxious and angry RS than autistic males. The literature on sex differences of adolescent RS in the general population is both limited and mixed. In non-autistic samples, Marston et al. (2010) found that RS is greater in males during late adolescence, while Giovazolias (2023) found significantly higher anxious RS in female adolescents. Elevated RS in autistic females could be attributed to sex differences in social camouflaging (i.e., masking) in autistic youth as several studies have shown that female autistic youth engage in more camouflaging behaviors than males, a strategy used to suppress and compensate for autistic behaviors to appear non-autistic (Dean et al., 2017; Simcoe et al., 2018; Tubío-Fungueiriño et al., 2021). Since camouflaging behaviors require identifying traits in oneself that deviate from the "social norm" and modifying them with the intent to be more socially accepted, it is possible that autistic females are more hypervigilant of their behaviors and more expectant of rejection in social situations, contributing to increased RS. More broadly, research also shows that adolescent girls experience greater concerns regarding peer acceptance and rejection than boys (Henrich et al., 2001; Liu & Kaplan, 1999; McLean & Anderson, 2009), which may contribute to higher levels of RS. In aggregate, it is possible that autistic female adolescents experience greater hypervigilance about their conformity to social situations and may mask to avoid rejection, contributing to apprehension about peer rejection and therefore greater RS. Longitudinal studies are needed to test these potential explanations and interactions.

The present study also established that autistic teenagers with more autistic traits also reported a greater degree of both anxious and angry RS. This is consistent with previous work suggesting that greater autistic traits heightened sensitivity to rejection in a general adult population without formal autism diagnoses (Lin et al., 2022). Conversely, Gurbuz et al. (2024)'s work in autistic adult males did not find autistic traits to be an independent predictor of rejection expectancy. Similarly, fMRI work in a smaller sample of autistic adolescents revealed that autistic teens process social rejection differently from non-autistic teens at the neural level, but both groups report equivalent levels of distress in response to rejection (Masten et al., 2011).

Much like sex differences, the literature on autistic traits and RS is both sparse and mixed, and, to our knowledge, has not been examined in a sample of autistic adolescents prior to the present study. Our findings extend knowledge of RS to a clinically-diagnosed autistic adolescent population, accounting for the unique social dynamics of having a clinically significant number of autistic traits.

Importantly, this study also explores the potential effects of RS on several mental health outcomes; namely depression, generalized anxiety, and social anxiety. While anxious and angry RS did not appear to uniquely contribute to depression or generalized anxiety above and beyond more traditional risk and protective factors (e.g., self-esteem and peer victimization), anxious RS presented a unique risk for increased social anxiety. This finding appears intuitive as social anxiety symptoms are characterized by a fear of negative social evaluations by other people (Leichsenring & Leweke, 2017; Morrison & Heimberg, 2013), which aligns with anxious RS processes. It is important to note, however, that anxious RS and social anxiety are not synonymous constructs, as anxious RS represents a cognitive-affective vulnerability that may contribute to the development and cyclical maintenance of social anxiety symptoms. This predictive association demonstrates that anxious RS captures a key variance in social anxiety beyond a fear of social interactions. In summary, while RS may not be a key contributor to depression or generalized anxiety in this autistic adolescent sample, it may be an important treatment target for social anxiety in this population.

It is worth discussing that negative self-esteem was strongly, consistently associated with all adverse mental health outcomes. This relation is well-documented in non-autistic populations (Downey & Feldman, 1996; Lei et al., 2024; Watson & Nesdale, 2012), and the present study suggests that high self-esteem may be protective against poor mental health for autistic adolescents in the same way. Importantly, however, autistic youth tend to have lower self-esteem on average than their non-autistic counterparts, highlighting the unique vulnerabilities of this population to mental health problems (Cooper et al., 2017; McCauley et al., 2019; Van Der Cruijsen & Boyer, 2021) as well as potential promise for future treatment interventions. In support of this, a neuroaffirming, cognitive-behavioral intervention targeting self-esteem in autistic adolescents showed improvements in both depressive and anxiety symptoms (Schwartzman et al., 2024); additional intervention research in this area is needed. Collectively, the present study's findings suggest that mental health in autistic adolescents is shaped by various risk and protective factors, and that anxious RS may be important for addressing social anxiety.

There are both strengths and limitations in the present study. The primary strength is our exploration of RS in autistic adolescents from their perspectives, filling a large gap in the literature on autistic adolescents while empirically supporting the lived experiences of autistic people. In addition to highlighting important clinical implications of RS for social anxiety, we also were able to identify that self-esteem should likely be addressed in tandem with RS to support the mental health of autistic youth. Additionally, findings were based upon reports of autistic adolescents rather than caregiver reports, directly capturing their perspectives of their own internal experiences rather than relying on a proximal informant. Lastly, the use of a community sample of autistic adolescents, as opposed to a sample recruited for comorbid internalizing disorders, strengthens the external validity of our findings to more autistic youth.

There are also limitations to the study. First, our study is cross-sectional in design and therefore causal direction is unable to be defined. Second, although it is a strength to include adolescents' personal reports of their feelings and experiences, self-report measures can be impacted by participants' level of insight into themselves, recall bias, and differences in respondent interpretations of questionnaires, among other biases. Third, there is an absence of a comparison group of non-autistic adolescents, which limits the ability to determine if these findings are unique to autism. Lastly, race and ethnicity were collapsed into binary groups for analytic purposes and thus, findings do not accurately reflect the diversity of lived experiences in this sample. Future research should include a longitudinal design, a comparison group of nonautistic adolescents, and multimodal measurements of the concepts analyzed. Nonetheless, considering the large empirical gap on the experiences and consequences of rejection sensitivity for autistic teens, our findings serve as an important foundation for future scientific work in this area.

Conclusion

The present study characterizes rejection sensitivity as a common, elevated experience for autistic adolescents that has potential implications for mental health. In particular, it identifies a potential relation with social anxiety, and highlights other important risk and protective factors, such as sex assigned at birth and self-esteem. Future longitudinal research is needed to establish the directionality of these associations, as well as the inclusion of non-autistic comparison samples to identify potential unique associations in autistic adolescents.
