Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Preferences underpin a range of fitness behaviors for many mammals such as foraging, object interactions, and even social relationships. Despite the ubiquity of domestic dogs in modern culture, the basis for preferences driving their behavior and choices are not well understood. Past work has found that dogs perceive and respond to certain characteristics of items, specifically an object's familiarity and quality when making choices.

However, in the real world, these characteristics don't exist in isolation, and understanding the interaction between familiarity and valuation as they relate to object choice can provide insight into how dogs make decisions. We aimed to explore how item familiarity and quality intersect to form dogs' preferences for one food over another in a series of two-alternative forced choice tasks. Over three studies, dogs (N = 125) were presented with a classic preference test between two items of unequal quality (Study 1), systematic pairings of four items that contrasted familiarity and quality (Study 2), and aggregates of two items of unequal quality (Study 3). Overall, we found that dogs' choices were driven only by the quality of the food item, and the prior familiarity of the item did not impact choice behavior.

Consumption measures revealed preferences that were not consistently reflected in choices.

Determining what motivates dogs and contributes to their preferences has implications for understanding decision-making at large, as well as for advancing canine science.

Like other species, dogs regularly make decisions such as what to eat (e.g., a piece of their kibble or mystery food found on a walk) and what to play with (e.g., a kong or a ball). In making these decisions, it is beneficial to evaluate the characteristics of the objects at hand. For instance, choosing to eat or interact with a novel item, like a new food found on a walk, carries some degree of risk, but may provide a valuable resource if that is a new delicious food source. Making quality-based decisions requires integrating multiple characteristics of an object (such as tastiness and novelty), and trading off potential risks (e.g., the likelihood the food will cause illness) with the potential benefits (e.g., discovering a new high-calorie food source), to form a preference and make the resulting choice.

Preference-Based Choices & Novelty-Seeking

Preferences, which we define here as an underlying, relatively stable assignment of value to one item over another, are driven by the items' features. Choices, the observable behavior of selecting an item in a given context, can also hinge on other characteristics of the individual or the environment. Food preferences and choice behavior in social mammals appear to be influenced by several internal and external factors. Studies with human and nonhuman primates have found that internal forces shaping preferences may include individual differences (e.g., sex or age) and tastes (Drewnowski, 1997), an individual's nutritional needs (Laska et al., 2000; Verspeek & Stevens, 2020), or even early developmental experiences and exposure (Birch, 1999; for a review see Ventura & Worobey, 2013). External forces, such as the current social environment or competition (Hopper et al., 2011; Kumpan et al., 2019), resource density (Parrish et al., 2020), conditions of (un)certainty around obtaining the food (Huskisson et al., 2021), the relative value of one food compared to other available options (Freeman et al., 2013; Hopper et al., 2014), or even how much effort it would take to obtain one item over another (Bonnie et al., 2019) can also influence which choices are made. Together, this prior work underscores the complex nature of preference formation and how it supports behavior. In particular, it highlights that choices may not be a straightforward reflection of an item's subjective or relative value for the chooser, but rather a behavior that is strongly influenced by both the context of the choice and the item's characteristics. The interaction of preferences and choices is particularly relevant to understand for animals that depend on humans for care and wellbeing, such as those housed in zoos or are domestic species used in farming or kept as companions.

Another facet shaping preferences and choice behavior is balancing neophilic and neophobic strategies in food gathering. A neophilic approach promotes exploration and the discovery of new food sources, while a more cautious approach can buffer against eating potentially harmful foods (i.e., the "omnivore's dilemma"; Milton, 1993). Behavioral flexibility also supports incorporating new foods in primates (e.g., Biro et al., 2003), an important skill that can help an individual meet their nutritional needs when resource availability fluctuates in the environment (for a review see Lambert & Rothman, 2015). Such skills are also highly relevant to domestic dogs, a species that has evolved to thrive on a starch-rich diet similar to humans (Axelsson et al., 2013) but that preferentially select food that is high in protein when it is available (i.e., meat-based foods; Sarkar et al., 2019). To date, dogs have received comparatively little attention when it comes to food preferences, even though this is highly relevant for many applied fields where selecting appropriately motivating rewards can enhance learning and inform care standards. Here, we extend the broader literature on preference formation and novelty-seeking, to examine whether dogs' initial choices track their underlying preferences, or instead reflect more context dependent decision processes.

Dogs and Choice Behavior

As a species that is commonly kept as a companion, dogs provide a particularly interesting model for studying how object characteristics influence quality-based choices under variable conditions. As social, domestic carnivores who have evolved alongside human beings, research with dogs has provided insights into the effects of domestication on decision making. For example, dogs are sensitive to item quantity (Petrazzini & Wynne, 2016; Ward & Smuts, 2007) but perform more poorly on numerical reasoning tasks than wolves, as evidenced by dogs showing a comparatively weaker preference for the larger quantity of a desirable food (Range et al., 2014), suggesting that domestication may alter item value or item choice strategies. In addition, while distantly related genetically, dogs regularly interact with human social partners, and their social decision-making and preferences are often evaluated relative to those exhibited by human children. For instance, in the social domain dogs, like human children, prefer to interact with or follow gestures given by people who were previously accurate, helpful to others, and knowledgeable (Catala et al., 2017; Pelgrim et al., 2021; Silver et al., 2020), providing evidence that a preference for reliable social informants is not unique to humans Individual differences in preferences, which then drive choice behavior, are found in numerous species (Drewnowski, 1997; Huskisson et al., 2021). Individual dogs differ in their preferences, for example, some are particularly food motivated, whereas others prefer social rewards (specifically human praise) (Cook et al., 2016). Understanding dogs' preferences provides an important context for understanding their cognitive abilities. This is particularly important because dogs are relied on in a variety of service roles such as guiding visually impaired individuals or alerting to changes in medical status. Understanding what motivates dogs and how they form preferences can help to advance canine science and improve training for working dogs.

Some features of items are preferentially chosen by dogs over others. For example, as discussed previously, dogs will choose items based on their quantity. Dogs also form preferences for items based on the item's qualities. It may be intuitive to think that a high quality food item (e.g., something rich in fat or with high caloric quality) will be preferred over a low quality item, but this has not been explicitly examined. Dogs have preferences for foods and make consistent choices based on these preferences (Cameron et al., 2021; Vicars et al., 2014). Previous preference tasks in dogs have found ranked food item preferences, and demonstrated that dogs' relative preference for different food items also predicts how effective the food will be as a reinforcer during learning tasks (Vicars et al., 2014). Dogs are also willing to work harder, as measured by running speed, for food rewards that are higher quality (Riemer et al., 2018). Dogs, like humans, may also have a preference for variety in their foods (Bremhorst et al., 2018; Ratner et al., 1999). When given the choice between a location that provides varied foods (one of which is their favorite) over a location that provides only their favorite foods, some will choose the variable location. However, the majority of dogs either significantly preferred the location with the consistently preferred food reward or showed no preference (Bremhorst et al., 2018).

Dogs also choose items based on the items' novelty or familiarity. For instance, given a choice of toys, two of which are familiar and one is new, dogs prefer the novel toy (Kaulfuss & Mills, 2008). A preference for new things, known as neophilia, is not unique to dogs.

Neophilia has previously been explored in a variety of species including non-human primates (Kendal et al., 2005) and birds (Heinrich, 1995). Neophilia is associated with innovation, and neophilic individuals may be more likely to discover novel solutions to problems and may be able to exploit new resources (Amici et al., 2019). However, a preference for novelty comes with risk. New items may be harmful or dangerous, and acquiring those items may expose the individual to elevated risks of predation or injury. Individuals who avoid this risk are neophobic. They display a strong preference for familiar items and rarely interact with new foods or objects. Critically, preferences for novelty can vary based on context, meaning an individual may be neophilic in one domain (i.e., choosing a novel toy), but be neophobic in another (i.e., choosing a familiar food) (Amici et al., 2019; Kuczaj, 2017). While dogs are neophilic when selecting toys, it's not currently known whether they prefer novel items in other domains, such as foods.

In the present research, we examined the role of item value and familiarity in dogs' food preferences and choice behavior. In Study 1, we tested whether dogs systematically chose high-quality food items in a laboratory setting using a classic, two-alternative forced choice task. Across four task variations that manipulated the presentation and visual access to the items (in the experimenters' hands, on plates, dropped into cups, or in aggregate inside of jars) dogs were shown two food items of unequal quality and familiarity (hotdog = high value and familiar, versus dried corn kernel = low value and unfamiliar) and allowed to choose one.

In Study 2, we aimed to disassociate item value and item familiarity, two features known to individually impact dogs' choices between food items, to explore how they intersect in dogs' choice of one food over another. We presented dogs with a total of four distinct food items (high value familiar & unfamiliar; low value familiar & unfamiliar) across six possible pairings, allowing for the exploration of the impact of familiarity and value in isolation, as well as their combined impacts (i.e., if dogs are willing to take a risk on an unfamiliar food when the alternative is of low value). Food items were selected for each dog by their guardian, as past work in homes has shown that dog guardians are highly accurate at predicting their dog's ranked order preferences for food items (Vicars et al., 2014). This allowed for the exploration of dogs' preferences for novel vs. familiar foods, and for their subjectively high vs. low value food items. In Study 3, we explored if a novel choice paradigm would impact dogs' displayed preferences for high quality items, relative to a classic two-alternative forced choice.

Study 1 was conducted in-lab. In part due to the COVID-19 pandemic, Studies 2 and 3 were conducted via Zoom with a synchronous citizen science design, where dog guardians acted as experimenters with real-time virtual support from a researcher. This approach has expanded in recent years, and provides reliable experimental results relative to those seen in the lab, while increasing the generalizability of findings through reducing barriers to participate in research (Pelgrim et al., 2024). Studies 2 and 3 were pre-registered [1].

Study 1: High-versus low-quality preference test in the lab

For the first step in our investigation of dogs' choice behavior, we focused on item value in a simple two-alternative forced choice task. Since prior research suggests that dogs may be integrating diverse dimensions of an item when deciding whether to eat or interact with it, we created a choice scenario between two items that differed in quality or value (i.e., tastiness). We controlled for other features such as item size, quantity, numerosity aiming to motivate choice behavior supported by dogs' underlying preferences for high quality food items alone. We predicted that when dogs were offered a choice between two food items, they would select and eat the high quality option more often compared to the low quality option. Further, we predicted that value would outweigh other aspects of the choice scenario, such as simultaneous or sequential item presentation which might impact the salience of the items. To test whether dogs integrate item value into their choice behavior when in a lab environment, we tested four types of item presentation that have been commonly used in studies of canine cognition: placing on plates, holding in hands, dropping inside cups, and in aggregate inside of transparent jars.

This study was approved by the [BLINDED] University Animal Care Committee (UACC), guardians gave informed written consent prior to participating, and all procedures were in accordance with university and federal ethics guidelines.

Materials and Procedure

We truly randomly assigned dogs to participate in a baseline condition (nBaseline = 10), or one of four experimental conditions of the two-alternative choice task (nHands = 10; nPlates = 11; nCups = 10; nJars = 10). Across all tasks, each dog had a brief acclimation period in the room while off leash, 3-5 minutes of exploration time. During the task, dogs worked on standard 6-foot leash and were handled by a trained research assistant to ensure they were a consistent distance from the choices on all trials and were stationary, facing the experimenter during the demonstration.

In baseline, we presented dogs with a choice between a white plate with a freeze-dried beef liver treat and a plate with nothing, to confirm that dogs would consistently approach a location with a food item over an empty location (rather than exhibiting random behavior).

We controlled for human enhancement of the search locations during item presentation by simultaneously moving both hands laterally to the search locations and placing the item at one and holding the empty hand above the other.

To motivate a preference-based choice from the dogs in the four experimental conditions, we selected two edible items of similar size but that differed substantially in color and quality. The high quality items were microwaved beef hotdog slices and the low quality items were dried Peruvian choclo (dried giant white corn kernels). With similar size items, we controlled for the possibility that dogs made choices based on reward quantity rather than item preference. The four experimental conditions all involved a choice between these items while presentation of the items was varied systematically. In the Hands condition, dogs watched the experimenter simultaneously present a single high and low quality item in the palms of her extended hands (arms at 45˚ lateral angle from the median, at dog's head height). The Plates condition was similar, but items were presented on two 9-inch blue plastic plates that were set on the floor ~0.9m apart (Figure 1). Presenting both items simultaneously allowed for equal viewing of the items prior to making a choice. The experimenter timed item presentation across trials and tasks so that there was a three-second interval between item placement and verbally releasing the dog to make a choice. In the Cups and Jars conditions, items were presented sequentially, the high or low quality one at a time, in a counterbalanced order across trials. In the Cups condition, the experimenter placed both cups at the search locations, then showed the dog one item one at a time, before dropping it into a blue plastic cup and repeating the motion with the second item. In the Jars condition, the experimenter picked up each jar one at a time from the floor and held it out toward the dog to show that it was full of either the high or low quality items, before placing it at one of the search locations and repeating the motion with the second jar. Sequential presentation allowed the dogs to view each of the items prior to making a choice, then they could approach one jar and receive an item sampled from within. The cups were blue plastic cups that were 10 cm in diameter at the rim and 12 cm in height. The jars were clear plastic jars with opaque lids that were 10 cm in diameter and 20 cm in height.

In all conditions, the interval between placement and releasing the dog was three seconds. We intentionally equalized odor cues across all conditions so that scent could not differentially inform the dogs' choices, allowing us to focus on visual cues. This approach is consistent with other two alternative forced choice paradigms in canine cognition that control for odor, and effectively removes scent as a quality or location cue. We amplified the highquality item scent in the environment and rubbed hotdog slices on all stimuli (including the experimenter's hands) before each session. To help with air movement and cover light background noises that might be distracting to the dog, a small table-top fan was placed at the back of the room and set to a low setting during the test session.

In-lab Warm-up and Item Introduction.

All dogs participated in a brief warm-up after acclimating to the space. The experimenter sat on the floor facing the dog while the handler brought the dog into a stationary position facing the experimenter, about 1.3 meters distant. The handler held the dog at the starting position throughout the demonstration until release. The experimenter drew the dog's attention calling "look" and extending a treat at arms-length in front and around eye-level for the dog. The experimenter placed the treat on the floor and verbally released the dog to approach and eat the treat. Dogs that hesitated to approach were encouraged vocally by the experimenter and the actions were repeated until they approached without signs of hesitance or anxiety (typically 2-4 trials).

Following the warm-up, dogs assigned to the experimental condition were introduced to the high quality item (hotdog slice) and low quality item (corn kernel), so that each was familiar. The same showing and baiting procedure from the warm-up was used, each item was introduced twice (four total trials). Item introduction began with the high quality item and alternating between the two options, and ended with the low quality item. This brief item introduction allowed dogs to learn about the items prior to the choice task and establish a similar level of familiarity prior to the choice task. Dogs assigned to the baseline condition did not have an item introduction and proceeded immediately to test trials following the warm-up.

Two-Alternative Choice Test.

In all of the test conditions (including baseline), the dog and experimenter were at a distance of 1.3 meters facing one another, the dog was released verbally by the experimenter to make a choice, and was recalled to the starting location by the handler following their choice on each trial (Figure 1). To minimize human influence on the dogs' choices during the test, the handler and experimenter did not look or gesture at either of the items or directly to the dog, and were silent apart from the verbal release and recall words. A brief pause of three seconds occurred between baiting and verbal release, allowing the dog to observe the two options. Upon making a choice (i.e., touching one of the items or the container that held it), the other option was removed from access to prevent exhaustive searching. Locations were baited in a counterbalanced order across trials, so that the treat (or the high value item) never appeared in the same location more than twice consecutively. Dogs completed a total of 10 choice trials. In the Cups and Jars conditions, where food items were presented sequentially rather than simultaneously, the order of showing high or low quality item first was counterbalanced to control for primacy effects.

Procedure timing and the lateral search locations were consistent across all conditions, including baseline.

Data Scoring and Analysis

Dogs in all conditions completed 10 test trials of the Two-alternative Choice task and were live-coded by the experimenter for their approach and choice on each trial. Live-coded choice results were confirmed through video coding by a trained research assistant, with 100% agreement. Choice per trial was coded based on the dog's first touch of the food item or container (cup, plate, jar, or hand) with their muzzle or forepaw. We scored correct choices as one (defined as touching the high quality item first or, in the case of baseline, the plate with the treat) and incorrect choices as zero (defined as touching the low quality, or empty plate, first). Choices were summed across trials per dog, to a possible total score out of 10. Consumption of the item following choice was coded post-hoc from video (for details on consumption coding and results of analyses, see the accompanying Supplemental Information).

We analyzed the data in R (v4.5.3; R Core Team, 2026), all scripts and data are available on OSF following publication. To understand if dogs, as a group, were above chance at selecting the treat in baseline or the high value item in experimental conditions, we used two-tailed t-tests (α = .05), where chance performance was 5/10 correct trials. To test the impact of the presentation style on choice behavior, in a series of linear regression models we compared performance in experimental conditions, as well as performance in experiments (pooled) to baseline. Finally, to explore if dogs increased or decreased in accuracy over trials, we performed mixed logistic regression analyses with accuracy as the dependent variable, a 318 random intercepts per dog, and trials nested within the dog.

When dogs were presented with two visually distinct food items of the same size, one low and one high quality, they chose the high quality item approximately 58% of the time, which, while above chance, is not strong evidence of preference-motivated choice behavior in this context. In the baseline condition (choosing a treat over nothing) dogs showed a strong tendency to select the treat which suggests that they are not choosing randomly. However, when tasked with differentiating between two items based on quality, their choices only weakly reflected this preference. In two of the tasks, item presentation was simultaneous, which may have caused the dogs to split their looking time between the two or even choose one item to fixate on. In both the Hands and Plates conditions where both items were shown and placed at the same time, dogs' performance did not differ from chance. In the Cups and Jars conditions, we used a sequential presentation to ensure that dogs had looked at each item prior to making a choice. While performance was still near chance levels, dogs were selecting the high quality item slightly above the simultaneous presentation conditions, suggesting that perhaps forcing attention to each item could be helpful for decision making.

Looking at the choice data alone, it would be reasonable to infer that dogs do not have a strong preference for hotdog versus dried corn, as they choose the two items at similar rates.

However, this could be mistaking task demands and decision making challenges for a weak preference between the items. To take a closer look at the possibility that dogs did not have an underlying preference for one item over the other, in follow-up analyses we used data on whether the chosen item was consumed after choice. We found that on trials where dogs selected the hotdog, the probability that they would eat the item was 99%, while if they had selected dried corn, then the probability dropped to 19.3% (see Supplemental Information for the details of this post-hoc analysis). This strongly suggests that dogs prefer to eat hotdogs over corn, and that perhaps there are other challenges that prevent this preference from being consistently expressed in their choices. Such challenges could be due to information gathering strategies that may rely less on vision (i.e., scent detection, and everything in the room smelled like hotdog), or dealing with the demands put on inhibitory control in various problem-solving scenarios (e.g., Bray et al., 2014), or even possibly an evolutionary adaptation for an indiscriminate diet and scavenging behavior. Finally, the task itself may not have been an optimal situation for measuring dogs' preferences. However, one additional possibility is that dogs' preferences for foods are formed from a combination of the item's quality and familiarity. Specifically, dogs may be assigning value based on how novel the items are, and end up making choices based on a composite quality judgment that integrates multiple features of an item. None of the dogs in this study had previously been exposed to a Peruvian choclo. Giant corn kernels may have indeed been low preference, but potentially high novelty food items, with these two criteria in competition. Even though the item introduction was intended to reduce novelty and put the items on equal footing with regard to familiarity in the task, it is possible that two repetitions were not enough exposure to accomplish this goal. In contrast to the corn, it is likely that some, but perhaps not all of the dogs had previously encountered hotdogs over the course of their general training. This opens the possibility that, in addition to individual differences in neophilia that may exist between dogs, the dogs in this study may also have differed in their familiarity with the specific food items, and this could have influenced their choice behavior.

Study 2: Familiarity vs. quality over Zoom

In Study 2, we aimed to explore how dogs make preference-based choices when integrating multiple features of objects, such as item quality and object familiarity. To address this, we carried out another study directly examining the influence of item familiarity and quality on behavior in a two-alternative choice task. In Study 2, dogs completed six types of two-alternative forced-choice pairings of four guardian-selected foods: high quality familiar, high quality unfamiliar, low-quality familiar, and low quality unfamiliar. Across comparisons, dogs' choices could be driven by the quality of the item alone, in which case we would expect that they would choose the high (vs. low) quality items in all pairings where that comparison is present. We would also expect that when the items did not differ in quality, they would choose between the two at chance levels. Dogs could also make choices driven by the relative familiarity of the item alone, in which case, we would expect that they would show strong preferences when comparing relatively familiar and unfamiliar items, but when comparing items of equal familiarity we would expect choices at chance levels. Dogs may also display a ranked preference that is dependent on both factors where, for example, dogs most prefer one item and may rarely choose another item. Finally, they might not show strong preferences for item quality or familiarity as measured by our choice metric. Procedure & Materials

This study was conducted over Zoom with dog guardians acting as experimenters and while being virtually coached in the procedure by a trained experimenter. Foods were chosen individually for each dog by their guardian (for more details see Preference Sessions). After a 12-trial baseline (treat vs. empty plate as in Study 1), each dog completed six counterbalanced two-alternative forced-choice pairings across two Zoom testing sessions.

Each pairing was presented in a block of 8 trials.

Prior to sessions, dog guardians were instructed to ensure their dog had not eaten for a few hours. During the sessions, dogs were presented with a choice between food items on plates. As in Study 1, choice was defined as making physical contact with either the food item or the plate beneath it. The unchosen plate was removed immediately, and dogs received only the chosen item.

Choices were recorded by the experimenter on Zoom. In the event that a dog failed to make a choice for > 30 seconds after the guardian gave their verbal release, the trial was repeated (this occurred on 3 out of 1824 trials). Dogs waited approximately 1m away from their guardian while the guardian placed down plates with treats as instructed by a researcher over Zoom. When a second person was available, dogs were held approximately 1m away from the guardian-experimenter during the food placement. Zoom Baseline Task.

Prior to participating in the choice task, all dogs completed a simple object choice task between a plate with a treat on it and an empty plate comparable to the Baseline condition performed in-person from Study 1. This was part of a prior study (Pelgrim et al., 2024), and the initial session mimicked the structure of our Preference sessions. We used dogs' performance on the baseline task as a screening criterion, with dogs required to complete the full 12 trial baseline without significant guardian-experimenter errors. We also required the dog to select the plate with the treat over the empty plate on at least 7 out of the 12 trials 2F [3] to continue to the preference task, with the rationale that dogs who did not choose a treat over an empty plate would not be sufficiently food motivated to participate in the later food preference task. Dogs meeting these criteria were invited to participate in this task until we reached the sample size from our pre-registration. This task also provided a baseline for each dog's choice performance, which we evaluated as a predictor for dogs' subsequent preferences between high and low quality items.

Warm-up Trials. To begin the baseline session, we first conducted warm-up trials.

During warm-up trials, dogs were shown a single plate with a treat on it (one that was not used in other parts of the experiment) that was placed directly in front of their guardian. Once the plate was on the ground, the guardian verbally released their dog from the waiting position to eat the treat off of the plate. We repeated this procedure for a minimum of 3 trials, or until both guardians and dogs were comfortable (maximum of 6 trials observed in our sample).

Baseline. After completing warm-up trials, the guardian presented two plates placed just in front of their knees. Plates were located approximately 0.75m apart from each other.

The order of placement (treat or empty plate placed first) as well as the side the treat was on was counterbalanced. To reduce potential error caused by coordination issues, we elected to have dog guardians place the plates sequentially. After placing both plates onto the ground, the guardian verbally released their dog. If the dog chose the plate with the treat on it, they were allowed to eat the treat. If the dog chose the empty plate, the plate with the treat was removed, and they did not receive any reward on that trial. This was repeated for 12 trials. Dogs qualified for the test session of Study 2 after completing all 12 trials of the initial session baseline task, as long as there were no significant guardian-experimenter errors, and the dog chose the treat (vs. the empty plate) on at least 7 out of the 12 trials as discussed above.

We also conducted an exploratory analysis to see if dogs' preference for familiar vs novel items changed over trials and to examine individual differences. When items differed in familiarity, we found a significant effect of pairing χ 2 (3) = 33.61, p < .001. This is in line with results reported earlier, namely that dogs preferred the higher quality item whether it was familiar or unfamiliar (Table 3). There was no effect of trial, χ 2 (1) = .18, p = .67, meaning that dogs' preferences were consistent across the task.

Impact of Other Factors on Choice Behavior

We found no effect of presentation order for both pairings where quality differed between the items, χ 2 (1) = 2.67, p = .10, and for the pairings where items differed by familiarity χ 2 (1) = .04, p = .84. For the four pairings that differed in quality, there was no difference in dogs choice of the high quality food by pairing, F(3, 99) = .64, p = .59, past performance on the baseline task, F(4, 33) = 1.19, p = .34, or an interaction between past performance and pairing, F(12, 99) = 1.28, p = .25. For pairings differing in familiarity, we also found no interaction between past performance and pairing, F(12, 99) = .811, p = .64.

This suggests that dogs' performance on the preference task is not driven by a more general ability on choice tasks. Unlike the quality analyses, however, there was a significant effect of pairing, meaning that dogs chose differently depending on how foods were paired F(3, 99) = 6.64, p < .001. This is in keeping with findings presented earlier that the dogs choose the high value item regardless of its familiarity.

Overall, dogs preferred high to low quality items, regardless of the item's familiarity, however as in Study 1 these preferences as measured by choices were not exceptionally strong. While it is possible that dogs do not have as strong of preferences between food items as demonstrated by other species, it remains possible that dogs have a difficult time using their item preferences to make decisions in two-alternative forced-choice tasks. Past work has shown that dogs can visually discriminate between high and low quality food items, looking longer at the high quality item than the low quality item in a two alternative forced choice task (Espinosa et al., 2021). It is also worth noting that our familiarity manipulation concerned dogs' prior familiarity with the foods. We allowed dogs to sample each food item twice prior to the test trials, so that they could have direct experience of each item's quality.

Therefore, our findings concern the effects of prior or relative familiarity and do not establish how dogs respond to entirely unencountered foods.

Dogs usually consumed their chosen item in Study 2, fully consuming it on 1,738 of 1,807 codable trials (96.2%). However, as in Study 1, when dogs did not fully consume their chosen item, it was almost always a low quality item (61 of 69 trials, 88.4%). This suggests that the lower quality item was in fact less preferred, though dogs in Study 2 were more likely to consume the low quality item than in Study 1 (see Supplementary Materials for a summary of item consumption). This may be because even the low quality foods were selected individually by guardians and sampled before testing to establish that they were edible. One possible explanation is that task demands contribute to dogs' apparent weak preferences (as measured by choices). For example, dogs may have a difficult time with impulse control, resulting in choices that are not in line with their true preferences. Pet dogs may also have low motivation to gather information about food items. Pet dogs often need to locate food in their environment, and perhaps as a result they are skilled at tasks requiring locating a food item. However, evaluating and then choosing between two items may be a much less frequently encountered task in their everyday environment, and may therefore be less ecologically relevant. This may be because there is little pressure on pet dogs to make optimal choices between food items (i.e., only missing an opportunity to eat the preferred food), or the selection of the non-preferred item could reflect dogs' lack of understanding about the first-touch rule.

Study 3: An Alternative Paradigm over Zoom

Studies 1 and 2 demonstrated that both in-lab and over Zoom, dogs prefer high value to low value foods, that familiarity with the items does not have a significant impact, and that preferences expressed through choices are comparatively weak, with dogs choosing the higher value item approximately 60% of the time across Studies 1 and 2. It's possible that dogs' performance on food choice tasks is limited by the choice context itself -namely that due to social, experiential, procedural (i.e., low cost of choosing the less-preferred item) factors, dogs have a difficult time choosing between two food items. As a result, we created a revised version of a forced-choice task.

Dogs were presented with two plates, each of which contained several high or low quality familiar food items (rather than an individual item). One plate was always placed significantly closer to the dog, and the other a farther distance away. If dogs have a strong preference for the high quality item then we would predict that when the plate with low quality items is closer to the dog, they will leave most if not all of the items on the plate, in order to quickly begin eating from the far plate (which contains the high quality items).

Alternatively, when the high quality plate is closer to the dog, we predict that dogs will leave few if any items on the closer plate, before moving on to the low quality items on the far plate. It is also possible that dogs will not display a strong preference for the high quality item, in which case we would predict they will leave few if any items on the close plate before proceeding to the far plate, regardless of what food items are on that plate. Though less likely, dogs could instead have a preference for the far plate, in which case we would predict that they would leave many or all items on the close plate regardless of what food items are on that plate.

By allowing dogs the opportunity to switch to an alternative choice and providing dogs with additional information before their choice (specifically by allowing them to look closely at, smell, and even taste both options) we aim to address possible challenges of twoalternative forced-choice tasks that may have resulted in past weak preferences. This study was approved by the [BLINDED] University Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), guardians gave informed written consent prior to participating, and all procedures were in accordance with university and federal ethics guidelines.

Methods

Participants

The participants were 36 dogs (16 Female, Mage = 64.94 mo, range = 13 -177 mo) who had not participated in Study 1 or Study 2. In accordance with our pre-registration 3F [4], an additional 1 dog was excluded due to a video experimenter error.

Our sample size was determined by a power analysis of simulated data, see pre-registration for further details.

Dogs in this study were not required to have any prior experience with Zoom studies.

Materials & Procedure

As in Study 2, this study was conducted synchronously over zoom with dog guardians acting as experimenters. Unlike in Study 2, Study 3 was conducted in one session and used a different testing configuration. To demarcate the testing area, 3 markers were placed on the ground. The first marker indicated where the dog would start. The second and third markers indicated where the food plates were placed, and they were 0.5m and 1.69m in front of the dog respectively (Figure 5). These distances were chosen from past work showing that, on average, dogs are willing to travel 1.69 m for a larger quantity of food, relative to the closer smaller amount (Stevens et al., 2022).

Figure 5

Study 3 Experimental Set Up.

Warm-Up.

We conducted warm-up trials to ensure dogs were comfortable taking food off of a plate from both locations, and to give dog guardians a chance to practice the procedure. Once the dog was in place behind marker 1, the guardian-experimenter showed one piece of a warm-up food (a food their dog would find rewarding that was not either the high or low quality food). The guardian then allowed their dog to sniff the food by holding the plate a maximum of six inches from the dogs' nose. This ensured that dogs had both visual and olfactory information about the contents of the plate. Guardians then placed the plate on either Marker 2 or 3 (order counterbalanced), and walked behind their dog. This ensured that, at the time of their choice, dogs were not able to see their guardians. Once behind their dog, the guardian experimenter provided a verbal release, and the dog was allowed to approach and consume the warm-up food. This process was repeated twice so the dog had the chance to eat the warm-up food at both Marker 2 and 3. As a final step in warmups, guardians handed their dog a piece of each test food and verified the dog would eat it.

Test Trials.

All dogs completed two test trials comparing the high and low quality items. On one trial, the high quality item was closer to the dogs, and on the other the low quality item was closer. The order of trials was counterbalanced across dogs. To begin a trial, the guardian spread 8 pieces of the food items onto each plate. This ensured dogs could not consume all food pieces in one bite. With the dog ready at Marker 1, the guardian experimenter would show their dog the plate containing either the high or low quality food items and allow their dog to sniff the contents, as in the warm-up trials. The order of presentation was counterbalanced, and was conducted in the same manner as the warm-up trials. A trial ended either after the dog consumed all foods present, or after 30 seconds had elapsed since the dog stopped eating.

Data Scoring & Analysis

As in Study 1, the video experimenter live coded all trials, recording the first plate the dog touched, first ate from, and the first plate they finished (meaning all food items had been consumed). In addition, the experimenter recorded the number of food items left on the closer plate at the time that the first item was eaten from the far plate. An additional coder who was blind to the purpose of the study and the study's hypotheses also coded for each of these measures on 25% of the videos (n = 9 dogs) to ensure the data was recorded correctly. Initial re-coded reliability was very high across our measures (97.22%). As in Study 2, when disagreements between the experimenter and the naive coder occurred, the co-first-author (MHP) re-coded the trial.

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.3; R Core Team, 2026), scripts and data available on OSF following publication. We conducted a series of mixed-effects logistic regressions predicting dogs first touch, first eaten from, and first plate finished (close vs. far) as a function of the item on that plate with a random effect per dog. For analyses involving first plate finished, we excluded dogs who did not finish either plate. A total of 3 dogs were excluded from these analyses. Finally, we examined whether the number of items left on the close plate at Marker 2, at the time when the food was first consumed from the far plate (Marker 3), was predicted by the contents of the close plate. We used a mixed effect model to explore the number of items left as a function of the quality (high or low) of the items on the close plate with a random effect of dog.

Results & Discussion

Dogs tended to first touch the closer plate (chosen on 65 / 72 total trials); however, this did vary by what was on the closer plate. When the high quality item was close, dogs touched that one first on 35 / 36 trials, whereas when the low quality item was close, dogs touched the low quality item first on 30 / 36 trials. We found a significant effect of block, or that their first touch was different as a function of the contents of the closer plate, χ 2 (1) = 5.27, p = .02. Similarly, dogs tended to first eat the closer plate (chose on 61 / 72 total trials), and this varied by what was on the closer plate. There were only 4 trials where dogs first touched the closer plate but first ate from the further one. When the high quality item was close, dogs first ate from the high quality item on 35 / 36 trials, whereas when the low quality item was close, dogs chose the low quality item first on 26 / 36 trials. As in first touch, we found a significant effect of block with dogs first eating from the closer plate more when it contained the high quality item, χ 2 (1) = 6.22, p = .01.

After first eating from one plate, many dogs were willing to leave some items on the closer plate if it was of a low value (Figure 6). For items left, there was a significant effect of the quality of the close item, χ 2 (1) = 16.62, p < .001. Dogs left more items on the close plate to begin eating from the second plate when the close plate contained low-quality items (Mean Left = 2.86 food items, SD = 3.67) than when the close plate contained high-quality items (Mean Left = .42 food items , SD = 1.75). Finally, dogs were more likely to finish the close plate first when it was high quality (31 / 33 dogs finish close plate first) than when the close plate was low quality (19 / 33). When conducted the same way as the previous models, this one failed to converge. Because of this, we introduced a different optimizer (bobyqa), and found a significant main effect of the quality of the items on the plate, χ 2 (1) = 9.45, p = .002.

Trials could have ended after 30 seconds had elapsed since the dog stopped eating, however generally trials ended with dogs eating all foods. For instances where a food item was initially left behind (i.e., on the closer plate), dogs typically returned after finishing the far plate and consumed the remainder of available food. When items were left, they tended to be on the low quality plate (Figure 6). When the high quality item was close to the dog, 3 dogs didn't eat all items, leaving 1, 7, and 8 low quality items respectively on their far plate.

On those trials, every dog ate all the high quality pieces. When the high quality item was far away, one dog left all the high quality items uneaten (and consumed all the close low quality items), and another dog left all the low quality items uneaten.

Overall, these results support that dogs show a preference for food items based on their quality, and suggest that classic two alternative forced choice tasks may underestimate relatively robust item preferences in dogs, which may emerge more clearly when the task demands are reduced and sequential sampling of the food items is allowed.

General Discussion

Across three studies we examined dogs' food preferences in-lab (Study 1) and using a synchronous citizen science approach (Studies 2 and 3). In Study 1, we used four task variations of a classic, two-alternative forced choice task in a laboratory setting. We manipulated the presentation of the items and found that when presented with direct comparisons between a high quality item and a low quality item, dogs preferred the high quality item, but chose it at rates only slightly above chance (~60%). In Study 2, we explored the impact of both item quality and familiarity on dogs' food preferences using a Zoom set-up with guardians acting as experimenters. When evaluating preferences, we found that the quality of the food, and not its familiarity, drives dogs' food choices. Dogs preferred higher quality items relative to lower quality items, choosing the higher quality item significantly more often, though still only (~60% of the time, as in Study 1). We also found that when choosing foods relative familiarity does not play a significant role. Dogs were neither neophobic nor neophilic when choosing between food items. Put another way, in a twoalternative forced choice task dogs choose foods based on their quality alone, choosing the higher quality item no matter how familiar it was to them, or how familiar the alternative was. As in Study 1, we found that while as a group dogs chose the higher quality food item significantly more often, few dogs individually displayed strong preferences, as measured by choice behavior.

In Study 3, we introduced a novel alternative to the two-alternative forced-choice task by presenting high-and low-quality foods on separate plates that dogs could approach sequentially, enabling more naturalistic measures of first contact, amount consumed, and switching between plates. We found further evidence that dogs do prefer high quality to low quality items. Dogs were more likely to first touch, eat from, and consume the entirety of the high quality plate, relative to the low quality plate. Dogs also left significantly more food items behind when the close item was low quality, relative to high quality. This evidence of food-related preferences is more apparent when we used a methodological variation compared to Studies 1 & 2 and considered additional indicators of preference. While all methods in the present paper are iterations of the two-alternative forced choice paradigm, perhaps measuring behaviors other than first-touch (e.g., consumption) is an important additional finding; it raises the broader question of whether first-touch as a response is an accurate, species appropriate, method for understanding dog preferences. Put another way, when the closer item was low quality dogs were more likely to leave the plate unfinished and move on to the far away high quality item, as compared to when the closer item was high quality.

Our findings are in line with previous work that dogs form strong preferences for food items (Cameron et al., 2021; Vicars et al., 2014). In the current Studies 2 and 3, dogs as a group preferred to eat the higher quality item over a lower quality item, regardless of how novel the items were. In a similar vein, when dogs have been tested in paradigms that required greater effort to obtain a high quality reward, dogs would consistently work harder to receive a preferred item over a non-preferred item (Riemer et al., 2018), even when greater quantities of the non-preferred were available. Together, these findings suggest that there are reliable preferences for food items underlying dog choice behavior and that this outweighs the importance of exploring new or greater aggregates of items.

Interestingly, even though dogs form strong preferences and can act on them to make choices, these preferences were only weakly reflected in their choices in Studies 1 and 2.

Instead, choice behavior appears to be highly variable. One explanation for inconsistent choices across tasks is that different experimental set-ups have different cognitive demands, which may impact impulsive behavior. Prior work has examined dogs' executive function in various paradigms from physical detours (e.g., Bray et al., 2014), adapted delay of gratification tasks (Brucks et al., 2017), and in personality assessments of impulsivity as a general factor underlying behavior (Riemer et al., 2014; Barela et al., 2024). Across these studies there is general consensus that choice behavior varies greatly by individual, and that there is low transfer across contexts. In Study 1 of this investigation, dogs displayed a marked preference for hot dogs over corn kernels in their consumption, sometimes spitting out the corn, but chose the higher quality item only modestly above chance. This could point to potential distracting features of the experimental set up that made it more challenging for dogs to inhibit motor impulsivity and select the higher quality option. For example, in Study 1, equalizing odor cues with the hotdog scent everywhere was an effective control, but also could have contributed to the decision-making difficulty. It would have ensured that the high quality item was not more salient, however it could have sent a very strong signal that would have been difficult for dogs to ignore in favor of visual cues. These results highlight the need for tasks that decouple what dogs value from their decision making, particularly under different cognitive loads.

Limitations and Future Directions

Future work could consider exploring food preferences in a different dog population, such as free-ranging dogs, where there may be greater pressures to obtain better foods, and where novel foods may present both greater risks and benefits. Further, all dogs in our sample are pets being presented with food items by a human. In Studies 2 and 3, having the guardian act as an experimenter in the home was advantageous as it allowed us to customize food choices for individual dogs. However, social expectations and past reward history with their guardian may have impacted dogs' performance. While in Study 3 we had guardians move out of view of their dog to minimize any social cues or expectations at the time of choice, the presentation of the foods themselves was social across our three studies and could have impacted dogs' performance. Future work could consider an entirely a-social version of this task to determine if dogs display stronger preference through choice behavior without the presence of a human.

Conclusions

In all, we found dogs prefer to eat higher quality vs. lower quality items but did not consistently choose the higher quality item first in variations of two-alternative choice tasks.

Dogs displayed this preference for higher (vs. lower) quality foods across two contexts: both under standardized conditions in the lab and in the home where food items could be tailored to individuals' preferred food types. Surprisingly, though dogs are often characterized as neophilic (Kaulfuss & Mills, 2008), the novelty of items (which we operationalized as familiarity) does not appear to play a strong role in dogs' food choices, suggesting that dogs are neither neophobic nor neophilic when choosing food items. Across studies, we observed a disconnect in which food dogs preferred to eat and which was chosen first, suggesting that some choice paradigms may not accurately capture preferences. These results have an impact on future canine science studies and experimental design, as well as dog training. Finally, this helps us to better understand how dogs make decisions and form preferences about the items in their environment.
