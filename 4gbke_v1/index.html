Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Prolonged military deployments expose personnel to sustained vigilance, disrupted sleep, uncertainty, repeated high-consequence decisions, separation from social support, and restricted opportunities for recovery. Military organizations routinely monitor physical fitness and medical readiness, but stress-related neurocognitive functioning remains assessed primarily through self-report, observation, performance outcomes, and clinical contact. These sources are indispensable, yet they may not reveal the physiological effort required to maintain performance or the capacity to recover after cognitive demand. This paper introduces NPRO-8 MIL, a military adaptation of the NPRO-8 Core mental-fitness screening framework. NPRO-8 MIL combines behavioral performance with functional near-infrared spectroscopy during five structured cognitive task modules. Eight NeuroProbes inform three higher-order pillars: Stress Load, Recovery Capacity, and Functional Impact. The underlying NPRO-8 Core has completed internal validation through 178 deterministic tests, 32,000 Monte Carlo examinations, 50,850 repeated robustness classifications, and parameter-sensitivity analyses. These findings establish computational coherence and predictable behavior, while prospective military reliability, external-validity, and predictive-validity studies remain necessary. Because fNIRS cannot feasibly be administered to every member of a large military population, a two-tier pathway is proposed: brief population-level digital screening followed by targeted NPRO-8 MIL assessment. Ethical safeguards addressing voluntariness, privacy, command access, purpose limitation, and protection from unauthorized selection or disciplinary use are integral to the framework. NPRO-8 MIL is presented as a screening and longitudinal monitoring approach, not a psychiatric diagnostic or autonomous fitness-for-duty instrument.

Prolonged Deployment and the Mental-Fitness Readiness Gap

Military readiness depends on people sustaining effective performance under conditions that are deliberately demanding. Prolonged deployments can combine extended duty hours, disrupted sleep and circadian rhythms, sustained vigilance, environmental constraint, uncertainty, separation from family and social support, and repeated exposure to high-consequence decisions. These demands do not imply that every deployed service member will develop a mental-health condition. They do create a setting in which load can accumulate, opportunities for recovery can narrow, and previously manageable demands can become progressively more difficult to regulate.

Fatigue is already recognized as a readiness concern in naval operations. The U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO, 2023) reported that sailors in the surface fleet averaged 6.26 hours available for sleep and 5.25 hours of sleep obtained, both below the Navy standard of at least 7.5 hours available for sleep. The GAO linked inadequate rest to health, safety, and readiness risks and identified heavy workloads and crewing shortfalls as continuing contributors. A Department of Defense review similarly concluded that sleep deprivation can impair attention, learning, executive function, mood, and operational performance (Department of Defense, 2021b). These findings support viewing recovery as a readiness variable rather than merely a matter of personal comfort.

The extended 2025-2026 deployment of the USS Abraham Lincoln carrier strike group provides one contemporary illustration. The Acting Secretary of the Navy stated that the deployment had been extended because of mission requirements and acknowledged that combat operations disrupt normal routines and intensify uncertainty (Department of the Navy, 2026). Congressional correspondence subsequently described more than 250 days of deployment and more than 200 consecutive days without a port call, while seeking formal information about fatigue, morale, mental health, and crew readiness (King et al., 2026). The Navy later confirmed eight suicide attempts across the wider strike group during the deployment (Gillibrand & Schneider, 2026). These reports must not be treated as evidence that deployment duration caused those attempts, nor do they imply that an objective screening instrument would have predicted or prevented them. They illustrate a broader problem: organizations may have limited systematic information about accumulating strain and recovery before concerns become visible through self-report, clinical presentation, performance failure, or a serious event.

The relevant scientific question is therefore broader than any one ship or deployment: how can military organizations monitor stress-related neurocognitive functioning and recovery early enough to support personnel, training, and readiness without converting normal responses to difficult operations into psychiatric labels? Answering this question requires a framework that distinguishes exposure from impairment, present performance from compensatory effort, and temporary fatigue from a persistent failure to recover.

From Physical Readiness to Objective Mental-Fitness Measurement

The military already uses preventive measurement extensively. Department of Defense policy requires a Periodic Health Assessment every 12 months. The standardized process combines self-report, medical-record review, preventive screening, a person-to-person mental-health assessment, and evaluation of medical and deployment readiness (Department of Defense, 2025). DD Form 3024 formalizes the annual assessment and its role in evaluating health, force readiness, and suitability for deployment (Department of Defense, 2021a). The same policy links the annual process to required deployment-related health assessments conducted at defined stages (Department of Defense, 2025). Thus, military readiness is already supported by repeated measurement rather than waiting for a severe physical event to reveal an underlying problem.

Blood-pressure screening offers a useful analogy. Blood pressure does not diagnose every cardiovascular disorder, and a single elevated reading does not establish that a heart attack will occur. Its value is that it provides an objective physiological indicator that can reveal risk, prompt repeat measurement, and direct proportionate follow-up before a catastrophic outcome. The analogy does not imply that mental fitness can be reduced to one biomarker. It shows why preventive systems combine accessible screening, objective measurement, professional interpretation, and follow-up.

Mental and behavioral health are not absent from military assessment. Annual mental-health assessment and deployment health screening already provide important opportunities to identify symptoms and facilitate care (Department of Defense, 2025). The remaining gap concerns objective functional measurement. Questionnaires depend on recognition, recall, interpretation, and willingness to disclose. Clinical interviews are appropriately focused on health needs but cannot be repeated continuously across an operational population. Command observation and performance metrics identify visible behavior, yet acceptable performance can be maintained through increasing compensatory effort. A service member may therefore perform correctly while requiring progressively greater neurocognitive recruitment, or may recover more slowly after demand without immediately showing an overt error.

Objective brain-based measurement can contribute a complementary layer. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) uses near-infrared light to estimate changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin in superficial cortical tissue. Portable systems can measure prefrontal hemodynamic responses during structured tasks in settings that are less constrained than conventional neuroimaging laboratories (Ayaz et al., 2012; Pinti et al., 2018). fNIRS does not read thoughts, intentions, memories, political beliefs, or hidden motives. It provides a physiological measure whose meaning depends on the task contrast, behavioral performance, data quality, systemic physiology, and context (Tachtsidis & Scholkmann, 2016; Yücel et al., 2021). The measurement sequence is illustrated in Figure 1. The purpose is consequently not to replace self-report or professional judgment with a brain signal. It is to examine how a person functions under controlled demand, what physiological effort accompanies that performance, and how readily the response recovers. This distinction is central to NPRO-8 MIL.

NPRO-8 MIL Objective Neurocognitive Assessment

Measurement Architecture

In practical terms, NPRO-8 MIL asks three operational questions: How much neurocognitive demand is present? How well does the measured response settle after demand? Is the pattern accompanied by a change in functional performance? A service member completes short cognitive tasks while a wearable fNIRS sensor records prefrontal oxygenation. The analysis combines the physiological response with accuracy and response time, then organizes the result into three pillars. The technical architecture below explains how those questions are operationalized.

NPRO-8 MIL is the proposed military application of the NPRO-8 Core. It is a within-person assessment system combining computerized cognitive tasks, behavioral performance, and prefrontal fNIRS. Rather than relying exclusively on a comparison with population norms, the method examines prespecified contrasts within the examination and evaluates the size of relevant changes in relation to the individual's observed variability (Rotgans, 2026a (Rotgans, , 2026b)). This approach is intended to make the assessment sensitive to the pattern of demand, response, and recovery within the person being assessed.

The examination contains five short cognitive task modules derived from established experimental paradigms. The modules create demands involving sustained engagement and response inhibition, interference control and selective visual attention, cognitive flexibility following a rule change, rule integration and working-memory manipulation, and processing of unexpected or inconsistent feedback. These functions are central components of executive control and goal-directed behavior (Diamond, 2013). Behavioral accuracy and response speed are recorded while fNIRS measures associated prefrontal hemodynamic responses.

Eight NeuroProbes

Within NPRO-8, a NeuroProbe is a prespecified experimental contrast designed to challenge and quantify a neurocognitive function. It is not an isolated sensor channel, a questionnaire item, or a stand-alone diagnosis. Each probe combines a controlled task contrast, behavioral performance, and the associated fNIRS response (Rotgans, 2026b). The eight core NeuroProbes are summarized below (see Table 1).

Three Pillars

The NeuroProbe and module-level findings are organized into three higher-order pillars: (1) Stress Load, (2) Recovery Capacity, and (3) Functional Impact. The pillars are related but intentionally distinct. Stress Load represents evidence of elevated physiological or neurocognitive demand during baseline and task conditions. Recovery Capacity represents the extent to which measured activity stabilizes or returns toward baseline following demand, consistent with the broader occupational-health distinction between exposure and recuperation (Sonnentag & Fritz, 2007). Functional Impact represents evidence that load or impaired recovery is accompanied by meaningful alteration in task-related neurocognitive functioning (Rotgans, 2026a). The three-pillar architecture is summarized in Figure 2.

Separating the pillars prevents a single stress score from obscuring different functional profiles. High load can coexist with preserved performance. Preserved performance can be achieved through unusually high neural recruitment, suggesting compensation. Poor recovery can occur before overt performance loss. Conversely, reduced performance with low activation might reflect fatigue, disengagement, inadequate task understanding, or another explanation rather than excessive stress. Interpretation therefore requires the combined pattern, behavioral results, data quality, and context. Screening Output and Longitudinal Use

Each pillar and the final output are classified Green, Amber, or Red according to prespecified deterministic rules. Green represents the least concerning classification range, Amber an intermediate or uncertain finding, and Red the most concerning range within the screening system. These categories are follow-up priorities, not probabilities of illness. Amber is especially important as a zone in which evidence may be mixed, incomplete, or sensitive to measurement variation and may therefore call for contextual review or repeat assessment rather than a conclusion of impairment (Rotgans, 2026b).

The framework is designed for repeated measurement. A single examination describes the measured state under specified conditions; a trajectory can show whether Stress Load is increasing, Recovery Capacity is normalizing, or Functional Impact is changing over time. Continuous measures, proximity to decision boundaries, and data-quality indicators should remain available rather than reducing longitudinal interpretation to color changes alone. Internal Validation of the NPRO-8 Core

Before a software-based assessment can be evaluated against field outcomes, it must be shown to implement its own analytical specification correctly and to behave predictably when inputs, noise, data availability, and parameters vary. The internal validation of NPRO-8 Core therefore examined computational validity rather than attempting to establish diagnostic accuracy or prediction of operational outcomes (Rotgans, 2026b).

Deterministic Rule Verification

A suite of 178 prespecified tests evaluated the input contract, epsilon calculations, minimum evidence requirements, exact decision boundaries, module rules, pillar classifications, and final report aggregation. All 178 tests passed. This establishes that the tested software version performed the calculations required by its analytical specification.

Monte Carlo Known-State Simulation

Monte Carlo simulation generated synthetic NPRO-8 examinations representing stable profiles and combinations of elevated Stress Load, reduced Recovery Capacity, and Functional Impact. The complete generated classification was recovered in all 8,000 zero-noise control examinations. Across the full 32,000 examinations, noise was introduced at four levels. Detection of generated concerning profiles remained high, while increasing perturbation produced progressively more Amber and Red classifications among generated stable profiles. This behavior is consistent with a conservative three-pillar roll-up in which the most concerning pillar determines the overall result.

Perfect zero-noise recovery demonstrates internal consistency, not external construct validity. The generating states were defined using the same conceptual framework implemented by the classifier. Simulation-based sensitivity and specificity therefore cannot be translated into clinical predictive values or treated as estimates of performance in service members.

Robustness and Parameter Sensitivity

A further 50,850 repeated classifications mapped stability at different distances from the decision boundaries and under different levels of noise. Stability depended strongly on distance from the boundary and on the magnitude of noise relative to epsilon, the within-person variability parameter. Canonical event ordering produced no changes across 7,200 randomized event-table comparisons. Missing channels, loss of event markers, and use of the fallback analysis pathway altered some lower-level module or pillar classifications, although the overall result often remained unchanged because another Red pillar continued to determine the final classification.

Parameter-sensitivity analysis showed that the Functional Impact requirement for a Red classification and the adaptive epsilon multiplier were among the most influential design choices. The analysis did not support changing the production configuration. This does not establish that the current settings are uniquely optimal; it means that no alternative had sufficient independent evidential support to justify replacing the prespecified settings.

Empirical Basis and Next Evidentiary Step

Six opportunistically collected empirical sessions anchored plausible feature ranges and provided realistic examples of signal magnitude, behavioral variability, missing channels, and event-marker structure. They were not a representative military or occupational sample and cannot support norms, subgroup analyses, or estimates of diagnostic accuracy. One participant completed a short-interval repeat assessment, offering only a limited single-case indication of stability. The appropriate conclusion is therefore positive but bounded: NPRO-8 Core has a substantial internally validated computational foundation and is ready for prospective reliability and external-validation studies.

The Scalability Constraint and the Two-Tier Solution

Once the potential value of objective neurocognitive measurement is recognized, an immediate practical limitation becomes apparent. A military organization cannot routinely fit every member of a carrier crew, brigade, air wing, or installation with an fNIRS device. Sensor fitting, signal-quality checks, task administration, cleaning, and interpretation require trained personnel and time. Universal fNIRS assessment would be inefficient and, in many settings, operationally impossible.

The proposed solution is not to abandon objective measurement but to target it. NPRO-8 MIL should be embedded in a two-tier pathway modeled on preventive screening: a rapid, accessible first tier identifies people or measurement occasions for which the more intensive second tier is likely to be informative (Rotgans, 2026c). The two tiers describe assessment intensity; they do not replace existing medical, mental-health, safety, or command-support systems. The military screening and follow-up pathway is summarized in Table 3.

Tier 1 Broad Digital Screening

Tier 1 is a two-to three-minute digital screen combining brief self-report with behavioral measures. It can be offered at planned intervals rather than only after a complaint or visible performance decline. Potential measurement points include pre-deployment baseline, scheduled intervals during prolonged deployment, periods following unusually intense operations, return from deployment, and post-deployment recovery.

Green, Amber, and Red outputs would indicate provisional follow-up priorities. Green would lead to routine monitoring; Amber to contextual review and possible repeat screening; and Red to timely confidential contact and consideration of Tier 2 or conventional professional assessment. These categories require prospective military calibration. Self-referral, peer concern, medical referral, and urgent safety pathways must remain available regardless of Tier 1 status so that a brief screen never becomes a barrier to care.

Tier 2 Targeted NPRO-8 MIL Assessment

Tier 2 is the approximately ten-minute fNIRS and cognitive assessment, with additional time for explanation, consent, fitting, signal checks, cleaning, and discussion. Possible indications include Amber or Red Tier 1 results, deterioration across repeated screens, persistent recovery concerns, voluntary request, inclusion in a defined high-exposure cohort, or evaluation of a recovery intervention. The Tier 2 result should be considered alongside the service member's account, sleep and duty context, medical information when appropriately available, and relevant operational circumstances. Disagreement between sources should not automatically be resolved in favor of the physiological measure. Preserved self-reported functioning with elevated neurocognitive demand may reflect compensation, transient context, measurement error, or an emerging vulnerability. High reported strain with a non-concerning fNIRS profile may reflect demands not reproduced by the assessment, symptoms outside the measured constructs, or limits of the instrument. Discordance is a reason for inquiry, not for dismissing either source. An illustrative repeated-measurement and intervention cycle is shown in Figure 4.

Military Validation and Phased Implementation

Implementation should proceed through staged validation rather than immediate fleet-wide adoption. The first phase should establish feasibility, acceptability, completion rates, fNIRS data yield, refusal patterns, time requirements, and logistical burden in a small military sample. The second should estimate test-retest reliability under stable conditions at the NeuroProbe, module, pillar, and overall levels. Contextual variables such as sleep, caffeine, medication, time of day, prior duty, pain, and recent exercise should be recorded.

A third phase should follow personnel longitudinally across deployment and recovery. Measurements should include a pre-deployment baseline, prespecified deployment intervals, high-intensity operational periods when feasible, return, and post-deployment recovery. Independent comparators might include validated stress and recovery instruments, sleep and duty schedules, psychomotor vigilance, task performance, expert assessment, health-service contact, and prospectively defined functional outcomes. No single comparator should be assumed to constitute an infallible criterion for stress.

A fourth phase should evaluate incremental validity: whether Tier 2 adds meaningful information beyond Tier 1, standard questionnaires, behavioral performance, sleep measures, and existing readiness processes. Algorithms and decision thresholds should remain fixed during primary validation and any recalibration should be tested in an independent sample. Multisite work should examine transportability across services, occupations, sex, age, rank, and relevant demographic groups.

Finally, decision-utility research must determine whether the pathway improves outcomes. Relevant endpoints include earlier recognition of independently confirmed difficulty, time to appropriate support, recovery monitoring, training modification, and identification of excessively demanding systems or schedules. Potential harms-including unnecessary referrals, missed concerns, stigma, reduced trust, and career consequences-must be measured alongside potential benefit.

Military Ethics and Governance

Voluntariness Within a Hierarchy

Military hierarchy creates a distinctive consent problem. Participation may be formally voluntary while a service member reasonably believes that refusal could affect reputation, evaluation, assignment, or career. The appropriate response is not to deny this pressure but to design procedures that make refusal practically possible and socially safe. Consent should be managed outside the direct command line; personnel should not have to refuse publicly; and participation or refusal should not be recorded as a performance or behavioral problem (Rotgans Research, 2026).

Privacy, Access, and Purpose Limitation

Individual raw fNIRS data and profiles should not be routinely available to supervisors. During research and early validation, organizational reports should ordinarily be aggregated, anonymized, or focused on task and system demands. Individual feedback should be provided privately and only under an agreed protocol. The data should not be repurposed for promotion, selection, discipline, or deployability decisions without a separate evidentiary and ethical basis, appropriate authorization, and transparent policy.

Data governance should specify who controls the raw data, how identifiers are separated, who may access the code key, retention periods, withdrawal procedures, cybersecurity controls, and conditions for secondary use. Data minimization is especially important because neurophysiological data may be perceived as more revealing than their actual scientific content.

Not Mind Reading and Not Diagnosis

fNIRS measures hemodynamic changes in superficial cortical areas during specified tasks. It cannot reveal thoughts, memories, loyalties, beliefs, or concealed intentions. NPRO-8 MIL is not intended to diagnose posttraumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety, burnout, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, or any other medical or psychiatric condition. Nor should it function autonomously as a psychological selection or fitnessfor-duty instrument.

The Positive Ethical Case

Objective measurement may also offer ethical benefits. Observable behavior does not always show why a task is difficult. Two personnel can produce the same correct response while one does so efficiently and the other through unusually high neurocognitive recruitment. A system that makes this difference visible may support fairer training, more appropriate recovery, safer watch schedules, better interfaces, and improved automation. The ethical objective is therefore not to determine whether a person is suitable, but to understand where demands exceed current resources and how training, systems, or support can be improved.

Discussion

NPRO-8 MIL addresses a specific gap between broad mental-health screening and detailed laboratory neurocognitive assessment. Its central proposition is that military mental fitness should be examined as a dynamic pattern of load, recovery, and functioning rather than inferred from symptoms or observable performance alone. This proposition is consistent with models of allostatic load, effort-recovery, work ability, and executive function, all of which distinguish exposure from adaptation and adaptation from functional consequence (McEwen, 1998; Meijman & Mulder, 1998; Rotgans, 2026a).

The framework is especially relevant to prolonged deployment because performance can be preserved for some time through compensation. A service member may continue to meet standards while the physiological effort required increases and recovery becomes less complete. Conversely, a temporary high-load response during a difficult task may be appropriate and should not be pathologized. By separating Stress Load, Recovery Capacity, and Functional Impact, NPRO-8 MIL creates a structure for distinguishing these patterns.

The internal-validation evidence is an important strength. The NPRO-8 Core is not entering military research as an untested scoring idea: its programmed rules, known-state recovery, robustness, event ordering, and parameter dependence have been examined systematically. At the same time, computational correctness is not the same as field validity. The Monte Carlo evidence cannot establish that a Red profile corresponds to actual operational impairment or that a Green profile predicts safety. Those questions require prospective military data and independent outcomes.

The two-tier architecture makes the proposal operationally plausible. Broad screening supplies reach; targeted fNIRS supplies depth. Repeated measurements allow trajectory-based interpretation rather than reliance on a single state. Importantly, the pathway should augment rather than displace existing medical and behavioral-health systems. Urgent concerns require conventional safety and clinical responses irrespective of a screening result.

The eight NeuroProbes should be regarded as the validated core architecture. Additional military-specific probes could later address divided attention, threat monitoring, vigilance decrement, spatial working memory, prospective memory, multitasking, automation monitoring, alarm prioritization, team coordination, or recovery after sustained watchkeeping. Each extension would require a defined task contrast, internal verification, reliability analysis, and independent empirical validation before inclusion in operational reporting. NPRO-8 MIL therefore offers a research and decision-support framework for objective assessment of stress-related neurocognitive functioning. Its appropriate next step is a carefully governed prospective military program establishing feasibility, test-retest reliability, external validity, sensitivity to deployment and recovery, incremental value, and decision utility. If those stages are successful, the framework could complement established readiness systems by making the neurocognitive cost of sustained performance and the capacity for recovery more visible.

Conclusion

Military organizations measure physical and medical readiness repeatedly because important risks cannot be managed solely by waiting for symptoms or failure. Stress-related neurocognitive functioning presents an analogous, although more complex, measurement challenge. NPRO-8 MIL provides a structured approach combining behavioral performance and task-evoked fNIRS across eight NeuroProbes and three pillars: Stress Load, Recovery Capacity, and Functional Impact.

The underlying NPRO-8 Core has demonstrated computational coherence through deterministic testing, Monte Carlo known-state simulation, robustness analysis, and parameter-sensitivity testing. This foundation supports progression to prospective military research but does not substitute for it. A two-tier pathway-broad digital screening followed by targeted objective assessment-offers a feasible model for scale. Its legitimacy depends on rigorous validation and equally rigorous governance protecting voluntariness, privacy, purpose limitation, and human review. Used within those boundaries, NPRO-8 MIL may help military organizations identify accumulating strain, incomplete recovery, and emerging functional vulnerability earlier and more objectively.
