Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Abstract

Art viewing in museums inherently relies on physical movement through galleries. While research has begun to examine movement in art perception, it has focused mainly on abstract paintings and small-scale case studies. We investigated how bodily movement, gaze behaviour and memory interact with a mobile-eye-tracking study in Vienna's Leopold Museum (145 visitors viewing 66 paintings across six rooms). The results show that a) although visitors increased their viewing distance for larger paintings, they did not maintain a constant visual angle, b) visitors repositioned themselves mainly laterally in front of paintings, and c) they frequently compared neighbouring artworks by repeating gazes between paintings. Longer viewing times and repeated returns to a painting were associated with better subsequent memory. These findings suggest that the museum's "white cube" is not a neutral backdrop for independent encounters but a spatially organised environment that shapes horizontal exploration as well as visual comparison between adjacent works.

1. Introduction

The perception of painting in the gallery has historically been inseparable from movement. Long before the existence of the museum in its modern institutional form, spaces for the display of art were conceived in relation to the moving body and to sequential acts of looking. Bianchi (2023) traces the origin of the gallery to Vitruvius's ambulationes: elongated porticoes designed for walking in nature and, occasionally, among sculptures; a spatial model later adopted in the Baroque palaces of sixteenth-and seventeenth-century Rome (Furlotti, 2015). In private noble houses, the earliest galleries were fundamentally spaces for walking, with paintings providing visual interest, display of wealth, and opportunities for conversation along the route (Girouard, 2019).

With the emergence of the public museum as a didactic institution, visitors' movement through galleries came to be understood as a means of acquiring and organising knowledge. This relationship remained a persistent feature of the museum despite its constant transformations throughout history. In the late eighteenth-century organisation of the imperial collection in Vienna, Christian von Mechel was the first to arrange the galleries of the Belvedere according to chronological and geographical criteria (Swoboda, 2014). This resulted in a "visible history of art" that visitors could apprehend as they moved through the collection (Bazin, 1967; MacGregor, 2007). The same principle remained operative in modern and postmodern museums, despite the shift from densely packed walls towards simpler viewing environments (Klonk, 2009). For MoMA's 1939 exhibition Art in Our Time, the museum space became a medium to communicate the development of modern art both chronologically and conceptually. Major works occupied prominent positions along the principal route, while works considered peripheral were placed in secondary galleries. Visitor circulation thus functioned as an interpretative mechanism, translating art-historical classifications into a physical experience of progression while also allowing for deviations from the canonical sequence (Fernie, 1995).

Besides movement in the gallery, movement in front of an individual artwork can also transform the perceptual appearance of the work itself-a phenomenon which has an equally long history in art criticism. Already in 1568, discussing Titian's paintings, Vasari explicitly connects pictorial effect to the viewer's physical distance, observing that they were executed with bold strokes and a "broad and even coarse sweep of the brush, " so that "from near little can be seen, but from a distance they appear perfect" (Vasari, 1915, p. 174). Diderot makes the relationship between movement and pictorial appearance even more explicit in his discussion of Chardin in the Salon de 1763. Attempting to account for the painter's technique, he describes an image that alternately dissolves and reconstitutes itself as the observer changes viewing distance: "You can't understand this magic [. . . ] Come closer, everything becomes blurred, flattened and disappears. Step back and everything comes together and reappears" (Diderot, 1763 (Diderot, /1984, p. 220), p. 220). Similar descriptions can be encountered more and more often in art history from the second half of the nineteenth century onward, as avant-garde movements increasingly tested different ways to enhance the visibility of brush strokes. The Impressionists discussed the issue of viewing artworks dynamically almost scientifically. They claimed to ensure the optimal perception of fragmented colour, which would mix "on the retina" rather than on the canvas. Brushstrokes of pure colour would appear separate from each other from up close but blend seamlessly from a distance; hence paintings could often shift from confusion to clarity as viewers moved backwards (Webster, 1944). This also meant that details were rendered only in the general sense in favour of the overall impression, causing a great perceptual dissonance according to viewing position (Hemmings, 1958).

Movement, whether in the gallery, in the museum or in front of an individual artwork, can therefore be understood as a constitutive component of art perception. At the exhibition level, movement is theorised to help viewers establish sequences, comparisons, and hierarchies between artworks; at the scale of the individual object, approaching and withdrawing alter the visual information available to the observer.

In the empirical study of perception the question of dynamic viewing has already been addressed in mobile eye-tracking investigations. However, current research is built on case studies, which share some limitations in terms of spaces, types of artworks and numbers of participants.

2. Literature review

The study of movement in the museum is at the centre of different intersecting research clusters as it inevitably involves variables such as artwork properties (size and visibility), display (arrangement of the museum or gallery space), visitor profiles (individual visiting styles and preferences), and more (Griswold et al., 2013). A comprehensive and practice-oriented literature review on the topic of movements in gallery spaces has been compiled by Kühnapfel et al. (2024). For the sake of the present study, we will concentrate on three strands of research, which all concern the movements specifically performed in front of an artwork while looking at said artwork (dynamic viewing). The existing literature on dynamic viewing can be broadly organised into three main areas: (1) investigating the relationship between artwork size and viewing distance, (2) investigating viewers' repositioning in front of artworks, and (3) investigating the role of movement in art perception and processing.

2.1. Artwork size and viewing distance

One of the most intuitive assumptions in the investigation of dynamic viewing is that a viewer's elective distance is heavily influenced by artwork size. For example, Clarke et al. (1984) found that if the viewing distance is at participants' discretion (condition: a 5 × 5.6 m room), it increases with artwork size (stimuli included a selection of either 175 × 117 cm artworks vs. 67 × 46 cm artworks). Similarly, Locher et al. (2001) observed a linear relationship between viewing distance and painting size, this time in an ecologically valid setting: the Metropolitan Museum in New York, where participants viewed nine different representational paintings. Similarly, Yang and Kawabata (2023) reported a positive correlation between canvas size and viewing distance, with an added dimension: taller canvases requiring greater viewing distances than wider canvases of the same area.

Elective or preferred distance is often associated with subjective ratings and artwork preference; research tends to suggest that artworks are best appreciated from one or more self-selected, rather than imposed, distances. For instance, Carbon (2020) investigated viewing distance in relation to abstract art (canvas reproductions of Pollock and Rothko) in two conditions: from eight assigned distances and from a range of participants' own preferred distances. Among other results, the study reports that self-chosen distance had higher average ratings for liking, interestingness, and emotional value than assigned distance, and that freedom to choose viewing distance was associated with more positive responses. This was possible because elective distances allowed closer scrutiny of some of the artworks. Similarly, Kühnapfel et al. (2024) reported that a closer viewing distance to a painting is associated with higher subjective ratings of the artwork (Franz Marc's painting Kämpfende Formen) for the attributes of meaningful and interesting; participants who spent time closer to paintings in a gallery-like setting also reported feeling more stimulated by the artworks they saw. Yang and Kawabata (2024) also report correlations between viewing distance and subsequent evaluation of a painting.

2.2. Dynamic repositioning within artwork encounters

Viewing distance in art viewing has been examined not only as a set variable but also as a temporally unfolding aspect, specifically to monitor repositioning during individual encounters with paintings. Kapoula et al. (2011) used posturography to continuously record centre-of-pressure excursions while observers viewed paintings, showing that pictorial depth could modulate anteriorposterior and lateral body sway. Ganczarek et al. (2015) similarly combined measurements of body sway with eye tracking, allowing bodily dynamics to be related to fixation location within the pictorial space; greater attention to visually distant regions of paintings was associated with increased sway.

In terms of more articulate movement, two recent studies examine individual artwork encounters. Kühnapfel et al. (2024) continuously tracked freely moving viewers while they engaged with an artwork in a gallery-like setting. Using infrared depth cameras, they recorded standing and walking positions, including movement towards and away from the artwork and lateral repositioning, without constraining participants' movement, and combined these measures with mobile eye tracking. Linden and Wagemans (2024) used mobile eye-tracking recordings to code museum behaviour over time, distinguishing stationary orientation from bodily "changing perspective" behaviours such as approaching an artwork, pulling back, viewing it from the side, and repositioning directly in front of it, to establish a taxonomy of movement as a sequence of observable behaviours. Art interest and expertise seem to predict movement in front of paintings (less "strolling along", less "centering").

2.3. Movement coupled with art processing

Estrada-Gonzalez et al. (2020) used mobile eye-tracking to assess the viewing distance of visitors combined with fixation duration while they freely viewed 15 paintings in the Art Gallery of New South Wales, where participants could move around, and in the laboratory, where they were sitting at a fixed location. The study employed Indigenous and non-Indigenous art; the subjects were mostly abstract and geometrical patterns. Average fixation duration was impacted by painting type, a trend that was more pronounced in the museum setting than in the laboratory. However, the effect of the on-screen presentation condition, as opposed to the museum condition, on fixation duration was not significant. Similarly, Gulhan et al. (2021) compared the gallery setting with a virtual reconstruction in VR, reporting mainly similarities in dwell time and viewing durations and some significant differences in fixation durations. As long as the movement conditions are similar and participants are able to interact with the (albeit digital) space, the study indicates no major differences between digital reproductions and real artworks-in fact, it claims that VR spaces can be considered "ecologically valid" settings. Finally, Kühnapfel et al. (2024) report that exploration and repositioning around the artwork were associated with more insightful and more positive engagement.

2.4. Gaps in the Literature and Research Questions

Together, the results of these studies suggest that dynamic viewing is an organic and relevant part of the experience of art. Size and distance appear closely linked; self-chosen distances enhance engagement and appreciation, and close-range viewing seems to be associated with more stimulating visual encounters. Yet, these investigations share some limits: all eye-tracking results describe the perception of abstract artworks (Vermeersch, Rothko, Pollock, American Abstract, Australian Aboriginal, Mondrian, Franz Marc's later production) and do not specifically take into account figurative paintings. However, subject visibility changes according to the beholder's position, and stimulus content such as faces and nudity often redirects the beholder's gaze (Birmingham & Kingstone, 2009; Miscenà et al., 2024). Moreover, eye-tracking results were always based on a relatively small number of participants (< 40).

The present study builds on this work by examining such dynamics across unrestricted encounters with multiple figurative artworks in an actual museum, separately quantifying changes in viewing distance and lateral position and relating these changes to concurrent gaze behaviour.

In the present study, RQ1 asked how artwork scale relates to viewing distance: whether visitors compensate for differences in artwork size by adjusting their position, and whether other artwork characteristics further explain viewing distance. RQ2 examined how visitors reposition themselves during single encounters with paintings, focusing on the relative extent of in-out movement and lateral movement as well as repositioning patterns across artworks of different sizes, and the relationship between viewing distance and gaze allocation. RQ3 asked whether viewing behaviour including movement and viewing distance is related to fixation duration over time and to the later memory of artworks.

To address these questions, we conducted a mobile eye-tracking study followed by a questionnaire, across six rooms of the Leopold Museum, encompassing 77 artworks, 68 of them planar wall-mounted works. Two of the planar works were not paintings (a framed poem and an exhibition poster) and were excluded from analysis, leaving 66 paintings. Participants had complete freedom of movement and no time restrictions. For each of the exhibited paintings we monitored participants' viewing distance, viewing time and fixation duration.

3. Methods

The Leopold Museum, housing the estate of Rudolf and Elisabeth Leopold, is organised on four floors. The paintings of Richard Gerstl and Egon Schiele make up half of the third floor: two rooms are dedicated to Gerstl and four rooms to Schiele. The works of the two Viennese artists are curated in a monographic fashion, with informative texts illustrating their life, together with letters, photographs and memorabilia displayed in glass cases. Nine sculptures, a framed poem, an exhibition poster, and two paintings by other avant-garde artists (Arnold Schönberg and Max Oppenheimer) were also included in the exhibition at the time of our study (June 2022); however, these represent a relatively small percentage of the artworks included in the six rooms (total n = 77, of which 66 paintings; Figure 1). The collection is displayed in a "white cube" fashion, on white walls, evenly and artificially lit.

Due to the structure of the gallery space, viewers are compelled to walk through all six rooms (Gerstl, then Schiele) as they make their way through the third floor; however, they can freely go back and revisit rooms they have already passed through. The route recommended by the museum begins on the fourth floor and heads downwards towards the ground floor. This implies that before the Gerstl and Schiele exhibitions, visitors have usually already seen one floor of the museum and are also likely to remain in the building to visit the rest.

3.1. Participants

We recruited a total of 234 participants, of whom 225 completed an eye-tracking study and a follow-up questionnaire. A total of 145 eye-tracking datasets were selected for analysis. The selection was made on the basis of data quality (percentage of detected gaze reported by the Tobii glasses > 90%). Of these, 142 participants completed the questionnaire: 74 identified as female, 67 as male, and 1 did not specify; they were on average 34 years old (min = 18, max = 71, SD = 13.5). Most were tourists (123 vs. 18 Viennese residents, 1 unspecified) and most were visiting with a companion or in a group (113 vs. 26 alone, 3 unspecified). Participants were required to have normal/corrected-to-normal vision in order to join the study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Vienna (approval number EK00840).

3.2. Materials

Eye-tracking data collection was performed with two sets of Tobii Pro Glasses 3 (Tobii Technology, Stockholm, Sweden, www.tobii.com). The glasses were paired with external power banks to ensure battery durability. Recordings were exported from Tobii Pro Lab (1.194), whose I-VT filter supplied the fixation events; gaze was assigned to paintings with the computer-vision pipeline described in the Analysis section. The questionnaire was displayed on a tablet screen.

3.3. Procedure

The study ran in the month of June 2022 for three consecutive weeks during regular opening hours. It comprised two phases: an eye-tracking experiment and a follow-up questionnaire. The eye-tracking phase took place in six rooms on the third floor of the museum; these rooms display the works of Richard Gerstl (room 1 and room 2) and Egon Schiele (rooms 3, 4, 5, 6).

Museum visitors were greeted by researchers on the third floor of the Leopold Museum (room 14a on the map, see Figure 1), where they were introduced to the study in either German or English and asked for their voluntary participation. Upon agreement, they were asked to fill out a consent form, handed the eye-tracking device, and given a short technical briefing about the glasses to avoid interruptions during data collection (instructed not to touch or move the glasses, not to take them off by themselves).

A calibration was performed with a Tobii marked target held at arm's length (0.5 to 1 m). After successful calibration, participants were asked to continue their visit normally; they were told that a researcher would collect them at the end of the gallery on the same floor and that they would then need to fill out a questionnaire regarding their experience. The layout of the museum ensured that participants would take the same route and enter the six rooms in the same order. The room in which the recruiting took place was considered an acclimatisation room, to allow for some adjustment time before data recording.

During their visit, researchers did not observe the participants, who could mix with the rest of the public in the gallery space. They were given as much time as necessary to ensure that the data could reflect a normal museum experience. Although participants were aware of the eye-tracking glasses, a majority reported having forgotten about the device at some point during the visit (88 of 137 respondents).

Participants handed in the glasses and were escorted by a researcher into the museum atrium, where they were handed a tablet with a questionnaire assessing the experience of the six rooms they had just visited. The questionnaire asked "From the rooms you have just visited, which artwork do you remember the strongest?" to which participants could answer by recollecting their encounters descriptively, without being shown the artworks again. Following the open answer, they were asked to select the thumbnails of the artworks they described: "Please indicate which artwork you are referring to in the previous question" (multiple selections allowed).

3.4. Analysis of eye-tracking results

All analyses were based on viewing geometry reconstructed directly from the scene-camera recordings. A computer-vision pipeline described in detail in a companion methods paper (manuscript submitted for publication; the reference is withheld while that paper is under anonymous review) identified paintings in the head-camera video and reconstructed, for each matched fixation, the viewer's standing position on the museum floor and viewing distance from the canvas. This provided both fixation-level viewing distance and two-dimensional standing-position information, allowing viewing behaviour to be analysed in terms of distance from the artwork as well as movement across the floor plane.

Three measures are used throughout the analyses. Viewing Distance (VD) is the distance in metres from the viewer's eye to the canvas; Viewing Time (VT) is the summed duration of fixations confidently matched to a given painting; and Fixation Duration (FD) is the duration of a single fixation, which enters the analyses fixation by fixation and is never summed or averaged per painting. Because VT includes only gaze that could be confidently assigned to an artwork, it represents a matched lower bound on total viewing time. Each painting was therefore also associated with a matchability measure, defined as the proportion of the gaze directed at that painting that the computer-vision pipeline could successfully assign to it, estimated on the groundtruth recordings described in the companion paper. Matchability ranged from 0.26 to 0.91. For the least matchable painting, VT therefore captures about a quarter of the time actually spent looking at it, for the most matchable about nine tenths. Matchability was taken into account in analyses involving VT and, where relevant, as a potential predictor of other measures.

The dataset comprised three linked levels of observation: 155,609 fixations successfully matched to paintings, drawn from 582,096 recorded fixations across 145 museum visits; 7,219 visitor × painting encounters; and 66 paintings. An encounter was defined as one visitor viewing one painting, while an episode was defined as one contiguous viewing bout within an encounter. Encounter-level VD was calculated as the duration-weighted median of the constituent fixations. Throughout, individual works are identified by the room in which they hang and a letter distinguishing the works within that room (e.g., 4d hangs in room 4).

Unless otherwise specified, inferential analyses were conducted using mixed-effects models fitted across paintings and participants simultaneously. Crossed random effects for participant and painting accounted for repeated observations from the same visitors and systematic differences among artworks. Standardised regression coefficients (β) are reported where appropriate, together with 95% confidence intervals.

For analyses concerned with participants' moment-to-moment positioning rather than absolute differences among artworks, VD was additionally expressed relative to each participant's habitual viewing distance (habit-controlled VD). Habitual distance was defined as the median of the participant's per-painting median viewing distances, with dispersion estimated robustly as IQR/1.349. Habit-controlled VD was expressed both in standardised units (denoted z) and, where useful for interpretation, in centimetres relative to the participant's habitual position.

Movement during viewing was represented primarily using continuous measures. Gross floor path was defined as the summed step-to-step ground distance travelled within an episode and therefore captured both lateral movement and approach/withdrawal movement. The lateral range and the in-out range of an encounter quantified the extent of repositioning parallel to the wall and along the approach-withdrawal axis, respectively. Categorical descriptions of movement were used principally for visualisation and descriptive comparisons rather than as substitutes for these continuous inferential measures.

4.1. RQ1. Viewing distance and artwork scale a) Size and distance

Artwork size was tested as a predictor of viewing distance at both artwork and encounter levels, with additional models assessing artist, canvas shape, year, matchability, and the presence of faces and of nudity in the depicted subject. Viewing distance increased systematically with artwork size, defined as ℓ = √ area, but the relationship was sublinear. At the artwork level, the log-log relationship yielded an exponent of 0.65 (95% CI [0.61, 0.69], R² = 0.95; Figure 2). The corresponding exponent was 0.69 at the encounter level and remained 0.69 after adjustment for artist. Thus, viewing distance increased less than proportionally with artwork size: an artwork twice as large was viewed from approximately 1.6×, rather than 2×, the distance. This result refines previously reported approximately linear size-distance relationships (Clarke et al., 1984; Locher et al., 2001). Across the substantially wider range of artwork sizes examined here, the relationship showed clear sublinear scaling. The observed exponent matches the estimate of the companion methods paper on the full 68-work set of the same visits. Artwork size was also the only artwork-level feature that reliably accounted for variation in VD. Artist, canvas shape, year, and matchability did not explain additional variation once artwork size was taken into account. Raw correlations with habit-controlled viewing distance were +0.14 for canvas shape, +0.19 for year, and +0.53 for matchability; after controlling for artwork size, these associations decreased to -0.16, -0.09, and +0.18, respectively, and none was reliable. The difference between works by Schiele and Gerstl was similarly small both before (+0.01 z, p = 0.95) and after (+0.07 z, p = 0.12) adjustment for artwork size. The presence of faces and of nudity in the depicted subject likewise explained no additional variation: in an encounter-level model with a participant random effect and artwork size included, the coefficient on log viewing distance was +0.004 (95% CI [-0.013, +0.021]) for faces and -0.015 (95% CI [-0.034, +0.003]) for nudity, neither reliably different from zero. The absence of an independent matchability effect indicates that the observed size-distance relationship was not attributable to differences in how reliably individual artworks could be matched by the computer-vision pipeline.

b) Artwork-level deviations

Although artwork size accounted for most of the variation in typical viewing distance, individual works differed systematically from the general size-distance relationship. Artworklevel residuals had an SD of 11 cm and ranged from -26 to +39 cm.

Residuals from the overall size-distance relationship were used to identify paintings viewed unusually near or far relative to their physical size. Several works were viewed substantially closer than predicted from their physical size, including 6l Lovers (-26 cm), 4c Revelation (-24 cm), 6b Still Life with Books (-22 cm), and 6a Mother with Two Children (-21 cm). In contrast, 5k Crescent of Houses II (+39 cm) and 4b The Procession (+28 cm) were viewed from farther away than predicted.

Two of the 68 planar works differed from the remainder in medium: 5a was a framed poem and 6m an exhibition poster. Both were excluded from the analysis set, so all results reported here are based on the remaining 66 paintings. The exclusion is not merely taxonomic: in a preliminary 68-work analysis the poem was the closest-viewed work in the dataset by a wide margin, consistent with visitors approaching it in order to read it, a behaviour governed by legibility rather than by the pictorial viewing that is the subject of this study.

c) Angular size

Apparent angular size was calculated to test whether visitors increased viewing distance sufficiently with artwork size to maintain approximately constant visual size. Visitors did not increase viewing distance sufficiently to maintain a constant apparent size across artworks. Apparent angular size increased with artwork size according to approximately ℓ 0.33 (95% CI [0.30, 0.37]). Consequently, larger works occupied a greater proportion of the visual field despite being viewed from farther away.

Across the dataset, the smallest works subtended approximately 25 • of visual angle and the largest approximately 44 • , with a median of approximately 33 • . Visitors therefore compensated only partially for differences in physical artwork size: viewing distance increased as works became larger, but not enough to preserve a constant retinal or angular size.

d) Distance and viewing time

Viewing time was analysed in relation to viewing distance both between artworks and within individual visitor-artwork encounters, while accounting for matchability. The relationship between viewing distance and viewing time depended on the level of analysis (Figure 4). At the between-artwork level, works that were typically viewed from farther away also received more viewing time (Spearman's ρ = +0.46, p < 0.001). At the encounter level, however, the relationship reversed: when a visitor stood farther from an artwork than was typical for that visitor and artwork, the encounter tended to be shorter (β = -0.19).

This negative encounter-level association was robust across alternative model specifications. Controlling for artwork size yielded β = -0.21, while analyses using habit-controlled viewing distance produced estimates of β = -0.18 without and β = -0.20 with artwork size included. Across the four specifications, standardised coefficients therefore ranged from -0.18 to -0.21, with 95% confidence intervals approximately 0.07 units wide and all excluding zero (p values between 4 × 10 -25 and 3 × 10 -33 ; n = 5,471 encounters with at least five matched fixations).

The encounter-level effect was therefore statistically reliable but modest in magnitude.

Its consistency after adjustment for artwork size and participant-specific viewing habits indicates that it was not simply a consequence of the general size-distance relationship. The positive between-artwork association should be interpreted more cautiously because VT represents a matched lower bound on total viewing time and is therefore partly dependent on artwork matchability.

Larger works therefore elicited a greater proportion of episodes involving active repositioning, consistent with the broader lateral and in-out movement ranges observed for larger artworks.

These categories provide a descriptive summary of movement. Single-fixation episodes were classified as stationary by construction; consequently, the estimated stationary proportion is also affected by the lower bound imposed by fixation-to-artwork matchability.

b) Memory and viewing behaviour.

Whether a visitor later named an artwork as the one remembered most strongly (see Procedure) was related to viewing time, fixation count, returns, viewing distance, movement, interest, and prior knowledge to identify behavioural predictors of later recall. Viewing encounters were typically brief (Figure 11), with a median matched viewing time of 2.7 s and a median of 12 matched fixations. Nevertheless, 34.5% of encounters comprised more than one viewing episode, indicating that visitors frequently left an artwork and subsequently returned to it. Returning encounters accumulated substantially more viewing time than single-episode encounters, with a median of 5.0 s compared with 1.8 s, corresponding to approximately 2.7× as much viewing time. Across the dataset, individual encounters contained as many as 15 separate viewing episodes.

Memory responses were then linked directly to the corresponding eye-tracking encounters. For participants with matched behavioural and questionnaire data, 292 encounters corresponded to artworks that the visitor later named as remembered. Memory was modelled as a binary outcome using crossed random effects for visitor and artwork, with artwork size controlled, over the 5,373 encounters with at least five matched fixations from the 142 questionnaire respondents.

When predictors were examined individually (Figure 12), viewing time, fixation count, returns, movement, and being picked as interesting in the questionnaire were each positively associated with subsequent memory, whereas habit-controlled viewing distance was not (β = -0.004, p = 0.37). When predictors were entered simultaneously, repeated viewing emerged as the strongest behavioural predictor of memory: each standard-deviation increase in return behaviour was associated with a +0.018 change in the probability of remembering the artwork. Viewing time remained independently associated with memory (+0.015), indicating that the effects of accumulated viewing and returning were not interchangeable. Gross floor path retained a smaller independent association, whereas the individual movement extents did not.

Prior familiarity showed only a weak association with memory when considered alone (+0.007) and did not retain an independent effect once viewing behaviour was included. Habit-controlled viewing distance likewise remained unrelated to later memory. These results indicate that subsequent recollection was more strongly associated with the duration and repetition of viewing than with the absolute distance at which an artwork was viewed.

Serial position was also associated with memory. Artworks encountered during the first fifth of a visit were remembered least often (1.6%), whereas the proportion rose to approximately 9% by the middle of the visit. This pattern does not indicate a primacy advantage; however, interpretation is constrained by the fact that encounter order was also determined by the spatial sequence of the museum display.

5. Discussion

>Discussion

5.1. RQ1. Viewing distance and artwork scale

The most consistent determinant of viewing distance was the physical size of the artwork. Larger paintings were generally viewed from farther away, whereas subject matter and other painting characteristics did not show comparable effects. However, the adjustment was not proportional to painting size. Although visitors increased their distance from larger paintings, they did not move sufficiently far away to maintain a constant visual angle. As a result, larger paintings still occupied a greater proportion of the visual field (Figure 3). This finding refines the results suggested by the reported literature and suggests that visitors do not select viewing positions only according to a fixed optical relationship between observer and artwork. Viewing distance may reflect a compromise between the dimensions of the work and the spatial possibilities offered by the gallery. The available room in front of a painting, the position of neighbouring works, circulation routes, and the presence of other visitors may constrain how far a viewer can or is inclined to move. "Preferred viewing distances" should therefore not necessarily be interpreted as distances demanded by individual paintings; a more nuanced definition may result from the interaction between properties of the artwork and the opportunities for movement provided by the exhibition space.

Several paintings, including Lovers, Revelation, Still Life with Books, and Mother with Two Children, all by Egon Schiele, were viewed from comparatively short distances. This indicates that size cannot explain all variation between artworks. One possibility is that lower contrast, fine surface information, brushwork, or other locally distributed visual details encourage closer inspection. Such an explanation remains tentative, however, and would require systematic analysis of image properties before conclusions can be drawn about why particular works elicit closer viewing.

5.2. RQ2. Dynamic repositioning

Standing positions were not fixed but varied substantially between artworks. Moreover, movement in front of paintings resembles a fan shape (Figure 6). Visitors occupied a range of lateral and frontal positions, indicating that viewing develops through combinations of horizontal and depthrelated movement. This differs from the T-shaped pattern reported by Kühnapfel et al. (2024) in which viewers approach and withdraw along a perpendicular axis before moving on-possibly due to the experimental design, in which participants only observed one painting rather than a longer sequence of artworks.

Horizontal movement along the wall was particularly prominent. Visitors appeared to modify their position more frequently by moving laterally than by repeatedly moving directly towards and away from a painting (Figure 7). The amount of stationary behaviour also decreased as artwork size increased, suggesting that larger paintings encouraged more movement across the space in front of them. This may reflect the need to explore a larger pictorial surface or to adjust the visual field while examining different regions of a work.

Changes in fixation location provide further evidence that distance is associated with different modes of visual exploration. At greater distances, fixations were concentrated more strongly around the centre of paintings. As visitors approached, fixations became increasingly distributed towards peripheral regions (Figure 9). Close viewing therefore appears to change what parts of the image are explored. The works by Schiele and Gerstl included in the exhibition employ relatively central compositions, with one or more figures positioned prominently against surrounding areas in which brushwork, ground, or repeated visual structures remain visible. From a greater distance, the central figure or compositional focus can be apprehended efficiently. At closer distances, however, viewers may increasingly inspect the surrounding pictorial field, including the background and contextual details that are less salient from afar. The increase in peripheral looking during approach may therefore reflect a transition from processing the principal compositional elements to examining background information.

5.3. RQ3. Movement and visual processing

Fixation duration varied systematically with movement (Figure 10). During stationary viewing, fixation durations tended to increase over time. Fixations made while approaching or withdrawing from an artwork were generally shorter, indicating that visual processing during movement differs from that occurring once a relatively stable viewing position has been established. Importantly, approaching and withdrawing showed different temporal trends. Fixations tended to become shorter as participants approached a painting, but longer as they moved away. Combined with the shift towards peripheral exploration at closer distances, this pattern may indicate that approach is associated with increasingly rapid sampling of fast-processed information. In the context of the present exhibition, such information may include brushwork, background areas, repeated patterns, and other peripheral details. These elements may invite multiple short inspections rather than the longer fixations associated with interpreting central figures or compositional relationships. The opposite pattern during withdrawal is equally suggestive. As visitors move away, longer fixations may reflect a reintegration of longer processing. The asymmetry between approaching and retreating indicates that these movements should not be treated as equivalent changes in viewing distance: moving towards an artwork and moving away from it may correspond to different perceptual phases within the viewing episode.

Viewing distance itself did not predict subsequent memory or recollection; memory was more intuitively associated with viewing time. A particularly informative aspect of the present data, however, concerns the fragmented nature of viewing episodes. Participants rarely looked at a painting in a single uninterrupted encounter. Instead, they repeatedly lifted their gaze or redirected it elsewhere before returning to the same work. Such returns occurred across visitors and were often separated by interruptions of less than ten seconds.

These interruptions reveal that attention in the gallery is distributed across the surrounding environment. Approximately 53% of in-between glances were directed towards another artwork, most often one located close to the artwork that had just been viewed; 47% of intervening glances were directed towards the rest of the room, including labels, other visitors, or other environmental elements. Longer interruptions showed a related pattern: when participants returned to an artwork after more than ten seconds, about half of these returns followed attention to a work directly adjacent. This suggests that looking away from a painting should not automatically be interpreted as disengagement. In many cases, the interruption forms part of a larger viewing sequence in which artworks are compared. Visitors alternate between works, return to previously inspected paintings, and construct relationships between objects distributed across a wall, a finding which mirrors Linden and Wagemans (2024). The "perceptual unit" in the context of the museum experience is therefore potentially larger than the individual artwork. What appears to be a single viewing episode may at the level of the exhibition constitute a continuous comparative process.

Visitors move predominantly along walls, and their gaze behaves in a comparable way: attention shifts laterally between neighbouring paintings and subsequently returns to previously viewed works. Spatial movement and visual comparison thus appear closely intertwined. From this perspective, the exhibition functions as a perceptual structure that encourages sequential and comparative viewing. Visitors may, in effect, "read" the wall, moving between artworks and establishing relationships among them. This dynamic deserves more direct investigation. Future work could examine whether the probability and direction of gaze transitions depend on adjacency, spacing, alignment, thematic relationships, formal similarity, or the order in which works are installed. Such analyses could distinguish effects of pictorial similarity from those generated simply by spatial proximity.

The same framework may also help explain differences between wall-based artworks and objects positioned elsewhere in the gallery. Sculptures located in the centre of a room, for example, may fall outside the dominant lateral sequence established by paintings arranged along the walls and could therefore be bypassed more frequently. Rather than necessarily attracting attention through their physical accessibility or three-dimensionality, they may interrupt an established spatial and visual trajectory. This possibility would need to be tested directly, but it reinforces the broader implication that exhibition layout organises attention by structuring the relationships among objects.

6. Conclusions

The present findings confirm that viewing behaviour in the gallery is shaped by the relationship between artworks, viewers, and the spatial organisation of the exhibition. They give an extra layer of interpretation to the reported literature, which is important both in terms of results and in terms of methodology.

Across the analyses, artwork size emerged as the clearest predictor of viewing distance, whereas other features, including subject matter and orientation, appeared to play a more limited role. Preferred viewing distance, however, provides only a partial account of visitors' behaviour. Surprising findings came from considering not only individual encounters but the relationship between them: visitors adjusted their position dynamically on the horizontal axis, moved predominantly along the wall and repeatedly shifted their gaze between neighbouring works. This behaviour also impacted subsequent memory of artworks, with more returns associated with stronger memory.

Taken together, these findings suggest that gallery viewing, at least in the "white cube" setting considered here, should be understood not only as a series of encounters with individual objects, but also as a spatially, mostly horizontally, organised process of dynamic comparison between multiple artworks.

In his assessment of the modern museum space, O'Doherty (1999, p. 14) describes the modern gallery as one that aims to remove contextual cues, allowing the artwork to appear as "isolated from everything that would detract from its own evaluation of itself". This conception of the gallery as a seemingly neutral background has been influential in shaping exhibition practices, contributing to the persistence of a relatively standardised gallery typology (Hourston Hanks, 2021). The present findings offer a more nuanced understanding of the white cube, demonstrating that even a space conceived as "neutral" establishes implicit relationships between artworks and shapes how visitors move through the gallery and distribute their attention. This understanding is in line with contemporary museological and curatorial approaches, which increasingly challenge the notion that the museum's spatial framework can be separated from the exhibition itself (Pilegaard, 2024).

These results also have methodological implications for experimental research on art perception. Laboratory paradigms in which artworks are presented individually on a screen remove precisely the spatial relationships that appear to structure much naturalistic viewing. Even gallery-like experiments may substantially alter behaviour if only one artwork is displayed within a room. Such settings preserve the possibility of physical approach and withdrawal but eliminate the opportunity for neighbouring artworks to compete, interact, and invite comparison.

Consequently, ecological models of art viewing may need to shift from treating encounters with artworks as largely independent events towards treating an exhibition as a spatially organised visual system-moving beyond the question of how viewers position themselves in front of individual paintings and towards the broader question of how they navigate, compare, and integrate multiple works across an exhibition space.

7. Limitations

This paper has focused on dynamic viewing in the museum; however, there are some limitations that future research could further investigate. One such limitation is that movement affects-and is affected by-more variables than visual attention alone. Future studies might choose to analyse the interplay of other bodily responses related to movement, such as head rotation, blinking, heart rate, and pupil size. Research grounded in this embodied approach may enhance our understanding of museum behaviour, expanding our knowledge of various forms of attention and the physiological responses they elicit. Additionally, it could integrate data on the cognitive processes that occur as we move, as visual attention alone cannot fully capture the complexity of the aesthetic experience-especially one that varies significantly from painting to painting.

Another limitation lies in our use of paintings that were part of the regular exhibition at the Leopold Museum. While this approach provided ecological validity, a more controlled setting could facilitate closer examination of the visual properties of artworks, in order to empirically determine whether differences in correlations (positive or negative) are due to features such as colour contrast, visual complexity, semantic difficulty, or others.

Finally, more dimensions of the alternating gaze could be explained by including in the analysis non-artwork-related elements, such as the presence of other participants, of accompanying visitors and of explanatory labels.
