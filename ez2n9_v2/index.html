Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

I propose a formal theory of selection by consequences building on the generalized Price equation (GPE), which replaces the standard parent-offspring relation used in biological models of selection with a graded recurrence relation between two arbitrary sets. The GPE thus provides an overarching framework for behavioral selection across all levels of analysis: evolution, culture, and learning. I further adopt the GPE to derive a quantitative model of selection by consequences, alongside a simple method of statistical estimation, and sketch its empirical application using simulated data. The proposed framework formally links evolutionary theory with operant learning and offers a new methodological approach to the analysis of behavioral data.

1 Introduction

The idea that individual learning can be understood as a selection process is a recurrent theme in behavior analysis. For example, Thorndike conjectured that learning "is a process of selection among reactions [...] within individuals" (Thorndike, 1900, p. 91). Since then, several authors have promoted the idea that individual behavior is shaped by processes that are analogous to evolution (e.g., Broadbent, 1961; Campbell, 1956; Gilbert, 1970; Herrnstein, 1961; Pringle, 1951; Staddon & Simmelhag, 1971). Skinner (1981) even proposed an overarching theoretical principle for evolution, culture and learning, which he called "selection by consequences." The view that behavior is the target of selection at different aggregate levels continues to be a popular theme in behavior analysis (e.g., Becker, 2019; Borgstede & Simon, 2025; De Haan & Simon, 2024; Donahoe, 2011; Simon & Hessen, 2019) and is eventually finding its way into evolutionary biology (Edelaar et al. 2023; Edelaar, 2026; Jablonka & Lamb, 2005). However, it is difficult to pinpoint how exactly mechanisms of individual learning connect to the principle of selection by consequences. For example, Donahoe et al. (1993) hold that selection of behavior requires an analogy between neurophysiology and genetics, whereas Baum (2017) approaches the problem in purely behavioral terms. Hull et al. (2001) stress the multilevel nature of behavioral selection and its functional relation to evolutionary processes, while McDowell (2004) treats learning and evolution as distinct, yet computationally equivalent, mechanisms. Despite considerable advances in recent years, these different approaches to behavioral selection remain fragmented and lack an overarching perspective that unifies them at a theoretical level and goes beyond a shared vocabulary that is borrowed from evolutionary biology.

The aim of this article is to provide such a unifying theoretical framework. In the following, I will present a formal model of selection by consequences based on a generalized version of the Price equation (Price, 1970; 1972). To achieve this, I will first clarify what exactly is meant by the term "selection" and specify the class of systems (be it biological, behavioral, or physical) to which it applies. I will then propose a quantitative theory that captures all processes of behavioral selection, irrespective of their physiological or computational foundations. To demonstrate the feasibility of the framework, I will demonstrate how the proposed framework can be used to statistically estimate the amount of selection in behavioral experiments using simulated data. Finally, I will discuss the implications and scope of the theory, including its relation to existing formal theories of behavioral selection, such as the Evolutionary Theory of Behavior Dynamics (McDowell, 2004), the Behavioral Price Equation (Baum, 2017), the Unified Reinforcement Principle (Donahoe et al., 1993), and the Multilevel Model of Behavioral Selection (Borgstede & Eggert, 2021).

2 The Price Equation

The Price equation (PE) was originally formulated to characterize biological selection processes in a quantitative genetics framework (Price, 1970 (Price, , 1972)). The PE has become one of the cornerstones of theoretical biology (cf. Baravalle et al., 2024; Lehtonen et al., 2020; Luque, 2017). It has been shown that well-known theorems of natural selection, such as Hamilton's rule and Fisher's fundamental theorem can be derived from the PE (Frank, 1998; see also Borgstede, 2023). However, as noted by Price (1995), the PE is far more than a formal description of genetic evolution. In fact, it is a general definition of selection that does not depend on any specific mechanism (such as genetic transmission or mutation). Consequently, the PE has been successfully applied outside the realm of biology, including the study of "cultural evolution" (Aguilar & Akçay, 2018; El Mouden et al., 2014) or the organizing principles of "cosmological" selection (Gardner & Conlon, 2013). Frank (2017 Frank ( , 2020) ) even argues that the PE can be understood as a general invariance principle that appears in various forms throughout the sciences, such as theoretical physics, Bayesian statistics, and information theory.

The PE was introduced into behavior analysis by Baum (2017) and further developed into the Multilevel Model of Behavioral Selection by Borgstede and Eggert (2021). Here, I will not elaborate on multilevel selection. Instead, I shall start with an accessible presentation of the PE and move straight to its generalization to so-called "fuzzy" systems (i.e., structures where sets and relations have no clear boundaries) as introduced in Borgstede (2025). This generalized Price equation (GPE) will then be used to state the formal core of behavioral selection.

4 A Quantitative Model of Selection by Consequences

Given the conceptual and mathematical background of the GPE, it is now possible to provide a quantitative model of selection by consequences. I will derive a minimal model that captures the core idea of behavior being selected by consequences within an individual, while being flexible enough to incorporate more complex mechanisms. I will explain the model mainly informally, thereby limiting mathematical notation to the core equations. A rigorous formalization, including all mathematical proofs, is provided in the Appendix.

4.1 Preliminaries

The core idea of behavioral selection is that, as behavior varies within an individual, environmental contingencies (such as different schedules of reinforcement) produce a covariance between behavior and its consequences, which then acts as the main driver of subsequent behavior change (cf. Baum, 1973). However, not every covariance qualifies as an instance of selection. Following the GPE, behavior must covary with fitness, which is defined as the average over all source recurrence weights. Therefore, only consequences that are reliable proxies for fitness can be interpreted in terms of selection. At the level of natural selection, such fitness proxies can easily be found, since biological fitness essentially relies on survival and reproduction. Consequently, any event that is (directly or indirectly) predictive of survival or reproduction is a biological fitness predictor. In previous work I have argued that statistical predictors of biological fitness (such as food) should also drive individual-level selection because individuals who adapt their behavior to increase such fitness predictors will, on average, have an evolutionary advantage over those who do not (Borgstede, 2020; Borgstede & Eggert, 2021). Similarly, Baum (2005) proposes that individual behavior is predominantly shaped by phylogenetically important events (PIE), which are events that are predictive of increases (or decreases) in biological fitness, such as signals for the availability of resources or mating opportunities but also signals of threat. The idea that fitness predictors, rather than fitness itself, are the main driver of individual-level behavioral selection theoretically plausible but difficult to translate into a quantitative model, since the biological fitness of an individual is usually unknown (unless the individual is dead). However, since the GPE can be directly applied to within-individual selection, it returns an explicit definition of behavioral fitness (i.e., average behavioral recurrence) and thus does not require a link to biological fitness to be empirically applicable. Instead, one may specify behavioral fitness proxies with respect to behavioral recurrence directly, which leads itself to a straightforward implementation of behavioral fitness proxies into the GPE framework. In a nutshell, if we can predict behavioral fitness from consequences, the empirical covariance between behavior and consequences is in fact sufficient for the calculation of behavioral selection. The following basic model exploits such behavioral fitness predictors to derive an empirical approach to estimating the amount of selection by consequences within a single individual.

4.2 The Basic Model

I start with the basic concepts of the GPE and their behavioral counterparts, followed by an implementation of the hypothesis that behavioral selection is essentially driven by consequences.

First and foremost, it needs to be clarified what the source and outcome measurements (𝑧𝑧 𝑖𝑖 and 𝑧𝑧 𝑗𝑗 ) are supposed to be, and how different realizations of these measurements vary over time. The second step is the definition of fitness at a purely behavioral level (i.e., independent of biological fitness), such that empirical covariances between behavior and its consequences can be utilized to derive corresponding estimates of behavioral selection. I will introduce these concepts informally here using a simple example, leaving the general case for the formal Appendix.

Imagine we are interested in how the behavior (e.g., lever pressing) of a certain individual is selected by consequences (e.g., food delivery). The individual is repeatedly observed in subsequent sessions, each consisting of a certain number of trials (i.e., each session contains at least two behavioral measurements). Suppose we make food delivery contingent on lever pressing and track the rates of responding and food delivery for every trial. Now imagine we inspect the data of all trials in the first session -if food delivery is contingent on lever pressing, higher lever pressing rates necessarily come with higher rates of food delivery. This contingency is an immediate consequence of the programmed schedule of reinforcement. In other words, as long as the individual's behavior rate varies over trials, the realized contingency enforces a covariance between responses and consequences observed over several trials within a single session We can calculate the empirical covariance between response rate and food rate for every session, using the data observed in the respective trials. In addition to this covariance, we may calculate the average response rate for each session, which we use to calculate the change in average behavior from one session to another. Note that, in principle, the time scale does not matter -sessions may span over minutes, hours, or even days -as long as there are at least two observations in each session (two is a natural lower limit because a single observation has no variance and, consequently cannot covary with anything). We can thus apply the same framework at different time scales, in line with the conceptual framework of multiscale behavior analysis (Baum, 2013; 2018).

For each trial we record the observed response rate and the obtained amount of food, such that the change in average response rate between sessions can be calculated for each pair of adjacent sessions. We can further use the observed behavior and consequences to calculate the covariance between response rate and food rate for each session. We will use these two quantities (average change and covariance between behavior and consequences) to estimate the slope of the fitness function, from which we can then derive the amount of behavioral selection between each pair of adjacent sessions.

To give a concrete numerical example, data was simulated for 20 successive sessions consisting of three trials each, using an iterative procedure. First, the response rates (𝑧𝑧 𝑖𝑖 ) for each trial in session one were randomly sampled from a normal distribution with mean 10 and standard deviation five.

5 Discussion

In this paper, I introduced a quantitative model of selection by consequences building on the generalized Price equation (GPE, Borgstede, 2025). The GPE provides an abstract description of how selection works, irrespective of the units of selection and the specific underlying mechanisms. Most importantly, it replaces the concept of parenthood between parents and offspring with a graded recurrence relation that connects source measurements with outcome measurements. I used the GPE to derive a formal model of selection by consequences, which yielded the covariance based law of effect (CLOE) as a fundamental principle, as well as a straightforward estimation procedure for the amount of behavioral selection using standard linear regression.

5.1 Implications and Scope of Behavioral Selection Theory

The proposed selection framework has wide implications for behavior analysis. From a pragmatic view, the GPE can be understood as a comparatively simple tool for the statistical analysis of behavioral data. However, the GPE is much more than a statistical model -it is the formalization of a fundamental theoretical principle: selection by consequences. As such, it clarifies in which respect behavior change at the individual level (learning) mirrors behavior change at the population level (culture) and at the species level (evolution). Moreover, the framework is scalable to include different levels of selection, simultaneously (see Borgstede et al., 2026). Being a mathematical identity, the GPE is more than an empirically derived law -it is a theoretical commitment to the idea that behavior is best analyzed in terms of selection (cf. Borgstede & Luque, 2021). What makes such fundamental principles useful is that they provide an abstract formal vocabulary for a certain theoretical approach and thus point researchers towards what to look for. In other words, the GPE allows us to analyze any process of behavior change in terms of selection, thereby telling us that the essential part of behavior analysis lies in understanding the mechanisms underlying behavioral recurrence. The present paper is only a first step towards developing a corresponding methodology for the empirical estimation and evaluation of GPE based selection models, which will be addressed in future work.

One important feature of the GPE is that it treats behavioral selection as a molar concept. In other words, selection always acts at the level of average trait values (𝑧𝑧̅ ). Consequently, the GPE makes no a-priori assumptions about the molecular mechanisms underlying selection. At the evolutionary level, for example, recurrence may be largely mediated by the genetic architecture of a specieshowever, average behavior may also be partially transmitted by non-genetic factors such as parental instructions or a tendency of children to imitate their parents' behavior. The same holds for the cultural level, where recurrence probably builds on several mechanisms such as imitation and instruction. At the level of individual learning, selection is very likely mediated by various molecular mechanisms, which feed into the recurrence relation. Whereas the basic selection model introduced in this paper focuses on the role of behavioral consequences, other pathways of behavioral recurrence are possible, including time-dependent or history-dependent recurrence weights. In other words, the recurrence weights in the GPE can be an arbitrary function of past behavior, context effects, or learning history, resulting in context-dependent predictors of behavioral fitness.

Interestingly, although the concept of behavioral selection is intrinsically linked to the molar paradigm of behavior analysis, its application to model building still requires informed hypotheses about the molecular mechanisms underlying behavioral recurrence. In other words, although the level of analysis is molar, the GPE acknowledges the importance of studying molecular mechanisms.

One might say that selection by consequences provides a molar account of average behavior change (in terms of selection), in the same way as thermodynamics provides a molar account of average particle motion (in terms of heat).

5.2 Relation to Previous Work

In the following, I will briefly relate the GPE to existing formal approaches to behavioral selection, namely the Unified Reinforcement Principle (URP) by Donahoe (1993), the Evolutionary Theory of Behavior Dynamics (ETBD) by McDowell (2004 McDowell ( , 2013)), the Behavioral Price Equation (BPE) by Baum (2017), and the Multilevel Model of Behavioral Selection (MLBS) by Borgstede and Eggert (2021). I will use the GPE as an analytic framework to compare these approaches. Since the GPE is essentially a formal definition of selection, it tells us exactly which aspects of the different approaches are universal, and which are specific to the model. Formally, every model that relates past observations to present observations can be locally approximated by a linear projection using a corresponding recurrence relation. Consequently, at a very abstract level, all existing models of selection by consequences are special instances of the GPE. However, they differ substantially regarding the units of selection (i.e., the range of the index 𝑖𝑖), the kinds of traits (i.e., the quality of the character 𝑧𝑧), and the mechanisms underlying behavioral recurrence (i.e., the values of 𝛾𝛾 𝑖𝑖𝑗𝑗 ). Donahoe et al. (1993) provide a formal model of behavior dynamics that is inspired by neural processes. Their Unified Reinforcement Principle (URP) is formally implemented by artificial neural networks that evolve over time, which they call "selection networks." The units of selection (𝑖𝑖) are collections of neurons, whereas the trait of interest (𝑧𝑧) is the firing rate of these neurons.

Consequently, if we were to apply the GPE to partition average behavior change into a selection and a residual component, we would have to calculate the average neural firing rate at one point in time and compare it to the average neural firing rate at a later point in time. In other words, in the URP, selection occurs at the level of average firing rate varying over different neurons within trials, but not over different trials. Donahoe et al. (1993) make very specific assumptions about the molecular mechanisms of behavioral recurrence (𝛾𝛾 𝑖𝑖𝑗𝑗 ), which in their model corresponds to the dynamic equations of the neural network. In particular, they attempt to emulate parts of the neural system underlying behavior, rather than merely simulate behavioral dynamics. However, the connection between their model and the theory of behavioral selection remains largely rhetorical (i.e., they make use of selectionist vocabulary), without making the analogy between operant learning and evolution explicit at a formal level.

McDowell (2004, 2013) follows a radically different path in his Evolutionary Theory of Behavior Dynamics (ETBD). Instead of neural mechanisms, the ETBD focuses on the computations underlying behavioral dynamics, which is hypothesized to be equivalent to the computations implemented by natural selection. The general approach of ETBD is to simulate (rather than emulate) behavior dynamics. The units of selection (𝑖𝑖) are collections of potential behaviors that vary in a quantitative character (𝑧𝑧). Like in the URP, the different values of 𝑧𝑧 are realized over different units (here: potential behaviors) at a particular point in time. Formally, McDowell implements an evolutionary algorithm that is modeled after genetical selection occurring in sexually reproducing species.

Consequently, the ETBD introduces such concepts as "father-behaviors" and "mother-behaviors" who produce "child-behaviors." The ETBD makes very specific assumptions about the computations of present behaviors from past behaviors. Although, due to the complexity of the algorithm, it is difficult to derive precise functional relations between source and outcome measurements analytically, it would be possible to derive an approximate recurrence relation with specific values for the different recurrence weights (𝛾𝛾 𝑖𝑖𝑗𝑗 ).

Yet another proposal of formalizing behavioral selection was made by Baum (2017). His Behavioral Price Equation (BPE) is conceptually very similar to the account put forward in this article. The units of selection (𝑖𝑖) are observed behaviors that vary over time in their intensity (𝑧𝑧), such as response frequency or time allocation. However, since the BPE builds on the standard Price equation, Baum (2017) had to introduce some additional assumptions to make his model work. Specifically, the BPE defines fitness as the ratio of target behaviors during test sessions and training sessions (i.e., fitness is just the rate of increase in behavioral frequency). Moreover, lacking an observable criterion for "parenthood" between behaviors, the BPE defines the recurrence weights (𝛾𝛾 𝑖𝑖𝑗𝑗 ) by means of numeric identity. In other words, if an individual responds 20 times in a trial during session one, all trials in sessions two with 20 responses are treated as its "offspring." Therefore, although the BPE is very general in that it does not rely on any specific molecular mechanisms, it is also quite limited in scope, since the way it implements the recurrence relation forces the residual part of the Price equation to be zero (cf. Borgstede & Eggert, 2021).

Baum's BPE was further developed by Borgstede and Eggert (2021) who integrate natural selection and behavioral selection within a single formal framework -the Multilevel Model of Behavioral Selection (MLBS). Like in the BPE, the units of selection (𝑖𝑖) are observed behaviors varying over time and the quantitative character (𝑧𝑧) is response intensity (such as frequency or time allocation). The MLBS avoids the limitations of the standard Price equation by formally linking "reinforcers" to the concept of Phylogenetically Important Events (PIEs; Baum, 2005), thereby treating PIEs as statistical predictors of evolutionary fitness. From a theoretical perspective, the functional link between natural selection and behavioral selection is a great advantage (cf. Borgstede & Luque, 2021). However, the MLBS lacks an explicit definition of behavioral recurrence (𝛾𝛾 𝑖𝑖𝑗𝑗 ), which is why empirical estimates of behavioral selection so far relied on counterfactual reasoning (Borgstede & Anselme, 2024), rather than empirically derived recurrence weights.

5.3 Conclusion

The purpose of this article was to adopt the generalized Price equation (GPE) for the level of individual behavior change and derive a quantitative model of selection by consequences that can be readily applied to behavioral data. The proposed model builds on previous applications of the Price equation to behavioral selection but for the first time allows for the construction of empirically derived estimates of behavioral fitness.

The introduction of the GPE into behavior analysis is a major theoretical contribution, since it solves the long-standing problem of how the biological concepts of reproduction and inheritance can be linked to behavioral dynamics within individuals. The more general concept of graded recurrence covers all levels of selection by consequences: evolution, culture, and learning. The GPE also offers a consistent and scalable toolkit for the experimental analysis of behavior in terms of selection. Furthermore, whereas in the standard PE, the residual term is expressed by fitness-weighted deviations from parental trait values (𝑤𝑤 𝑖𝑖 Δ𝑧𝑧 𝑖𝑖 ), the GPE defines the residuals from the perspective of outcome measurements (Δ𝑧𝑧 𝑗𝑗 ), thereby avoiding the fitness weighting. In other words, residuals are defined as the difference between the observed outcome measurements 𝑧𝑧 𝑗𝑗 and the expected outcome measurements one would predict if there were no other sources of change apart from selection.
