How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How reproducibility_check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Cognitive diagnosis models (CDMs) are often applied to constructs that are largely unidimensional in nature (e.g., mathematics, reading, mental health status) where a set of discrete attributes coexists with a dominant general proficiency dimension. This article presents the bi-factor CDM model (BF-CDM), which partitions item responses into a direct effect of continuous general proficiency and a discrete attribute-mastery tier, allowing mastery classifications to be made beyond what is attributable to general ability. By modeling both tiers explicitly, the BF-CDM makes it possible to evaluate the contribution of the discrete tier itself. Specifically, it can show whether the hypothesized attributes carry diagnostic information beyond general proficiency, both through model comparison with the nested unidimensional model and through the reliability of the attribute-specific component. A simulation study manipulating sample size, test length, number of attributes, and attribute-tier discrimination shows accurate recovery of item and person parameters, and of the modelimplied response functions. An application to reading comprehension data from PISA illustrates the practical value of the model: reliability of attribute-mastery classifications drops substantially once variance attributable to general proficiency is partialled out, indicating that much of the apparent diagnostic precision obtained under a saturated attribute structure reflects a single dominant general factor.

Introduction

In educational and psychological measurement, practical assessment increasingly demands the diagnosis of narrow, actionable attributes nested within broader proficiency domains. For instance, one may aim at identifying whether a student masters finding common denominators or simplifying fractions within the broader domain of fraction subtraction ability (Tatsuoka, 1984). Cognitive diagnosis models (CDMs) were developed precisely for providing fine-grained, criterion-referenced profiles of attribute mastery rather than a single continuous ability estimate (e.g., de la Torre & Minchen, 2014). However, most CDMs accommodate the influence of overall proficiency only indirectly. In higher-order CDMs, a higher-order continuous ability influences attribute mastery, which in turn determines item responses (de la Torre & Douglas, 2004). In CDMs with a saturated attribute structure, dependence among attributes is instead represented directly through their joint latent-class distribution, without defining a higher-order ability (e.g., de la Torre, 2011; Henson et al., 2009; Junker & Sijtsma, 2001). In neither formulation is a separate general proficiency dimension included directly in the item response function.

The main consequence of this modeling decision is that attribute mastery cannot be cleanly distinguished from general proficiency. Because the model only specifies the influence of narrow attributes on item responses, any variance attributable to general proficiency is projected onto the attributes themselves. The estimated mastery of an attribute therefore reflects a mixture of attribute-specific mastery and the proficiency level shared across the entire set of items. The extreme case is strictly unidimensional data, in which attributes have no true influence on responses. Fitting a CDM to such data will still yield diagnostic profiles, and these profiles may show substantial reliability even when little attribute-specific variation is present, because part of their apparent precision can be driven by true variance in the unmodeled general proficiency dimension (e.g., see Liu et al., 2018, for an application of CDMs to essentially unidimensional data). This projection of general ability into attributes has theoretical and practical consequences. At the theoretical level, a researcher may specify diagnostic categories on conceptual grounds that instead largely recover ordinal levels of a single underlying ability, rather than genuinely distinct competencies. In this scenario, the estimated attributes may be highly intercorrelated (e.g., see Sessoms & Henson, 2018), with mastery profiles concentrated at the extremes (complete nonmastery or complete mastery), and little ability to distinguish which specific attributes are mastered among respondents sharing the same total number of mastered attributes. In effect, the number of attributes mastered may become a function of the ordinal ranking of respondents on general ability. At the practical level, this invites interpretation errors: a student may be directed to remediate a specific competency when the classification actually reflects overall proficiency. Furthermore, the reliability of a mastery classification mixes attribute-specific information with shared variance from general proficiency, so an attribute may appear to be classified with high precision when that precision mostly reflects how well general ability alone predicts the response pattern. Finally, at the statistical level, fitting an overparameterized model can reduce estimation efficiency, increase capitalization on chance, and limit classification accuracy (Ma et al., 2016).

These issues point to two unmet needs. First, practitioners need attribute classifications whose meaning is not confounded with overall proficiency. Second, researchers need a way to evaluate whether a hypothesized attribute structure carries diagnostic information beyond a single general dimension.

A way to reconcile general ability and discrete attribute mastery is a two-tier (bi-factor) structure, in which a continuous general proficiency dimension and a set of discrete attributes act as parallel, simultaneously operating tiers rather than a hierarchy of dependence. In the bifactor CDM, the general dimension has a direct and independent effect on the item response probability. This differs from the models above in that it requires no latent-class membership to define alternative response processes. Instead, it provides a general measurement framework in which general ability and attribute-specific mastery are distinct and concurrent influences on item responses. Because the bi-factor structure partitions the variance in item responses into generalability and attribute-specific components, a discrete diagnosis acquires a clearer meaning: mastery beyond what would be expected from general ability. Consider two students with identical overall proportional reasoning proficiency (e.g., see Ma et al., 2020). A bi-factor CDM can reveal that one has mastered constructing ratios and proportions beyond what general ability predicts, whereas the other's apparent mastery under a saturated or higher-order structure may be entirely accounted for by high general proficiency. Such distinctions are directly relevant to instructional decisions (e.g., whether remediation should target a specific skill or reflects a broader ability deficit) in a way that standard CDMs, which confound the two sources of variance, cannot support.

Beyond scoring, the bi-factor structure serves as a diagnostic check on the attribute tier itself. Under saturated or higher-order structures, attribute reliability can be high simply because attributes absorb variance from a general ability that is precisely estimated from the full item set, so such reliability is not evidence that the attributes are distinct. Under a bi-factor CDM, general proficiency is modeled explicitly, and the reliability of the attribute-specific component reflects only how precisely mastery can be classified beyond what general ability explains. High attribute-specific reliability therefore supports the separability of the diagnoses. Low reliability indicates that the classifications carry little reliably recoverable information beyond general proficiency. Because low reliability may also stem from few items per attribute or weak item discrimination for the attribute tier, it is best read as a signal to examine the attribute structure and test design, and high reliability as necessary, but not sufficient, evidence of separability.

The idea of combining continuous and discrete latent variables is not new, but existing models differ in what the discrete variables represent and in how the two types of variables enter the item response function. In Yamamoto's (1989) HYBRID model, latent-class membership separates respondents whose responses follow an IRT model from those whose responses have fixed, class-specific probabilities. Similarly, mixture IRT models (Mislevy & Verhelst, 1990) use latent classes to define distinct response strategies, each with its own item response function. Hong et al. (2015) proposed a model whose item response function is the product of a noncompensatory IRT term and a conjunctive CDM term, so that the continuous and discrete variables act conjunctively. The closest antecedent of the bi-factor structure in CDMs is the scaling individuals and classifying misconceptions (SICM) model (Bradshaw & Templin, 2014). The SICM is a nominal-response model for multiple-choice items in which a continuous ability and binary misconceptions jointly predict option selection, with ability assumed independent of the misconceptions, as in the bi-factor model (Gibbons & Hedeker, 1992). Its discrete variables are misconceptions, whose presence lowers the probability of selecting the correct option; they may correlate with one another; and the model is estimated by MCMC. However, the aforementioned models were designed for specific response processes (different strategies, misconceptions). No general framework has yet added a bifactor structure to standard CDMs in a way that leaves the attribute structure intact and only redefines the attributes as mastery beyond general proficiency. This article introduces the bi-factor CDM (BF-CDM), a bi-factor extension of generalized CDMs in which a continuous general proficiency dimension is modeled alongside discrete attribute mastery profiles, with attributes reflecting specific competences shared by subsets of items as defined in the Q-matrix (Tatsuoka, 1983). The BF-CDM is grounded in the log-linear CDM (LCDM; Henson et al., 2009) and reduces to nested conjunctive (e.g., deterministic input noisy AND gate, DINA; Junker & Sijtsma, 2001), disjunctive (e.g., deterministic input noisy OR gate, DINO; Templin & Henson, 2006) or additive (e.g., linear logistic model, LLM; Maris, 1999) special cases through constraints on the discrete-tier parameters. Like the SICM, the BF-CDM combines a continuous and a discrete tier in the item response function. Unlike the SICM, which relies on a different (nominal responsebased) parameterization, the BF-CDM is a direct extension of the LCDM. It is therefore closely tied to the family of LCDM-based CDMs and can benefit from the methodological developments built around them with little or no adaptation, whereas such developments would require adaptation for the SICM. The BF-CDM targets the common CDM setting of dichotomously scored responses and skill-mastery attributes (de la Torre & Minchen, 2014; Sessoms & Henson, 2018). In this setting, the BF-CDM makes it possible to evaluate skill mastery after partialling out general proficiency and provides a framework for evaluating the incremental diagnostic value of the attribute tier by assessing the reliability of the attributespecific component.

In this article, the feasibility of the BF-CDM is evaluated through a simulation study of parameter and item response function recovery. Its practical relevance is then illustrated in a real-data application that compares the reliability of mastery and proficiency scores under unidimensional IRT, a saturated attribute structure CDM, and the BF-CDM, and discusses the implications for score interpretation and for evaluating the utility of the attribute tier.

4. Estimation

The BF-CDM model may be estimated via marginal maximum likelihood (MML) using the expectation-maximization (EM; Dempster et al., 1977) algorithm, with item parameters updated through standard numerical optimization procedures (e.g., BFGS). The continuous general dimension 𝜃 can be approximated using rectangular quadrature between a feasible range (e.g., -6 to 6). Letting 𝑀 denote the number of quadrature nodes used to approximate the density of 𝜃, and 𝐿 = 2 𝐾 denote the number of discrete attribute classes, the E-step requires evaluating the model-implied response probability over the full cross of continuous and discrete latent space (i.e., 𝑀 × 𝐿 points in total). This grid grows exponentially in the number of attributes (𝐿 = 2 𝐾 ) but only linearly in the quadrature size (𝑀), so the added computational burden from the general dimension relative to standard LCDM estimation is modest, provided 𝑀 is kept moderate (e.g., 31).

The intercept 𝜆 𝑗0 and the discrimination on the general ability, 𝛾 𝑗𝐺 , are identified by fixing the distribution of the continuous latent trait to a standard normal distribution.

Identification of the discrete-tier parameters, however, depends on the same conditions required for the LCDM model and is therefore governed by properties of the Q-matrix and test design (Gu & Xu, 2021). In particular, the Q-matrix must be complete, meaning that each attribute is measured by at least one item requiring that attribute alone (Köhn & Chiu, 2017).

Furthermore, each attribute should be measured by multiple items to permit separation of item and latent-class parameters, and the Q-matrix must satisfy the relevant structural identifiability conditions established for the LCDM family. Under these conditions, the discrete latent classes are distinguishable and the attribute-effect parameters are identifiable. Accordingly, the BF-CDM inherits the identification requirements of the underlying CDM while introducing only the additional scale-setting constraints associated with the continuous general ability.

Although the BF-CDM model presented here utilizes the LCDM response function for the discrete tier, reduced forms such as bi-factor extensions of DINA and DINO models, or the LLM, among other CDMs defined through the logit-link, can be easily obtained through appropriate constraints on the BF-CDM parameter vector 𝛌 𝑗 as in LCDM (e.g., see Henson et al., 2009). Thus, the BF-CDM should be viewed as a general latent-variable framework within which a broad class of reduced CDMs can be embedded.

5. Score Interpretation

The latent variable structure of the BF-CDM implies a shift in how mastery status should be interpreted compared to saturated attribute structure or higher-order CDMs. Under a classical CDM, mastery of attribute 𝑘 is an absolute classification, reflecting whether a respondent's response pattern provides sufficient evidence, against a fixed standard, that the attribute has been acquired. Under the BF-CDM, by contrast, mastery status is more accurately interpreted as relative to a respondent's own general proficiency level. That is, it reflects whether attribute-specific performance exceeds, equals, or falls short of what general proficiency predicts for the respondent.

As an example, under BF-CDM, for a respondent with low general proficiency, nonmastery of an attribute indicates weakness on that attribute beyond the low performance already implied by their general level. Conversely, a respondent with low general proficiency classified as a master on some attribute exhibits a genuine relative strength, since overcoming a low general baseline requires sufficiently strong attribute-specific knowledge. The same logic holds for high-proficiency respondents: a non-mastery classification for such a respondent reflects a specific deficit that persists despite a strong general baseline.

From a summative perspective, it may be reasonable to define mastery in absolute terms, against a fixed standard derived from the Q-matrix, even when that classification partly reflects general proficiency. From a formative perspective, nonetheless, it may have great practical value to separate general proficiency from specific attribute mastery. This distinction would allow feedback to address both the respondents' overall level of proficiency and their specific strengths and weaknesses relative to that level. In other words, it could indicate whether the respondent would benefit from reviewing the test content in general, while directing efforts at particular attributes if necessary.

Additionally, the reliability of attribute classifications under the BF-CDM offers evidence on the validity of applying CDM framework to the data. In essence, it indicates the extent to which variation in estimated attribute status reflects true variation in mastery rather than estimation error. Under a saturated attribute structure CDM, high reliability of attribute classifications may reflect the true variation of a general proficiency dimension underlying attribute mastery, rather than variation specific to each attribute. Under the BF-CDM, by separating the general and specific tiers, low reliability of attribute classifications, once the general proficiency component has been removed, would suggest that the remaining, attributespecific variation is largely random error rather than meaningful differentiation among examinees. Beyond model fit statistics, accounting for attribute profile reliability provides evidence on the practical utility of the scoring framework, that is, on whether attribute-level scores contain information beyond what is already captured by the general proficiency estimate.

To define reliability of discrete tiers in the metric commonly used for continuous scores, the empirical reliability of attribute classifications may be computed as the coefficient of determination of observed mastery given true mastery (de la Torre et al., 2026), that is, the proportion of true-score variance captured by the estimated scores. For binary mastery classifications, this coefficient reduces exactly to the squared Yule's (1912) 𝜙 coefficient of the attribute cross-classification matrix, which tabulates the expected true mastery status, (𝛂 𝑖 ) 𝑘 , against the estimated one, (𝛂 ̂𝑖) 𝑘 (Wang et al., 2015).

6. Simulation Study

This simulation study aimed at evaluating the effect of relevant assessment characteristics on the recovery of BF-CDM parameters and item response functions.

6.1. Method

Four factors were manipulated: sample size (𝑁 = 500, 1000, 2000), number of attributes (𝐾 = 3, 5), test length (𝐽 = 30, 60), and attribute-tier discrimination (manipulating guess and slip parameters) either low, i.e., 𝑃(𝛂 = 𝟎) and (1 -𝑃(𝛂 = 𝟏))~ 𝑈(.15, .25), or high, i.e., 𝑃(𝛂 = 𝟎) and (1 -𝑃(𝛂 = 𝟏)) ~ 𝑈(.05, .15). This resulted in a fully crossed 3 × 2 × 2 × 2 design (24 conditions), each replicated 100 times, for a total of 2,400 simulated datasets.

6.1.1. Data Simulation

The Q-matrices used for each condition contain items with different complexity (requiring varying numbers of attributes) and are properly identified. The Q-matrices were constructed from the built-in Q-matrices provided in the GDINA package (Ma & de la Torre, 2020): for 𝐾 = 3, the 10-item Q-matrix in sim10GDINA object (with equal structure as the one presented in Figure 1) was vertically stacked three times; for 𝐾 = 5, the 30-item Q-matrix in sim30GDINA object was used directly. To generate the 𝐽 = 60 condition, the resulting Qmatrix was further duplicated (row-stacked) to double the number of items while preserving its attribute structure. For simplicity, the population distribution of attribute profiles was fixed to be uniform across the 2 𝐾 possible classes, 𝜋 𝑙 = 1 2 𝐾 for all 𝑙. The general ability discrimination parameters 𝛾 𝑗𝐺 were generated from a log-normal distribution with a log-scale mean of 0 and a log-scale variance of 0.25 (e.g., as in Wallmark & Wiberg, 2026).

To manipulate the discriminative strength/item-quality of the discrete CDM tier in a similar fashion to previous studies (e.g., Kreitchmann et al., 2023; Nájera et al., 2023), we first generated an identity-link G-DINA parameter matrix with guessing and slipping parameters according to the simulation condition. The success probabilities of the intermediate latent groups were sampled from a uniform distribution between the guessing parameter and 1 minus the slipping parameter, and were ordered so that monotonicity was respected. This true item parameter matrix was then transformed into logit-link item parameters 𝛌 𝑗 . If integrating out the continuous proficiency, the generated BF-CDMs correspond to standard LCDM simulation conditions.

6.1.2. Data Analysis

For each of the 2,400 replications, item responses were generated from the BF-CDM (Equation 3) using the simulated 𝛾 𝑗𝐺 , 𝛌 𝑗 , the uniform latent class population probabilities and standard-normally distributed 𝜃. The study assessed the recovery of item and person parameters, as well as of the response functions themselves.

Recovery was assessed for the general discrimination parameter, 𝛾 𝑗𝐺 , and for the 𝛌 𝑗 parameters of each item's latent groups. This study illustrates the application of the BF-CDM to real assessment data. The BF-CDM results are compared with those obtained from a unidimensional IRT 2PL model and a saturated LCDM in terms of model fit, reliability, and validity with external criteria.

7.1. Data Description

The empirical analysis uses data from the reading assessment of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2000 for the United Kingdom, specifically Booklets 8 and 9, as analyzed in Chen and de la Torre (2014) and Nájera et al. (2021). The dataset contains 2,012 respondents who responded to 26 reading comprehension items. Only fully correct answers were considered as correct responses in the reading comprehension items, whereas partially correct were treated as incorrect, and non-responses were treated as missing at random and handled via full-information maximum likelihood, so that each respondent's likelihood contribution was computed only from the items to which they responded. The Qmatrix, proposed by Chen and de la Torre (2014) and empirically validated by Nájera et al. (2021), involved six attributes, namely: 1) locating information, 2) forming a broad general understanding, 3) developing a logical interpretation, 4) evaluating a number-rich text with number sense, 5) evaluating the quality or appropriateness of a text, and 6) related to test speediness. Sample descriptives and attribute conceptualizations can be found in Chen and de la Torre (2014).

In addition to the item responses, five items from the PISA 2000 student questionnaire were used as external criteria to evaluate the validity of the scores estimated under each model.

These variables capture students' reading habits, home reading resources, and self-reported performance in English. Item 34 measures the amount of time students spend reading for enjoyment each day, ranging from no reading to more than two hours across five ordinal categories. Item 35 assesses, through nine sub-items, attitudes toward reading using four-category agreement scales, covering aspects such as enjoyment of reading, reading as a hobby, willingness to discuss books, and difficulties sustaining reading.

The total score for Item 35 is calculated by summing the sub-item scores after reverse-coding negatively keyed items. Item 36 measures how often students voluntarily read different types of material, including magazines, comic books, fiction, non-fiction, emails and web pages, and newspapers. The six sub-items use five-category frequency scales ranging from never or hardly ever to several times a week. The total score is calculated by summing the scores across the six sub-items. Item 37 measures the number of books in the student's home across seven ordinal categories, from none to more than 500, providing an indicator of the home reading environment and access to books. Finally, item 41 records students' self-reported marks in English from their most recent school report prior to the PISA assessment; these grades were discretized into six categories in the original PISA dataset. The number of valid responses was 1,961 for item 34, 1,979 for item 35, 1,978 for item 36, 1,968 for item 37, and 845 for item 41. The complete PISA 2000 Student Questionnaire is available from the OECD at https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/data/datasets/pisa/pisa-2000-datasets/questionnaires/PISA2000_Student_Questionnaire.doc.

7.2. Data Analysis

A 2PL model, a saturated LCDM, and BF-CDM were calibrated for PISA response data. Comparative fit was assessed using deviance (with the associated number of estimated parameters), AIC, and BIC. Reliability estimates were computed following the procedures described in the simulation study. Namely, the empirical reliability (Equation 11) of general proficiency (in the 2PL and BF-CDM), and the coefficient-of-determination approximation proposed by de la Torre et al. (2026) for the reliability of attribute mastery in the LCDM and BF-CDM. Under the 2PL, reliability is expected to be overestimated because the model ignores local dependence induced by the specific attributes, artificially inflating discrimination parameters (Reise et al., 2018). The magnitude of this overestimation should increase with the relevance of the attributes to the response process (i.e., the variation within 𝛌 𝑗 ). Lastly, the validity of the person-parameter estimates was evaluated using the five external criteria. For each model, the validity index was the 𝑅 2 obtained from a linear regression of each criterion variable on the corresponding EAP estimates of the latent variable, with missing data handled via pairwise deletion. Statistical significance of differences in 𝑅 2 estimates between models was assessed using a nonparametric bootstrap procedure with 500 resamples drawn with replacement from the original data. For each bootstrap sample, the difference in 𝑅 2 estimates between models was computed, and statistical significance was evaluated based on the proportion of bootstrap differences greater than zero. All illustration datasets and codes are available at https://osf.io/2jcdm/overview?view_only=0fd9b15045e1420292c79d0d6333fdb5.

7.3. Results

All three models converged successfully. Under the LCDM, some of the populationlevel correlations between attributes, computed as Yule's 𝜙 from the population class probabilities marginalizing per attribute, were moderate or high (see Table 5), suggesting a considerable amount of variance shared across attributes. The correlation between general proficiency scores estimated under the 2PL and under the BF-CDM was .99, indicating that the two models recover largely the same general dimension. Point-biserial correlations between the LCDM attribute classifications and BF-CDM general proficiency scores were .70, .68, .72, .68, .74, and .68 for attributes 1 to 6, respectively. Together, these results suggest that under a saturated attribute structure, LCDM attribute mastery classifications largely capture general proficiency rather than attribute-specific variance. Consistent with this pattern of positive inter-attribute correlations, a large proportion of profile classifications under the LCDM concentrated in two classes: mastery of all attributes (𝜋 ̂𝑙 = .28) and non-mastery of all attributes (𝜋 ̂𝑙 = .17). Together, these two extreme profiles accounted for 44% of respondents, consistent with attribute mastery behaving largely as a single all-or-none dimension rather than as six differentiated constructs. Under the BF-CDM, by contrast, these two extreme profiles accounted for only 3% of classifications combined, with the remaining probability mass distributed evenly across the other profiles.

7.3.1. Model Fit Comparison

The deviance, along with AIC and BIC goodness-of-fit indices are presented in Table 6. The BF-CDM was the best-fitting model by both deviance and AIC, indicating that the attribute-specific structure meaningfully improved fit over a unidimensional representation.

Under BIC, however, which imposes a stronger penalty for model complexity, the 2PL was preferred. This mixed pattern admits two, non-exclusive readings. Under one reading, general proficiency accounts for the majority of the variance in the data, with the additional attributespecific structure contributing comparatively little once model complexity is taken into account. Under a second reading, the BIC penalty may be flagging an overfitting risk after the inclusion of the attribute tier.

7.3.2. Reliability Comparison

Empirical reliability of the general proficiency factor under the 2PL and the BF-CDM was .86 and .84, respectively. Under the LCDM, attribute-level reliability (expressed as 𝑅²) was .61, .53, .58, .62, .61, and .55, across the six attributes. Expressed instead using the classification accuracy index proposed by Wang et al. (2015), defined as the proportion of respondents whose estimated attribute mastery status matches their true status, without any adjustment for the level of agreement expected by chance alone, these same attributes yielded values of .89, .87, .88, .89, .89, and .87. Although the classification-accuracy index appears high, the 𝑅²-based reliability values reveal comparatively low precision, illustrating how classification accuracy alone can mask substantial measurement error at the attribute level.

Under the BF-CDM, attribute-level reliability decreased substantively, taking values of .25, .19, .22, .32, .18, .32 across the six attributes, respectively. This drop illustrates how most of what looked like attribute-specific reliability under the LCDM was in fact general-factor variance masked as attribute-specific information. The large population correlations among attributes reported in Table 5 support this interpretation. The BF-CDM's tier-separated reliability estimates thus provide a stricter validity check than LCDM reliability alone, precisely because they isolate the variance that is actually attribute-specific rather than shared.

7.3.3. Score Validity Comparison

As shown in Table 7, scores under the BF-CDM showed higher 𝑅² values than the 2PL and the LCDM for all external criteria, with the largest advantage for the prediction of school marks in English, where the BF-CDM explained approximately 4% more variance than the LCDM and roughly 2% more than the 2PL. The bootstrap comparisons indicated that 𝑅² was significantly higher for the BF-CDM than for the 2PL and the LCDM across all external criteria (p < .05). Finally, BF-CDMΔα indicates the incremental validity of the attribute tier, that is, the change in 𝑅² when predictions use all latent variables in the BF-CDM compared to the general proficiency alone. This increment was at most 2.9%. These results support the validity of the BF-CDM's score structure: by separating general proficiency into a continuous general factor and attribute-specific mastery profiles, the model gains predictive power over both a purely unidimensional representation and a purely discrete representation. No statistically significant differences were found between the 2PL and LCDM on any of the criteria.

8. Discussion

This article introduced the bi-factor cognitive diagnosis model (BF-CDM) as a framework for separating a continuous general proficiency dimension from a discrete, 𝐾dimensional attribute-mastery profile in the analysis of item response data. Unlike saturated attribute structure or higher-order CDMs, in which the general dimension shapes item responses only indirectly, through its influence on the joint attribute distribution, the BF-CDM specifies both tiers as parallel, additive sources of item response variance. This reformulation changes what a discrete mastery classification means: rather than an absolute statement about a fixed content domain, mastery under the BF-CDM is defined relative to what a respondent's general proficiency already predicts, and its reliability provides a direct check on whether the attribute tier contributes information beyond that shared, dominant dimension.

The simulation study demonstrated that item and person parameters, as well as the underlying response functions, can be recovered with good accuracy under the proposed marginal maximum likelihood procedure, across a broad range of realistic testing conditions.

As expected, recovery of the discrete-tier parameters and of attribute mastery classifications was most sensitive to sample size, test length, number of attributes, and attribute-tier discrimination, mirroring the results in the standard (non-bifactor) CDM literature. Recovery of the general discrimination parameter, by contrast, was comparatively consistent across conditions, suggesting that estimation of the continuous tier is less demanding than estimation of the discrete tier, at least for the range of test lengths and attribute numbers considered here.

The real-data application illustrated one of the main contributions of the model in practical settings. Under the saturated LCDM, attribute mastery classifications were strongly intercorrelated and dominated by two extreme, all-or-none profiles, and attribute-level reliability appeared moderate. Once the shared general-proficiency variance was isolated under the BF-CDM, however, attribute-level reliability dropped substantially, indicating that most of what appeared to be attribute-specific precision under the LCDM was in fact general-factor variance recovered six separate times. At the same time, BF-CDM scores showed a small but consistent validity advantage over both the 2PL and the LCDM against a broad set of external reading-related criteria, illustrating that separating the two tiers need not come at the cost of predictive power, even when the discrete tier itself carries limited independent information in a given application. For test developers, these results suggest that attribute-level reporting in largely unidimensional data will require more indicators per attribute or more discriminating items than a blueprint designed for a single general ability. By making this quantifiable, the BF-CDM offers test developers a means of judging whether a given blueprint can support attributelevel reporting.

Several limitations of the present work, and of this application, should be noted. First, identification of the BF-CDM rests on the structural independence assumptions in Equations 4 and 5, which allocate all shared variance among all items to the general dimension by construction. This is a strong assumption, directly analogous to the proportionality and local independence constraints underlying the bi-factor IRT model (Gibbons & Hedeker, 1992; Reise et al., 2010 Reise et al., , 2018)), and it is not statistically tested within the current framework. If some fraction of the association among attributes is not, in fact, mediated by general proficiency, the BF-CDM will still attribute it to 𝜃, and reported attribute reliabilities will be conservative accordingly. Extensions incorporating partial correlations among attributes after accounting for 𝜃, or model comparisons against models that relax Equation 5, would help establish, in a given application, how much a drop in attribute reliability reflects this assumption rather than a genuinely low attribute-specific information.

Second, the real-data results should not be read as a general statement that discrete attributes are unnecessary once general proficiency is modeled. Model-fit indices sent a mixed signal in the PISA application: AIC favored the BF-CDM over the unidimensional 2PL, whereas BIC favored the simpler model. Because attribute-level reliability under a bi-factor structure is jointly a function of the true attribute-specific information in the data and of the adequacy of the Q-matrix used to define the attribute tier, the low reliabilities obtained here are consistent with either (a) the six PISA reading attributes carrying limited information beyond general reading proficiency, or (b) a Q-matrix that does not optimally isolate attribute-specific variance for this item set. Sensitivity analyses using alternative, empirically or theoretically motivated Q-matrices (e.g., following the balancing procedures as in Nájera et al., 2020 Nájera et al., , 2021) ) would help disentangle these two explanations in future applications.

Third, the simulation study fixed the discriminative strength of the general dimension and varied only the attribute tier; how recovery depends on the relative discrimination of the two tiers, particularly when the general dimension is weak relative to the attributes, remains to be studied. Relatedly, the current estimation procedure evaluates the model-implied likelihood over the full cross of quadrature nodes and attribute classes; while this grid remained computationally manageable for the number of attributes studied here, it will become prohibitive for tests measuring substantially more attributes, a limitation the BF-CDM shares with saturated CDMs more broadly. Adaptive quadrature, Metropolis-Hastings Robbins-Monro estimation, or variational approximations are natural candidates for extending the model to higher-dimensional attribute spaces.

Future work could extend the BF-CDM to polytomous responses, following the generalization of the LCDM to the GDM (von Davier, 2008), to multiple correlated general dimensions, or to longitudinal designs in which growth in general proficiency and in attribute mastery are of separate substantive interest. Because attribute-tier reliability under the BF-CDM offers a direct empirical check on the validity of a proposed attribute structure, it may also prove useful as a routine diagnostic tool during test development and Q-matrix validation, independent of whether a bi-factor structure is ultimately adopted for operational scoring.

In summary, the BF-CDM offers a way to answer a question that saturated and higherorder CDMs cannot directly address: how much of an attribute classification reflects genuine, attribute-specific mastery once general proficiency has been accounted for. As illustrated here, the answer can be sobering, but it is precisely this capacity to distinguish real diagnostic value from an artifact of a dominant general factor that gives the model its practical relevance for both test developers and users of diagnostic score reports.
