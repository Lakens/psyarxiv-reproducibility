Files that do not belong in the repository

We left out 146 files that software creates by itself, not the authors: operating system (146). They hold nothing a reader can use, but they clutter the repository, and software can mistake them for real files (for example a ._ copy of a .csv for data).

Remove these: Operating system files: macOS adds a __MACOSX folder and a ._ copy of every file it puts in a zip, and .DS_Store files to folders; Windows adds Thumbs.db and desktop.ini. On a Mac, create zips from the command line with zip -r -X, or remove them afterwards with zip -d archive.zip "__MACOSX/*" "*.DS_Store". RStudio session files (.Rproj.user/) hold open tabs and unsaved edits; R history (.Rhistory) lists the commands typed in a session and can hold file paths and passwords; Jupyter checkpoints (.ipynb_checkpoints/) are automatic copies of notebooks; compiled Python files (__pycache__/, .pyc) are recreated when the code runs; open-document lock files (~$...docx, .~lock...#) are left while a document is open; editor backups (file~, .bak, .swp) and temporary files (.tmp, .temp) are copies a program made by itself.

Keep only on purpose: version control folders (.git, .svn, .hg) hold the project’s history, which can include earlier versions of files and data that was later removed; editor settings (.idea, .vscode) can hold paths on your own computer. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Listeners navigate complex acoustic environments by selectively attending to relevant sounds amid competing noise. This capacity is classically attributed to top-down goals and bottom-up salience, but recent work implicates a third influence: selection history, whereby incidental exposure to statistical regularities shapes attentional prioritization. We investigated how incidental experience of selecting specific acoustic features influences attentional prioritization across different levels of auditory processing. Participants listened to two concurrently presented sounds to detect a target that contained a brief temporal gap. Unbeknownst to the participants, during a learning phase, one sound was more likely to contain the target gap; in a subsequent testing phase all sounds were equally likely to be the target. Experiment 1 used pure tones of varying pitches, and Experiment 2 used instrument chords (piano, trumpet, guitar). Across both experiments, participants responded faster to high-probability targets during learning, indicating facilitated attentional selection based on the statistical regularities across different levels of acoustic representations. This response time advantage persisted robustly throughout the unbiased testing phase for Experiment 1, but less so for Experiment 2, possibly suggesting that the persistence of experience-driven auditory attention may depend on the complexity of the learned feature. Overall, our findings parallel selection-history effects observed in vision, providing evidence that similar influences of incidental experience on attentional priors exist across sensory modalities.

Introduction

In many situations, we are confronted with multiple auditory inputs at once, requiring us to select which sounds are most relevant for ongoing behavior: We might need to detect a bike bell amidst traffic noise, pick out our phone notification in a loud café, or notice a baby crying while music is playing. How does the auditory system identify and prioritize behaviorally relevant sounds amidst competing acoustic inputs? Classic work on auditory attention has focused on explicit, top-down influences of attention, where observers are tasked to selectively attend to one auditory stream over another (e.g., left vs. right ear, female vs. male voice, etc.), demonstrating that top-down, intentional selection can enhance processing of attended relative to unattended input (Hansen & Hillyard, 1980; Woldorff et al., 1993; Degerman et al., 2006; Broadbent, 1957). Other research has shown that sudden, salient sounds capture spatial attention reflexively and improve sensory processing at the sound's location (Picton & Hillyard, 1974; Näätänen et al., 1977; Rhodes, 1987; Mondor & Zatorre, 1995; Wu et al., 2007; Popov et al, 2023). Indeed, attentional selection -in both the auditory and visual domain -has traditionally been understood as arising from two main sources: explicit, top-down task goals and bottom-up, stimulus-driven saliency. However, emerging evidence indicates that another source influences perceptual selection: incidental experience with frequently encountered targets, often termed selection history (Awh et al., 2012).

In the visual domain, numerous studies have shown that selection history shapes how information is processed in the future. Even without explicit instructions, people are faster in identifying targets that are more frequently associated with a particular location (Addleman et al., 2019; Geng & Behrmann, 2005; Giménez-Fernández et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2013), non-spatial visual features such as color (Addleman & Störmer, 2023; Conn et al., 2020; Sha et al., 2017), or particular real-world objects (Addleman, et al., 2024). These incidentally learned biases can persist even after regularities are removed and all items are equally likely targets (e.g. Jiang et al., 2013; Addleman & Störmer, 2023; Addleman et al., 2024), indicating that selection history can have robust and relatively long-lasting effects on how and what information is prioritized.

In audition, statistical regularities play an important role in facilitating perceptual and attentional processes as well (Alards-Tomalin et al., 2017; Johnstrude et al., 2023; Hicks & McDermott, 2024; Woods & McDermott 2018). For example, listeners incidentally learn to prioritize spatial locations that frequently contain target sounds, with effects that persist even when targets are no longer likely to occur in those locations (Addleman & Jiang, 2019). Another study showed that participants respond faster to a highfrequency target voice in a four-voice dichotic listening task, revealing sensitivity to auditory regularities of voice type (Daly & Pitt, 2023).

Yet it remains unknown whether such learning extends to other acoustic features. Voices are a highly overlearned stimulus class that recruits auditory processing mechanisms shaped through a lifetime of social experience, which may uniquely favor the formation of attentional priors. Spatial location is not directly encoded in the auditory system but computed from acoustic cues such as interaural time and level differences, and may thus represent a distinct organizing dimension for auditory attention. We therefore tested whether experience-based attention extends to pitch and instrumental timbre -stimuli that do not share these distinctive properties yet differ from each other in important ways: pitch is a lowlevel feature processed early and automatically in the auditory hierarchy, whereas timbre requires the integration of more complex spectrotemporal information at later processing stages (Johnston & Heinz, 1978). Whether selection history effects emerge for such stimuli bears directly on how broadly experience-driven attention operates across the auditory hierarchy.

Experiment 1

This experiment tests whether incidentally learned pitch regularities facilitate attentional selection and whether such learning persists once statistical regularities are removed.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from introductory psychology and neuroscience courses and received course credit for their participation. Prior to the experiment, they provided written informed consent. All study procedures were approved by the institutional review board at Dartmouth College. Sample size was determined based on a pilot experiment (N = 29) that used an identical task but without the thresholding procedure, which yielded an effect size of d ≈ 0.30. We considered effect sizes in the range of d = 0.20-0.50 to be theoretically meaningful for this paradigm and anticipated that equating perceived loudness across sounds via the thresholding procedure would increase the effect size toward a medium effect (d ≈ 0.50), which requires N = 34 to achieve 80% power (α = 0.05) in a paired t-test. Accordingly, we aimed to collect approximately 34 participants for Experiments 1 and 2.

Data was collected from 34 participants (mean age = 19 yrs; SD = 1.33; 23 female, 11 male). Data from one subject had to be excluded from the final analyses because they only completed five trials of the experiment. For the first four subjects, there were technical issues with the loudspeakers during the first block of the experiment; thus, to avoid losing data, we added an additional learning block for these participants during data collection. Thus, for those participants, 31 blocks of data with 30 trials each were collected, with data from the learning phase reflecting blocks 2-16, and data from the testing phase reflecting blocks 17-31 (as block 1 contains data where the loudspeakers were not working properly).

After exclusion, data from 33 participants were retained for the main analyses.

Stimuli and Procedure

Participants performed an auditory selection task in which two sounds were presented simultaneously from two separate loudspeakers. Stimuli were created digitally at a 44,100 Hz sampling rate using Audacity software (version 3.7.3.0) and presented through external loudspeakers located on the left and right side of the participant using Psychtoolbox 3 (Brainard, 1997; Pelli, 1997) running on MATLAB R2020b (The MathWorks Inc.). During the experiments, participants sat in an enclosed soundproof auditory booth, 60 cm away from a computer screen and 80 cm away from each of two laterally placed loudspeakers, and were instructed to maintain their gaze on a central fixation cross throughout the experiment. The loudspeakers were located at approximately 30° of azimuth and ±20° of elevation relative to the subject's head. Two sounds (one target and one nontarget) were presented simultaneously on each trial, with one sound emanating from the left loudspeaker and a different sound emanating from the right loudspeaker (see Figure 1A). One of the sounds contained a short 25ms gap (the target), and the other did not, and participants were tasked to detect the sound with the gap and report which side this target tone came from (left vs. right) by pressing the left or down-key on a keyboard, respectively. Stimuli were 425 ms in duration and ~65 dB SPL in loudness. Across the experiment, three different pitches were used as sound stimuli: a pure tone of 200 Hz ("low-pitch sound"), a pure tone of 650 Hz ("medium-pitch sound"), and a pure tone of 1530 Hz ("high-pitch sound"; see Figure 1B). These stimuli were chosen to (a) maximize discriminability for sound segregation and (b) match the range of human speech (200 -2,000 Hz). 2) were used. C) The target sound occurred disproportionately often in one of three possible pitches (Exp. 1) or instrument chords (Exp. 2). Valid trials contained the frequent sound as the target and an infrequent sound as the nontarget; invalid trials exhibited the opposite pattern (i.e. the frequent sound was the nontarget and the infrequent sound was the target); neutral trials contained the two infrequent sounds.

The experiment was separated into two phases: learning and testing. The learning phase included the first 15 blocks in which, unbeknownst to the participants, the target gap sound appeared more frequently in one pitch compared to the others (see Figure 1C). Specifically, the target appeared in one of the three pitches on 60% of the trials (frequent target pitch), paired with one of the remaining two pitches each with equal probability (valid condition); on another 20% of the trials, the target sound appeared in one of the infrequent pitches but was paired with a nontarget sound that matched the frequent target pitch (invalid condition); finally, on the remaining 20% of trials, the target was equally likely to appear as one of the two infrequent pitches (neutral condition). Target sounds were counterbalanced across participants to ensure that the effects would not be due to one particular pitch. The subsequent testing phase included the last 15 blocks where this biased probability structure was removed and all sounds were equally likely to be the target.

Each block contained 30 trials. Trials were separated by an 800-1,000 ms inter-trial interval, resulting in a 30-to-40-minute-long experiment. Participants received feedback on their overall gap detection accuracy at the end of each block (every 30 trials) across both phases. Critically, this feedback was orthogonal to the statistical learning manipulation: it reflected only overall accuracy on the gap detection task, with no trial-level information about which responses were correct or which sound contained the gap. Block-level feedback was included solely to maintain motivation across a repetitive task. Before beginning the experiment, participants familiarized themselves with the task for about 4-5 blocks in a practice experiment until they confirmed they understood the task. The tone pitches used during this practice were increased by 20% to dissuade learning of the specific sounds used in the experiment, and each pitch was equally probable to be the target during this practice session.

To reduce the influence of differences in perceived loudness between the three pitches, before beginning the experiment, participants completed a sound thresholding task to adjust the pitch intensities based on perceived loudness for each subject. Specifically, participants were asked to compare the loudness of two sounds, hearing them in succession and incrementally adjusting the first sound to match the second sound's perceived loudness. Overall, 12 comparisons were conducted, six on each side (left, right), between the three pitches whereby both pitches were different with respect to order. Participants practiced the sound threshold task by undergoing the same task with the sounds used in the practice experimental task (20% increased in pitch).

Following the experiment, participants completed a post-experiment questionnaire assessing awareness of the statistical structure and strategies used. Most participants did not report attending to a specific sound, and when asked to identify the most frequent target sound, performance was roughly at chance (with the exception of the high-pitch sound, which participants were more likely to select regardless of assignment, suggesting some influence of bottom-up salience). As self-report questionnaires provide limited sensitivity for measuring explicit awareness (Smyth & Shanks, 2008; Giménez-Fernández et al., 2020; Vadillo et al., 2016 Vadillo et al., , 2020;; Holtz & Lee, 2025), these results should be interpreted with caution. Full details are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

Data Analysis

As we expected accuracy to be relatively high and thus less informative for uncovering differences due to probability learning, we used response time (RT) as our primary measure. We nonetheless evaluated accuracies across conditions for completion, following the exclusion criteria and the same statistical comparisons as for RTs. Data were processed as follows: Responses occurring within the first 200 ms and intra-individual RT outliers (> 3 SDs from an individual's mean RT), were excluded from the analysis; for RTs, all incorrect trials were excluded as well. We also decided a priori that data from subjects with an overall accuracy below 75% would be excluded from the analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using a 3 x 2 repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with two factors:TrialType (valid, neutral, or invalid) and Phase (learning, testing), with Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons computed as post-hoc tests of significant interactions. Where Mauchly's test for sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction (Greenhouse & Geisser 1959; Girden 1992) was applied for both degrees of freedom as well as p-values to control Type 1 error (Bianca et al., 2023). We report the adjusted statistical results and the correction factor in the ANOVA analyses.

Experiment 2

Experiment 2 tested whether learning extends to differences in timbre for different musical instruments, a more complex feature requiring later processing stages than pitch (Johnston & Heinz, 1978).

Methods

Participants

Our goal was to match the sample size of 33 participants from Experiment 1. To achieve this, we collected data from overall 37 participants between the ages of 18 and 23 years old (mean age = 19.5 yrs; SD = 1.26; 26 female, 11 male). Individuals provided informed consent before the experiment and were compensated for their participation with course credit or cash ($15/1 hour), following the methods of Experiment 1.

Data from three subjects were excluded from the final analyses because their overall accuracy fell below the apriori determined 75% threshold. Data from an additional subject was excluded from the analyses because they only completed 18 blocks in the experiment and reported having trouble detecting the gap during the experiment. Thus, data from 33 participants were retained for final analysis.

Stimuli and Procedure

The experimental procedure was identical to Experiment 1 except for stimulus type and duration. Three different stimuli were used across the experiment, each 525 ms in duration: a guitar chord, a piano chord, and a trumpet chord, all created using GarageBand (Apple Inc.). We increased the stimulus duration by 100ms to provide more time to process the more complex stimulus, since differences in timbre (not low-level features) needed to be processed to make the stimuli discriminable. All stimuli were composed in C-major (~250 Hz), and instruments were chosen based on general knowledge/familiarity of instrument characteristics. Participants also completed a post-experiment questionnaire and results are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether learning about statistical regularities of auditory characteristics (pitch and timbre) improves attentional selection. Our results demonstrate that people can incidentally learn to prioritize the most relevant feature for both simple and more complex sounds, suggesting that learned attention operates at multiple stages of the auditory processing hierarchy. Overall, our findings extend prior work demonstrating selection history effects for auditory location (Addleman & Jiang, 2019) and voice category (Daly & Pitt, 2023), as well as different visual features (Addleman & Störmer, 2023; Conn et al., 2020; Geng & Behrmann, 2005; Jiang et al., 2013; Sha et al., 2017; Addleman et al., 2019; Addleman, et al., 2024), suggesting incidental learning of statistical regularities is a general principle of attentional selection across domains.

While we found significant learning effects for both pitch and instrument chords, we also observed important differences. First, our results from the feature repetition-priming analysis suggest that pitchbased learning reflects genuine long-term statistical learning and not only short-term effects of priming, whereas instrument-based learning may be -at least in part -attributable to short-term repetition priming. Thus, at least for more complex auditory objects, short-term priming may play an important role in scaffolding the acquisition of learned selection biases, functioning as a signal that helps orient to true regularities and distinguish them from coincidences (Daly & Pitt, 2023). Second, and consistent with the repetition priming results, the validity effect persisted into the testing phase for learned pitches but was not statistically significant for learned instrument chords. Notably, the effect sizes were comparable across experiments in both learning (d=0.49 vs. d=0.46) and testing phases (d=0.26 vs. d=0.24), and a post-hoc cross-experiment comparison confirmed no significant difference between them (learning phase: p = 0.87; testing phase: p = 0.86). The absence of significance in Experiment 2 for the testing phase should therefore not be over-interpreted as evidence of a fundamentally distinct learning mechanism for instrumental timbre vs. pitch; it more likely reflects greater between-person variability or differential influences of feature repetition in the instrument condition.

Yet, it is worth noting that the overall pattern of results are compatible with a multimode view of auditory attention (Johnston & Heinz, 1978), according to which attention can operate flexibly at multiple levels of perceptual analysis, but may differ in its implementation -short-term priming vs. longer-term learning -as well as its effectiveness and durability. On this view, the analysis of simple features like pitch occurs relatively automatically, potentially allowing statistical regularities to be tracked and retained with ease, whereas selection on the basis of higher-level features such as timbre engages more complex processing stages, potentially yielding biases that are more context-dependent and less durable. This interpretation is consistent with Daly and Pitt (2023), who found a strong probability benefit for voice targets in isolation but a substantially reduced effect in more complex situations with nontargets present. At a more mechanistic level, it is plausible that the observed differences are due to how the respective acoustic features are represented in the auditory system: pitch is encoded tonotopically from early stages of auditory processing (for review, see Saenz & Langers 2014), providing a stable representational substrate for statistical learning. Instrument chords, by contrast, require the integration of spectrotemporal information across multiple frequency bands (Bregman 1990; Darwin & Carlyon, 1995; Darwin 1997; Shinn-Cunningham, 2008) -a conjunctive process that may permit learning but render the resulting biases more fragile. We emphasize, however, that these interpretations remain tentative given the between-experiment nature of the comparison, and direct confirmation in a within-subjects designwhere pitch and timbre learning are tested in the same participants -is needed before strong conclusions can be drawn. Future studies could also examine different auditory characteristics, such as rhythm, melody, or real-world (e.g. natural vs. manmade) sounds, to further explore this hypothesis.

Our data also revealed notable variability in the magnitude of the learning effects across specific target sounds, with high-pitch tones (and to a lesser extent the trumpet chord) showing numerically stronger effects (see Fig. 2B and 3B). Although we employed a thresholding procedure to equate perceived loudness across sounds, this seemed to not have fully equated perceptual salience across the stimuli. We acknowledge this as a limitation but also believe it points to a broader and rather underexplored question in the auditory statistical learning literature: stimulus salience and sound identity may meaningfully constrain the magnitude of learning effects. This may also speak to why effects in Experiment 2 appeared smaller overall, for sounds that are less intrinsically salient (in isolation) or perceptually distinct (when combined in a soundscape) may "steepen the learning curve" of statistical regularities. Our primary analyses were focused on addressing whether statistical learning occurs at all across auditory features -not to fully characterize how specific stimulus properties modulate it -and future work will be needed to disentangle the contributions of statistical learning and stimulus salience to attentional prioritization in audition.

How might learned auditory attention to pitch and instrument timbre be implemented? Most models suggest that learning affects attention by modifying weights on priority maps, in which top-down goals, incidental experience, and bottom-up saliency combine to shape what is attended (Awh et al., 2012; Ferrante et al., 2018; Addleman & Jiang, 2019). Priority map models were originally developed with location as the primary coding scheme, leaving it somewhat unclear how they extend to non-spatial features. Our findings, alongside work showing robust selection history effects for non-spatial features in the visual domain (Addleman & Störmer, 2023; Ortego et al., 2025; Addleman et al., 2024), suggest that learned attention can operate over non-spatial low-level features as well as more abstract and complex perceptual dimensions. The same argument extends to habit-based accounts of attentional learning, which emphasize behavioral routines over detailed probability representations and have likewise been framed primarily in spatial terms (Jiang, 2018 (Jiang, , 2019)). Thus, our findings suggest that both priority-map and habit-based mechanisms of attentional learning must be capable of operating at a featurerepresentational level, beyond spatial maps.

The present findings add to a growing literature on experience-dependent attentional prioritization, which includes reward-driven attentional capture, where stimuli associated with differential reward value acquire attentional priority (Anderson, 2016; Asutay & Västfjäll, 2016; Cheng et al., 2020). While it is possible that implicit reward signals contribute to the RT advantages we observe in the present study, we note an important distinction: reward-driven capture paradigms typically involve differential reward contingencies across stimuli, whereas in our paradigm all sounds were associated with identical feedback and thus reward outcomes (i.e., finding the correct target, regardless of pitch/timbre). The highprobability sound did not yield more reward -it simply occurred as the target more frequently. Statistical frequency, rather than differential reward value, is therefore the more parsimonious account of the present findings, though both mechanisms may rely on overlapping reinforcement signals at the neural level.

Overall, our results demonstrate that the auditory attentional system can be attuned to a variety of attributes through incidental experience. The acquisition and durability of such learning may be constrained by the format of the perceptual representations, with higher-level, complex features potentially proving less durable than simple unidimensional ones, and possibly also relying more heavily on short-term priming rather than long-term statistical learning. Thus, past experience, in conjunction with bottom-up salience and top-down goals, emerges as an important force shaping which sounds we come to prioritize in everyday listening.
