Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Nearly a decade ago, Mollon, Bosten, Peterzell, and Webster (2017) asked what individual differences can teach visual science and what counts as good experimental practice. Studies since then have changed both answers. The study of individual differences is a paradigm, not a technique, with its own objects, standards of evidence, and products. Long practiced in the shadows by a small group, it should move into the mainstream and integrate with existing paradigms, treating differences among observers as evidence rather than error. Its central object is the latent factor, which can earn roles from latent mechanism to established mechanism, computational phenotype, heritable phenotype, endophenotype, or factor-targeted marker, each requiring stricter evidence. Twelve pillars organize what the paradigm buys, including links across domains and levels and tests of whether constructs are unitary. Several 2017 conclusions need correction, above all the definition of true individual differences as stable, long-term traits, and the 2017 prescriptions (rank statistics, required test-retest reliability, fixed test order, mandatory blocking, blanket correction for multiple comparisons) each trade one error for another. Because global artifacts produce general factors, tuned factor structure is itself evidence against them. The long-term goal is a computational atlas of human visual variation: its mechanisms, their sources, and optimal markers for the clinic and for design.

[div-01]

Most experiments manipulate a stimulus, measure a handful of observers, and treat the differences among them as error. The standard observer of colorimetry and the standard contrast sensitivity function are monuments to this approach. Cronbach (1957) called experimental and correlational psychology "the two disciplines of scientific psychology." The experimental discipline brings situational variables under control so that causes can be isolated. The correlational discipline studies what the experimenter has not learned to control, or never can: the variation that nature has already imposed on people through their genes, development, environment, and history. Cronbach argued that they needed "combined, not parallel, labors," and Wilmer (2008) opened his call for individual differences research in vision with the same passage.

The core logic is simple. If two measurements vary together across observers, they probably share a source of variation; if they vary independently, they probably do not. Measure an observer's sensitivity at many points along a stimulus dimension, such as spatial frequency, wavelength, hue angle, orientation, or temporal frequency, and the pattern of correlations among those points reveals how many independent sources of variation underlie performance and over what range each one operates (Peterzell, 2016; Peterzell & Teller, 1996; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995; Wilmer, 2008). Information invisible in a group mean becomes visible in the covariance among observers, and the two kinds of information are independent: neither can be recovered from the other (Cronbach, 1957; Mollon, Bosten, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995).

1.2 Why call it a paradigm? Mollon et al. (2017) presented individual differences as a resource to be mined with caution, and devoted half their paper to pitfalls. That framing undersold the approach. Several of the 2017 recommendations were not merely debatable but wrong, and they steer researchers toward worse designs and away from analyses that work. I argue here for a stronger claim: the study of individual differences in vision is a paradigm, not a technique. It is not a new paradigm.

It has existed for more than a century, but mostly in the shadows of the experimental mainstream, kept alive by a small group of stalwarts who often worked in isolation and published in scattered places (e.g., Billock & Harding, 1996; Bosten, 2022; de-Wit & Wagemans, 2015; Emery & Webster, 2019; Grzeczkowski, Clarke, Francis, Mast, & Herzog, 2017; Luna & Serrano-Pedraza, 2020; Peterzell, 2016; Peterzell & Teller, 1996; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993; Vogel & Awh, 2008; Wilmer, 2008). The word is not new to this literature either. Thirty years ago, Peterzell and Teller (1996) described "the individual differences paradigm" as one that "uses simple detection data to assess the unadapted, unmasked visual system" and so avoids many of the assumptions on which masking and adaptation depend. What is new is the scope of the claim. I use the word in Kuhn's (1970) narrow sense of a set of shared exemplars: model problems and their accepted solutions, which tell practitioners what counts as an object of study, as evidence, and as a result. The covariance paradigm has its own exemplars, from König and Dieterici's (1886) derivation of cone sensitivities to the factor-analytic recovery of photopigment spectra, spatial channels, and hue mechanisms described below. It has its own central object, the latent factor, its own standards of evidence, and its own products: counts and tuning functions of mechanisms, individual phenotypic profiles, heritability estimates, and efficient, targeted tests.

The paradigm is not meant to replace the study of the average observer. Experiments establish causes; the covariance paradigm establishes how variation is organized. The two answer different questions about the same visual system, and each makes the other stronger. The shift I argue for is therefore the rise of an old paradigm from the margins to the mainstream, and with it a change in how vision science treats variability among observers: from error to be averaged away to evidence about how vision is organized.

Integration with existing paradigms is the point, not a concession. Nearly every psychophysical experiment tests more than one observer, so nearly every experiment can report the covariance among its observers alongside its means, at no extra cost in data collection if the stimulus dimension is sampled densely enough. Models of vision can be required to reproduce both the mean function and the pattern of individual differences (Peterzell, in press-a).

Physiology and brain imaging can define their units by covariance across people as well as by group averages. Genetics needs phenotypes, clinical vision science needs targeted tests, and the paradigm supplies both. In each case it adds a second, independent test of the same theory.

The title of this paper echoes the question Mollon et al. (2017) asked, "what can be learned and what is good experimental practice?", because I give different answers to both halves of that question. Far more can be learned than the 2017 paper claimed, and much of what it called good practice is not.

1.3 A short lineage

Galton (1883) founded the systematic study of individual differences and the use of twins to separate inherited from acquired variation. Spearman (1904) introduced factor analysis to explain the positive correlations among mental tests. Thurstone (1938) rejected a single general

Thurstone and his students carried the program into what he called visual thinking. By 1950 he described seven primary visual abilities, perceptual speed, three space factors, two closure factors, and visual memory, which "seem to represent distinct functional unities so that those who excel in one of these factors do not necessarily excel in the others" (Thurstone, 1950, p. 521). His students and successors refined the closure and perceptual factors (Botzum, 1951; Mooney, 1954; Pemberton, 1952; Roff, 1952; Street, 1931) and tested whether closure extends to symbolic and verbal material (French & Messick, 1973). Guilford (1967) placed figural abilities within his structure of intellect, and Carroll (1993), reanalyzing more than 460 datasets, organized visual abilities into a broad visual-perception domain with narrow factors ranging from visualization and closure to resistance to perceptual illusions and perceptual alternations.

In vision science, the paradigm's oldest success predates factor analysis. König and Dieterici (1886) used the variation between normal trichromats and dichromats to derive the spectral sensitivities of the cones. A century later, MacLeod and Webster (1983, 1988; Webster & MacLeod, 1988) factor-analyzed the archival color matches of Stiles and Burch (1959) and recovered separate sources of variation corresponding to lens pigment, macular pigment, and the cone photopigments. Sekuler, Wilson, and Owsley (1984) modeled the structure of individual differences in spatial vision, and Billock and Harding (1996) recovered separate spatial and temporal channels from the correlational structure of spatiotemporal contrast sensitivity. My own work with colleagues began in 1990, with structural models of infant spatial vision (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1990 , 1991) and a factor analysis that separated habituation and sensitization processes in infant visual attention (Peterzell, 1993). Over nearly four decades since, it has applied the same logic to the development of contrast sensitivity in infants (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995), to contrast sensitivity in adults (Peterzell & Teller, 1996), to chromatic and luminance mechanisms and equiluminance (Dobkins, Gunther, & Peterzell, 2000; Peterzell, Chang, & Teller, 2000; Peterzell & Teller, 2000), to scotopic vision (Peterzell, Schefrin, Tregear, & Werner, 2000), to evoked potentials (Peterzell & Kelly, 1997), and to transgenic mice (Peterzell, Crognale, et al., 1997). More recently, with Ignacio Serrano-Pedraza, Jenny Read, Michael Webster, Kara Emery, and others, it has extended to stereopsis and stereomotion, hue scaling and color naming, cortical color coding, numerosity, further work on engineered mice, and the separation of optical from neural factors with adaptive optics (e.g., Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017a , 2023; Kaneko, Kuriki, Andersen, & Peterzell, 2025; Llamas-Cornejo, Peterzell, & Serrano-Pedraza, 2024 , 2025; Peterzell, 2026h; Peterzell, Arrighi, et al., 2026; Peterzell, Bloxham, & Jacobs, 2018; Peterzell & Crognale, 2016; Peterzell & Elliott, 2026; Peterzell, Enongue, Read, & Serrano-Pedraza, 2026; Peterzell, Serrano-Pedraza, Widdall, & Read, 2017).

The turning point came with Wilmer (2008), a paper that deserves more than a place in a list. It was a primer, but also a program: it defined what the approach is for and what it could achieve. It built on a literature that had already worked out the logic and taught it intuitively: Peterzell, Werner, and Kaplan (1993, 1995) and Peterzell and Teller (1996) had shown, with plots of individual curves and of correlations against spatial frequency, how the pattern of covariance reveals the number and tuning of channels. What Wilmer added was scope and reach.

It explained plainly why differences among observers carry information that group means cannot. It defined the approach's three pillars, isolating the functional organization, the biology, and the utility of visual functions. And it showed how each could be pursued with concrete, testable proposals for stereopsis. It was generous to the small literature that preceded it, describing the line of work above as the most extensive of its kind. Above all, it was written for experimentalists. It made the approach legible to vision scientists who had been taught to average differences away, and it spurred a generation of new researchers to take individual differences seriously. Wilmer and his colleagues then showed what the program could deliver: face recognition as a reliable, heritable, specific ability (Wilmer et al., 2010 (Wilmer et al., , 2012)), two motion mechanisms that predict two phases of smooth pursuit (Wilmer & Nakayama, 2007), and webbased testing that made samples of thousands routine (Germine et al., 2012). Several of its own proposals have since been carried out. Stereoscopic channels have been mapped by factor analysis and shown to be tuned to retinal rather than object-relative spatial frequency (Peterzell, Enongue, et al., 2026; Peterzell, Serrano-Pedraza, et al., 2017; Reynaud & Hess, 2017), and the question it posed as open, whether stereomotion has separate sustained and transient mechanisms, now has an answer (Llamas-Cornejo et al., 2024). Its three pillars are the foundation on which the twelve pillars of Section 3 are built, and much of this paper extends the program Wilmer set out. Peterzell (2016) reviewed two centuries of sporadic work and issued a factor-analytic manifesto. Mollon et al. (2017) introduced a special issue of Vision Research devoted to individual differences, including a factor analysis of 25 measures in 1,060 observers (Bosten et al., 2017).

1.4 What has changed since 2017

Much has changed in a decade. First, factor analysis has recovered tuned structure in domains where it had rarely been applied: the spatial-frequency, temporal-frequency, and orientation tuning of stereoscopic mechanisms, and whether they are tuned to retinal or objectrelative spatial frequency; the spatiotemporal mechanisms revealed by duration thresholds (Luna & Serrano-Pedraza, 2020); hue and motion-direction scaling; cortical color coding; and the chromatic tuning of numerosity adaptation (Section 3.2.1). Second, adaptive optics has made it possible to separate optical from neural factors in the same observers (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026).

Third, engineered animals have provided a ground truth against which the method itself can be tested (Peterzell, 2026h). Fourth, work on specific abilities, above all face recognition, has shown that a visual ability can be reliable, heritable, and largely independent of general intelligence (Wilmer, 2017). Fifth, two neighboring fields have supplied concepts the paradigm lacked: computational psychiatry, which treats model parameters as computational phenotypes (Montague, Dolan, Friston, & Dayan, 2012; Patzelt, Hartley, & Gershman, 2018), a concept now carried over to vision (Peterzell, 2026f), and machine learning, which has shown that deep networks differing only in their random initialization develop different internal representations and so form populations of artificial observers (Mehrer, Spoerer, Kriegeskorte, & Kietzmann, 2020), whose covariance can be compared with that of human observers (Peterzell, in press-a).

These are only the developments closest to my own work. Wagemans' and Herzog's groups have built sustained programs on individual differences in Gestalt perceptual organization and visual illusions (Section 3.1.4). Aesthetics and emotion have their own long tradition of individual differences, from Pickford's (1951 Pickford's ( , 1972) ) studies of color vision and visual aesthetics to current work on why color preferences differ among people (Palmer & Schloss, 2010), how color-emotion associations vary across individuals, languages, and nations (Jonauskaite et al., 2020), the components of aesthetic experience and the dimensions of beauty (Marković, 2012; Marković, Bulut Allred, Čokorilo, Rančić, & Trkulja, 2026), and how much aesthetic taste is shared and how much is individual (Vessel, Maurer, Denker, & Starr, 2018). Applied vision science has taken up the approach as well, using factor analysis to find the independent dimensions in occupational vision screening and in oculomotor markers (Seemiller et al., 2023; Seemiller, Taylor, & Winterbottom, 2025). The paradigm now reaches further than any one review can cover.

These developments also show that several conclusions of the 2017 paper were wrong, and that several of its recommendations for practice should be abandoned. The corrections that follow are made plainly.

2. What is a factor? Roles earned by evidence

The covariance paradigm turns observed measures into latent factors, and latent factors into claims about mechanisms, individuals, genes, and clinical use. Each of these is a different claim, and each requires different evidence. Much of the confusion and skepticism in this literature comes from treating them as one. On the basicscience route, a latent mechanism becomes an established mechanism that constrains theories of vision. A factor-targeted marker is measured at the single stimulus where the target factor loads highest and competing factors load near zero. 2.1 From observed measure to latent factor

Every factor begins with observed measures: thresholds, matches, scaling responses, evoked-potential amplitudes. The first requirement is that the measures be reliable as measures of differences between people. This is not the same as reliability for detecting differences between conditions. Tasks designed to produce robust effects across conditions often do so by minimizing variation among observers, so a task can be excellent for experiments and poor for individual differences (Hedge, Powell, & Sumner, 2018; Wilmer, 2008).

A note on terms. Throughout this paper, factor means a latent source of shared individual variability, however it is estimated, and factor analysis means the family of covariance-based methods used to estimate such sources. Statisticians rightly distinguish common factor analysis, principal component analysis, independent component analysis, multidimensional scaling, and structural equation models, which rest on different assumptions and can give different answers; a methods section should name the one used. But the word factor predates them all and belongs to none of them. Spearman and Thurstone used it for the underlying sources of individual differences that their methods were designed to find, and earlier work in this tradition referred to "statistical 'sources' of individual variability (or factors)" (Peterzell, Kelly, et al., 1996). I use it in that broad sense.

A latent factor is a statistical summary of shared variance: a replicable pattern of covariance among measures. On its own, a factor is a description, not a cause. Stable covariance can arise from a common cause, from direct interactions among the measured variables, or from shared artifacts, and the factor model alone cannot tell these apart (Borsboom, Mellenbergh, & van Heerden, 2004). The converse also needs care: physiologically distinct mechanisms can produce indistinguishable behavior, so the absence of covariance is not always the absence of a mechanism (Billock, 2020). The factor's earning criterion is therefore replicability: the same structure in independent samples, preferably with different observers, laboratories, and apparatus. Achromatic contrast sensitivity is an encouraging case, because a consistent factor structure has appeared across more than six very different datasets (Peterzell, 2024).

2.2 From factor to latent mechanism

A latent mechanism is a hypothesized process that the visual system carries out: a spatialfrequency channel, a cone photopigment, a sustained or transient temporal mechanism, a hueselective cortical population. Reading a factor as a mechanism requires evidence beyond covariance, of three main kinds. The first is theory: the factor's tuning should be interpretable in terms of known or plausible processes. The second is convergent and discriminant evidence: the factor should appear in different tasks that tap the same process and should separate from factors that tap different processes (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). The third, when it is available, is ground truth: a known answer the method should recover.

Ground truth is rare in perception, but it exists. In mice engineered to express a human long-wavelength photopigment (Jacobs, Williams, Cahill, & Nathans, 2007), factor analysis of spectral sensitivity recovers factors that match the known middle-and long-wavelength opsin spectra (Peterzell, 2026h). This is the modern counterpart of König and Dieterici's (1886) result, and it is a direct test of the claim that factors can correspond to mechanisms.

The same evidence can also show that a factor is not the mechanism it appeared to be.

For decades, factor analyses of contrast sensitivity recovered factors at high spatial frequencies that were read as neural channels. When contrast sensitivity was measured with and without adaptive-optics correction in the same observers, the factor that dominated sensitivity above about 2 cycles per degree disappeared under correction. It reflected the optical quality of the eye, not a neural channel (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026). A factor that earns the mechanism role is a factor whose interpretation has survived tests like this one.

2.3 The basic-science route: established mechanisms

Above the latent mechanism, the path divides. On the first route, which stays with the visual system in general, a latent mechanism becomes an established mechanism when its interpretation converges with independent evidence: physiology, anatomy, computational models, and, where it exists, ground truth. Peterzell, Werner, and Kaplan (1993) described individual differences as "a powerful set of 'converging' operations," which allow theoretical models generated from one type of data, representative functions, to be tested against a completely different type, patterns of individual differences.

Established mechanisms are among the paradigm's most important products, and they need no clinical or genetic payoff to justify them. Spatial-frequency factors in contrast sensitivity match the coarsest channels of Wilson's computational models, and in infants they shift to higher spatial frequencies with age as a developmental change in spatial scale predicts (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1995; Peterzell & Teller, 1996; Wilson, 1988; Wilson & Gelb, 1984). Monte Carlo simulations made the test explicit: a model in which spatial mechanisms shift to higher frequencies with age reproduced the infants' correlational structure, and a model with adultlike, unshifting mechanisms did not (Peterzell, 1991 (Peterzell, /1992;; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995).

That fit to Wilson's spatial-frequency channels has held up consistently across infants, adults, and viewing conditions, and it remains a robust description of how contrast sensitivity divides along spatial frequency. Two newer results change what the factors are taken to be. Across orientation, the factors for contrast sensitivity are broad, not narrowly tuned as Wilson-style channels predict, which also challenges estimates of narrow orientation channels drawn from masking (Peterzell, Bachtoula, Llamas-Cornejo, Martín-García, & Serrano-Pedraza, 2026). And with adaptive optics, the factor that dominates high spatial frequencies in normal viewing turns out to be optical, while the neural factor revealed beneath it is too broadly tuned in spatial frequency to be a narrow channel (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026). The spatial-frequency factors are therefore established as mechanisms, but more likely as broadly tuned pathways, such as the magnocellular and parvocellular streams, than as the narrow cortical channels of the models they fit. Factors in color matches and spectral sensitivities recover known photopigment and pigment spectra (MacLeod & Webster, 1983 , 1988; Peterzell, 2026h). Hue scaling yields many narrowly tuned factors, which challenges the classical opponent-process account of color appearance (Emery et al., 2017a (Emery et al., , 2023)). Each is a claim about how vision works, not about any individual, and theories of color, spatial vision, and neural coding must accommodate it. This route does not require individual-level precision: a mechanism can be established even when no single observer's score on it is reliable enough to serve as a phenotype.

2.4 The individual route: computational phenotypes

The second and third routes concern individuals. The term computational phenotype comes from computational psychiatry, where Montague et al. (2012) defined it as "a measurable behavioral or neural type defined in terms of some computational model. " Patzelt et al. (2018) described how parameters of a model fitted to each person's behavior serve as phenotypes. I extend the term to vision, with one change of emphasis. A latent mechanism is a claim about the visual system in general. A computational phenotype is a claim about individuals: an observer's stable position on a validated mechanism, measured precisely enough to characterize that person (Peterzell, 2026f). To earn the role, a factor needs reliability across sessions, replication across samples, and validity against independent measures. In return, it lets each observer be described by a profile of factor scores rather than by a single threshold. The parameters of sequentialsampling decision models, fitted to each observer, are a ready example in vision. The decision stage is a source of individual differences of its own, distinct from the sensory mechanisms that supply its evidence. Its parameters are trait-like: drift rate and boundary separation keep high rank-order stability over two years, with correlations of .64 or higher for drift rate and .83 or higher for boundary separation (von Krause et al., 2021), and over a week (Lerche & Voss, 2017), and drift rate covaries with ability across tasks (Ratcliff, Thapar, & McKoon, 2010). They therefore meet this bar and are candidate computational phenotypes. Whether decision parameters form one factor or several, and whether those factors are tuned to the stimulus or domain-general, is a question for the covariance logic of this paper (Section 4.3).

2.5 The genetic and clinical-use routes

Above the computational phenotype, the individual route branches again. On the genetic route, a phenotype becomes a heritable phenotype when twin, family, or genomic studies show that a substantial part of its variance is genetic. Examples include face recognition ability (Wilmer et al., 2010; Zhu et al., 2010) and individual variation in personal color space (Bimler & Kirkland, 2004; see also Paramei, Bimler, & Mislavskaia, 2004). A heritable phenotype becomes an endophenotype only when it also meets the criteria Gottesman and Gould (2003) set out: association with illness in the population, heritability, state independence, co-segregation with illness within families, and elevation in unaffected relatives. Psychiatric genetics offers two cautions. Endophenotypes do not necessarily have simpler genetic architecture than the disorders they index (Flint & Munafò, 2007), and an endophenotype can index genetic liability without lying on the causal pathway to the disorder (Kendler & Neale, 2010).

On the clinical-use route, a factor becomes a factor-targeted marker: a measure of the factor, taken at the single stimulus where the target factor loads highest and competing factors load near zero, and validated against a disease or physical state (Peterzell, in press-b). Choosing that stimulus is itself a discriminant-validity criterion in Campbell and Fiske's (1959) sense. In the FDA-NIH BEST glossary, a biomarker is a measured characteristic that indicates a biological process, and it is "not a measure of how an individual feels, functions, or survives"; a measure based on a standardized task is a performance outcome (FDA- NIH Biomarker Working Group, 2016). A marker derived from an electroretinogram or a visual evoked potential is therefore a biomarker in the BEST sense, while a marker derived from a psychophysical threshold is a performance-outcome measure. I use "marker" for both. Endophenotypes, in turn, are a subset of biomarkers (Lenzenweger, 2013), so an endophenotype can also serve as a marker. A factor-targeted marker earns its role only when both analytical validation (is the measure accurate and reliable?) and clinical validation (does it index the clinical state in a stated context of use?) are shown (Goldsack et al., 2020; Pepe et al., 2001).

Either route can begin directly from a latent factor, before any mechanism is known (dashed lines in Figure 1). Heritability can be estimated for a factor score whatever the factor turns out to represent, and a marker can be useful for diagnosis without a mechanistic interpretation. Huys, Maia, and Frank (2016) drew the same distinction in computational psychiatry between data-driven and mechanistic routes to the clinic. The shortcut has a cost. A marker with no known mechanism can support diagnosis or risk prediction, but it should not be used as a surrogate for treatment effects, because associations off the causal pathway can mislead (Fleming & DeMets, 1996).

2.6 A worked example: face recognition as a specific ability

Face recognition shows how one factor can earn several roles. Wilmer and colleagues set out criteria for a specific ability: it should be consistent with theory, measured reliably, show convergent and discriminant validity, have norms, and allow precise individual scores (Wilmer et al., 2012; Wilmer, Germine, & Nakayama, 2014). Face recognition meets these criteria. The Cambridge Face Memory Test is reliable and normed (Duchaine & Nakayama, 2006), and face recognition ability dissociates from general intelligence and from memory for other visual objects (Wilmer et al., 2010 (Wilmer et al., , 2012)). That earns the latent factor and computational phenotype roles. Face recognition is also highly heritable in twins (Wilmer et al., 2010; Zhu et al., 2010), which earns the heritable-phenotype role. It has a long upper tail of super-recognizers, which is useful for selection (Russell, Duchaine, & Nakayama, 2009). Without an established disease link, it has not earned the endophenotype role.

Two points follow. Wilmer's criteria for a specific ability correspond closely to the evidence that earns the computational-phenotype rung. And specific abilities and low-level mechanisms are the same idea at different grains: a high-level ability such as face recognition and a low-level mechanism such as a spatial-frequency channel are both narrow, separable sources of variation, judged by the same evidence. My reanalysis of Thurstone's (1944) data, which recovered separate factors for geometric illusions, perceptual switching, and Gestalt closure (Peterzell, 2020), makes the same point within perceptual organization.

2.7 Why the roles must be kept apart

The roles in Figure 1 are a communication device, not a natural kind; Bilder et al. (2009) said the same of the seven levels in their genome-to-syndrome schematic, whose number was chosen to be easy to hold in mind. What matters is that each role is a separate claim with its own evidence. A mechanism established for vision in general says nothing yet about any one observer, a heritable phenotype is not yet an endophenotype, and a correlate is not yet a validated marker. Keeping the roles apart protects the paradigm against its most common failure, overclaiming, and lets each study state plainly which roles its factors have earned and which remain open.

3.1 Five kinds of payoff

3.1.1 Information about mechanisms and markers

Group means show what the average observer can do; covariance shows how the work is divided. It counts the mechanisms that underlie a function, maps the range and coordinate frame of each, and separates stages such as optics, photoreceptors, and cortex, non-invasively and often from existing data. Once the factors are known, a single well-chosen stimulus can measure each one, turning a long test into a short one and a threshold into a profile (Section 3.5.3).

3.1.2 New statistical approaches

The paradigm has developed methods of its own: confirmatory models that adjudicate between competing architectures (Emery et al., 2023); nonmetric multidimensional scaling for structure that is non-linear (Kaneko et al., 2025); Monte Carlo simulation and resampling, which test whether a hypothesized model reproduces the observed correlations and how many observers a structure requires (Emery et al., 2017a; Peterzell, 2026h; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995); a formal test of whether loadings are tuned (Peterzell, Enongue, et al., 2026; Peterzell & Serrano-Pedraza, 2026); populations of artificial observers with known answers (Peterzell, in press-a); and intuitive factor analysis, which often shows the structure before any statistic is computed (Peterzell, 2016, in press-b). Section 4.10 takes these up in detail.

3.1.3 Relationships across domains and levels

The correlations between factors are themselves findings, perhaps the paradigm's most underused payoff. An experiment on the mean can show that two tasks respond to the same manipulation; only covariance among observers can show whether they share a source of variation in people, and it can do so for any pair of measures taken in the same observers. Within a single domain, two orientation-tuned contrast factors correlate at about .67, so orientation mechanisms are partly shared rather than independent (Peterzell, Bachtoula, et al., 2026), and optical and neural factors are correlated, so observers with poorer optics also have lower neural sensitivity even after optical correction (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026).

Across stimulus domains, Dobkins, Gunther, and Peterzell (2000) measured luminance contrast sensitivity, red-green chromatic contrast sensitivity, and red-green equiluminance settings in the same adults at 16 combinations of spatial and temporal frequency. Each measure formed its own factors, but equiluminance settings correlated with luminance sensitivity at high temporal frequencies and, negatively, with chromatic sensitivity at low ones, implying that the fast equiluminance mechanism is governed by luminance mechanisms and the slow one receives a chromatic contribution: a claim about how two pathways interact that no group mean could support. Red-green and luminance spatial channels are independent in adults but coupled in infants (Section 3.4.1). Measured in the same observers, disparity thresholds and luminance contrast thresholds are uncorrelated across orientation, so the mechanisms serving stereopsis and those serving luminance contrast are separable (Serrano-Pedraza, Boegaerts, Read, & Peterzell, 2018). Min and Reynaud (2026) report that spatial channels for luminance and for texture reorganize differently with age, although the luminance result may partly reflect optics, which were not corrected (Peterzell, 2026e). Hue has narrow factors and motion direction broad ones, although both are defined by an angle around a circle, hue angle in a chromatic plane and direction of motion in the visual field (Emery et al., 2017a (Emery et al., , 2023)). And two motion mechanisms predict two phases of smooth pursuit, which links perception to action (Wilmer & Nakayama, 2007). The question is old: Thurstone (1950) asked whether visual and auditory closure are one ability, and noted that the decisive experiment, a factor analysis of both in the same people, had not been done.

Across levels, factors from psychophysics can be set beside factors from neural measures taken in the same observers. Visual evoked potentials yield spatial-frequency-tuned factors comparable to psychophysical ones in infants and adults (Peterzell & Kelly, 1997; Peterzell, Kelly, et al., 1996), and steady-state potentials yield a structure for hue that can be compared directly with hue scaling (Emery et al., 2017a; Kaneko et al., 2025). When a psychophysical factor and a neural factor correlate across people, and in the same stimulus range, they are candidates for the same mechanism. When they do not, one of the two interpretations is wrong.

Brain imaging is the largest untapped opportunity. Most functional maps define regions and their tuning from group means and treat variation among people as noise. The alternative is to define units by covariance across people, as studies of structural covariance already do for anatomy (Alexander-Bloch, Giedd, & Bullmore, 2013). Precision mapping of single brains also reveals network features that group averaging hides (Gordon et al., 2017). In vision, the size and magnification of an observer's primary visual cortex predict acuity (Duncan & Boynton, 2003) and perceived size (Schwarzkopf, Song, & Rees, 2011; see also Kanai & Rees, 2011). The next step is to factor-analyze the maps themselves, treating the response or tuning at each cortical location as a variable measured across observers, and to relate the resulting neural factors to psychophysical factors in the same people. The requirements are those for any factor: reliability, replication, and validity (Dubois & Adolphs, 2016).

3.1.4 Tests of construct validity

Perhaps the most important thing individual differences can do for vision science as a whole is to test its constructs. The field names constructs, such as global processing, magnocellular function, illusion susceptibility, and visual ability, and measures each with a favored task, assuming that different tasks for the same construct measure the same thing. That is a claim about construct validity in Cronbach and Meehl's (1955) sense, and it can be tested only across individuals: if two tasks measure the same construct, observers who do well on one should do well on the other. When they do not, calling both tasks measures of one construct is an instance of the jingle fallacy, giving different things the same name (Kelley, 1927).

Herzog and colleagues have pressed this argument harder than anyone. Across six basic visual tasks, only 4 of 15 correlations were significant, despite high test-retest reliability (Cappe, Clarke, Mohr, & Herzog, 2014). Variants of the same illusion correlate strongly across observers, but different illusions correlate weakly, so illusion factors are largely illusion-specific rather than feature-specific (Cretenoud et al., 2019; Grzeczkowski et al., 2017). These individual differences are stable across eyes, sessions, and measurement methods (Cretenoud, Grzeczkowski, Kunchulia, & Herzog, 2021), which rules out the objection the 2017 paper would have raised, that the weak correlations merely reflect unreliable measures. The same pattern holds in healthy aging and in mild cognitive impairment: vision declines, but not through a common factor (Garobbio, Pilz, Kunchulia, & Herzog, 2023; Shaqiri et al., 2019). The conclusion is direct: a single test cannot stand for a construct, and claims built on one test are claims about that test. Wagemans and colleagues reached the same conclusion for perceptual organization. Local-global processing bias "is not a unitary construct" but a set of discrete abilities measured by different tasks (Chamberlain, Van der Hallen, Huygelier, Van de Cruys, & Wagemans, 2017), and in 14 tasks used to measure perceptual style, neither a single global-tolocal continuum nor a two-factor model fit the data; only a narrow disembedding factor emerged (Milne & Szczerbinski, 2009). Tasks used interchangeably as tests of magnocellular function in dyslexia, autism, and schizophrenia also share little reliable variance (Goodbourn et al., 2012).

Covariance across locations in the visual field needs the same care: crowding covaried with both perceived spacing and position sensitivity around the field, but once the group mean at each location was removed, only the link with sensitivity remained an individual difference (Haseeb, Wolfe, & Kosovicheva, 2023). Just outside vision, Vogel and Awh (2008) made the same case for cognitive theory: visual working memory "capacity" turned out to combine separable components. The number of items held and the resolution with which each is held vary independently across people (Awh, Barton, & Vogel, 2007), only the number predicts fluid intelligence (Fukuda, Vogel, Mayr, & Awh, 2010), and differences in capacity partly reflect how well people keep irrelevant items out of memory (Vogel, McCollough, & Machizawa, 2005).

The question of how much common variance exists is not fully settled. The oldest counterevidence comes from the first factor-analytic study of perception: Thurstone (1944) found a factor defined by geometric illusions, which my reanalysis confirmed, along with separate factors for perceptual switching and Gestalt closure (Peterzell, 2020), and Carroll's (1993) survey of the factor-analytic literature lists resistance to perceptual illusions as a narrow factor of visual perception. More recent studies find a common illusion factor accounting for about a quarter (Mehrvarz, Popat, & Rouder, 2026) to nearly half (Makowski, Te, Kirk, Liang, & Chen, 2023) of the variance, and hierarchical models that separate trial noise from true variation can support a broad discrimination factor (Rouder & Mehrvarz, 2026). Across a broad visual battery, a general factor accounted for about a fifth of the variance, beside eight specific factors that replicated across random halves of 1,060 observers (Bosten et al., 2017). In the other direction, steady-state VEP and psychophysical measures of contrast sensitivity, acuity, and orientation discrimination correlated neither with each other nor across modalities (Morea, Garobbio, Kunchulia, & Herzog, 2026). The emerging answer is the one the paradigm predicts: at most a modest general factor, sitting on top of strong specific ones. That is not a problem for the paradigm. A visual system built from narrow, specialized mechanisms predicts exactly this result, and the specific factors are where the information lies. Either way, whether two tasks measure the same thing must be settled across individuals before the tasks are used interchangeably. This is the logic of the one-stimulus-per-factor principle: a marker should target a factor demonstrated by covariance, not a construct named in advance.

3.1.5 New life for archival data

Any published dataset that reports individual observers is a factor study waiting to be done. Reanalysis produced one of the paradigm's landmark results, the factor analysis of Stiles and Burch's (1959) color matches (MacLeod & Webster, 1983 , 1988; Webster & MacLeod, 1988), and the approach has since been applied to archival spectral sensitivities of engineered mice (Peterzell, 2026h). It is cheap and fast, and open data make it easier (Section 4.2).

3.2 What vision is made of

3.2.1 Functional organization: how many mechanisms, and how each is tuned

In plain terms: vision is built from many specialized mechanisms, and covariance among observers is one of the few ways to count them in a living human and to see what each one does.

A textbook diagram of vision is a claim about this organization. Individual differences test it.

The evidence now spans most of early and middle vision. Luminance contrast sensitivity contains low-and high-spatial-frequency factors that are present by two months of age and shift with development (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1995), and the same structure replicates across many datasets (Peterzell, 2024). Stereoscopic sensitivity to sinusoidal depth corrugations contains two spatial-frequency-tuned factors (Peterzell, Serrano-Pedraza, Widdall, & Read, 2017; Reynaud & Hess, 2017). Those factors are tuned to retinal spatial frequency, not to frequency relative to the size of the object (Peterzell, Enongue, et al., 2026). Stereomotion contains a slow, sustained factor and a fast, transient factor (Llamas-Cornejo et al., 2024).

Stereoscopic sensitivity across orientation contains two broadly tuned factors (Llamas-Cornejo et al., 2025), and so does luminance contrast sensitivity across ten orientations (Peterzell, Bachtoula, et al., 2026). Both results conflict with the textbook view, drawn from masking and adaptation, that orientation is coded by many narrow channels.

Color shows the opposite pattern. Hue scaling reveals many narrowly tuned factors rather than the two opponent axes of classical theory, while motion-direction scaling reveals only a few broad factors aligned with the cardinal directions of visual space (Emery et al., 2017a (Emery et al., , 2023)).

Individual differences in steady-state evoked potentials to hues sweeping around the color circle show a structured pattern of correlations that bears on how the cortex codes hue (Kaneko et al., 2025). And chromatic tuning appears even in a cognitive task: adaptation to numerosity is tuned to hue through two factors, one for small and one for larger hue differences (Peterzell, Arrighi, et al., 2026).

Covariance can also separate stages, as when adaptive optics revealed a supposed neural channel to be optical (Section 2.2).

The same mathematics describes what vision is built from. Receptive fields learned from natural images are themselves a factor solution, and the edge detectors of visual cortex follow from a simple-structure rotation of natural image patches, so differences among observers are, in effect, differences among factor analysts (Peterzell, 2026j).

What it buys: direct, quantitative constraints on models of vision, drawn from humans, often from existing data, and able to overturn textbook accounts.

3.2.2 Abilities and construct validity: separate talents, and whether tasks measure the same thing

In plain terms: people differ in how well they see faces, objects, depth, and patterns, and these talents are largely separate. Knowing which visual talents are separate tells us how vision is divided at the level of whole abilities, not just early mechanisms. The same evidence tests the field's constructs: if two tasks are supposed to measure one ability, people who do well on one should do well on the other. Thurstone (1944 Thurstone ( , 1950) ) found separable perceptual abilities rather than one general factor. Figure 3 reproduces his sample items for five of the seven abilities he described in 1950.

The factors have outlived their names. Carroll (1993) placed them at the narrowest level of a hierarchy, beneath broad abilities and a general factor, and they survive in the reference tests that mark them (Ekstrom, French, Harman, & Dermen, 1976) and in the current Cattell-Horn-Carroll taxonomy (Schneider & McGrew, 2018): perceptual speed as Perceptual Speed; the first space factor as Speeded Rotation, the fast rotation of simple figures now usually called mental rotation; the second as Visualization, the slower, multistep transformations of paper folding and mechanical movement; the two closure factors as Closure Speed and Flexibility of Closure; and visual memory as Visual Memory.

The space factors were reorganized, with speed and complexity doing much of the dividing (Lohman, 1979), but not discarded. The third space factor is the most striking case. Thurstone (1950) defined it by the observer's own body orientation, linked it to pilots and to reading instruments, and called it "somewhat of a puzzle" (p. 518). Half a century later it returned as perspective taking: separable from mental rotation in confirmatory factor analysis (Kozhevnikov & Hegarty, 2001), and dissociated from it experimentally even though the two correlate highly, which explains why factor studies had found it so hard to isolate (Hegarty & Waller, 2004). A correlational finding waited fifty years for the experimental work that explained it, which is Cronbach's (1957) argument in miniature.

Face recognition is the model case (Section 2.6; Wilmer, 2017). How much of this variation is general is set out in Section 3.1.4: at most a modest general factor, on top of many specific ones. Herzog and colleagues turned that result into a test of construct validity, and Wagemans and colleagues did the same for local-global processing. What it buys: a map of vision's separable abilities, tests that measure each one, a check on whether the field's constructs are unitary before tasks are used interchangeably, a basis for selection (for example, of super-recognizers; Russell et al., 2009), and evidence about how modular vision is.

3.2.3 Experience and appearance: private perceptual worlds

In plain terms: each of us sees the world through a different eye and brain, so each of us may see a slightly different world. Individual differences are the only way to ask how different those worlds are, and why they are so often the same.

Observers differ widely in pigment densities, cone ratios, and photopigment spectra, yet often agree closely on what looks white or pure yellow. That agreement despite different hardware reveals normalization processes that calibrate perception to each observer's own visual system (Mollon et al., 2017). Young observers wearing filters that mimic an aging lens partly recalibrate their white point within a day (Tregillus, Werner, & Webster, 2016), and anomalous trichromats see red-green differences as more vivid than their threshold losses predict, which implies a postreceptoral gain of about fourfold (Boehm, MacLeod, & Bosten, 2014; Emery, Kuppuswamy Parthasarathy, Joyce, & Webster, 2021). Covariance also shows that the unique hues are not tied together: the wavelengths different observers choose for unique blue, green, and yellow vary widely and largely independently (Webster, Miyahara, Malkoc, & Raker, 2000).

Even a judgment as basic as where an object is carries an observer-specific signature: each observer's pattern of localization errors around the visual field is reproduced across response methods, including saccades, and across sessions up to 24 weeks apart, yet is only weakly correlated with other observers' patterns (Kosovicheva & Whitney, 2017), and these distortions covary with each observer's local acuity and apparent size (Wang, Murai, & Whitney, 2020).

The dress image showed that the agreement can also fail, and fail dramatically (Winkler, Spillmann, Werner, & Webster, 2015; Witzel, Racey, & O'Regan, 2017). Individual differences in hue scaling do not track individual differences in color naming (Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017b), so the structure of color appearance is not simply a reflection of language.

Individual differences in simultaneous color contrast show that induction increases with stimulus complexity, and that its apparent relation to age can be dissociated from induction itself (Grasso, Peterzell, et al., 2026). More broadly, a "color" stimulus varies along many physiological and psychological dimensions at once, and individual differences show which of them actually vary across people (Peterzell, 2026a).

What it buys: a science of appearance that goes beyond the average observer, explanations for disagreements such as the dress, and a basis for calibrating displays and images to individual viewers.

3.3 Where differences come from

3.3.1 Behavioral genetics: genes, evolution, and engineered animals

In plain terms: some differences in how people see are inherited. Finding out which ones, and which genes are involved, connects visual mechanisms to their biological roots.

Twin studies show substantial heritability for face recognition (Wilmer et al., 2010; Zhu et al., 2010) and for the structure of personal color space (Bimler & Kirkland, 2004). Twin comparisons also show individual variation in color perception beyond common genetic inheritance (Paramei, Bimler, & Mislavskaia, 2004). Genome-wide studies of large cohorts have reported associations between visual phenotypes and specific genetic regions, including a visual phenotype associated with the 1q21 risk region for autism and schizophrenia (Goodbourn et al., 2014) and ocular phoria (Bosten et al., 2014). The oldest genotype-phenotype link in vision remains the clearest: a single amino-acid polymorphism in the long-wavelength photopigment shifts the Rayleigh match (Winderickx et al., 1992). Engineered animals take the logic further: adding a single human photopigment gene to mice changes their color vision (Jacobs et al., 2007), and factor analysis recovers the added mechanism, a test of the method against a known answer (Section 2.2). That work began in the 1990s, more than a decade before Wilmer (2008), with a factor analysis of transgenic mice expressing a human photopigment (Peterzell, Crognale, et al., 1997), and continued in later abstracts (Peterzell, Bloxham, & Jacobs, 2018; Peterzell & Crognale, 2016) and a full report (Peterzell, 2026h). Factor scores make better genetic phenotypes than single thresholds, because they are more reliable and closer to a single mechanism, so heritability is easier to detect and to interpret.

What it buys: the biological basis of visual mechanisms, purer phenotypes for genetic studies, and a bridge from genes to perception through animal models.

3.3.2 Environment and culture: what experience does to vision

In plain terms: where and how people live changes how they see. Diet, light exposure, age, season, and language all leave measurable traces, and individual differences let us separate those traces from inherited ones. Macular pigment density varies with diet (Hammond et al., 1997), and lens density increases with age and light exposure (Pokorny, Smith, & Lutze, 1987). Color perception shifts with the seasons, as the colors of the environment change (Welbourne, Morland, & Wade, 2015). Within a single language, individuals vary widely in how they name colors, and some speakers share naming patterns more with speakers of other languages than with their own linguistic community (Lindsey & Brown, 2009). A non-uniform perceptual space, estimated from individual differences in hue scaling, may also help explain where languages place their color boundaries (Peterzell, 2026c).

What it buys: a way to separate nature from nurture in vision, an account of how vision adapts to the world each person lives in, and a basis for designing for real populations rather than for a standard observer.

3.3.3 Retinal and neural substrates: linking behavior to eye and brain

In plain terms: individual differences test whether a retinal or brain measure and a behavior share a mechanism: if people with stronger responses also perform better, and in the right stimulus range, the link is strengthened; if not, it is in doubt.

Factor analysis applies to neural data as readily as to behavior: evoked potentials, steadystate responses, and electroretinograms all yield interpretable factors (Kaneko et al., 2025; Peterzell & Kelly, 1997; Peterzell, 2026h), and brain structure predicts individual differences in several visual functions (Kanai & Rees, 2011). The retina is especially accessible. Adaptiveoptics imaging shows that the ratio of long-to middle-wavelength cones varies more than tenfold among observers with normal color vision (Hofer, Carroll, Neitz, Neitz, & Williams, 2005), and optical coherence tomography gives structural measures of each retina that can be set beside psychophysical and electroretinographic factors in the same people. A model case comes from visual working memory: the contralateral delay activity, an event-related potential, rises with the number of items held and levels off at each person's own capacity limit, so its amplitude predicts individual capacity (Vogel & Machizawa, 2004). Wilmer (2008) pointed out that any experiment linking a neural index to behavior contains a second, independent test of that link: whether the index predicts behavior across individuals. Section 3.1.3 describes how neural and psychophysical factors can be linked, and how brain imaging could define cortical units by covariance across people.

What it buys: non-invasive links between levels of the visual system, from retina to cortex, and a built-in second test of every brain-behavior claim.

3.4 Beyond the typical adult observer

3.4.1 Development, aging, and change within a person

In plain terms: vision changes over a lifetime, and also over a day, a season, and a course of training. Individual differences show which mechanisms change, when, and why, and they separate changes in the eye's optics from changes in the brain.

Infant contrast sensitivity already contains separate spatial-frequency factors by two months of age, and their tuning shifts with development, a line of work that began with a longitudinal dissertation (Peterzell, 1991 (Peterzell, /1992;; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1991 , 1993 , 1995; reviewed in Peterzell, 2016). Color and luminance contrast sensitivity were first analyzed together for factor structure in the evoked potentials of infants and adults (Peterzell, Kelly, et al., 1996), and then psychophysically in infants (Peterzell, Chang, & Teller, 2000) and adults (Peterzell & Teller, 2000). In infants, chromatic and luminance factors are coupled; in adults, they are independent. That coupling means early chromatic and luminance development cannot be treated as independent, which limits what late-sighted patients can tell us about the role of early color experience (Peterzell, 2026b; Peterzell, 2026i). In aging, chromatic discrimination declines after about 60, most steeply on the blue-yellow axis (Paramei & Oakley, 2014), while color appearance is largely preserved by recalibration (Section 3.2.3). An apparent reorganization of spatial channels in older adults (Min & Reynaud, 2026) may be partly optical rather than neural, because the high-spatial-frequency factor in uncorrected viewing reflects optics (Peterzell, 2026e). Within a person, state variables such as sleep, light history, circadian phase, and practice change performance. Mollon et al. (2017) treated these as contamination that masks "true" individual differences. That was wrong (Section 4.1): the mechanisms that vary from hour to hour, month to month, and year to year are themselves worth mapping.

What it buys: a lifespan map of how visual mechanisms differentiate and decline, a way to tell optical changes, which can often be corrected, from neural ones, and a framework for studying change within a person.

3.4.2 Disorders: profiles, not single deficits

In plain terms: visual disorders and conditions that affect vision rarely knock out a single mechanism. Individual differences let us describe each patient by a profile across mechanisms, and let us check whether supposedly related tests measure the same thing.

Color vision deficiency is a continuum, not a category: anomalous trichromats range from near-normal to near-dichromatic. Some visual measures behave as candidate endophenotypes for psychiatric conditions, such as visual backward masking in schizophrenia (Chkonia et al., 2010). Individual differences can also correct a field's assumptions, as when supposed tests of "magnocellular function" turned out to measure different things (Goodbourn et al., 2012; Section 3.1.4). And some differences are differences of kind rather than degree.

Deficiency is not only loss: dichromats detect color-camouflaged targets that trichromats miss (Morgan, Adam, & Mollon, 1992), multidimensional scaling reveals a color dimension that only deuteranomalous observers perceive (Bosten, Robinson, Jordan, & Mollon, 2005), and one carrier of anomalous trichromacy behaved as a tetrachromat on every test (Jordan, Deeb, Bosten, & Mollon, 2010). Women with genes for more than three photopigments also report seeing more distinct colors in a spectrum than trichromatic controls do (Jameson, Highnote, & Wasserman, 2001), although reports are a weaker test of four-dimensional color vision than discrimination.

Observers with poor chromatic discrimination show no chromatic tuning of numerosity adaptation at all (Peterzell, Arrighi, et al., 2026). When groups differ in their factor structure, that difference is itself a finding.

What it buys: diagnostic profiles in place of single numbers, earlier detection through departures from normal structure, and tests that have been checked to measure what they claim.

3.4.3 Artificial observers: populations of networks that differ like people

In plain terms: we can now build many slightly different artificial visual systems and ask whether they differ from each other the way people do. If they do, we can find out why, by changing one thing at a time, which is impossible with people.

Deep networks that differ only in their random starting weights develop different internal representations, so a trained network is one sample from a population of possible observers (Mehrer et al., 2020). Peterzell (in press-a) proposed a computational psychometrics of vision: build a designed population of artificial observers varying in architecture, initialization, training diet, optics, and cone sampling; give them the tests given to humans; and ask whether factor analysis recovers the human factor structure. A model that reproduces the average human but not the human covariance structure has not explained human vision. Safeguards keep the benchmark from becoming a target: analytic decisions fixed in advance, a held-out battery, and transfer to domains the model was not tuned on.

What it buys: a new and demanding benchmark for models of vision, and causal tests of where individual differences come from.

3.5 What it connects to and what it is good for

3.5.1 Vision beyond vision: cognition, language, emotion

In plain terms: sensory mechanisms do not stop at perception. Individual differences show where visual mechanisms shape counting, naming, and emotional responses, and where they do not.

Numerosity is a clear case. Correlations among observers in reproducing numbers by action fall off with numerical distance, and factor analysis finds two tuned number channels, one for lower and one for higher numbers (Anobile, Petrizzo, Paiardini, Burr, & Cicchini, 2024).

Numerosity adaptation is selective for salient features (Grasso, Anobile, Arrighi, Burr, & Cicchini, 2022), including color, a selectivity that depends on the automatic categorization of colored information (Grasso, Anobile, Gurioli, Cicchini, & Arrighi, 2025), and its chromatic tuning is narrow and differs across observers (Peterzell, Arrighi, et al., 2026). Individual differences in hue scaling can supply a realistic perceptual space for models of color naming (Peterzell, 2026c). Differences in color perception also bear on clinical assessment: color responses to the Rorschach inkblots depend on how each person sees color, a factor the standard scoring systems do not consider (Peterzell, 2026g). And separate motion mechanisms predict separate phases of smooth pursuit, linking perception to action (Section 3.1.3).

What it buys: bridges from sensory mechanisms to cognition, language, emotion, and action, and checks on claims that a higher-level effect is "really" sensory, or not.

3.5.2 Human factors and design: from the standard observer to the individual user

In plain terms: knowing how people differ tells us who will do well at a task and who will struggle, how to design displays, signals, lighting, and environments for everyone rather than for an average observer who does not exist, and how to fit a device to each user.

The oldest use of individual differences in vision is selection: color vision tests for railway workers followed the Lagerlunda collision of 1875 (Mollon & Cavonius, 2012), and face recognition tests now help select super-recognizers (Russell et al., 2009). Whether a vision test predicts performance depends on the task: among ship watchkeepers searching for life rafts, color vision and low-contrast acuity scores predicted daytime detection but not detection through night vision goggles, which remove color (Donderi, 1994). Factor analysis can also streamline selection itself: in an occupational vision-screening battery given to Air Force personnel and civilians, it identified a small number of independent dimensions, so that redundant tests could be dropped and operationally relevant ones kept (Seemiller et al., 2023). The larger use is in design standards, most of which are written for a standard observer: colorimetry rests on standard color matching functions, photometry on a single luminous efficiency function, and accessibility guidelines set contrast ratios computed from luminance alone (World Wide Web Consortium, 2018). Anthropometry learned long ago to design cockpits for a range of body sizes; vision has been slower.

The covariance paradigm supplies what better guidelines need. First, it shows how many independent dimensions a guideline must cover. A standard based on one measure, such as acuity, implicitly assumes one factor; where there are several, as for contrast sensitivity and stereopsis, a single criterion passes people it should flag and flags people it should pass, and a stereoscopic headset comfortable for one profile of mechanisms may fail for another. Second, it shows which limitations travel together. Aging changes several factors, but not in lockstep (Garobbio et al., 2023; Shaqiri et al., 2019), so a single correction for "older users" fits few of them. Third, it supplies the parameters for designing to a distribution. Colorimetry has begun to do this: the CIE physiological observer makes color matching functions depend on age and field size (CIE, 2006), and an individual colorimetric observer model generates populations of observers from the sources of variation that factor analysis of archival color matches first separated (Asano, Fairchild, & Blondé, 2016; MacLeod & Webster, 1983 , 1988). The question for design is the one Peterzell (2026d) asked of color, whom a choice helps and whom it hurts, and it applies to every visual dimension a designer can manipulate.

Two design strategies follow. Universal design serves the whole distribution at once: critical information is carried redundantly, by luminance, shape, or position as well as by color, in palettes that remain distinguishable with color vision deficiency (Peterzell, 2026d).

Individualized design fits the device to the person. Calibrating a display to the standard observer corrects the device, not the viewer (Asano et al., 2016). Displays, headsets, and vehicles can increasingly be tuned to each user, but tuning requires knowing which parameters to set. Factor structure supplies the answer: the number of independent factors is the number of calibration parameters, and the one-stimulus-per-factor principle (Section 3.5.3) gives the short test that estimates them. A user's factor profile becomes a calibration profile. Virtual-reality headsets make the case sharply. Each embodies one model of its user, with one interpupillary distance, one eye height, one focal distance, and one set of color-matching functions, and an apparent sex difference in cybersickness turned out largely to reflect how well the headset fit each user's interpupillary distance (Stanney, Fidopiastis, & Foster, 2020).

Driving shows how far the logic reaches. Driver distraction is usually treated as a single drain on attention, and hands-free laws treat it that way: they remove the manual demand of a call but not the conversation, which degrades simulated driving either way (Strayer & Johnston, 2001). Part of the cost is perceptual: moving gaze off the forward road slowed brake-light detection by roughly 450 ms, whereas cognitive load added little (Wolfe, Sawyer, Kosovicheva, Reimer, & Rosenholtz, 2019). Treated instead as a multidimensional, person-dependent event, distraction has latent sensory, attentional, imaginal, and affective-autonomic dimensions that covariance could recover from naturalistic driving data, together with the individual differences that predict who is most vulnerable (Peterzell & Service, 2026). The link to vision is old. Owsley, Sekuler, and Siemsen (1983) measured contrast sensitivity across adulthood, Sekuler et al. (1984) modeled the structure of individual differences in spatial vision, and Owsley went on to show that the useful field of view predicts crash risk in older drivers (Owsley et al., 1998), although the field it measures is graded rather than divided into useful and useless regions (Wolfe, Dobres, Rosenholtz, & Reimer, 2017); a few supertaskers show little cost from dualtasking (Watson & Strayer, 2010). Drivers also differ in how much they need to see. Younger drivers detected hazards in road video after about 220 ms and chose an evasive steer after about 390 ms, older drivers after about 400 and 600 ms (Wolfe, Seppelt, Mehler, Reimer, & Rosenholtz, 2020), and the age gap replicated online, with wide variation within each age group (Guidi, Kosovicheva, & Wolfe, 2024). Naturalistic driving research is now seeking drivingbased markers of Alzheimer disease and other disorders: GPS driving features distinguish older adults with preclinical Alzheimer disease (Bayat et al., 2021), and passive in-vehicle sensors are being developed into digital markers (Babulal et al., 2025). These analyses have used classifiers and regression on many driving features, not the covariance among them. A covariance analysis would ask how many independent dimensions of driving behavior there are and which carries the disease signal, and would then choose the measure that isolates it: a factor-targeted marker for the road. It has yet to be done, and it could improve both the assessment of drivers and the design of vehicles and driver-monitoring systems. Shared, annotated road videos already support repeated laboratory measures of hazard perception (Song, Kosovicheva, & Wolfe, 2023), and the same drivers tested on several such tasks would supply the data it needs. Guidelines built on the average driver protect the average driver.

What it buys: evidence-based selection and occupational standards, guidelines that cover the right number of visual dimensions, designs for the range of real observers rather than the standard observer, and devices calibrated to each user's factor profile.

3.5.3 Measurement and clinic: phenotypes, markers, and efficient tests

In plain terms: if we know how many mechanisms underlie a visual function, we know how many measurements we need, and which ones. That makes tests shorter, cleaner, and easier to interpret.

The one-stimulus-per-factor principle has two phases (Peterzell, in press-b): discovery, in which a function is sampled densely enough to resolve every factor, and assessment, in which one stimulus is placed where each factor loads highest and the others load near zero. The savings can be large. Two orientations can replace ten for contrast sensitivity (Peterzell, Bachtoula, et al., 2026). For stereopsis, a few spatial frequencies, two orientations, and two temporal frequencies capture the known mechanisms, whereas a standard clinical stereotest samples one point and can miss whole mechanisms. Each targeted measure becomes a candidate marker for one mechanism (Section 2.5), and an observer's set of marker scores becomes a computational-phenotype profile (Peterzell, 2026f). Treatment can then be aimed at a specific mechanism and checked against it, as adaptive optics does when it removes the optical factor and reveals the neural factors it had masked (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026). Thurstone (1950) made the clinical case three quarters of a century ago: two patients with the same audiometric hearing loss can differ markedly in understanding conversation because they differ in auditory closure (Karlin, 1942 , as described by Thurstone, 1950).

What it buys: shorter tests, cleaner markers, individual profiles in place of single numbers, and treatments that can be aimed at and verified against a specific mechanism.

4. How to do it well: there is no single right design

Nearly a decade ago, Mollon et al. (2017) handed down rules. This section hands them back. What follows is not a refinement of the 2017 recommendations but a repudiation of them.

They were offered as the one right way, and there is no one right way.

Several of the 2017 warnings remain useful, and I return to them at the end of this section (Section 4.11). But the central recommendations share a flaw: each treats one design choice as the safe one, when every choice in this field trades one error for another. The right standard is not a single correct design. It is a study whose conclusions survive the plausible alternatives, shown by replication, by converging methods, and by reporting how results change when reasonable analytic choices change (Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman, & Vanpaemel, 2016).

4.1 What counts as an individual difference: the most consequential error

Of all the claims in Mollon et al. (2017), the most consequential, and the most wrong, was the definition on which their methods rested. They divided the variance between observers tested on a single occasion into four sources: "constitutional, or other long-term, betweenindividual, variation"; "within-individual variation"; instrumental variation; and variation in the experimenter. They then stated that the last three "mask the size of any true individual differences." In that scheme, only stable, long-term differences count as true individual differences, and every way in which a person changes is placed in the same bin as a drifting photometer and a tired experimenter. The test-retest requirement discussed below rests on that definition.

The definition is not a harmless convention. It is wrong, and it came from left field. There are, fittingly, individual differences in how researchers define individual differences. But that distribution has a center, and the 2017 definition lies many standard deviations away from it. Cronbach's (1957) correlational discipline studies whatever variation nature has imposed on people, not only the variation that holds still. Changes in a person's vision over hours, days, months, and years are human variability, and they contribute to the differences measured between people on any given day. Lumping them with instrument error confuses a property of the observer with a property of the apparatus.

Human vision runs on cycles at every time scale.

There are circadian rhythms in visual function and retinal physiology, including the daily shedding of photoreceptor outer segments (LaVail, 1976); renewal cycles in the tissues of the eye over days, months, and years, with the cumulative effects of light across the lifespan (Werner, Peterzell, & Scheetz, 1990); monthly menstrual cycles, with accompanying changes in sensory function (Farage, Osborn, & MacLean, 2008); seasons, whose changing colors shift color perception (Welbourne et al., 2015); and sleep, light history, adaptation, practice, and learning. Mollon et al. (2017) cited several of these effects themselves, but only as threats to measurement.

Every one of them is a legitimate object of study. Variation that is not permanently stable has been factor-analyzed for nearly 80 years, and it contains meaningful, interpretable factors. Cattell, Cattell, and Rhymer (1947) introduced P-technique, factoring the correlations among many measures taken from one person across many days, and recovered definite psychological and physiological factors. Zevon and Tellegen (1982) applied P-technique to daily mood ratings over 90 days and found Positive and Negative Affect within individuals, not only between them.

Latent state-trait theory treats state variance as reliable, true-score variance to be estimated, not as error to be removed (Steyer, Schmitt, & Eid, 1999). Applied to salivary cortisol, it showed that the proportion of variance that is trait-like is itself governed by the daily cycle: about 60% in the morning, but only about a third in the afternoon and evening (Kirschbaum et al., 1990).

Under repeated sleep deprivation, the impairment produced by a state manipulation turned out to be a stable, trait-like individual difference organized along three distinct dimensions (Van Dongen, Baynard, Maislin, & Dinges, 2004). And in 100 daily sessions of cognitive testing, the factor structure of each person's day-to-day variation differed from the between-person structure of abilities (Schmiedek, Lövdén, von Oertzen, & Lindenberger, 2020). The general lesson, stated formally by Molenaar (2004), is that between-person structure cannot be assumed to describe the variation within any individual. Each of these discoveries would have been impossible under a definition that discards variation because it does not hold still.

Vision has documented the same kinds of variation but rarely analyzed them this way.

The timing of the cone-driven electroretinogram changes with the daily light cycle (Hankins, Jones, Jenkins, & Morland, 2001). Short-wavelength cone sensitivity can vary across the menstrual cycle in some women and not in others (Eisner, Burke, & Toomey, 2004). Perceptual biases for ambiguous stimuli differ between observers and also drift from day to day within them (Wexler, Duyck, & Mamassian, 2015). These are individual differences in cycles, and differences in who cycles at all. Within-person factor analyses of such variation, such as daily contrast sensitivity functions measured across circadian, monthly, and seasonal cycles, appear not yet to have been done in vision. They are an open opportunity, and exactly the studies the The case for a paradigm shift 32 2017 definition would have ruled out. Throwing such data away is not caution. It is discarding the evidence.

None of this denies the value of stability where stability is the point: a computational phenotype or a clinical marker must be stable enough for its purpose (Section 2). But that is a requirement for particular roles, not a definition of the field.

4.2 Imperfect data, real discoveries

The cost of a single-right-way approach is easiest to see in the paradigm's own landmarks. MacLeod and Webster (1983, 1988; Webster & MacLeod, 1988) factor-analyzed the color matches that Stiles and Burch (1959) had collected three decades earlier, in another laboratory, to define a standard colorimetric observer. Those data were not collected with repeated sessions, a design chosen to protect a factor analysis, or an independent trait against which to validate the factors. By the standard Mollon et al. (2017) set for demonstrating true individual differences, the reanalysis would not have been undertaken. Yet it recovered the separate pigment factors described in Section 1.3, produced direct estimates of the cone absorption spectra, and was cited by Mollon et al. (2017) themselves as an exemplary confirmatory analysis. That is not a matter of interpretation: applied as written, the standard excludes one of the paradigm's defining results, and a rule that rules out the field's own best exemplar is wrong.

The same holds across the paradigm's history. Peterzell, Werner, and Kaplan (1993) found interpretable structure in published data from six to eight infants per age group (Banks & Salapatek, 1981), and my reanalysis of Thurstone's (1944) battery recovered separate illusion, switching, and closure factors (Peterzell, 2020). Elliott et al. (2009) designed their adaptiveoptics study to examine optical aberrations in aging, not individual differences, yet its reanalysis separated optical from neural factors (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026). Mollon et al. (2017) conceded the point in their conclusion: "few studies meet all of the requirements of the best practices advocated, though many have yielded plausible and compelling accounts." That concession is the right lesson, and it should have been the rule rather than the exception. Data that fall short of an ideal design can still answer questions, provided the analysis is honest about what the data can and cannot show, deals openly with unruly observations rather than hiding or discarding them (McClelland, 2000), and the result is tested by replication or by converging evidence.

Discouraging such analyses does not protect the field from error. It leaves discoveries in the archive, and archives do not last: data sit on obsolete disks and failing drives, in files no current software can read, and in the offices of researchers who retire or die, and then they are lost for good, along with every discovery they still held. Some of my own data are already gone: the raw data from our study of infant visual evoked potentials for color and luminance are lost, and the results survive only in a conference abstract (Peterzell, Kelly, et al., 1996) and in the supplementary materials of a later commentary (Peterzell, 2026b). Datasets that report individual observers should be analyzed for their covariance while they can be, and deposited in public repositories so that others can analyze them later.

4.3 Tuned structure is evidence against artifacts

Most of the 2017 warnings concern artifacts that vary between observers but have nothing to do with the mechanism under study: fatigue, motivation, mood, distraction, response bias, and the drift of instruments and experimenters. The concern is real, but these artifacts share a property that Mollon et al. (2017) did not exploit. They act on an observer's performance across the board. An observer who slept badly, or who adopts a conservative criterion, performs worse across conditions, not only at spatial frequencies between 1 and 2 cycles per degree.

Global artifacts therefore produce a general factor, a positive correlation among all measures.

They do not produce narrowly tuned factors with dips in correlation between neighboring conditions. This is arithmetic, not opinion: a source of variation that acts equally across conditions adds the same amount to every covariance, and so cannot by itself create correlations that are high within one range of a stimulus dimension and low between ranges. An artifact that acts unequally across conditions predicts structure that follows its own profile, such as the order of testing, and that prediction can be tested.

The argument is not new. Peterzell, Werner, and Kaplan (1993) set out three possible causes of individual differences in contrast sensitivity and the correlational structure each predicts: measurement error predicts uncorrelated structure, an overall maturational or attentional level predicts equicorrelational structure, and spatial-frequency-tuned mechanisms predict correlations that are high between neighboring frequencies and fall off with distance. A study that finds only a general factor has good reason to worry about artifacts. A study that finds several factors, each confined to a contiguous range of the stimulus dimension, has found structure that global artifacts cannot easily produce, and that structure is itself evidence that the variation comes from the mechanisms. Many of the 2017 safeguards were designed for a threat that tuned factors already argue against. Whether a factor is tuned can now be tested formally rather than judged by eye (Section 4.10). Mollon et al. (2017) made test-retest reliability across "well-separated occasions" the "minimal requirement" for demonstrating true individual differences, and dismissed reliability from a single session as "seldom a secure argument." As a requirement, that standard is wrong, and not by a matter of degree. Test-retest reliability across sessions is valuable when it is available. It is not the only evidence, and in many studies it is not the strongest. The requirement also inherits the error of the definition behind it (Section 4.1). When a person's vision genuinely changes between sessions, a modest test-retest correlation may reflect that change rather than measurement error, and telling the two apart requires reliability within a session, the very evidence the 2017 paper dismissed.

4.4 Reliability: the evidence is plural

At least four other kinds of evidence show that individual differences are real. The first is replication of the factor structure in independent samples, which is far harder to obtain by accident than a single test-retest correlation (Peterzell, 2024). The second is recovery of a known ground truth, such as photopigment spectra in engineered mice (Peterzell, 2026h). The third is tuned structure, for the reasons just given. The fourth is split-half reliability with correction for attenuation, which Wilmer (2008) recommended and which is adequate when the question is whether a dissociation can be explained by measurement error. Replication, ground truth, and tuned structure show that the structure is real; whether individual factor scores are precise enough to characterize one observer is a separate question, answered by the reliability of the scores themselves (Section 2.4). What matters is reliability of differences between people, which a task optimized for differences between conditions may lack (Hedge et al., 2018). Researchers should report whatever reliability evidence they have, and say which kind it is.

4.5 Keep the metric

Mollon et al. (2017) recommended Spearman's rank-order correlation "as an alternative, or in addition" to Pearson's, because ranks resist the leverage of outliers. As a general rule, this is not a point for debate. It is wrong. When the data are approximately normally distributed, when outliers have been checked and dealt with, and when there are no ceiling or floor effects that restrict the range, there is no reason to give up the metric. Converting such data to ranks throws away information that was paid for in observer hours, sacrificing power and precision for nothing in return. Those conditions are not exotic: psychophysical thresholds are measured on meaningful metric scales, and on a logarithmic scale they are usually close to normally distributed.

For factor analysis, the cost is larger. Ranking attenuates correlations and produces a matrix of rank correlations that is a non-standard input to factor analysis, so the loadings that come out are harder to read as tuning functions. Looking at variability in two or three observers is not a crime, as I argue below. Discarding good metric data is closer to one.

Outliers are a real problem, but the remedy is due diligence, not a blanket transform. Judd and McClelland's model-comparison approach remains the best practical guide (Correll, Folberg, Judd, McClelland, & Ryan, 2026; Judd & McClelland, 1989; Judd, McClelland, & Ryan, 2017; McClelland, 2000). Plot the distributions and the scatterplot matrix, check for ceiling and floor effects, identify outliers with diagnostics designed for the purpose, ask whether each one reflects an error or a genuine observer, treat it accordingly, use robust covariance estimates or the bootstrap where needed, and report whether results change. When the data genuinely fail these conditions, being non-normal, range-restricted, non-metric, or non-linear, the right tool is a transformation or method chosen for that failure. Analyzing ranks is itself a transformation, and a crude one, because it discards the spacing between observations. A transformation chosen to make the distribution approximately normal and the variance homogeneous, such as a logarithmic or other power transformation, usually solves the same problem while keeping the information (Correll et al., 2026; Judd et al., 2017; McClelland, 2000). Psychophysicists already do this with log thresholds. Where no transformation suffices, a method designed for the data, such as nonmetric multidimensional scaling, is the next step (Kaneko et al., 2025). Ranks are one tool among these, chosen when the data call for them, not a default applied to everything. 4.6 Order and blocking: preferences, not prerequisites Mollon et al. (2017) argued that tests should often be given in the same fixed order to every participant, so that order-specific strategies and learning do not create spurious individual differences. A fixed order replaces a possible artifact with a certain one. The confound is not a risk that judgment can weigh; the design guarantees it. Fatigue and practice become part of the measure, and adjacent conditions become artificially correlated. The 2017 paper acknowledged this and left the choice to "nice judgement." The better default is to randomize or counterbalance, to record order, and to model it, entering order into the model as a predictor whose contribution can be tested rather than assumed (Correll et al., 2026; Judd et al., 2017).

The paper also warned that interleaving conditions can create spurious factors when observers attend selectively to some conditions, and it favored separate blocks. Here the recommendation itself is reasonable: blocking is often the better design, and its own cost, tying each condition to a time slot, can be reduced by counterbalancing the order of blocks.

What does not follow is that data collected without blocking should be set aside. A design preference is not a criterion for admissibility. When conditions were interleaved, the researcher's task is the one that applies to every imperfect dataset: acknowledge the possible artifact, state the alternative hypothesis it implies, and test it against the data. Selective attention to some conditions predicts a particular pattern, factors that follow whatever an observer could attend to, which can be compared with the tuned structure a mechanism predicts. Where possible, it can be settled directly by comparing interleaved and blocked designs in the same observers, and a dataset that leaves the alternative open should be reported with that caveat, not discarded. A design that makes an artifact impossible to detect is worse than one that lets it be measured. Mollon et al. (2017) raised the question of whether corrections for multiple comparisons should account for every behavioral measure in a study, in addition to the genes or voxels being tested. For factor-analytic work, blanket correction is the wrong tool, and this is not a question of how conservative one prefers to be. In the terms of the model-comparison approach, the question is which of several competing models best accounts for the data, how many factors and with what loadings, not how many of many separate tests reach significance (Correll et al., 2026; Judd et al., 2017). The real risk in exploratory work is overfitting, and the defenses against it are replication in new samples, held-out data, factor-retention criteria such as parallel analysis (Horn, 1965), confirmatory tests of structure proposed in advance, and pre-registration of the analyses that matter. A factor model does not test the correlations one at a time, so correcting them one at a time corrects for tests that were never made. Applying Bonferroni correction to every correlation in a small psychophysical sample mostly buries real structure. Mollon et al. (2017) concluded that "there is currently no solution" to the problem of separating perceptual effects from response biases in individual differences. That conclusion was wrong. Signal detection theory has provided ways to separate sensitivity from criterion for more than half a century (Green & Swets, 1966). The forced-choice methods used for most psychophysical thresholds were introduced to make performance largely independent of criterion, but they are not bias-free. Klein (2001) showed that a preference for one interval lowers the d′ computed from pooled percent correct. The loss grows with the square of the bias, so it is negligible when the bias is small and can inflate a threshold by tens of percent when it is large. Interval and position biases are common, and they vary in size and sign across observers and can depend on the stimulus conditions (García-Pérez & Alcalá-Quintana, 2011; Jäkel & Wichmann, 2006; Yeshurun, Carrasco, & Maloney, 2008). Mollon et al. (2017) cited Klein for the problem, but Klein also supplied the correction: record responses by interval and average the interval-specific z scores, a correction he was surprised to find "rarely used" (Klein, 2001 (Klein, , p. 1425)). His point is a reason to model bias, not to despair of it. A bias that acts alike across conditions contributes mainly to a general factor (Section 4.3), but its effect on uncorrected scores depends on where each condition sits on its psychometric function, so it cannot simply be assumed away. The remedy is to record the interval or location of every trial; to estimate bias and sensitivity jointly, including with models that allow an undecided response or that separate sensory from decisional bias (García-Pérez & Alcalá-Quintana, 2011; Linares, Aguilar-Lleyda, & López-Moliner, 2019); to analyze bias-corrected sensitivity; and to enter the bias estimates in the factor model as variables of their own, which tests directly whether bias rather than sensitivity carries any tuned structure. Bias is also an individual difference in its own right, not only an artifact to remove. Observers hold stable, idiosyncratic criteria that are linked, modestly, to how each encodes the stimulus (Rahnev, 2021), and choice-history biases are idiosyncratic, act on evidence accumulation rather than only on its starting point (Urai, de Gee, Tsetsos, & Donner, 2019), and are stable across sessions two to four weeks apart (Urai & Donner, 2022). A problem that can be measured is not a problem without a solution. The number of observers a study needs depends on how reliable the measures are, how much observers truly differ, how many factors must be resolved, and how the observers are distributed. Samples whose observers cluster near the mean estimate relations far less efficiently than samples that span the full range, including the extremes (McClelland & Judd, 1993). A small sample of observers who differ widely can carry more information than a large sample of observers who are alike. When signal is high, small samples suffice. Resampling from 86 mice showed that samples of 10 to 12 reproduced the full-sample correlation structure closely and recovered the expected photopigment factors (Peterzell, 2026h, in press-b). The point is old: Peterzell and Teller (1996) recovered the two coarsest spatial channels from seven adults, and Peterzell (2016) showed factor structure visible in the contrast sensitivity functions of three or four observers. When signal is low, much larger samples are needed. The right way to set sample size is to simulate it: generate data with the expected structure and reliability, and find the sample at which the structure is recovered. This matters most for expensive physiology and patient studies, where the 2017 advice would have discouraged feasible work.

4.8 Response bias: model it, do not despair of it

4.9 Sample size depends on signal, not on a rule

The belief that small datasets are useless is one of the most costly in this field, because it stops analyses before they start. The familiar rules of thumb, such as 100 cases or five to ten per variable, came from questionnaire research, where items are noisy and loadings modest.

Psychophysical thresholds from practiced observers are far more reliable, and their factors load strongly on many measures. Methodological work shows that the sample a factor analysis needs depends on the size of the loadings and communalities and on how many measures define each factor, not on a fixed number (MacCallum, Widaman, Zhang, & Hong, 1999). With high loadings, few factors, and many measures, samples well below 50 can recover the structure reliably (de Winter, Dodou, & Wieringa, 2009). Many datasets dismissed as too small already contain answers, and should be analyzed with the uncertainty reported honestly.

It is not a crime to look at variability in data from only two or three observers and venture hypotheses. In fact, it is a very good idea, as history has shown. Dalton (1798) began the scientific study of color deficiency by describing one observer who differed from others, himself, and König and Dieterici (1886) derived the cone sensitivities from a handful of observers who differed in kind. Classical psychophysics has long drawn conclusions about mechanisms from a few practiced observers, often the authors themselves, and there is no principled reason to accept inferences from a few observers' means and forbid inferences from their differences. When the curves of two or three observers differ systematically in one range of a stimulus dimension but not another, that pattern is information, and it is legitimate to plot it and venture a hypothesis about what produced it. This is intuitive factor analysis (Section 4.10), which Mollon et al. (2017) themselves endorsed as a way to see structure in the data. Exploration is not confirmation, and a hypothesis drawn from three observers must be tested in more. But generating hypotheses is how every paradigm begins, and a rule that forbids looking at small samples forbids discovery at its earliest and cheapest stage.

4.10 Use the whole toolkit

Factor analysis is the paradigm's workhorse, but it rests on assumptions: approximately linear relations, multivariate normality for maximum-likelihood estimation and its tests, and a rotation that is not unique. Exploratory factor analysis proposes structure; confirmatory analysis and replication test it. Deciding how many factors to keep needs the same care. The usual rules, Kaiser's criterion, the scree test, parallel analysis, and the minimum average partial test, ask how large a factor is. The MacLeod-Webster criterion asks instead whether a factor's loadings change in an orderly way along the stimulus dimension (MacLeod & Webster, 1983; Webster & MacLeod, 1988), which is the question that matters when mechanisms are expected to be tuned.

The loading-structure significance (LSS) test turns that criterion into a hypothesis test, comparing the observed loadings with loadings from simulated random data and with loadings whose stimulus labels have been shuffled. In simulations with 50 observers, it detected a weak tuned mechanism in most samples, where parallel analysis kept it in fewer than 1% (Peterzell, Enongue, et al., 2026; Peterzell & Serrano-Pedraza, 2026). It has already identified tuned factors in stereopsis and in the spectral sensitivities of engineered mice (Peterzell, 2026h; Peterzell, Enongue, et al., 2026). Oblique rotation should be the default when mechanisms may be correlated, because forcing orthogonality hides the connections between mechanisms that are themselves findings. Nonmetric multidimensional scaling is better when the data are non-linear or do not fit simple structure (Kaneko et al., 2025). Individual-differences scaling, in the INDSCAL tradition (Carroll & Chang, 1970), goes further: it fits a shared perceptual space and gives each observer a weight on each of its dimensions. Applied to color, it has shown that men give less weight than women to the red-green axis and more to lightness (Bimler, Kirkland, & Jameson, 2004), that the structure of personal color space is partly heritable (Bimler & Kirkland, 2004), and that mercury-exposed workers and patients with type 2 diabetes show a blue-yellow compression confined to desaturated colors, a loss the standard desaturated cap test tends to miss (Bimler, Paramei, Feitosa-Santana, Oiwa, & Ventura, 2014). Monte Carlo simulation shows what a method can and cannot recover, and whether a hypothesized model of mechanisms reproduces the observed pattern of correlations. The paradigm has used it from its early days (Emery et al., 2017a; Peterzell, 1991 Peterzell, /1992 Peterzell, , 2016;; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995; Sekuler et al., 1984). And some of the most convincing analyses need no inferential statistics at all. In intuitive factor analysis, taught from the paradigm's early papers (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995; Peterzell & Teller, 1996) and named by Peterzell (2016), the researcher simply looks: at the individual curves, the scatterplots, and the matrix of correlations plotted against the stimulus dimension, where tuned mechanisms appear as bands of high correlation between neighboring conditions that fall off with distance. A pattern seen this way is often more persuasive than any statistic, because readers can check it with their own eyes. The fullest stepby-step tutorials are Peterzell (in press-b) and Peterzell (2016). Worked examples, each showing individual curves and correlation matrices alongside the formal analysis, can be found for infant contrast sensitivity (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1995), adult contrast sensitivity (Peterzell & Teller, 1996), visual evoked potentials (Peterzell & Kelly, 1997), scotopic contrast sensitivity (Peterzell, Schefrin, Tregear, & Werner, 2000; reanalyzed in Peterzell, 2016), steady-state evoked potentials to hue (Kaneko et al., 2025), and spectral sensitivity in engineered mice (Peterzell, 2026h). These plots should be published routinely.

4.11 What to keep from 2017

Several 2017 recommendations remain sound as best practices. Cases and controls should be tested interleaved in time and place, not cases first and controls later. Reliabilities should be reported where they are available, so that effect sizes can be corrected for attenuation and absent correlations can be interpreted. Genetic association studies should guard against population stratification. And instruments drift: displays, light sources, and anomaloscopes change with time and temperature, so drift should be controlled or measured where possible. These recommendations let artifacts be detected or prevented without sacrificing information, which is a useful test to apply to any recommendation in this field.

But they are best practices, not conditions of admission. A dataset that lacks calibration logs, reliability estimates, or interleaved controls has not thereby lost its value. It has gained an alternative hypothesis that must be stated and, where possible, tested. Instrument drift, for example, predicts structure that follows the order or date of testing rather than the stimulus dimension, and a general factor rather than tuned ones, so it can often be checked in the data themselves. Where it cannot be checked, the caveat belongs in the report and the data belong in the analysis. The aim of every recommendation in this section is the same: not to rule data out, but to make clear what each dataset can and cannot show.

5. What the 2017 paper got wrong

Section 4 argued each correction at length. In brief, individual differences include all human variability, not only stable traits, and changes within a person contain interpretable factors (Section 4.1). Imperfect data are not suspect: they produced the paradigm's landmark results and should be analyzed, with limitations stated, before they are lost (Section 4.2).

Artifacts need not be removed by design alone, because global artifacts produce general factors and tuned structure is evidence against them (Section 4.3). Test-retest reliability across sessions is not the minimal requirement; replication of structure, ground truth, tuned structure, and withinsession reliability all count (Section 4.4). Rank statistics are not a default: well-behaved metric data keep their metric, and misbehaving data get diagnosis and a transformation chosen for the failure (Section 4.5). A fixed test order guarantees a confound; randomization or counterbalancing with order modeled is better, and blocking is a preference, not a condition for using the data (Section 4.6). A factor model is one model, defended against overfitting by replication, held-out data, and confirmatory tests, not by correcting every measure (Section 4.7).

Response bias has solutions: forced choice is not bias-free, but signal detection, bias-corrected scoring, and joint models of bias and sensitivity address it, and tuned structure argues against a global bias (Section 4.8). Large samples are not always necessary: the sample depends on reliability, effect size, the spread of observers, and the number of factors, and small samples of practiced observers can recover structure (Section 4.9). And individual differences are not a resource to be mined with caution, but a paradigm with its own objects, standards, and products (Sections 1-3).

My own earlier work needed correction too. For decades, I and others read a high-spatialfrequency factor in contrast sensitivity as a neural channel; adaptive optics showed that it was optical (Section 2.2). That correction came from the paradigm itself, which is how it should work.

The 2017 paper also got important things right. It assembled the special issue that brought much of this work together. It recognized that agreement among observers with very different eyes reveals normalization, a finding only individual differences can show. And several of its practical warnings remain sound (Section 4.11). The error was not in caring about artifacts.

It was in turning preferences into rules, and rules into reasons not to look.

6. Toward a computational atlas of human visual variation

The twelve pillars of Figure 2 hold up a roof, and the roof is the paradigm's long-term goal: a computational atlas of human visual variation. By analogy with the phenome of genetics, it could be called the visual phenome (Bilder et al., 2009; Peterzell, 2026f). An atlas is not a list of findings but a common map, built to shared standards, that anyone can consult and extend.

6.1 What the atlas would contain

The atlas has three layers, one for each route in Figure 1. The first is a map of mechanisms: for each visual function, how many factors underlie it, how each is tuned and in what coordinate frame, the stage at which it arises, and how strongly it is coupled to the others, together with the decision and confidence parameters observers bring to every task, which are idiosyncratic yet stable over weeks (Navajas et al., 2017) and only partly domain-general (Mazancieux, Fleming, Souchay, & Moulin, 2020). The correlations between factors belong in this layer, because they are the connections between mechanisms (Section 3.1.3). The second layer records where each factor's variation comes from: heritability and genes, development and aging, environment and culture, and the daily, monthly, and seasonal cycles along which it changes within a person. The third layer records how each factor is best measured and used: the optimal stimulus for it, normative distributions, its value as a marker for disease, and its role as a parameter in calibrating devices to individual users.

Every entry would carry the role it has earned in the sense of Figure 1, and the evidence for it. That grading is what keeps an atlas from becoming a catalog of overclaims.

6.2 How to build it

Much of the atlas can be built now, and some of it only now. Four steps would go most of the way.

First, report the covariance. Nearly every study in vision tests more than one observer.

Publishing individual data and the correlation matrix alongside the mean costs little beyond preparing the data, and depositing the data in public repositories turns every study into a contribution to the atlas (Section 3.1.5). Archival data should be recovered and analyzed while they still exist (Section 4.2).

Second, use common reference measures. A shared battery, designed by the onestimulus-per-factor principle and sampled densely enough during discovery to resolve every factor, would let results from different laboratories, populations, and decades be placed on the same map (Peterzell, in press-b). Broad batteries exist (Bosten et al., 2017; Cappe et al., 2014); what is needed is dense sampling within each function, linked across functions in the same observers.

Third, connect levels and domains. The atlas is most valuable where it links psychophysics to evoked potentials, imaging, genetics, and the clinic in the same people, and where it links one domain to another, color to luminance, stereopsis to contrast, perception to action and cognition (Section 3.1.3). These links are where individual differences give answers that no other method can.

Fourth, use artificial observers as a test bed. Populations of model observers that differ the way people do can test whether a proposed atlas entry reflects a mechanism or an artifact of analysis, and whether a model of vision reproduces the human covariance structure as well as the human mean (Peterzell, in press-a).

The atlas does not compete with the experimental study of vision; it completes it.

Experiments tell us what the average visual system does and why; the atlas tells us how that system is divided, how its parts vary together, where the variation comes from, and how to measure it in one person. A theory of spatial vision must explain both the mean contrast sensitivity function and the factors beneath it, and a theory of color both the average hue circle and the many narrowly tuned factors on which observers differ. Cronbach (1957) asked for "combined, not parallel, labors" between the experimental and correlational disciplines. In vision science, the correlational discipline has existed for more than a century, but mostly in the shadows, kept alive by a small group. Nearly a decade ago, Mollon et al. (2017) brought it into the light and then handed it a set of rules that would have kept many of its best results from being found. This paper has argued that those rules should be set aside and that the paradigm has earned a place beside the experimental mainstream. What it offers is broad. It identifies the mechanisms of vision, optical, retinal, neural, and psychophysical, counts them, and maps their tuning. It tests whether the field's constructs measure what they claim to. It traces variation to genes, development, aging, environment, culture, and the cycles of each person's life, and detects genetic variation and its consequences. It supplies markers that diagnose disease, track its progress, and aim and verify treatment at a specific mechanism. It holds models of vision, human and artificial, to the standard of reproducing human covariance as well as the human mean. And it lets displays, devices, and standards be designed for real observers rather than an average one. Its long-term goal is a shared atlas of human visual variation that brings these together.

6.3 Conclusion

To return to the informal title, the good is what the paradigm buys: mechanisms counted and tuned, phenotypes and markers, tests of construct validity, links across domains and levels, and designs that fit real people. The bad is a set of prescriptions that mistook preferences for requirements. The ugly is the data already lost because no one looked at the differences while they still could. The good is within reach, the bad can be corrected, and the ugly can be stopped.

Every experiment with more than one observer already contains a second experiment. The task now is to analyze it.
