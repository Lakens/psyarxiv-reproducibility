What we found

We found 17 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Rapid automatized naming (RAN) performance is highly predictive of reading outcomes when administered prior to the start of formal reading instruction. Children with dyslexia struggle with this task and RAN difficulties are described as a main deficit in this population. Perhaps unsurprisingly, some children with dyslexia also exhibit deficits in rapid auditory temporal processing of tones. Given that automaticity tasks rely on speed, the goal of the current study was to evaluate rapid processing tasks, their relationship to RAN, and associated genetic variants of interest. Children with or without dyslexia completed an assessment battery, provided a saliva sample for genotyping, and completed self-paced tasks of rapid auditory and visual perception online. Children with dyslexia were significantly slower to respond during tasks that included a linguistic element (speech sounds or letters). Performance on the visuospatial working memory task was correlated with rapid digit naming. Among children with dyslexia, there was a significant main effect of a KIAA0319 variant previously associated with neural inconsistency on reaction times in the rapid speech task and a nominally significant effect on RAN performance.

Overall, we provide evidence for general processing speed deficits in dyslexia, though more research is needed to determine the relationship to candidate-dyslexia genes.

Introduction

The ability to read is a fundamental skill that enables both academic success and daily functioning in modern society (Bretherton & Holmes, 2003; Boada & Pennington, 2006; Cassidy, Reggio, Shaywitz, Holahan, & Shaywitz, 2021; Raschle, Stering, Meissner, & Gaab, 2014). Although most children learn to read with sufficient instruction and practice, approximately 10% of the population fails to acquire fluent reading and are diagnosed with developmental dyslexia (Snowling & Melby-Lervåg, 2016), though it is more prevalent within lower socio-economic status communities (Friend, DeFries, & Olson, 2008). Individuals diagnosed with dyslexia demonstrate below-average reading ability despite normal non-verbal intelligence and adequate access to education (Peterson, Pennington, & Olson, 2014; Peterson & Pennington, 2012). The development of the reading network requires rapid and automatic communication between the visual cortex and the language network to facilitate fluent recognition of letter-sound relationships and decoding of words. Given the importance of fluency for reading, it is not surprising that assessments measuring automaticity of letter-sound relationships are highly predictive of future reading outcomes. Rapid automatized naming (RAN) measures the ability of an individual to identify a grid of stimuli quickly and accurately (e.g., digits, letters) and is considered a primary deficit in children with dyslexia (Wolf et al., 1999).

Thus, the goal of the current study was to evaluate whether the RAN deficit correlates with more general processing speed deficits and if dyslexia-candidate genes influence these skills.

There are likely several causal factors, both environmental and genetic, that each contribute differently to behavioral deficit profiles in dyslexia. For example, the variance in reading ability for those in high socioeconomic status households is primarily explained by genetic factors, whereas the same variance for those in low socioeconomic status households is equally accounted for by environmental and genetic factors (Friend, DeFries, & Olson, 2008).

Given the complexity of the gene-environment interactions contributing to dyslexia, understanding the specific behavioral outcomes and mechanisms of these genetic factors is an integral step to understanding the complex nature of this disorder. One approach to understanding genetic nature is genome wide association studies (GWAS), which have identified several candidate genes that are significantly associated with the disorder (Darki, Peyrard-Janvid, & Matsson, 2012; Galaburda, LoTurco, Ramus, Fitch, & Rosen, 2006 Mascheretti et al., 2017), the most studied among these being doublecortin domain-containing protein 2 (DCDC2; Chen, Zhao, Zhang, & Zuo, 2017; Marino et al., 2012), KIAA0319 (Mascheretti et al., 2014; Paracchini et al., 2008), roundabout guidance receptor 1 (ROBO1; Mascheretti et al., 2014), and dyslexia susceptibility 1 candidate 1 (DYX1C1; Rendall, Tarker, Contreras-Mora, LoTurco, & Fitch, 2017; Wang et al., 2006). Each of these genes is suspected to contribute to the disorder's heterogeneity by impacting distinct and separate pathways that can each create the reading impairment that defines the disorder (Centanni et al., 2018; Galaburda, LoTurco, Ramus, Fitch, & Rosen, 2006; Hancock, Pugh, & Hoeft, 2017; Liebig, Friederici, & Neef, 2020; Mascheretti et al., 2017; Peterson, Pennington, & Olson, 2013; Wolf et al., 1999). A more complete understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying reading disability would provide valuable insight into the development of the disorder and help those seeking to optimize interventions for those with the disorder.

One such candidate gene that is associated with developmental dyslexia is DCDC2, a member of the DYX2 gene family that is located at the 6p.22 site of the human genome (Massinen et al., 2011; Meng et al., 2005). The protein produced by DCDC2 stabilizes the microtubule structure of the primary cilia which allows for efficient and proper neuronal migration from the ventricular to pial surfaces (Galaburda, LoTurco, Ramus, Fitch, & Rosen, 2006; Wang et al., 2011). Variation at this gene modifies or prevents the formation of this protein, which in turn leads to errors in the migration of these neurons, particularly the neurons located in regions that are involved in fluent reading (Burbridge, Wang, & Volz, 2008; Che, Truong, Fitch, & Turco, 2016; Marino et al., 2014). Rodent models have been useful to understand some of the specific roles of this gene. Work using these models has shown that DCDC2 variation is associated with deficits in processing rapidly presented stimuli, suggesting a potential RAN deficit when this gene varies in humans (Centanni et al., 2016; Truong et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2011). Four single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of interest have been identified throughout the sequence of DCDC2, denoted as rs793842, rs1087266, rs807701, and rs807724 (Neef et al., 2017). Those who have been diagnosed with dyslexia have been shown to vary at these specific sites in their genome more than their typically developing peers (Meng et al., 2005; Schumacher et al., 2006; Wilcke et al., 2009). While the role of this gene is beginning to be understood at the cellular level, it is not known how variation in this gene, particularly at these specific SNPs, is related to behavioral outcomes. Thus, it could be hypothesized that variation at any one of these SNPs may lead to a deficit profile that would not be seen in someone with variation at one of the other SNPs.

Another commonly studied gene, KIAA0319, has been associated with deficits in spelling (Neef et al., 2017), possibly by way of decreasing neural consistency to auditory stimuli, including speech sounds (Centanni et al., 2018; Hancock et al., 2017; Hornickel & Kraus, 2013; Neef et al., 2017). Some evidence suggests that variants in this gene may also influence rapid naming of stimuli in a sample of Chinese children, such that RAN mediates the relationship between genetic risk and reading ability (Zhao et al., 2023). It is unknown whether this gene's influence on neural consistency leads to poor RAN performance or if this gene is associated with poor rapid stimulus processing more broadly.

The goal of the present study was to investigate the potential roles of several SNPs in DCDC2 and one SNP in KIAA0319 in a sample of children with and without dyslexia on rapid stimulus processing and RAN skills. We hypothesized that variation at the SNPs for DCDC2 would be associated with rapid processing skills as well as RDN and RLN behavioral outcomes such that those possessing a minor (risk) allele at these sites would perform poorer on these standardized measures compared to those with a major allele. We also hypothesized that the KIAA0319 SNP would be related to rapid processing of speech sounds and tones, but not rapid processing of other stimuli.

Methods

Participants and Neuropsychological Testing Procedure

Data were collected for this study during the COVID-19 pandemic, so all stages of this study were completed fully online. We recruited potential participants through online postings (e.g., Children Helping Science, Facebook, Twitter, etc.) and screened for eligibility. Screening for eligibility took place across two steps. The study was approved by the Texas Christian University Institutional Review Board. Parents provided informed consent by typing their full name into an online consent form prior to the collection of any data, and children offered verbal assent at the start of the Zoom session prior to completing any tasks.

First, parents completed a short background survey online on behalf of their child which asked questions regarding family and questions about language and reading delays, home reading environment, medications and diagnoses, and socioeconomic status. Participants that (a) reported no history of neurological disorders, diagnoses, or medications, (b) were within the age range of 7 to 12 years old, and (c) native English speakers were then invited to meet with a trained researcher for a 45-minute Zoom session (Zoom Video Communications, San Jose, CA) to complete standard English neuropsychological assessments.

To be fully eligible for the study as a typically developing child, participants also (d) achieved a KBIT-2 Matrices (Kaufman & Kaufman, 2004) standard score of 85 or higher and (e) achieved a standard score of 90 or higher on each of four measures on timed (TOWRE-2; Torgesen, Wagner, & Rashotte, 2012) and untimed (WRMT-3; Woodcock, 2011) single word and pseudoword reading. Children that achieved standard scores below 90 on two, three, or all four reading measures on the measures in (e) qualified as a child with dyslexia. Children also completed the Digits and Letters subtests from Rapid Automatized Naming (RAN; (Wolf & Denckla, 2005)).

Of the 103 children that were evaluated for eligibility, nine were excluded for having a low KBIT-2 Matrices (standard score below 85), 22 were excluded for not having reading scores that fit into a typical reader or child with dyslexia category, and six were excluded for scheduling issues or not responding to follow-up emails. Thus, the final sample included 33 typically developing children and 33 children with dyslexia. All scoring was verified independently by a second trained researcher, and discrepancies were resolved by arriving at a consensus.

Participant characteristics are provided in Table 1. After the Zoom session, children then completed four internet-based tasks at their own pace. All online tasks were administered through PsyToolKit (Stoet, 2010 (Stoet, , 2017)). Children received a $20 electronic Amazon gift card as compensation.

Rapid Processing Tasks

The tone task was designed to exactly mimic prior work on tone discrimination as described in (Tallal, 1980). In brief, children were told that two robots escaped the lab, and they were instructed to use the robots' beeps to identify them. A 305 Hz tone was used to indicate a female robot and a 100 Hz tone was used to indicate a male robot. Tones were presented in pairs (high-high, high-low, low-high, or low-low) and children reported the order of the robots' beeps (i.e., female-female, female-male, male-female, or male-male) using buttons on the keyboard.

Children completed eight practice trials with feedback and then practiced identifying the tones with feedback. Children then completed 8 training trials on the sequencing task and 24 test trials without feedback. Both accuracy and reaction time data were collected.

The speech task contained two stimulus conditions (consonant-vowel-consonant (CVC) and speech), each presented at four compression levels (full length, 50% compressed, 60% compressed, and 80% compressed). The child was instructed to listen to an alien either practicing her speech sounds (CVC condition) or practicing sentences (speech condition). During the CVC condition, children heard CVC syllables presented in a stream by a female, native-English speaker. They were instructed to indicate whether the target sound 'dad' was part of the sequence. During the speech condition, children listened to short sentences (e.g., 'Lemons are yellow', 'Dishes can whistle') spoken by the same female, native-English speaker, and were instructed to indicate whether the sentence made sense or did not make sense.

In each stimulus condition, presentation rate was blocked in sets of five stimuli each. CVC stimuli were yoked to the speech stimuli by number of syllables and by correct response ('yes' or 'no'). Children completed 8 practice trials with feedback and 20 test trials without feedback in each condition.

Both accuracy and reaction time data were collected.

The rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) task included two levels of difficulty (one or two targets) and two types of stimuli (letters or shapes). Conditions were blocked so that the easier level of difficulty (one target) immediately preceded the more difficult trials (two targets).

First, children completed five practice trials. During single letter test block, children viewed 20 trials of 18 letters each at an inter-stimulus interval of 100 ms. Half of the trials contained the target letter 'X' and half did not. Trials containing the target were randomly interleaved with trials not containing the target. During the two-target letter block, children viewed 40 trials of 18 letters that either contained no targets, the first target only ('X'), or both targets ('X' followed by 'O'). The shapes condition followed the same pattern as the letters condition, with the targets being a cross (target one) and a circle (target two) to mimic the visual characteristics of the target letters without the linguistic meaning. Both accuracy and reaction time data were collected.

The visuospatial working memory (VSWM) task was designed to mimic an existing validated VSWM task used across the lifespan (Sander et al., 2011). In brief, children completed a color change detection task in which they saw two sets of ladybugs and were asked to indicate whether the two sets were the same or different. Difficulty varied across two dimensions: speed and working memory load. For speed, the inter-stimulus interval between the two sets was either slow (1000 ms), medium (500 ms), or fast (100 ms). For load, the number of bugs per set was either two, four, or six. Stimuli were blocked by speed and load. Both accuracy and reaction time data were collected.

Gene Collection and Processing

Since this study was conducted remotely, eligible participants were mailed pre-labeled saliva collection kits. The kits included instructions to not eat or drink anything for 30 minutes prior to completing the saliva collection portion of the study. Of the 66 children that participated in the study, we received samples from 29 typically developing children and 29 children with dyslexia. At the conclusion of the study, all samples were shipped to and processed by LGC (BioSearch Technologies, 2023) using allele-specific PCR-based KASP assay at LGC (Beverly, MA), with initial genotyping quality control and SNP genotype calls conducted using LGC's inhouse Kraken software and standardized protocols. Genotyping was performed at four a priori SNPs identified in the literature as SNPs of interest for either phonological awareness (rs6935076 on KIAA0319; (Centanni et al., 2018; Neef et al., 2017)) or reading speed (rs807724, rs807701, rs1087266, and rs793842 on DCDC2, (Neef et al., 2017)). Minor allele frequencies and Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) values were 29.88% (HWE = .029) for rs807701, 15.24% (HWE = 2.65) for rs807724, 45.12% (HWE = .019) for rs1087266, 36.59% (HWE = .238) for rs793842, and 28.66% (HWE = .158) for rs6935076.

Data Analysis Plan

Data were pre-processed and analyzed using custom scripts in MATLAB (MathWorks, Portola Valley, CA). First, two-tailed, independent-samples t-tests were conducted to compare children with and without dyslexia on the various standardized assessments. Descriptives statistics are provided in Table 1 as M ± SD. Next, mixed-design ANOVAs and post-hoc t-tests were used to evaluate the effect of reading group and task condition on task performance and reaction time. All post-hoc tests underwent Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons as appropriate. Separate one-way between-groups ANOVAs of genotype on RAN and task performance were also conducted to probe the effect of each gene variant. Descriptive statistics are presented as M ± SEM. It is important to note that not all children in the sample completed all the tasks. Thus, we report sample sizes in Tables 2-3 and in the text below as we present results for each task.

Results

Standardized assessments

Our sample was composed of two groups of individuals: those with dyslexia and those that were typically developing readers. There was no difference between the proportion of males to females in each group. Although all participants scored in the average to above average range on the non-verbal IQ measure, there was a significant difference in non-verbal IQ between the two groups (p = 0.046). As expected, the typically developing children performed significantly better than the children with dyslexia on all standardized assessments, including the WRMT-3

Word Identification and Word Attack, the TOWRE-2 Sight Word Reading and Phonemic Decoding Efficiency, and the Rapid Digit/Letter Naming measures (ps < 0.001). See Table 1 for mean performance on each of these tasks and associated descriptive statistics.

Slower reaction times in dyslexia despite typical accuracy

Across all tasks and conditions, there was only one nominally significant group difference in accuracy and two trends (Table 2). During the tone task, children with dyslexia (N = 29) were marginally worse than typical readers (N = 32; one-tailed t-test, t (57) = 1.62, p = 0.056). During the speech task, there were no differences between children with dyslexia (N = 30) and typically reading (N = 32) controls during the speech condition (one-tailed t-tests; ps > 0.24). In the CVC condition, there was a nominal difference at the slowest speed (0% compression) such that children with dyslexia were more accurate than typical readers (onetailed t-test, t (60) = 1.67, p = 0.049), but this comparison did not survive correction (all other ps > 0.32). There were no differences in accuracy on the RSVP task (ps > 0.12). In the VSWM task, children with dyslexia (N = 33) were nominally worse compared to typical readers (N = 33) at the slowest speed at the high WM load (one-tailed t-test, t (61) = 1.75, p = 0.043), but this difference did not survive correction. Despite the marginal effect of group on accuracy, children with dyslexia exhibited slower reaction times in several of the tested tasks (Table 3). There was a trend in this difference in the tone task (t (59) = 1.43, p = 0.079) and significant differences in the rapid speech task (ps < 0.01) and RSVP task (1 letter target; t (60) = 2.14, p = 0.018). However, there were no differences in reaction time during the VSWM task (ps > 0.25).

RAN performance relates to processing speed

We next evaluated whether processing speed, measured by task reaction times, was related to deficits on rapid automatized naming performance. Performance on Rapid Letter Naming (RLN) was not correlated with reaction times during the VSWM task (ps > 0.17). Only one correlation between VSWM and Rapid Digit Naming (RDN) survived correction (fastest speed, lowest WM load; r = -0.35, p = 0.005, R² = .12). There was a nominally significant relationship between RDN and RSVP performance at the one letter target condition (r = -0.25, p = 0.047) and a trend with RLN (r = -0.23, p = 0.07). There were trends in the relationship between tone task reaction times and RDN (r = -0.23, p = 0.072) and RLN (r = -0.23, p = 0.076).

There were no correlations between RDN and any of the speech task conditions or speeds (ps > 0.16). There was a trend in the relationship between the 80% compressed speech and RLN (r = -0.25, p = 0.055) and a nominal relationship between 60% compressed CVC stimuli and RLN (r = -0.27, p = 0.038), but this did not survive correction.

Effect of speech specific stimuli in dyslexia

We next utilized one-tailed paired t-tests to determine whether the processing speed deficits in dyslexia were specific to linguistic stimuli or represented a general processing speed deficit. We focused on the two tasks that included variation along this parameter. In the RSVP task, there was a trend such that children with dyslexia were slower to respond to letters than to shapes (t (29) = 1.48, p = 0.076). The speech task evaluated whether semantic content improved performance by comparing full sentences with CVC strings. There was no effect of condition on reaction times in this task among children with dyslexia (ps > 0.16), though it is important to note that both sets of stimuli did include human speech sounds and so only evaluated the effect of semantics on performance and cannot account for lower-level speech sound perception deficits.

Genetic links to performance in dyslexia

We utilized ANOVAs for each SNP to evaluate the main effect of the minor allele on performance in each task within the dyslexia group. To improve statistical power, scores and reaction times were averaged across conditions for the computer tasks. There was a significant main effect of the minor allele at rs6935076 (KIAA3019) on both RDN (F (2, 57) = 5.31, p = 0.008) and RLN (F (2, 57) = 5.25, p = 0.008), though neither effect survived correction. There were also significant main effects of the minor allele at this SNP on reaction times during the speech task for speech trials (F (2, 54) = 10.85, p = 0.0001) and CVC trials (F (2, 54) = 9.64, p = 0.0003), with both effects surviving correction. There were no other significant main effects on the other tasks (ps > 0.14).

There were a few nominally significant main effects for SNPs on dyslexia-candidate gene DCDC2, though none survived correction. At rs807724, there were significant main effects of minor allele on reaction time during RSVP (F (2, 48) = 5.22, p = 0.0267) and a marginal effect on the tone task (F (2, 46) = 4.01, p = 0.051). At rs1087266, there was a nominally significant main effect of minor allele on the tone task (F (2, 46) = 3.5, p = 0.038). There were no other main effects at any SNP.

Discussion

In the current study, we report significantly slower reaction times during a series of rapid stimulus processing tasks in children with dyslexia compared to their typically reading peers and this effect was present regardless of whether the stimuli were linguistic (speech sounds or letters). Interestingly, the reaction time differences were concentrated on the tasks that did include a linguistic component (speech sounds or letters). Within the group of children with dyslexia, we observed significant effects of a minor allele in KIAA0319 (rs6935076) on RAN and on the rapid speech task reaction times, and only nominal relationships between rapid processing performance and variants on DCDC2. These findings support the presence of general perceptual processing time deficits in dyslexia but will require further research to probe the genetic correlates of such deficits.

Rapid stimulus processing in dyslexia and effects on reading

Rapid automatized naming is a speed measure, where performance is largely measured by the number of seconds required to finish the grid. Although RAN measures are scored with respect to speed, performance on rapid naming may rely on cognitive factors other than perceptual processing speed. A meta-analysis of 137 studies reported that RAN scores were most strongly associated with single word reading and text reading abilities (Araújo et al., 2015). One version of the RAN is an abbreviated form included in the CTOPP (Wagner et al., 1999). The other commonly used form, and the one used in the current study, is the RAN/RAS measure (Wolf & Denckla, 2005). The shorter CTOPP version is thought to rely more on phonological processing and phonological memory compared to the more cognitive effort required for switching between stimulus conditions in the RAN/RAS version (Norton & Wolf, 2012). Given that reading is a complex skill requiring many distinct but related subskills, including phonological awareness, rapid automatized naming, working memory, and language (among others), it is not surprising that seemingly similar measures may yield different findings given differences in task design, and both rapid naming tests are used in clinical and research settings to study the same population. Given these differences, it could be that poor RAN performance is due to a deficit in the rapid processing of perceptual information itself (Mascheretti et al., 2018; Visser et al., 2004) or due to other deficits such as poor phonological representations interfering with naming (Centanni et al., 2018; Hancock et al., 2017; Hornickel & Kraus, 2013) or an information flow problem between brain regions responsible for encoding the letter vs those responsible for linking the letter to its speech sound and name (Boets et al., 2013). The choice of task, as well as the known heterogeneity in dyslexia, both behaviorally and biologically, likely impacts the nature and significance of the predictive relationship of RAN for reading outcomes.

First, deficits in visual or auditory processing have been inconsistently reported. Prior studies reporting a broad rapid visual processing deficit have utilized visual motion tasks as well as attentional blink tasks (including those similar to our RSVP task) and prior studies reporting rapid auditory deficits typically utilize tone tasks. Children with dyslexia exhibit deficits in rapid perception tasks, including those measuring tone order processing (Mascheretti et al., 2018; Tallal, 1980) and attentional blink (Mascheretti et al., 2018; McLean et al., 2011; Visser et al., 2004). Our tone and RSVP tasks were designed to mimic these prior studies. In prior studies, when CTOPP RAN subtests were used, rapid color and object naming were significantly related to single word reading and fluency. However, lower-level perceptual speed, defined as performance on tasks such as attentional blink (via an RSVP task) and go-no go reaction time, was associated with single word reading only (McLean et al., 2011). Thus, the authors argued that low-level visual perceptual speed may contribute to reading independently of rapid naming ability. Similarly, one study using multiple stimulus types reported specific deficits for stimuli that require accessing phonological codes (e.g., letters and digits) which contradicts the idea of a general rapid visual processing deficit (Ziegler et al., 2010). Our use of the longer RAN/RAS form makes direct comparisons with these prior studies difficult and future work is needed to probe the subskills supporting each version of RAN.

A second possible brain basis for poor RAN performance could be increased neural inconsistency (i.e. neural noise; (Hancock, Pugh, & Hoeft, 2017)). Neural inconsistency is defined as decreased correlation between the neural responses across multiple presentations of the same stimulus (i.e., increased variance). In the typically developing brain, it is important that the encoding of a stimulus occurs consistently over time so that the stimulus can be reliably identified in a range of conditions (e.g., high background noise, varying speaker accent, etc.).

Inconsistent neural responses to speech sounds make it difficult for the brain to associate an auditory speech sound representation with a visual letter (grapheme) and may therefore also interfere with RAN tasks that rely on knowledge of these relationships and automatic recall. Up to 50% of children with dyslexia exhibit poor neural consistency and this neural feature is linked with variants on KIAA0319 (Centanni et al., 2018; Neef et al., 2017), which supports the current finding of a significant relationship between RAN and the same SNP on this gene.

Finally, poor RAN performance could be explained in a subset of the population by intact phonological representations but poor white matter integrity connecting visual and auditory regions. White matter tract volume is reduced in children (Christodoulou et al., 2017) and adults (Boets et al., 2013) with dyslexia. This anatomical deficit is associated with variants on DCDC2 (Darki, Peyrard-Janvid, Matsson, Kere, & Klingberg, 2014), and reduced white matter volume could restrict the speed of information in different brain regions to link the visual letter symbol with its associated speech sound. In the current study, we did not collect neural imaging data, so we cannot speak to this possibility. However, the lack of a relationship between DCDC2 variants and RAN indicate that more research is needed to investigate this possibility.

Genetic markers of reading, RAN, and poor processing speed

The significant nominal relationships reported here between both RAN subtests and a KIAA0319 SNP associated with neural inconsistency (Centanni et al., 2018; Neef et al., 2017) are an intriguing and unexpected finding. One potential explanation for this finding is the importance of phonological representations in mapping letter symbols to speed sounds. As noted above, the RDN and RLN subtests used in the current study were shorter versions that may load more on phonological awareness than higher cognitive ability (Norton & Wolf, 2012). Thus, these measures may be more sensitive to picking up individuals with poorer phonological awareness, including those caused by increased neural inconsistency. We observed significant associations between the KIAA0319 SNP and reaction times during the rapid speech/CVC task in the current study, which also supports the idea that some children in our sample may have had poor speech sound encoding, leading to an inability to process these stimuli quickly. Future research should consider neural inconsistency and the role of KIAA0319 in tasks that combine phonological awareness with speed pressure, given this finding. There is also evidence that this gene is related to RAN in other orthographies. Interestingly, a large study in children learning to read in Chinese reported no relationship between genetic risk scores (13 SNPs in KIAA0319) and either phonological awareness or RAN independently, despite a significant relationship with reading fluency (Zhao et al., 2023) that was mediated by the child's RAN skills.

Prior work investigating the role of DCDC2 variants on reading subskills revealed a significant association between a principal component made up of the four SNPs genotyped here and reading speed (Neef et al., 2017). Our own previous research demonstrated that suppression of the rodent homolog of this gene (Dcdc2) led to significant deficits on rapid auditory processing tasks (Centanni et al., 2016). It was therefore surprising that none of the DCDC2 SNPs were significantly related to our current tasks that relied on speed, including a modified version of the task used in the rodent model study as well as standardized rapid naming. There are a few reasons why these results appear contradictory. The first possibility is that the tasks we chose to use in this study rely on separate, though perhaps overlapping, subskills that support passage reading. Given that RAN scores are highly predictive of reading ability, we designed our tasks to probe whether RAN skills were influenced by more general rapid perceptual processing and unfortunately did not include a passage reading comprehension measure for replication.

Thus, the prior association between these DCDC2 SNPs and reading speed could be driven by other cognitive abilities not measured here and those that may not even load onto RAN, such as working memory.

Preclinical work investigating the impact of the rodent homolog of DCDC2 (Dcdc2) has utilized auditory processing tasks as well as learning and memory tasks. Dcdc2 knockout mice exhibited not only significant impairments on rapid auditory processing but also deficits on working and reference memory during a radial arm maze task (Truong et al., 2014). Our prior rapid speech sound discrimination task revealed significant impairments in Dcdc2 knockdown rats at rapid speeds (anonymized), which could be explained either by poor rapid sensory processing or by the working memory load added by processing multiple stimuli in rapid succession. If DCDC2 interferes with working memory rather than lower-level rapid stimulus processing, it could explain why prior findings linked this gene with reading speed and why we did not see a relationship with lower-level perceptual tasks. However, if DCDC2 is impacting working memory broadly, we would have expected to see a link between the VSWM task and SNPs on this gene in the current study. Further, prior work in a transparent orthography did not find any association between two DCDC2 SNPs (including rs793842) and working memory (Riva et al., 2019). It is therefore possible that the relationship between DCDC2 and working memory and/or reading speed is mediated by the interaction between this gene and others rather than variants on a single gene alone. One strong possibility is the READ1 element (Powers et al., 2016). Indeed, in the Riva et al. study (2019), which failed to link DCDC2 SNPs to working memory or reading skills, the READ1 element was significantly related to these scores (Riva et al., 2019). Thus, future work on the genetic basis of dyslexia should prioritize the inclusion of mediating factors within and between genes of interest.

It is also important to note that prior work linking DCDC2 and reading speed included a large sample of children with diverse reading profiles, without specifically accounting for dyslexia in the population (Neef et al., 2017). In our genetic analysis, we focused on the group of children with dyslexia, given this gene's association with the disorder itself. To verify that results were not affected by excluding the typical readers, we also reran the analysis with the entire sample.

No SNPs were significantly related to our measures when the entire sample was included, the closest relationship being between rs793842 and RLN (F (2, 57) = 2.4, p = 0.10).

Limitations

This study had four main limitations to address. First, our sample was quite small in comparison to studies linking specific genes of interest to behavioral traits. Although targeted studies in small samples can be beneficial for narrowing down genes of interest, results yielded from such small samples should be interpreted with caution. Second, all computer tasks reported here were completed in the participant's home without a researcher present in any capacity. This was done to decrease attrition during the already stressful COVID-19 pandemic, allowing children to take breaks as needed and hopefully maintain attention during the tasks themselves.

However, it is entirely possible that there were distractions in the home interfering with performance and/or other individuals in the home helping or otherwise participating in the tasks.

While tasks conducted outside the lab may be more indicative of real-world performance, they may be less comparable with prior in-lab studies. Third, we did not include measures of passage reading in the current study, so although we did include timed single word reading, we were unable to assess how performance on our tasks load on to full passage level reading ability.
