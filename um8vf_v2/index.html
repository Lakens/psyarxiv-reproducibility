How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Recent theoretical models such as the generative adversarial framework propose that the ability to generate high-fidelity visual images may intensify the severity of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), particularly the intrusive re-experiencing of traumatic events. Meanwhile, individuals with minimal imagery (aphantasia) may be protected. To evaluate this relationship, 312 Veterans completed the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ) and the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5). Results indicated that lower VVIQ scores (less vivid imagery) were associated with greater PTSD severity. This inverse relationship was observed across all PTSD symptom clusters, with the strongest association for the negative cognition/mood cluster.

Five of the eight Veterans who reported very low VVIQ scores (consistent with aphantasia) had clinically significant PTSD and high levels of intrusive symptoms, further indicating that aphantasia was not protective. These findings have implications for imagery-based PTSD treatments and suggest that reduced visual imagery is associated with impaired processing of traumatic experiences.

1. Introduction

A major goal in neuropsychiatry research over the last 20 years has been the development of objective biomarkers for identifying the neural systems that underlie mental distress (Phillips & Vieta, 2007; Tost & Meyer-Lindenberg, 2008). This research aims to bypass rigid and subjective diagnostic categories based on self-report (Insel et al., 2010). At the same time, rigorous studies on the subjective experiences of delusions (Bell et al., 2008), auditory verbal hallucinations (Langdon et al., 2009; Powers et al., 2017), dissociation (Černis et al., 2020), and alterations in time perception (Vogeley & Kupke, 2007) have increased. Mental imagery is another internally generated phenomenon related to psychological well-being (Ji et al., 2019). It has been described as both an emotional amplifier (Holmes et al., 2008) and as an emotional "attenuator," i.e., a means of emotion regulation (Maxwell et al., 2017; Andries et al., 2024).

As in perception, visual imagery is the most widely investigated modality (reviewed in Ganis & Schenden, 2011; Pearson, 2019). The externally "unseen" end product of imagery involves the capacity to generate internal percepts from visual memory stores. The predominant theory proposes that visual imagery relies on some of the same mental representations and brain areas used for visual perception (Farah, 1988; Ishai & Sagi, 1995; Dijkstra et al., 2019).

Studies have suggested that visual imagery can elicit negative emotional states, which may exacerbate the symptoms of anxiety disorders, major depression, and eating disorders in susceptible individuals (reviewed in Ji et al., 2019). Similarly, intrusive images and memories are a key symptom of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), a serious condition associated with exposure to traumatic experiences (DSM-5).

Classic cognitive models of PTSD, such as the dual representation theory (Brewin et al., 1996), emphasize that traumatic events lead to two different types of memories. One is verbally accessible and intentionally retrieved, while the other is involuntary, intrusive, largely sensory in nature, and prone to trigger physiological and emotional responses (Brewin et al., 1996). A revised version of the theory incorporated the dysfunctional processes that occur while encoding an extremely stressful event (Brewin et al., 2010). Under more typical conditions, episodic encoding of an event includes contextual details that can be retrieved voluntarily at a later date. However, under extreme stress, the encoding of sensory details can overwhelm this voluntary system, resulting in memories that are inflexible and involuntary. This leads to intrusive images (flashbacks) that are untethered from more coherent contextual memories.

1.1 The Generative Adversarial Model

A novel theory of intrusive imagery suggests that a generative adversarial network (GAN) could "fool" a discriminator into thinking that an internal image is a real percept (Cushing et al., 2023). This model considers the two visual processing streams -the fast magnocellular M pathway (dorsal stream) and the slower parvocellular P (ventral stream) pathway -as adversarial. The M pathway sends a prediction signal to the discriminator, which returns topdown feedback to the P pathway. In PTSD, flashbacks could be perceived as real because of M/P processing biases or compromised reality monitoring in the discriminator. The model predicts that people lacking the ability to produce visual imagery should be protected from intrusions (i.e., unwanted or distressing memories of the traumatic event) and PTSD (Cushing et al., 2023).

More generally, a stronger imagery generator should lead to more vivid intrusions. What is the evidence for these predictions?

1.2 Trauma and Imagery

Several studies in traumatized civilian populations did not find evidence for a direct, positive correlation between vivid imagery and worse PTSD symptoms. In one study, survivors of interpersonal violence with current, past, and no PTSD were compared to non-trauma controls (Jelinek et al., 2010). Scores on the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ-2, Marks, 1989) did not differ between the current-PTSD group and the no-PTSD or non-trauma controls (Jelinek et al., 2010). However, the past-PTSD group had lower imagery than the other three groups. Although lower imagery vividness was associated with recovery, the maintenance of current PTSD symptoms was not related to an increase (or decrease) in VVIQ-2 scores. Laor et al. (1999) tested war-exposed civilians with PTSD (n=22) and without PTSD (n=23) in a psychophysiological experiment that recorded heart rate and blood pressure. Participants listened to audio scripts while they closed their eyes and imagined the images evoked by the scripts (e.g., positive -ocean waves; neutral -party chatter; traumatic event -public radio alert, missile siren, explosion). The PTSD group showed greater physiological responses to the trauma script than controls only when their imagery vividness was low (rated on Bett's visual subscale of the Questionnaire Upon Mental Imagery (QMI); Sheehan et al. 1967), suggesting those with vivid imagery abilities were more able to regulate their physiological responses.

Finally, multi-modal imagery abilities were assessed in 98 participants with PTSD using the QMI. The only significant predictor of vivid imagery was depression, not the severity of PTSD intrusion symptoms or demographic factors (Karatzias et al., 2009).

A recent study reported more promising results. Yeung and colleagues (2025, Study 1) identified 806 trauma-exposed adults out of a larger online population using the Brief Trauma Questionnaire (Schnurr et al., 1999), and then administered the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R; Weiss & Marmar, 1996) and the Object and Spatial Imagery Questionnaire (OSIQ; Blajenkova et al., 2006). Interestingly, object and spatial imagery abilities had opposing relationships with PTSD symptoms. Higher OSIQ-Object scores were associated with more severe PTSD symptoms (in support of the general adversarial model), while higher OSIQ-Spatial scores were associated with less severe PTSD symptoms. A strong dissociation between object and spatial imagery has been reported in neuropsychological case studies, which demonstrate that acquired brain injuries can impair one type of imagery but not the other, and vice versa (Levine et al., 1985; Farah et al., 1988). Although the results of Yeung et al. (2025) are informative, the mean IES-R (M=21.23) was under 33, the cutoff for provisional PTSD (Creamer et al., 2003), making it difficult to ascertain the link between visual imagery and PTSD. Additional studies on visual imagery in participants with a PTSD diagnosis are needed.

1.3 Visual Imagery in Military Populations

Russell & Mussap (2024, Study 2) examined the relationship between imagery and PTSD in 211 Australian military personnel, who completed the PTSD Checklist (PCL-M), the VVIQ, and the Spontaneous Use of Imagery Questionnaire (SUIS). They used a four-factor structure for the PCL-M to examine correlations with the VVIQ and the SUIS. There was no relationship between imagery vividness and any of the PCL subscales, including intrusions (r = 0.01). Further, VVIQ scores did not differ between military members with PTSD (M=60.89) and those without PTSD (M=60.59), suggesting that vivid visual imagery was not associated with more severe PTSD symptoms. In contrast, there was a small but significant increase in SUIS scores in the PTSD group. However, the specific hypothesis that spontaneous use of imagery would be related to intrusions was not supported.

1.4 Aphantasia

Another approach to examine the relationship between imagery vividness and PTSD symptoms is to test a unique population without imagery. Congenital aphantasia was defined as the lifelong absence of (or reduction in) voluntary visual imagery by Zeman and colleagues (2015). It occurs in approximately 1-4% of the general population, depending on the VVIQ cutoff (Zeman, et al., 2024; Wright et al., 2024). The near-absence of conscious imagery can also occur in other sensory modalities (Dawes et al., 2024). Popular science articles (Zimmer, 2010 (Zimmer, , 2015;; Friedersdorf, 2014) and autobiographical accounts (Ross, 2016) sparked a great deal of interest in this component of human imagination. A common theme from individual testimonies is the shock upon learning that other people have the ability to form images in their mind's eye, when they themselves cannot. Active online communities have formed to focus discussion among peers and to educate others (Aphantasia Network; r/Aphantasia subreddit).

The mental health implications of having aphantasia is a growing concern in the community (Reeder & Mawtus, 2025), given the assumption that aphantasics may not experience PTSD fully (e.g., Keogh et al., preprint). Thus, the need for empirical data is of direct import for these individuals.

1.5 Aphantasia and PTSD Symptoms

Two papers have examined whether participants with aphantasia are at lower risk for intrusion symptoms (Dawes et al., 2020; Ale & Burns, preprint). A sample of 267 individuals with aphantasia (mean VVIQ = 17.94) was recruited from online communities and compared to 203 age-matched controls (mean VVIQ = 58.12; Dawes et al., 2020). Participants also completed the PCL-5, QMI, and OSIQ. Total PCL-5 scores did not differ between groups, although Bayesian evidence supported a decrease in intrusions relative to controls and an increase in negative cognitions (i.e., a subscale describing negative or biased beliefs and emotions about one's self or the world). Importantly, aphantasia was not protective against PTSD (Table S8, Dawes et al., 2020), since the group of aphantasics with mean PCL-5 scores at the probable clinical cutoff (mean = 33.97) reported higher intrusion symptoms than aphantasics with PCL-5 scores below the cutoff (mean = 20.20). Another study included questionnaires to assess trauma exposure, alexithymia (difficulties identifying and describing one's emotions), and complex PTSD symptoms in addition to the PCL-5 (Ale & Burns, preprint). Replicating the null effect of Dawes et al. (2020), total PCL-5 scores did not differ between aphantasics and controls, nor did intrusions. Thus, the relationship between poor visual imagery and a lower risk of intrusive experiences is inconsistent.

An alternate possibility is that poor visual imagery could be associated with more severe PTSD symptoms. A prospective study of over 1,000 US Army soldiers who deployed to Iraq identified several risk factors for PTSD at a five-year follow-up assessment (Vasterling et al., 2023). These included higher stress exposure, less social support before deployment, and poorer visual memory before deployment. The authors suggested that, in addition to the beneficial role of verbal memory in reconstructing trauma memories (e.g., Brewin et al., 1996), "the ability to form a visual image may likewise facilitate productive habituation to the perceptual aspects of trauma memory" (Vasterling et al., 2023, p. 964). This study replicated their earlier findings with smaller sample sizes, where low scores on immediate visual reproduction of abstract geometric designs were associated with higher odds ratio for a PTSD diagnosis (Marx et al., 2009; Vasterling et al., 2018). This finding is intriguing, and predicts the opposite relationship between visual imagery and PTSD symptom severity compared to the generative adversarial model (Cushing et al., 2023).

1.6 Present Study

The current study examined the VVIQ-PTSD relationship in a population of 312 U.S. military Veterans who reported the full range of scores on both questionnaires, including 35% above the clinical cutoff for probable PTSD and eight participants who met criteria for aphantasia. We predicted that Veterans with vivid visual imagery would report more severe intrusion symptoms, in support of the GAN model (Cushing et al., 2023). Further, we predicted that aphantasia would be "protective" from experiencing severe PTSD symptoms, especially intrusions. The alternative finding, that Veterans with vivid visual imagery would report lower levels of intrusions, would support a role for imagery in processing the consequences of trauma, in line with Vasterling et al. (2023). Likewise, individuals with emotional regulation difficulties have reported lower abilities and usage of visual imagery (Maxwell et al., 2017), suggesting that imagery may promote productive coping strategies. We were also motivated by the fact that U.S. military Veterans are exposed to trauma events that differ from the civilian population (i.e., combat). Post-9/11 combat Veterans have 4.87 times the probability of reporting a PTSD diagnosis compared to civilians (Ortiz Diaz et al., 2026). Given that the "gold standard" trauma-based treatments employ visual imagery (e.g., prolonged exposure), we thought it was important to assess imagery in this population. Finally, prior studies on gender differences in imagery and PTSD have yielded inconsistent results. Since the prevalence of PTSD in female Veterans is twice as high as in male Veterans (Schein et al., 2021; National Center for PTSD, 2025), we examined whether gender differences in VVIQ scores contributed to this discrepancy. Women are more likely than men to report exceptionally vivid imagery (hyperphantasia; Zeman et al., 2020), which might impact their greater risk for PTSD.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Participants

The current study was part of a larger project on Long COVID in a nationally representative sample of U.S. military Veterans. Participants were identified via searches of the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) electronic health records, which were stratified into nine regions (i.e., Northeast, Mid-Atlantic, East North Central, West North Central, South Atlantic, East South Central, West South Central, Mountain, and Pacific according to U.S. Census Divisions). Within each region, a computer-generated random sample of Veterans was selected and then emailed a flyer about the study. Veterans were not excluded based on sex, racial-ethnic identity, pre-existing mental health conditions (e.g., PTSD, major depression, anxiety), or major medical comorbidities (e.g., mild traumatic brain injury, cardiovascular disease) unless their records indicated significant neurological or psychiatric history (e.g., epilepsy, dementia, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or schizoaffective disorder) or long-term chronic and/or severe acute substance abuse issues.

The study's sample size was initially calculated according to the project's primary aims (to examine COVID-19 symptoms and cognition). Because the current manuscript pursues secondary aims, a posthoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power. For a fixed hierarchical linear regression model with 1 main predictor and a total of 5 predictors, a=.05, power=.80, our sample size of 312 was sufficient for detecting small effect sizes (f²=.025). For the study, 20,519 Veterans were emailed between May 2024 through December 2025, wherein 530 (3%) Veterans expressed interest, 415 Veterans signed consents, and 312 Veterans completed baseline measures. Mean age (n=312) was 43.3 years old (SD = 14.3, Range: 18-70) and mean education reflected Associate's degrees or some college education. The sample consisted of 65.7% male and 34.3% female Veterans. The majority of Veterans were White (62.82%), followed by Black or African American (13.78%), Hispanic or Latino (11.22%), Asian (6.09%), Multiracial (4.81%), American Indian/Alaskan Native (0.96%), and Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander (0.32%). For more demographic information, see Table 1.

2.2 Procedures

The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Veterans Affairs (VA) Northern California Health Care System. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Veterans who received an email flyer and expressed interest in the study were then sent an email letting them know an informed consent form would be sent through VA DocuSign, and that questions about the study would be welcome over email or phone. Once signed informed consent was received, Veterans then received an email with a link to the questionnaires. When results were received, Veterans were paid $40 for each assessment.

2.3 Measures

The present study focuses on baseline measures characterizing demographics, visual imagery, and symptoms of PTSD.

2.3.1. Demographics

Veterans' age, email address, and state of residence were identified through VA electronic health records. Veterans further self-reported demographic data (e.g., race, sex, education) and military background (e.g., military branch, time of military service).

2.3.2. PTSD

Veterans also completed the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), a 20-item self-report measure that assesses the DSM-5 symptoms of PTSD. It may be used for screening, provisional diagnoses, or monitoring symptom change of PTSD (Weathers et al., 2013). It has four symptom clusters: Intrusion (items 1-5), Avoidance (items 6 and 7), Negative Alterations in Cognition and Mood (items 8-14), and Alterations in Arousal (items 15-20). Symptoms are rated on a 0 to 4 scale, with higher sum scores indicating greater severity. The total score range is 0 to 80.

Validation studies suggest that PTSD is provisionally present at a cut-off of 33 or greater on the PCL-5 (Weathers et al., 2013).

Questions on the PCL-5 also assess time since traumatic event, event severity (rated1 = "Yes, involved actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence"; and2 = "No, did not"), and how the event was experienced (1 = "happened to me directly";2 = "witnessed it";3 = "learned about it happening to significant person(s)";4 = "repeatedly exposed to details as part of job";5 = "Other"). We recoded event severity 2 as event severity 0 for the hierarchical regression.

2.3.3. VVIQ

The Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (Marks, 1973) consists of 16 items presented in sets of four. Each set begins with a descriptive statement (e.g., "Visualize a rising sun.

Consider carefully the picture that comes before your mind's eye.") before asking responders to rate their ability to visualize additional details (e.g., "The sun is rising above the horizon into a hazy sky," or "The sky clears and surrounds the sun with blueness.") using a 5-point scale (with1 = Perfectly clear and as vivid as normal vision and5= No image at all, you only "know" you are thinking of an object). The total score range is 16-80. For ease of understanding, we reversed the range so that higher scores indicate more vivid imagery (Maxwell et al., 2017; Zeman et al., 2020). Thus, a person who reports no imagery at all would score 16, and a person who rates each item as perfectly vivid and clear would score 80. A replication VVIQ in a subset of participants (n=92) was administered one year later, along with the PCL-5. Instructions for the follow-up VVIQ included a commonly used illustration of an apple, with exemplars for ratings of 1 to 5 (Fig. 1).

2.4 Statistical Analysis

Correlations between VVIQ scores and the PCL-5 (total score and the four subscales) were performed using Spearman's rank order correlations, with corrected α = 0.01. Hierarchical regressions predicting PCL-5 total score included demographics and event severity in the first step and VVIQ in the second step. Because we did not assume equal variances between groups, Welch's t-tests were used instead of Student's t-tests when independent samples t-tests were appropriate (for group comparisons based on diagnosis and gender), and the adjusted degrees of freedom are reported. A chi-square test was used to assess whether the percentage of female Veterans who met the threshold for a provisional PTSD diagnosis differed from that of male Veterans. Exploratory analyses examining the effects of trauma type are included in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical tests were performed using JASP statistical software Version 0.18 (JASP Team, 2023).

3. Results

Demographic information for the entire sample (n = 312), for participants without PTSD (n=204), and for participants with probable PTSD (n=108) is shown in Table 1. A PCL-5 score > 33 was used as the cut-off for provisional PTSD (Weathers et al., 2013). The overall means for selfreported visual imagery (VVIQ = 62.4) were higher than observed in studies with primarily civilian populations (e.g., mean VVIQ = 55 in Zeman, 2024). Notably, VVIQ scores were significantly lower in Veterans with PTSD compared to those without [t(185.88) = 4.99, p<0.001, d=.61], see Table 1.

In the full sample, lower VVIQ scores were associated with significantly higher PCL-5 total scores (ρ = -.312, p < .001), indicating that less vivid imagery was associated with greater PTSD symptom severity (Fig. 2). VVIQ was also negatively correlated with all four PCL-5 symptom clusters (Table 2). The strongest association was with negative cognition/mood (ρ = -.329), followed by hyperarousal (ρ = -.291), intrusions (ρ = -.258), and avoidance (ρ = -.243).

The standard Criterion A questions were included as part of PCL-5 administration. PCL-5 scores showed reliable differences based on the categorical variable event severity (rated1 = "Yes, involved actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence"; or0 = "No, did not"). PCL-5 scores for event severity 1 (30.59 + 21.38; n=189) were significantly higher than for event severity 0 (18.32 + 18.88; n=123), [t(282.83) = 5.32, p<0.001, d=.61], verifying this variable was effectively capturing trauma severity.

A hierarchical regression examining the relationship between VVIQ and PCL-5 scores included demographic variables and event severity as predictors in the first step and VVIQ scores in the second step (Table 3). The second model explained significantly more variance than the first, [F(1, 306) = 21.66, p < .001]. Results revealed that VVIQ scores were significantly linked to PCL-5 scores (B= -.378, SE= 0.081, β= -.243, p<.001), as were event severity and being younger and female. Demographic factors (age, education, and gender) and event severity accounted for 15.3% of PCL-5 variance, while adding VVIQ to the model increased the explained variance by 5.6% (Table 3). To examine whether Criterion A exposure affected the relationship between PCL-5 and VVIQ scores, we added the interaction term (Event Severity x VVIQ) to the regression model, and it was not significant (b = -1.719, SE = 2.380, t = -0.722, p = .471, β = -0.068). Further, the VVIQ scores of Veterans who were exposed to a Criterion A event (mean=61.5) did not differ significantly from those who were not (mean=63.8), [t(197.37) = -1.58, p=.115, d= -.18]. These results suggest that Criterion A trauma exposure did not affect VVIQ scores, nor did trauma exposure affect the relationship between the vividness of visual imagery and PTSD severity.

Female Veterans reported significantly higher PCL-5 scores than male Veterans [t(195.48) = 2.72, p=0.007, d=.33], see Table 4. Of the subscales, female Veterans had higher scores for intrusions [t(193.88) = 3.61, p<0.001, d=.44] and avoidance [t(205.88) = 3.25, p=0.001, d=.39], while negative cognitions [t(197.37) = 2.39, p=0.018, d=.29] and hyperarousal [t(194.07) = 1.39, p=0.167, d=.17] were not significant. The percentage of female Veterans who met the threshold for a provisional PTSD diagnosis (43.9%) was also significantly higher than the percentage of male Veterans (29.8%), χ 2 (1, N = 312) = 6.24, p = 0.013. However, VVIQ scores did not show a significant difference between female and male Veterans [t(190.50) = 0.41, p=0.68, d=.05].

3.1. Post-Hoc Analyses

The distribution of VVIQ scores indicated that our sample of Veterans included a high number of participants with very vivid imagery. Specifically, the proportion of hyperphantasic participants (23.72%; see Fig. 3, Table S1) was substantially higher than general population estimates of ~6% (Wright et al., 2024). To ensure that Veterans were not overestimating their imagery abilities due to the absence of concrete exemplars, we re-administered the VVIQ, instructing participants to use the "apple" images as a visual anchor (Fig. 1). A subsample of 92 participants completed the regular VVIQ at baseline and with the visual anchor images at 12-month follow-up (Fig. 4). The test-retest reliability of VVIQ scores at the two time points was acceptable (ICC = 0.731; Lower 95% CI = 0.619, Upper 95% CI = 0.814). Importantly, the negative correlation between VVIQ and PCL-5 was numerically stronger with the visual anchor (ρ = -0.361, p < .001) than at baseline (ρ = -0.272, p = .009), suggesting the relationship is strong.

Notably, eight participants (2.56%) met criteria for aphantasia (VVIQ ≤ 32). For qualitative purposes, we report detailed results for these individuals in the Supplementary Materials. Their PCL-5 scores ranged from 8 to 60, demonstrating that aphantasia does not prevent severe PTSD symptomatology, including the experience of intrusions (Table S2). Two participants with the highest total PCL-5 and intrusion scores (#2 and #4) reported experiencing no visual imagery.

Of greater relevance was trauma severity and the experience of trauma (e.g., "learned about it" vs. "happened to me directly" -see Supplementary Materials for the latter). PCL-5 scores showed reliable differences for these categorical variables across the entire sample. As in the general population, the type of trauma (e.g., combat, military sexual trauma), years post-event, and current PCL-5 score were highly variable across individuals with aphantasia.

Exploratory analyses examined whether VVIQ scores were linked to specific types of traumatic experiences, since combat was predominant in male Veterans (38%) and military sexual trauma (MST) was most common in female Veterans (40%). As reported in the literature, combat and MST are the most common types of trauma reported by male and female Veterans, respectively (Buccellato et al., 2025). We examined whether the relationship between imagery vividness and PTSD severity differed in these subpopulations. The correlations between VVIQ and PCL-5 diverged between these two groups: vivid imagery was significantly associated with less severe PTSD symptoms in men with combat trauma (ρ = -0.504, p<.001), but not in women with MST (ρ = -0.016, p=.920), see Supplementary Materials for details.

4. Discussion

Our study examined the relationship between VVIQ and PCL-5 scores in a group of U.S. military Veterans. We predicted that high VVIQ would be associated with worse PTSD symptoms, especially for the frequency and severity of unwanted intrusions. Instead, the findings demonstrated that Veterans with more vivid visual imagery experienced less severe PTSD symptoms. This relationship was strongest for the negative cognitions cluster but was also significant for hyperarousal, intrusions, and avoidance. Female Veterans were more likely than male Veterans to have a provisional PTSD diagnosis, but their VVIQ scores did not differ significantly from those of males. Exploratory analyses suggested that visual imagery and symptom severity were not correlated in female Veterans with MST. Finally, aphantasia (having little or no visual imagery) did not protect participants from intrusions or PTSD symptoms more generally.

4.1. The Generative Adversarial Network Model

According to the GAN model, a traumatic event may disturb the typical interactions between the nonconscious and conscious visual pathways, so that visual predictions generated by the nonconscious pathway have an undue influence on the conscious pathway (Cushing et al., 2023). Combined with faulty reality monitoring (inability to discriminate between imagined and real percepts), a more vivid image generator could generate visual flashbacks that seem real. The GAN model predicts that having a greater capacity to generate vivid visual images should be associated with worse intrusive symptoms (Cushing et al., 2023). We expected that our study would support this model, but the results came to the opposite conclusion: participants who reported more vivid imagery were less likely to suffer from intrusions.

What are some possible explanations for this discrepancy? A key consideration is the distinction between voluntary and involuntary imagery. The VVIQ measures imagery that is called to mind deliberately, while flashbacks occur automatically. Our data could suggest a dissociation between these two routes for generating imagined content, or that vivid imagery can be accompanied by a well-functioning reality monitor that rejects flashbacks, thereby reducing their emotional impact. These ideas are speculative, because we have no functional measures of the model's components: the nonconscious M-pathway, the conscious P-pathway, and the reality monitor.

Another explanation is that the intrusions cluster encompasses a broad range of symptoms beyond the re-experiencing of involuntary images. Although an elegant theory grounded in neuroscience, the GAN model of Cushing and colleagues (2023) does not consider the variety of intrusive experiences that can plague individuals with PTSD. Sensory intrusions are not limited to the visual modality, and imagery vividness can differ across sensory modalities (e.g., sound, smell; Andrade et al., 2014). More importantly, intrusive symptoms are not limited to percepts in the sensory domain. On the PCL-5, item #1 ("Repeated, disturbing, and unwanted memories of the stressful experience") can involve verbal and semantic memories. Likewise, items #4 ("Feeling very upset when something reminded you of the stressful experience") and #5 ("Having strong physical reactions when something reminded you…") are emotional and somatic in nature. These details are critical when considering participants with aphantasia.

4.2. Negative Cognition/Mood as the Strongest Correlate

Vivid imagery was associated with less severe symptoms across all four PTSD clusters, but negative cognition/mood showed the greatest reduction. This cluster includes difficulties remembering the event, negative beliefs about oneself and the world, distorted blame, diminished interest, detachment from others, and restricted positive affect. These cognitiveemotional symptoms are not grounded in the sensory representations (S-reps) of Brewin and colleagues (2010). Instead of a focused impact on intrusions, our data suggest that visual imagery can also enhance the capacity to process traumatic events. Voluntary visual imagery might be one of the strategies used to reconstruct traumatic memories, as suggested by Vasterling et al. (2023). This may be especially true among individuals with high attentional control (Andries et al., 2024). In contrast, some participants with low VVIQ may have greater difficulty constructing coherent trauma narratives without the use of imagery. One potential result is a reduction in the cognitive-emotional flexibility that may help manipulate and reframe traumatic memories. A related finding is that individuals who have difficulties with emotion regulation report lower VVIQ scores (Maxwell et al., 2017).

It follows, then, that higher VVIQ scores would be linked to better emotion regulation. Laor and colleagues (1999) demonstrated that PTSD patients with vivid imagery were better able to modulate their physiological responses while processing negative images evoked by a trauma script. This was contrary to predictions based on Lang (1979), who proposed that vivid imagery activates physiological and emotional responses. To resolve the paradox, Laor et al. (1999) suggested "there could be a gap between the trait measure of image vividness and the actual vividness of imagery produced during a state of flashback" (p. 45). The voluntary generation of visual imagery, whether to a trauma script or a request to visualize a rising sun (VVIQ), may differ from involuntary flashbacks. The latter are easily triggered by cues and poorly integrated with autobiographical context (Brewin et al., 2010). In contrast, our results suggest that voluntary visual imagery can act as an effective emotion regulation tool, in agreement with Laor et al. (1999 ), Maxwell et al. (2017 ), and Vasterling et al. (2023). Yeung et al. (2025) administered the OSIQ to trauma-exposed adults and found a critical distinction between two types of visual imagery. Higher object imagery was correlated with worse PTSD symptoms, while higher spatial imagery was associated with milder symptoms.

4.3. Object vs. Spatial Imagery

The former could reflect the negative impact of involuntary/persistent object imagery (Cushing et al., 2023), while the latter could reflect the use of flexible, schematic spatial representation to help integrate the trauma (Brewin et al., 2010). The VVIQ is unable to distinguish between these because it contains a mixture of object ("color and shape of trees") and spatial ("contours of the landscape") items. Thus, it's possible that our results may reflect the benefits of spatial imagery, rather than the "curse" of object imagery (Yeung et al., 2025). However, we find this unlikely because the VVIQ correlates with scores on the OSIQ-Object scale, but not the OSIQ-Spatial scale (Blajenkova et al., 2006). Despite the possible conflation, the significance of the negative correlation between VVIQ and PCL-5 suggests it is a robust effect.

4.4. PTSD Differs in Female Veterans

Female Veterans were more likely to have a provisional PTSD diagnosis, and their PTSD symptoms were more severe than those reported by male Veterans, consistent with previous observations (Schein et al., 2021; National Center for PTSD, 2025). Yet, their VVIQ scores were not significantly different from the scores in male Veterans. This suggests that another mechanism or pathway may contribute to PTSD in women. Two potential explanations come to mind. In Study 2 of Yeung et al. (2025), the beneficial effect of spatial imagery interacted with gender, such that better spatial imagery was protective in males but associated with worse symptoms in females. A similar effect here would be speculative, since we have no specific measures of object and spatial imagery.

Another possibility is related to the predominant trauma type. Military sexual trauma (MST) was reported by 40% of female Veterans (as in Barth et al., 2016), while combat was the most common trauma type in male Veterans (38%). Indeed, distinctly different relationships between imagery and PTSD severity were observed in these subpopulations: a strong negative correlation in male combat Veterans and no correlation in female Veterans with MST. One tentative explanation for the protective effect of visual imagery (whether object or spatial) in male combat Veterans is that it allows for the contextualization of these events in space and time (Brewin et al., 2010). An allocentric processing perspective, centered on the external environment, may reduce the "here and now" aspect of flashbacks, placing them further back in time. In contrast, MST involves themes of betrayal and shame (Flynn et al., 2024) that may have a more semantic, narrative character where imagery is less relevant (or not helpful).

4.5. Implications for Aphantasia

Our findings do not support the claims that aphantasia protects against PTSD. Five out of eight individuals with aphantasia had clinically significant PTSD symptoms, including high levels of intrusions. Aphantasics can experience PTSD through non-visual channels, including emotional responses, cognitive distortions, physiological arousal, and behavioral avoidance.

Mawtus and colleagues (2024) administered interviews and questionnaires to participants with aphantasia and identified individuals who sought mental healthcare for PTSD. They were much less likely to report visual intrusions than controls with typical imagery. Some aphantasics felt their PTSD was undiagnosed due to the lack of visual intrusions, and that imagery-based therapies were less effective (Mawtus et al., 2024). Importantly, the aphantasics and typical imagers experienced auditory, tactile, bodily sensation, and emotional intrusions to the same extent. Our study cannot identify the modality of intrusions in the Veterans with aphantasia, but the absence of visual imagery did not prevent high scores on this subscale.

4.6. Hyperphantasia in Veterans

A greater percentage of individuals in our sample (23.7%) reported highly vivid imagery (hyperphantasia, Table S1), compared to 3-6% in previous studies (Wright et al., 2024; Zeman, 2024). To verify this finding, a subset of participants repeated the VVIQ using visual exemplars to calibrate their ratings. The negative correlation between VVIQ and PCL-5 scores was numerically larger with the visual anchor, compared to the standard administration. Test-retest reliability of the VVIQ was high, even with the divergent format. One explanation for the relative preponderance of hyperphantasia may reflect self-selection for military enlistment, because many roles require excellent visual imagery and visual-spatial abilities. For example, pilots and air traffic controllers have significantly more vivid imagery than matched controls (Isaac & Marks, 1994). A previous study of Australian military personnel (Russell & Mussap, 2024) found that mean VVIQ was 60.7, compared to the mean of 55 in civilians (Zeman, 2024), establishing a precedent for higher VVIQ scores in military populations. Individuals with hyperphantasia may even have an advantage over average imagers when learning how to shoot (Bahmani et al., 2025). Another possibility is that extensive military training could enhance imagery capacity, as some studies of athletes have shown (e.g., Rhodes et al., 2024). To the best of our knowledge, no large population study has compared visual imagery in military and civilian groups.

4.7. Clinical Implications

Voluntary visual imagery might be one of the strategies used to reconstruct traumatic memories, as suggested by Vasterling et al. (2023). Potential therapeutic pathways include enhancements in cognitive flexibility (mental manipulation of memories), emotion regulation (capacity for imagery rescripting), memory integration (contextualizing traumatic memories), and conceptual change (generating alternative perspectives). Low imagery might be a vulnerability or risk factor for PTSD because of reduced cognitive flexibility, less efficient emotion regulation strategies, and fragmented, poorly integrated trauma memories.

Imagery-based treatments for individuals with low VVIQ scores may be less effective (and more frustrating). For example, imagery rescripting uses guided visualization techniques to reframe the traumatic event. Likewise, prolonged exposure therapy (PE) incorporates imaginal exposure, where the patient is asked to visualize and emotionally engage with the traumatic event. Instead, cognitive restructuring approaches that do not incorporate visual imagery may be more effective in those with low VVIQ. These ideas are speculative, but treatment matching based on VVIQ scores could help guide more appropriate therapeutic choices.

4.8. Limitations

This study has several limitations, including its reliance on self-report measures and the administration of only one visual imagery questionnaire. Further, there was no clinicianadministered assessment of PTSD (e.g., CAPS-5). All the participants were military Veterans, so the results may not generalize to civilians, given the range of VVIQ scores and the types of traumatic experiences. For instance, the number of Veterans with extremely high imagery abilities exceeded the occurrence of hyperphantasia reported in large population studies (Wright et al., 2024). On the other hand, the percentage of Veterans with aphantasia was small, in line with the same studies. This limits the conclusions we can draw about extremely low imagery. Examination of pre-existing risk factors for PTSD (e.g., childhood adversity, low socioeconomic status) was outside the scope of this study. Finally, the results are cross-sectional, so we cannot conclusively determine whether low VVIQ is a vulnerability factor for PTSD, or whether PTSD lowers imagery due to avoidance strategies.

4.9. Future Directions

Future research would benefit from using the OSIQ (to separate object and spatial imagery) and the SUIS (to capture spontaneous/involuntary imagery), along with the VVIQ for comparability with previous studies. This would provide a more complete picture for exploring the relationship between PTSD symptoms and specific types of imagery. Assessing whether visual images are perceived externally, i.e. projected onto visual experience ("prophantasia") or perceived internally, e.g. in the mind's eye, may help explain individual differences (Schwarzkopf, 2024; Azañón et al., 2025). A clinically relevant question is whether the VVIQ can predict treatment response to imagery-based therapies such as PE and imagery rescripting. Future studies could incorporate a set of imagery questionnaires to see whether those with poor imagery show less benefit from these treatments.

Longitudinal studies could address whether pre-deployment VVIQ can predict postdeployment PTSD. Assessing whether the present results replicate in trauma-exposed civilians with PTSD, and direct comparisons between military and civilian populations, will be important. Finally, using more objective measures of imagery to validate VVIQ findings will be a novel avenue of research.

4.10. Conclusions

Visual imagery can have a powerful impact on emotional states, exceeding the influence of verbal thought and thereby exacerbating the symptoms of several psychiatric conditions (Holmes et al., 2008; Ji et al., 2019). At the same time, a paucity of visual imagery has been linked to problems with emotion regulation (Maxwell et al., 2017), suggesting that imagery can both amplify and dampen negative affect. Our results indicate that vivid visual imagery is associated with a lower severity of PTSD symptoms in military Veterans. Conversely, the lack of visual imagery in Veterans with aphantasia did not prevent high rates of intrusions. These findings contradict proposals that vivid imagery should enhance PTSD symptoms, especially involuntary flashbacks and other intrusive experiences. However, the Intrusions cluster also encompasses symptoms that are verbal, emotional, and somatic in nature. We propose that voluntary visual imagery, as measured by the VVIQ, can be a productive means of processing traumatic experiences, especially for reducing negative beliefs about the self and the world. The results have implications for choosing psychotherapies that are appropriately matched to a patient's imagery abilities. Trauma Type

Since female Veterans had significantly higher PCL-5 scores than males but no difference in mean VVIQ, we considered the possible impact of trauma type in exploratory analyses. There were 195 Veterans who listed an event that met Criterion A (Disclosed), 42 who left the question blank or did not wish to disclose (Not Disclosed), and 75 who either stated "no event" or listed an event that did not meet Criterion A according to two independent raters (N/A). ANOVA revealed significant differences between these groups, F(2, 309) = 12.40, p < .001, η² = .074. As expected, the N/A group had the lowest PCL-5 scores (mean = 15.5), which differed significantly from Disclosed (mean = 29.8, p<.001) and Not Disclosed (mean = 25.3, p=.042). The latter two groups did not differ significantly from each other, suggesting the likelihood of trauma exposure in the Not Disclosed group. In contrast, there were no group differences for VVIQ, F(2, 309) = 1.29, p = .278, η² = .008.

In male Veterans, combat was the dominant trauma type, reported by 37.5% (n=45/120), compared to military sexual trauma (MST) reported by 40% of female Veterans (n=30/75). This agrees with previous findings that combat and MST are the most common types of trauma reported by male and female Veterans, respectively (Buccellato et al., 2025). Trauma type was mostly confounded with gender, because of low numbers of each in the opposite category. A much larger sample size would be needed to identify a sufficient number of male Veterans who endorse MST or to compile sufficient numbers of participants with other trauma events (e.g., knowing close comrades who died by suicide). Nonetheless, given the differential role of spatial vs. object imagery in men and women (Yeung et al., 2025, Study 2), an exploratory analysis examined the correlation of PCL-5 and VVIQ in this subpopulation of males (n=45) vs. females (n=40 when including abuse not specified as MST). First, MST in female Veterans was associated with more severe PTSD [t(82.37) = 2.85, p=0.006, d=.62], with a mean PCL-5 score of 46.6, compared to 34.6 for combat trauma in male Veterans. However, VVIQ scores were not significantly different for female (mean = 56.8) vs. male Veterans (mean 58.9) [t(76.29) = 0.59, p=0.55, d=.13]. Surprisingly, vivid imagery was strongly associated with less severe PTSD symptoms in men with combat trauma (ρ = -0.504, p<.001), but not in women with MST (ρ = -0.016, p=.920). The difference between groups was significant [Fisher Z = -2.388, p = .0179; permutation p=.0186 (5,000 shuffles)]. Power at an effect this size is .67 with n = 45/40. Thus, mean VVIQ scores showed no gender difference, but the relationship between visual imagery and PTSD symptom severity was absent in female Veterans with MST.
