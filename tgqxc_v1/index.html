How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Social disconnection has been proposed as a contributing factor to the youth mental health crisis. However, it remains unclear how social disruptions reshape social networks and their links to mental wellbeing across age groups. The present study examined whether social disruptions (during the COVID-19 pandemic) reshape social networks and how changes in social networks differently relate to mental health and loneliness in adolescents compared to adults.

The study used egocentric network analysis of data from 1,278 participants (88% female; aged 11-89 years, mean = 37.2±16.2 years) in the multinational (Australia, the U.K., and U.S.) prospective COVID-19 Risks Across the Lifespan (CORAL) Study. Social network properties of structural density, functional closeness, and interaction frequency were derived.

Linear mixed-effects modelling was employed to examine cross-sectional and prospective associations with mental wellbeing at baseline, three-month, and six-month follow-up.

Results: Adolescents reported more virtual interactions, and less in-person contact. Greater network closeness was associated with lower depression, whereas dense but less close networks were linked to greater loneliness. Reduced contact from pre-pandemic levels were also linked to poorer wellbeing, particularly among younger individuals.

The present findings highlight that age differences in social experiences, such as social network structure (density) and function (closeness and interaction frequency), may underlie young people's greater mental health vulnerability during social disruptions. This underscores the importance of developing age-sensitive and context-responsive strategies to better support mental wellbeing during times of social disruption.

Introduction

The global decline in youth mental health over the past two decades underscores the need to identify prevention and intervention targets (Keyes & Platt, 2024; McGorry et al., 2024). While a myriad of factors likely contribute to the youth mental health crisis, growing attention has focused on social connectivity and the lack thereof in young people today (Birrell et al., 2025; Holt-Lunstad & Sine, 2025). Social connection is a fundamental human need that promotes psychological wellbeing (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Adolescence (11-24 years; Sawyer et al., 2018), represents a pivotal developmental period when individuals increasingly foster new, independent social connections while transitioning into adulthood (Lam et al., 2014; Pfeifer & Berkman, 2018). Disruptions or changes in social connections and mental health during adolescence are particularly detrimental to development and mental health (Orben et al., 2020). The COVID-19 pandemic, which imposed widespread physical social restrictions, offers a unique naturalistic research opportunity to investigate how changes in social connections relate to mental health across the lifespan.

During the pandemic, increased self-reported loneliness was consistently associated with poorer mental health in young people (Cooper et al., 2021; Ernst et al., 2022). Whilst disruptions to social connection may have directly contributed to increased loneliness, they may have also led to decreased mental health by preventing young people from engaging in developmentally typical behaviours involving exploration and learning with/about their peers (Towner et al., 2023). This may also partially explain the greater detrimental impact of the pandemic on adolescent mental health and loneliness than on adults . The current study therefore investigated whether and how changes in social connections were differentially related to self-perceived loneliness and mental health in adolescents and adults.

Social network data provide an ecologically-valid measure of social connections by mapping social relationships and tracking changes over time (Rassaby et al., 2024). Social network analysis quantifies individual's social ties, and their structural (e.g., density index which measures how close-knit a network is) and functional characteristics (e.g., emotional closeness within a network) (Burgette et al., 2021; Mehrpour et al., 2025). While scarce, research during the pandemic showed that more cohesive networks and (emotionally) stronger relationships were associated with fewer depressive symptoms (Perry et al., 2024).

Importantly, these effects were more pronounced in adolescents than adults (Elmer et al., 2020; Perry et al., 2024). Beyond structural and functional changes, greater reductions in social interaction frequency, were also associated with elevated depressive symptoms during the pandemic (Sommerlad et al., 2021). These findings support social network analysis and contact-frequency as rigorous approaches to studying associations between (disrupted) social connection and mental health.

The mode of social contact should also be considered. Technological advances have reshaped social interactions, with the expansion of digital communications further accelerating during the pandemic (Nguyen et al., 2020). Unique affordances of digital social connection (e.g., impoverished social cues, delayed feedback) have been proposed to confer greater mental health risks, particularly among adolescents (Schweizer et al., 2023). Indeed, evidence consistently indicated that face-to-face contact had greater mental health benefits than digital interactions (Bilston et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2024; Stieger et al., 2023). Social connection with different partners (e.g., family versus peers) may additionally show differential associations with mental health, especially in adolescents. Adolescence is marked by a relative shift from predominantly family-based interactions towards more peer-oriented relationships, whereas adulthood which is characterised by more stable networks centred on family and close others (Antonucci et al., 2014). Although studies have linked greater closeness with family and friends to higher positive affect in older adults (Cavallini et al., 2021), and greater disruptions to peer relationships to mental health difficulties in young people (Magson et al., 2021), developmental differences in changes in social interactions with family versus friends remain underexamined.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study utilised data from the multinational COVID-19 Risks Across the Lifespan (CORAL) study. CORAL is a longitudinal study in individuals, aged 11-89 years which collected social network data thrice. This permitted examination of age-related differences in social network structure and function and their association with loneliness and depressive symptoms cross-sectionally and prospectively across the first year of the pandemic.

This study used an egocentric network analysis approach, which captures network properties from the respondent's perspective (Burgette et al., 2021). This design allowed for the following hypotheses to be tested: first, social network structure (i.e., density and frequency of contact) and function (i.e., closeness), would vary as a function of age (H1), and greater density and closeness would be associated with fewer symptoms of depression (H2a) and less loneliness (H2b). These associations (H2) would also vary by age (H3) and across the pandemic (H4). In addition, less frequent social interactions across modality type (i.e., face-to-face, phone, and other digital means) would be associated with more depressive symptoms and loneliness.

We also explored whether changes from pre-pandemic contact levels were associated with depressive symptoms and loneliness, and whether these associations varied by interaction partner (i.e., with family versus friends) and age.

Materials and Methods

Participants

A total of 3,208 participants were included in the CORAL study who met the inclusion criteria of residing in U.K., U.S., or Australia, fluent in English, (for detailed eligibility criteria see Minihan et al., 2023 Minihan et al., , 2024)). For the present study, only individuals with complete age, egocentric social network, mental health, and wellbeing data for at least one study timepoint were included (N = 1,278).

The study was approved by the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (HC200287). All participants provided informed consent, and assent and consent were obtained from parent or guardians for participants under 18 years of age.

Measures

Social Network

Using an egocentric network assessment, participants (also referred to as ego) first reported up to six people (hereafter referred to as alters) with whom they spent the most free time (i.e., time outside of work/school) in person or online. Participants reported the nature of the relationship for each alter (e.g., mother, friend, husband, sibling), by what means and how often they communicated with each alter, how close they were to each alter and the connections between each alter, including again the type of their relationship (e.g., no connection, relatives, friends) and how close they were to each other. From this, density, closeness, and frequency of contact were derived (see SI for details on items). At T1, participants were asked to respond based on experiences in the past year before the COVID-19 pandemic. They were then also asked to indicate whether the frequency of contact had changed since the onset of the pandemic. At T2 and T3, responses were based on experiences over the past month.

Density (structural index)

Density was calculated as the proportion of the number of observed ties relative to the maximum number of possible ties in the network (Burgette et al., 2021; Oliveira & Gama, 2012): n represents the number of alters and aa represents the number of alter-alter ties (i.e., the number of alters that have a connection).

For a given network size, density is maximised (density = 1) when all possible alter-alter ties are present and minimized when no alter-alter ties exist. In the present sample, the minimum possible density is 0.286, obtained when a total of 6 alters (n = 6) were reported but no alter-alter ties are present (aa = 0). Accordingly, observed density values ranged from 0.286 to 1 among participants reporting at least one alter.

Closeness (functional index)

Closeness is operationalised as the average strength of all reported connections. For every reported alter, participants were asked to indicate how close they feel to that alter on a scale of 1 (not close at all) to 10 (extremely close/closer than any other person I know). The variable 'closeness' referred to the average of these strengths transformed to a scale of 0.1 to 1 ( O'Malley et al., 2012).

Frequency of contact

Frequency of contact refers to how often egos interacted with their alters (Huxhold et al., 2013) via three contact modes: 1) face-to-face, 2) interaction by phone, and 3) interaction via other digital means. For each mode, participants indicated how often during the past month they interacted with each alter on a scale of 0 (never) to 4 (every day/ nearly every day). The mean of frequencies of interactions across all alters was computed for each participant, which resulted in three mean values per participant, one for each contact mode. This variable had an additional component: at timepoint 1, participants were also asked to indicate how often they interacted with each alter before COVID-19. This retrospective self-report made it possible to compare the frequency of interaction before COVID-19 and at baseline for each alter.

Average of difference in pre-pandemic and baseline contact-frequency across alters was calculated, and changes in frequency of contact were further separated into contact with family versus friends by averaging across family and friends alters separately.

Mental Health

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 8-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8; Kroenke et al., 2001). The eight items asked how often participants were bothered by certain problems (e.g., little interest of pleasure in doing things) on a scale of 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Symptom severity ranged from 0 to 24. The scale showed good internal consistency at all time points (ωT = 0.93-0.94).

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured using the 20-item UCLA loneliness scale (Russell, 1996). Participants responded to each item (e.g., I have nobody to talk to) on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (I never feel this way) to 3 (I often feel this way). Total summed loneliness scores ranged from 0-60. The scale showed excellent internal consistency at all timepoints (ωTs = 0.97).

Statistical Analyses

Analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2025). Detailed model specifications are described in Supplementary Information (SI). Linear regression models examined crosssectional associations at T1, and linear mixed-effects models examined prospective associations across T1-T3. Mixed-effects models included time (T1, T2, T3) as a fixed effect and participant ID as a random effect.

For H1, age was included as a fixed effect in separate models for each network index: density, closeness, and frequency of face-to-face, phone, and digital interactions. H2 models included density, closeness, and their interaction as fixed effects, with depressive symptoms and loneliness modelled as separate outcomes. H3 extended the H2 models by additionally including age x network index interactions. Time-interaction effects were included in prospective models for H4. To account for multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied within each hypothesis (e.g., across five models for H1, two models for H2-H4).

For H5, the three types of modalities (face-to-face, phone, and digital means) of contactfrequency were modelled separately as predictors of depressive symptoms and loneliness, resulting in six models. FDR correction was applied within each outcome, across the three contact-modality models, to avoid over-correction.

Exploratory analyses used the same modelling approach as H5 but replaced raw contactfrequency counts with changes from the pre-pandemic period to T1. Contact-frequency for family and friends were modelled simultaneously to estimate their relative associations with mental health outcomes. Age interactions were included in all models to examine age-related differences.

Sensitivity analyses additionally included country as a covariate in the main analysis models to account for differences in COVID-19 policies across the U.K., U.S., and Australia.

Discussion

The present study investigated changes in social connections across the lifespan and their associations with depression and loneliness across the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic.

An egocentric network analysis approach was adopted to examine social connections, whereby participants nominated up to six individuals with whom they spent their free time.

Our findings highlighted key age-related differences, with adolescents reporting more virtual interactions and less in-person interactions than older adults. Moreover, we found distinct yet complementary associations between structural (i.e., network density) and functional (i.e., network closeness) characteristics of social connections and loneliness, particularly among young people. Specifically, lower loneliness was reported by individuals with both highly interconnected networks (high density) and strong emotional closeness across social ties, suggesting a joint protective effect. However, those with highly interconnected but emotionally distant networks reported the highest level of loneliness. Further, high levels of social contact, particularly face-to-face interactions, were associated with better mental health and less loneliness in adolescents and emerging-to mid-aged adults, during the pandemic. Exploratory analyses also revealed that disruptions to social connections in the form of reductions in social contact from pre-pandemic levels, across all interaction modes and especially with friends, appeared uniquely damaging to the mental wellbeing of adolescents. Reduced family contact and its associations with depression and loneliness, however, emerged only later in the pandemic, indicating a potential delayed effect.

Partially consistent with our hypothesis, we observed age-related differences in social interaction frequency, but not in network density or emotional closeness. Specifically, adolescents reported more frequent virtual contact (i.e., phone and digital) and less frequent face-to-face interactions than older adults. These patterns are consistent with prior research suggesting a greater preference for virtual social interaction over face-to-face interaction in young people, potentially reflecting the changing norms of social connection in this increasingly digital era (Zheng et al., 2026).

Greater emotional closeness within one's social network, but not network density, was related to fewer depressive symptoms. This aligns with research showing that high quality social connections are associated with reduced depressive symptoms in general (Pearce et al., 2023), and particularly during the pandemic (Perry et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). Further, this association persisted across age groups and over the first year of the pandemic, which may reflect the relative stability of closeness as a functional characteristic of social connections across age groups (Bruine de Bruin et al., 2020). This highlights the emotional quality of social connections as a potentially important and enduring buffer against depressive symptoms across age and social contexts, including periods of social disruption.

In contrast to depression, loneliness showed a more nuanced relationship with the structural and functional characteristics of social connections during the pandemic. While greater emotional closeness was generally associated with less reported loneliness, the lowest levels of loneliness were reported by individuals whose social connections were both emotionally close and highly interconnected, indicating a joint protective effect. Conversely, highly interconnected networks characterised by lower emotional closeness appeared to confer greater risk for loneliness, suggesting that network interconnectedness alone is insufficient to buffer against feelings of social isolation. This aligns with the notion that mental health benefits from social connections do not come from the mere presence or quantity of social connections, but from their emotional quality (Baumeister & Leary, 1995).

Notably, these patterns were particularly pronounced among adolescents. This may be explained by increased social uncertainty in young people (Schweizer et al., 2023), which may heightened further in larger, more interconnected but less emotionally close networks. Dense social networks in adolescents likely reflect shared social contexts such as schools and extracurricular contexts. Abrupt disruptions to these contexts may have disproportionately reduced opportunities for social cues and the affirmation of social connections, partially accounting for the observed marked increases in loneliness among young people during the pandemic (Ernst et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2020). However, this is only speculative as social uncertainty was not examined in the present study. That said, emotionally close social connections appeared to buffer against loneliness despite reduced opportunities for interactions, consistent with research that similarly found perceived social relationship quality protects against loneliness and promotes mental health in adolescents (Cooper et al., 2021). Together, these findings highlight that the relationship between loneliness and social connections in adolescents is dependent on both structural and functional properties of their networks, underscoring the need for more nuanced approaches to understanding and addressing the growing loneliness epidemic among young people.

Beyond the structural and functional characteristics of social connections, greater interaction frequency, particularly face-to-face contact, was linked to lower depressive symptoms and loneliness, while more frequent phone contact was specifically linked to lower loneliness.

These findings are consistent with prior research documenting the mental health benefits of social interactions, especially in-person engagement (Liang et al., 2024; Stieger et al., 2023).

The consistency of these relationships across age and stages of the pandemic highlights frequent, particularly face-to-face, contact as a potential universal target for prevention and intervention across diverse social contexts and demographic groups. Indeed, interventions aimed at enhancing social functioning (e.g., improving social skills or increasing opportunities for social connection) and social prescription have shown promise in improving mental health outcomes such as loneliness and depression (Hayes et al., 2024; Mitchell et al., 2025). However, despite the possible benefits of greater social interaction, this perspective does not fully capture how disruptions or reductions in social contact, such as those experienced during the pandemic, relate to mental health outcomes.

Our results, albeit preliminary, showed that disruptions to social connections, in the form of reduced social contact across all modes (i.e., face-to-face, phone, and digital), were linked to poorer mental wellbeing, both immediately following the pandemic's onset and across the first year. Further, disruptions to connections with friends were linked to more immediate declines in mental wellbeing, while reduced connection with family was associated with delayed mental health risks later in the pandemic. Importantly, the mental health risks linked to reduced social contact were again most pronounced among adolescents, and to a lesser extent, among emerging-to mid-aged adults. While this may partly reflect a greater reliance on social connections for mental wellbeing in adolescents (Orben et al., 2020), it also underscores the importance of safeguarding social connections in contexts characterised by reductions in social contact (e.g., educational transitions, relocation, institutionalisation, forced displacement, and natural disasters), particularly for young people. The distinct temporal patterns of connections with family and friends may reflect the functional differences in these relationships. Friendships are theorised to be more central to experiences of belonging and social integration (Baumeister & Leary, 1995; Brown & Larson, 2009), particularly for young people (Lam et al., 2014). Abrupt disruptions in contact with friends may therefore carry more immediate consequences for mental wellbeing, consistent with evidence of the shorter-term effects of friendships on mental health outcomes (Manchanda et al., 2023). Conversely, as family relationships constitute more stable sources of emotional security and attachments (Antonucci et al., 2014; Bowlby, 1988), prolonged disruption to family contact, especially involuntary disruptions, may thus exert more cumulative effects on wellbeing over time (Liddell et al., 2022).

The present study has several methodological strengths including the use of large, multinational, prospective data collected across the first-year of the pandemic. However, results should be considered in light of the following limitations. First, despite efforts to recruit participants with diverse demographic backgrounds across three countries, the use of convenience sampling resulted in a sample comprised primarily of Caucasian females, thus limiting the generalisability of the findings given documented differences in social connections across gender (Friebel et al., 2021) and cultural contexts (Igarashi et al., 2008).

Second, although the present study identified important age-related differences and temporal variance in associations between disruptions to social connections and mental wellbeing, the underlying mechanisms remain unexplored. Future studies should further investigate explanatory factors, such as coping strategies (Geçer & Yıldırım, 2023), to better inform the development of targeted intervention and prevention efforts. Third, the present study considered average network density, emotional closeness, and frequency of interaction across one to six social connections but did not account for the stability of social relationships across assessment timepoints. For example, individuals who report on the same connections at all timepoints may be considered to have more stable relationships than those who report on different connections, which may have differing implications for their mental health outcomes. Finally, for feasibility reasons, the study employed an egocentric network analysis approach, which captures social connections from the perspective of the individual respondent, as opposed to a sociocentric approach that constructs network structures based on reports from all members within a network (Burgette et al., 2021). Consequently, our findings may be influenced by subjectivity and recall bias. However, given recent evidence highlighting the importance of subjective social experiences for mental health, this approach may be particularly relevant for understanding mental wellbeing outcomes (Nasirpour et al., 2025; Piera Pi-Sunyer et al., 2023). Nonetheless, future studies adopting a sociocentric approach could provide a more comprehensive understanding of how objective and subjective characteristics of social connections, independently and/or jointly, relate to mental health.

Conclusion

Across the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, disruptions to social connection were consistently associated with poorer mental health and greater loneliness. These associations were strongest among adolescents, suggesting that social network disruption may represent an important developmental risk factor for mental wellbeing. The findings highlight the distinct yet complementary roles of structural and functional characteristics of social connections, as well as the importance of both high interaction frequency and reductions in social contact in shaping mental wellbeing outcomes. Together, these findings have important implications for informing age-sensitive strategies to support mental wellbeing, particularly among young people.
