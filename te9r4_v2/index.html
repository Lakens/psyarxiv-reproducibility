Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Humans have long tried to make artificial versions of our minds and bodies and other life forms. Recent technological advances are poised to open Pandora's box and explore whether these artificial agents will become more human or alive. In this paper we argue that this question needs to be addressed in tandem with the equally valid counterpart: to what extent do interactions with artificial others make humans less human and more artificial? Living systems such as human organisms are fundamentally open systems, deeply connected with the natural world. Yet, ubiquitous interactions with artificial agents from the cradle and across the lifespan may transform us, humans, into hybrid creatures. It is not only the passive and rapid absorption of the increasingly complex cognitive content that matters, nor the mere sophistication of the conversational exchanges with AI agents or embodied interactions with robots. Rather, the very nature of interaction itself matters too. It's not what we learn, but how we learn and with whom we learn and what happens in between us and our creations.

1 Introduction

The old Master Geppetto, so the story goes, was feeling lonely and thus he created a wooden, toddler-like puppet called Pinocchio. After some magical interventions, the doll became alive and started to have his own agency, disobeying the old Master, creating serious troubles for both. Humans have long tried to make artificial versions of themselves, creating animated machines that mimic biological motion and hence life -such as automata (Riskin 2003). The newest and more sophisticated forms of machines, ranging from humanoid robots, to Virtual Reality (VR) characters to Artificial Intelligence (AI) powered agents, are likewise feared to develop their own agency, perhaps even consciousness and to "take over" and disobey their human makers the same way Pinocchio disobeyed the Old Master (Chalmers 2023). To what extent is this story just a science-fiction scenario or a feasible reality? Given the unprecedented speed of technological advances claiming that at least some of these new artificial agents are poised to "come to life" and to display thereby their own forms of selfhood, embodiment and agency, this question is more pressing than ever.

Here we approach this question by focusing on two aspects: i) one emphasizing the tacit presence of the human in the making of artificial systems; and ii) one outlining the effects of interacting with artificial agents on human minds and bodies. Consequently, two main claims will be made throughout the paper and will form a thread to guide our investigation.

First, we suggest that all artificial systems created by us, humans, biological life forms, are inherently hybrid by nature. By this we mean that all artificial systems have the human mind in the loop either overtly or covertly. We create artificial versions of our minds and bodies for various reasons but also to better understand ourselves. This is why we start our paper with a discussion of human agency, selfhood, and embodiment before we set out to understanding selfhood, embodiment and agency in artificial and further in hybrid systems. And this will be an important goal in our discussion -to outline the hidden variables in the equation which always lie within the tacit presence of Geppetto, the master, in the making of Pinocchio, the puppet.

Second, we suggest that the question whether interacting with artificial minds and bodies will eventually transform humans into more artificial-like, hybrid beings is the valid counterpart of the question whether artificial agents will become more human-like. These two questions, we claim, need to be addressed in tandem because interaction is two-way.

Recent technological developments and studies show that humans promptly attribute human-like abilities to all sorts of human-made artificial agents (Ziemke 2023). Less is known however of the effects of interacting with artificial others on the human mind, embodiment and sense of agency. This is an important question because self-organising biological living systems such as human bodies are open, porous and vulnerable systems and hence constituted by the everchanging embodied interactions with the world and others (Varela and Maturana 1980). Humans are not solid immutable cement blocks, but rather open systems and dependent upon constant open bridges with the world. Whatever system we interact with deeply impacts our psychological and embodied states. Hence, we become not just what we eat, breath and consume, but also what we interact with, especially our technologies. Humans are not just beings, but also "becomings". The English language affords concision and clarity, capturing important and subtle distinctions. For example, the word "being" can be used both as noun and a verb. It is generally admitted that human organisms are beings, i.e. spatiotemporally instantiated existing entities.

The problem here is that one may conceive beings as reified solid objects, more like chairs rather than clouds.

When we say that humans are "becomings" rather than beings, the aim is to outline the inherent transformatory and dynamic process that constitutes human existence (Noë 2004). Our interactive experiences with the world and others constantly modify our inner life and embodiment for the better and for the worse: whether, when we cry as a baby, we are faced with a smile or with hostility will deeply modify our neurobiology and stress response.

Persistent or repeated experiences like that can have effects across the lifespan.

As we will see shortly, agents act and interact with the world constantly. By definition, interactions are always bidirectional and humans, understood from the developmental perspective, are malleable, vulnerable and constantly influenced by interactions with the world and others. It is thus important to keep in mind i) what type of artificial and biological systems we interact with on daily basis, and ii) what type of interactions these artificial agents afford and their effects on our human minds. Humans are great imitators and we swiftly copy those who surround us. To quote the timeless wisdom of Aristotle passed on by our grandmothers: "be careful with whom you hang out, as you will become more like them unwillingly!".

This paper addresses the following three intertwined questions.

What is an agent and what is agency in biological and artificial systems? (Section 2) ii) What type of artificial versions of the human-like psychological and embodied abilities are currently implemented in the field as autonomous agents that may take over and develop their own "mind"

and agency? (Section 3 &4) iii) And finally, what are the consequences for human minds and bodies of interacting with artificial minds and bodies in our daily lives?

(Section 5)

In addressing this last question, we will explicitly endorse a developmental perspective because, unlike artificial agents, human bodies and minds grow and develop necessarily in relation to other minds and bodies (Ciaunica 2017). When it comes to understanding the human mind, the infant is, to use Wordsworth's famous metaphor, "father to the (hu)man". A robust body of evidence suggests that infants have much more sophisticated socialcognitive skills than previously suspected. The issue at stake is crucial since as Woodward and colleagues (2001) pointed out, assessing infants' understanding of others' behaviour provides not only a snapshot of the developing mind of the child, but also insights into the very nature of cognition itself.

But first, we review some working definitions of the key concepts of self, body, agent and agency in biological systems with a focus on humans. Why start with biological systems? Because without Geppetto, no Pinocchio.

2 What Agents are: Minds and Bodies

Who is Geppetto? Humans are self-organising biological systems that make artificial versions of themselves by modelling their own cognitive and/or embodied abilities. Making an artificial version of oneself may be thus added to the list of abilities that sets humans apart from other living beings in the animal world. But above all, Geppetto is an agent with a sense of control over his actions: for example, one day he wakes up and decides to make a toddler-puppet, instead of making a chair.

The term "agent" has its origins in the Latin "agere" which roughly means "doing". In this minimal approach, agents are entities that do stuff in the world. This definition however is too loose to bear any meaningful significance, hence one needs to refine it by inserting additional constraints. For example, an agent can be defined as an entity that acts in the world with a goal. This extra constraint introduces the critical feature of goaldirectedness (which some artificial agents may possess, e.g., the robot Roomba acts in the world to achieve the goal of cleaning my living room). Yet, one may argue that goal-directedness is not sufficient for agency: for example, I may have the intention or make a choice to clear or not to clean my living room, while Roomba doesn't: once I hit the button, the robot must follow the received instructions. Intentionality thus -not to be conflated with goal-directedness -may be considered a core part of what it means to be an agent and hence to have agency. Furthermore, to be agentic, an entity not only acts but importantly also flexibly inter-acts with the world and is capable of adjusting her goals and behaviours as a function of the outcomes of the interaction itself and the goals and behaviours of other agents in the environment.

An agent thus is typically defined as a system or entity that willingly and directly interacts with its surroundings through its physical embodiment to perform tasks and achieve goals (Gallagher 2020). Goals can be individual or collective or a combination of both. The tasks can be internally or externally driven. This is a distinction that can help show the difference between natural and artificial agents. For example, living systems or agents have the internal, intrinsic goal of looking for food and avoiding harm. When I was born, I did not choose to be hungry and hence to look for food. The goal of staying alive is inherently built-into my biophysiological make up. By contrast, artificially made systems or agents such as the robot Roomba have the externally driven goal of cleaning the room -without any care or concerns about harming or nurturing itself. The job the robotic system is doing has no bearing upon its ontological existence and it doesn't participate actively in its own creation. In short: if I don't eat, I will cease to exist. If my Roomba stops cleaning the room, it doesn't stop existing. As we will see shortly, this observation will be important in the distinction between biological and artificial agency and embodiment as defined by pioneers such as Maturana and Varela (1980).

Although there is some debate about the extent to which the ability of a biological or artificial system to have agency is related to the ability of that system to display self-organising or self-preservation behaviour (Ciaunica 2025), we think the notion of self (and self-organization) is intimately connected to the notion of agency. In this view, to be truly an agent with agency, means not only to act and interact with the world, but importantly to do so in order to sub-serve the fundamental goal of the system's self-preservation. As Jonas (1966) puts it, "this world is at once inviting and threatening." Biological organisms continuously construct and maintain themselves against the natural tendency toward dissolution and decay (Lyon 2006) and they do so by acting in the world as animated agents modifying their inner states, their behaviour and the world.

Some authors have argued that the concept of agency is crucially complemented by the concept of "sense of agency", which refers to the experience of being the initiator of one's own voluntary actions and through them influencing the external world (Haggard 2017; Beck et al., 2017; Hommel, 2017; Cornelio et al. 2022). The subjective feeling of being in control of one's actions is thus considered a key indicator of whether a system is truly agentic or merely does stuff in the world by obeying certain rules or set of instructions, like a Roomba or a thermostat. On this view, the sense of agency refers to the capacity to experience goal-directed motor actsmoving the body with intentional control to achieve a goal. In this respect the sense of agency has been defined as the experience that links intentions to their external consequences. For example, the match between the intended and actual outcome of an action produces a feeling of being in control (Synofzik et al., 2013). The brain mechanisms underlying this embodied and agentic experience are quite efficient and well known, acquired very early in development. Evidence of early sense of agency in infants as young as 2 months of age has been demonstrated for some basic bodily actions such as smiling and thumb sucking (Rochat and Striano, 2000). Later in development, we start to use our bodies to control the external world, with evidence showing that we promptly extend our sense of agency through the objects (tools) we interact with (Iriki et al., 1996 (Iriki et al., , 2001)).

It has thus been proposed that the sense of agency includes at least two aspects, body agency (the motor control aspect) and external agency (the intentional aspect of accomplishing a goal) (Wen, 2019; see Gallagher 2012).

Body agency refers to the experience of controlling one's own body and receiving the bodily feedback that results from the bodily movement one initiated voluntarily (e.g., moving my hand). External agency refers to the experience of acting to voluntarily control external events and obtaining the corresponding and matching external feedback from the objects in the world. The action-effect causality is key for our interactions with AI, VR and robotic technologies (see also Kagan, this issue).

More generally, and in the first place, for an entity to have a sense of agency requires that it have the capacity to experience something (Gallagher 2000 , Damasio 2000). One influential view in philosophy of mind and cognition stipulates that for the human and for some non-human animals prereflective bodily experiences anchor the first- Human agents thus have the property of embodied (as-subject) selfhood (Damasio 2000; Ciaunica 2025). 1 Even neonates are self-aware in this minimal prereflective sense, even though they can't recognize themselves in a 1 Damasio has developed a multilayered approach to self-processes, from protoself to core self to autobiographical self, with each advancing stage explained by specific brain-body architectures and the processes they execute (Damasio 2018).

3 Learning from the Mind -making neuro-inspired artificial agents

It is the human mind and not the body that was first in the spotlight in the contemporary race of making artificial versions of ourselves. By creating artificial systems with focus on what makes humans "special" (i.e., the mind, or cognitive brain power), the tacit hope was to capture the very essence of what makes us humans, what set us apart from other biological organisms say. Building artificial smart agents took early inspiration from the human brain and its complex neural architectures. The first pioneering attempts to build artificial minds focused on the computing power of the human brain (Anderson et al. 1993). The idea of artificial neural networks builds on early models of sensory processing by the brain (Krogh 2008). An artificial neural network is typically created by simulating a network of model neurons in a computer (Minsky & Papert 2017). By implementing algorithms that mimic the network and processes of real neurons, one can make the network 'learn' to solve many types of problems. The aim was to create artificial minds that equal or surpass human intelligence, defined as the capacity to flexibly and swiftly solve problems. As early as 1943, McCulloch and Pitts modeled a neuron as a switch that receives input from other neurons and is either activated or remains inactive, depending on the weight of the signal processed in between. Later it was demonstrated that networks comprising these types of neurons have properties similar to the brain and perform similar tasks (e.g. sophisticated pattern recognition) and they can do so even if some of the neurons are damaged.

However, over more recent years, the study of artificial intelligence has expanded from a purely computational and disembodied discipline into a highly interdisciplinary field. This remarkable AI advancement in the past decade has been propelled forward mostly by three key discoveries: i) formal and mathematical advancements in deep learning algorithms, ii) the invention of powerful new hardware, and iii) the availability of extensive datasets for training (see Paolo et al. 2024; Sandini et al. 2024). For example, the invention of Large Language Models (LLMs) like OpenAI's GPT-4 (Achiam et al., 2023) and Google's Gemini (Team et al., 2023) fuelled discussions pondering whether these models have reached certain levels of agency and even consciousness (Shanahan 2024). LLMs are computing systems operating within a linguistic-symbolic domain, representing textual information and generating new text by completing prompts (Brown et al., 2020; Devlin et al., 2019). Their foundational training relies on datasets methodically compiled and curated by teams of AI engineers working as behind-the-scenes human coders (i.e., "Geppettos"). These models have powerful explanatory outcomes: they have been shown to predict 3D protein structures (Jumper et al. 2021; Cramer, 2021), to control robotic movement (Brohan et al., 2023), and to generate images and audio (Ramesh et al., 2022).

LLMs reflect one key yet limited aspect of the human mind, i.e., its reasoning function (Xi et al., 2025).

However, it is unclear how exactly the emotional and perceptual, sensory-based dimensions of intelligence dynamically feed into the constant updating of beliefs and knowledge based on those experiences. Is the body a mere interface replaceable with other physical constituents or is it an integrative part of what experience-based knowledge is? Long before the LLMs became a craze topic, the seminal work by Lakoff & Johnson (1999, 1980) suggested that the process of reasoning is not based on abstract laws only but is grounded in bodily experiences. And long before Lakoff and Johnson, Anaxagoras argued that humans are the most intelligent animal because they have hands that are capable of dexterity and haptic exploration (something that Brooks [2025] considers beyond the reach of robots). This observation, alongside the difficulty in successfully aligning LLMs (Ouyang et al., 2022) together with their tendency to generate plausible but inaccurate information, (Huang et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023) dampens the initial enthusiasm around LLMs as valid candidates to be Pinocchio-like agents "taking over"

and making trouble in the world of Geppetto. As we will argue later, the only way these agents can become disruptive and chaotic, (Shapira et al. 2026) is when the human is involved; and, here we claim, humans are always involved. The "taking control" behaviour of these artificial agents can be qualified as chaotic when assessed through the human lens. It is chaotic for us.

However, despite the initial vogue and exponential interest, it rapidly became apparent that these disembodied systems represent only baby steps towards truly intelligent agents (Ciaunica 2025). These large cognitive architectures and networks are facing the criticism that they remain static and unable to make progress with time and experience. Basically, they are unable to do what human bodies, cats and ants do: develop and solve problems on the fly in the real world, as opposed to formal worlds (Ciaunica 2025; Gallagher, in press; Viale and Gallagher 2026).

A different strategy is to take life-inspired cellular activity as opposed to neural activity as a design principle sustaining autonomous behaviour in creating artificial agents. Not cognition added to a mechanical body, but selforganisation principles unfolding from within life itself. Living bodies are self-organising systems made of cells.

Some authors attempted to re-construct artificial systems by copying this configuration: superimposing a cognitive higher-order configuration upon a raw, basic physical material. 4 Learning from the Body -making bio-inspired embodied artificial agents

4.1 Embodied Artificial Intelligence (EAI) and homeostasis

Based on pioneering work by Varela and colleagues (1991) the key idea behind the embodied turn in AI is that minds and bodies are not separate, dualistic entities as Descartes (2012) famously posited (see also Damasio For example, it has been shown that agents driven solely by homeostatic objectives can reproduce distinct longterm feeding behaviours observed in nutritional biology by modulating internal metabolic parameters (Yoshida et al. 2024). Computational models and studies of homeostasis and the brain can be traced back to the era of cybernetics. In the field of dynamical systems theory, Ashby's famous homeostat coupled electrical and magnetic sensors and effectors such that when perturbed it executed a random parameter search until equilibrium was restored (Cariani 2009). Ashby treated this notion of homeostasis as a mathematical problem in dynamical systems and control theory and developed the concept of ultra-stable systems (Ashby 1952). The ultra-stable system was groundbreaking because it implements all the fundamental features of autonomous agents: observation of controlled goals, interaction with the environment, and autonomous adaptation through parametric modification of effectors to respond to the changing conditions (e.g.

Young's (1964) model of the octopus' brain as a homeostat).

Herbert A. Simon, a contemporary of Ashby, conducted early probabilistic analyses of the link between the environment and rational decision-making in agents navigating their surroundings (Simon 1956). According to this view, homeostasis is considered a core behavioural principle which may enable integrated intelligence. However, as Yoshida notes (2025), many of early implementations focused on reproducing specific biological mechanisms or information-processing components (Meyer & Wilson 1991). These contributions constitute pioneering efforts in biologically inspired robotics (Meyer 2008). By the 1990s, an interest in research on agent survival led to neural modelling with neural networks (Beer 1990), as well as reinforcement learning (RL) theories grounded in dynamical systems theory, control engineering, and statistical learning theory (Barto et al. 1983; Sutton 1988). One influential proposal to couple brain, body and environment is Karl Friston's Free Energy Principle (FEP henceforth). According to FEP, any self-organising system such as the human body has to resist the natural tendency to disorder (implied by the second law of thermodynamics) in order to maintain their states and form in the face of a constantly changing environment. Self-organising organisms complete this task through the dynamic updating of Bayesian sensory predictions. On this view the brain is thus processing dynamically shifting generative models of what is causing incoming sensory events, based on probabilistic predictions about how likely something is to have happened and what the likely causes are. This approach relies heavily on the capacity of the organism to engage in action in the world in order to "fulfil" its predictions and maintain inner states within a viable range for survival. Indeed, it has been recently suggested that the basic prereflective experience of being a self is the result of an ongoing predictive coding (PC) process within a generative model that is centered on the organism (Friston 2010; Clark 2013). On this view, the self is thus a result of dynamic self-modelling mechanisms.

Active Inference incorporates the control problem through action selection. In active inference, control is formulated under the same principles as inference (Pezzulo et al. 2015; Parr et al. 2022) introducing expected free energy as a measure of predicted sensory prediction error over future time steps to describe action selection and control. Building on these ideas, Gu and FitzGerald proposed the possibility of agent decision-making through interaction with the external world via active inference (2014).

Internal motivation as a key parameter and computational foundation for behaviour acquisition was demonstrated in work by Snel and Hayes who showed that constructing a basic value system via internal bodily information plays a pivotal role in adaptive behaviour for survival (Snel & Hayes 2008). This approach has been developed also in the context of multi-agent environments. For example, Strannegård and colleagues, attempted to simulate ecosystems involving multiple learning agents (Strannegård et al. 2022). In a recent study, Strannegård and colleagues trained RL agents with homeostasis-based rewards in complex terrains. They reported that these trained agents exhibit higher survival performance compared to agents controlled directly by humans (Strannegård et al. 2024).

These various approaches to homeostasis are connected to research on embodied autonomous intelligence which has progressively advanced from early conceptual and mathematical models to simulation-based studies, and more recently to its implementation into robotics. Deep neural networks are poised to build correspondences between internal homeostatic data and external sense data in relation to autonomous agents acting in the world.

4.2 Robotics

As fascinating as they are, artificial neural networks, despite being linked to homeostatic states, are restrained to limited, computer-based environments. There is however a whole new world outside the computer box and screens. Hence some researchers have dedicated their efforts to creating artificial agents that act in the world, with focus on the physical body and its agentic, autonomous interactions with the environment (Di Paolo 2000; Pfeifer & Iida, 2004; Pfeifer & Bongard, 2006).

Early in the 1950s, the Machina Speculatrix was famously created by William Grey Walter, an American neurophysiologist and cybernetics researcher (Walter 1953). The machine can recharge itself: if the battery voltage dropped, the response of the light sensors shifted, which prompted the robot to be attracted to the light source on the charging station to ensure autonomous recharging.

Later along the road, Ogata and Sugano (1996) developed the WAMOEBA series of robots aimed at selfpreservation via a self-preservation evaluation function that estimates the probability of physical failure based on inputs such as battery voltage, light intensity, travel distance, and sound level. Specific sensory inputs are updated through interaction with the environment, which prompts changes in the robot's behavior. Importantly, emotion modelling and human-robot interaction (Ogata & Sugano 1997) were implemented as key parameters: a virtual endocrine system was installed, influenced by the output of the self-preservation evaluation function, which in turn prompted change in behaviours in response to internal states.

An important target was to create artificial agents that can regulate their own flexible responses to environmental cues based on abstract internal variables (Parisi 2004). For example, the so called "the Cyber Rodent project"

(Doya and Uchibe 2005) explored the evolution of neural network controllers to support 'mating' and 'foraging' behaviour of robots that seek out conspecifics and battery packs in the environment. Specifically, in the simulation experiments making explicit reference to homeostasis, phototactic robots used 'neural plasticity' to restore adaptive behaviour following visual field inversion (Di Paolo 2000). Another example of behaviour-based robots, inspired by Grey Walter's tortoises (Walter 1950) has been seminally developed by Rodney Brooks with a focus on the embodiment of the agents ( 1991). In Brooks' view, it was essential to consider the link between the fact that the AI system had a physical body in continuous interaction with the environment-as a key component of intelligent behaviour. More recently, Lipson (2007), has consolidated this approach into the evolution of robots that model their own morphology to execute behavioural goals.

Biologically based behaviours in-the-world however are deeply motivated by internal and affective states of the biological systems. Hence, recent work expanded towards including emotional processing in the making of artificial bodies.

4.3 Affectivity and soft robots

Influential work linking fundamental features of living organisms and embodied robotics has been developed by thinkers within the enactive framework such as Di Paolo (2003). Building upon Hans Jonas's (1966) idea that metabolism is the crucial distinction between life and non-life, Di Paolo's work on optimized recurrent neural networks supports Maturana and Varela's theory of autopoiesis environments (Iizuka & Di Paolo 2007).

Autopoietic theory posits that living systems possess a self-referential property by which they define and reproduce themselves as a minimal characteristic of life (Maturana & Varela 1980). In this respect, embodied autonomous intelligence may contribute to biologically inspired emotion models. even if this may endanger its inner circuitry and crash its functioning. In short, the current Roomba system does not care if it exists or not. As Man and Damasio note, "without a biological framework, sensory processing that is not attached to a vulnerable body 'makes no sense'" (2019: 447).

However smart, creating a soft body does not intrinsically solve the "self-care" problem, or how to maintain homeostatic self-regulation within viable constraints. The vulnerability inherent to the softness of the body is not related to the degree of "fragility" of the material that makes the body. Rather the vulnerability of the living body is related to the vulnerability inherent to the openness and sharedness that characterize the living organisms related to their world. Living systems are not vulnerable because they are soft; organisms are vulnerable because they care about themselves and their connections to the world and others.

In this respect, one strategy is to consider top-down cognitive abilities, the "thinking" side. That is, one would implement statistical machine learning and deep learning algorithms for the creation of abstract representations.

These new computational techniques are specifically designed to propel maps of the inner and outer worlds into focus and use them reliably and flexibly to navigate uncertain environment (dust versus broken glass on the floor).

This approach also requires multisensory information processing (Tsakiris 2017), i.e., the ability to integrate information across sensory modalities, including exteroception, interoception and proprioception, linking a system's internal homeostatic states with its external perceptions and behaviour.

It has been proposed to start with life organisation (i.e. the body) as guiding fundamental design in making artificial agents, i.e., bioengineering. The so-called biorobotics and materials synthetic biology are relatively young disciplines, with steady progress in the past decades (Ayers 2018; Tang et al. 2021; Ricotti et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2020; Mestre et al 2021; Blackiston et al. 2023). The biohybrid systems, which integrates conventional or soft robotic materials with engineered cells (Ricotti et al. 2017) combines elements of bioengineering, stem cell biology, molecular biology, computer science, engineering, neuroscience, and robotics. In recent years, robots were built from unconventional components such as cardiac powered biohybrid designs (Sakar et al. 2012; Morimoto et al. 2018), pinecone and oat seed robots driven by hygromorphic actuators (Tamaru et al. 2020; Ochi & Matsumoto 2021), liquid droplets (Cejkova et al. 2017; Fan et al. 2020) and a light-driven Caenorhabditis elegans RoboWorm (Dong et al. 2021).

Developmental biologists traditionally refer to the concept of "animal cap" (Green 1999), a region of the developing frog embryo that gives rise to epidermis and neural tissue in the native system (see Blackiston et al. 2023 for a recent discussion). This small piece of ectoderm, if removed from early frog embryos, will mature to form a ball of skin covered in motile cilia, which can push the ball forward through water. Initial work reported several biohybrid robots, using a combination of synthetic scaffolds and cardiac muscle-based actuation to generate forward locomotion (Webster et al. 2016; Sakar et al. 2012; Kriegman et al. 2020). Importantly, in addition to self-preservation and movement, through the manipulation of these simple stereotyped behaviors the team was able to generate kinematic self-replication (Kriegman et al. 2021). Levin and colleagues developed the so-called, xenobots, i.e. biological machines (Blackiston et al. 2021; Kriegman et al. 2021) that are self-powered, self-driving, self-repairing, self-replicating. The material is Xenopus cells and the right shape is the one which, in computer simulations, maximized the likelihood of generating an interesting non-random behavior, such as forward locomotion.

Importantly, ''xenobots'' are autonomous: they are able to maintain their structure (and thus function) without human intervention and they exhibit a diverse array of behavioural repertoires. They are also adaptive: xenobots can repair their structure after significant damage. As Levin notes, if biological robots are one day built out of cells harvested from the African elephant (Loxodonta) instead of the African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis), then perhaps they will be called ''loxobots'' instead of ''xenobots.'' Indeed, developing frog eggs can survive in fresh water without food. By contrast, mammalian-derived robots such as loxobots would need special media with growth factors and nutrients and precisely tuned CO 2 levels. Accordingly, environment is key in creating bioinspired artificial agents such as chimeras (mixtures of species) and biohybrids (mixtures of living and artificial materials). Regardless of whether these agents are composed of cells, steel, or silicone, their embodiment as dynamic result of constant exchanges with the environment remains key in the design of the future useful technologies (Dreyfus 1967).

5 Becoming Hybrids

So far, we have discussed research on creating human brain-inspired neural networks for artificial intelligence as well as bio-inspired artificial bodies. Rapid technological advances promise that our future daily lives will be richly populated with artificial Pinocchios autonomously acting in the world, out-and-about and bursting with superhuman energy and performing actions drastically more sophisticated than the modest cleaning Roomba. Creating artificial agents per se is an interesting endeavour, yet interacting with them is perhaps the most important part of the journey. After all, Geppetto made Pinocchio to palliate his loneliness and for company. The interactive social aspect is a fundamental component in making artificial agents.

Interactions with artificial agents are poised to become ubiquitous in our modern world, occurring very early in development and stretching across the lifespan. Now, one may wonder whether AI systems or biorobots will become at some point soon conscious agents, and having experiences. This question is currently heatedly debated in the field (Chalmers 2023) and lies beyond our scope here.

Rather we propose to turn the investigatory lens / telescope around towards the human side of the equation. Let's have a closer look at us, humans, the mysterious makers of Pinocchio AIs and robots. What type of hybrid agencies are we co-constructing in our daily interactions with these new types of minds and bodies? What is the effect of interacting with artificial minds and bodies on the human mind and body? By interacting with artificial others, will these artificial agents become more like us, or us like them? Or both? And if the future is entirely hybrid, to what extent will the boundary between biological and artificial life become indistinguishable?

As we mentioned in the Introduction, two claims are proposed here which need to be considered necessarily in tandem, as two faces of the same coin.

First, all artificial agents are in a fundamental sense hybrid because they are made by us, humans (i.e. biological systems). By being an extension of our cognitive and embodied capacities, all artificial minds and bodies contain tacitly, at their very core, the biomimetic design principles that human agents used to implement at various levels within the AI and (bio)robotic architectures. This claim states basically that artificial agents do not burst into existence magically, as external creatures independent of the human mind. We don't wake up in the morning to find computers or LLMs in a forest the same way we find microbes under the microscope. Human thinkers had to work hard to test and implement the best principles to make these artificial agents pop into existence. To use a metaphor: consider an archer sending an arrow. Once the arrow is released towards its target and flies in the air, one may be tempted to forget about the archer and the bow she used to send the arrow out there in the world.

Yet, no matter how sophisticated the artificial arrows our technologies will produce, no matter how far they will reach, and no matter how autonomously they will adapt to hit their targets,foot_0 the archer's cognitive and embodied make-up remains a constitutive part of the entire trajectory, and it would be a mistake to ignore it.

The second important claim here is that interacting with artificial agents will ultimately change our minds and bodies deeply, transforming us into hybrid creatures. Hence the question whether AIs and (bio)robots will gradually become more human needs to be addressed in tandem with the equally valid counterpart. Aren't we humans, going to become more artificial as we gradually replace our daily human-to-human interactions with artificial others? Why is this important?

Because living systems such as human organisms are not fixed entities nor set in stone, but rather porous, vulnerable and flexible systems. Human agents are deeply and sometimes irreversibly transformed by the machines they interact with, regardless of whether the latter are perceived as being experiential, conscious agents. For example, in the 1936 classic film "Modern Times" featuring the famous Charlie Chaplin actor one can see how the agentic behaviour of the main character is transformed by the interactions with the gears and mechanisms of the machine. Yet, no debates were held in 1936 around gear/machine consciousness. The important aspect here is to keep the focus on how the human side of the agentic equation is affected by interactions with a machine or artificial agent, especially in early development and on regular basis across the lifespan. This is currently an open question which needs to be addressed systematically and rigorously at both theoretical and empirical levels.

As we saw earlier, living systems such as human organisms are fundamentally open systems, continuously shaped and constituted by exchanges and interactions with the external world. What is the effect of interacting with artificial agents (embodied or disembodied) on the human sense of self and agency? After all, Geppetto created Pinocchio because he wanted to feel better and less lonely, and not as some maladaptive self-defeating behaviour. We learn about ourselves and the world, from the outset, not just by observing, but by interacting with it. This idea has been demonstrated by a robust body of work in developmental psychology. For example, Sommerville and colleagues (2005, 2010) seminally demonstrated that 3-month-olds focus on the relation between an agent and her goal if they reached for (and not merely looked at) a toy before observing another actor grasping it. Also 10-month olds who received active training in pulling a cane to retrieve a toy subsequently registered another person's cane-pulling action as goal-directed behaviour, while infants who underwent mere observational training were unable to do this. Evidence has showed that infants' understanding of others is more robust within interactive contexts (Moll & Kadipasaoglu 2013). In other words, the more engaged the interactions infants'/agents' are the more robust the infants' understanding of others becomes. Agentic goal-relatedness is differently perceived by infants in social versus physical event configurations (Woodward et al. 2001 , Sommerville & Woodward 2010).

Studies using looking-time measures have revealed a difference between understanding passive visual (lookerobject) versus active, manual (actor-object) relations in infants (Woodward 1998 (Woodward , 2003)). Similarly, fMRI evidence is accumulating that infants' brain organization in typically developing children may be well-adapted to be an "intersubjective system" (Trevarthen & Aitken 2001; 1997 , Tzourio-Mazoyer et al. 2002). The importance of early bi-directional affective and imitative interaction sequences and their characteristic contingent 'protoconversational' turn-taking structure has been well documented in developmental studies (Beebe et al., 1997 , Jaffe et al. 2001).

The importance of the online interactive dynamics between infants and caregivers have been demonstrated in numerous studies (Reddy 2008.) More recently, Yiu and colleagues (2024) have explored the differences between infants and AI systems.

They described artificial intelligence (AI) models such as LLMs as cultural technologies that enhance cultural transmission and can be viewed as efficient and powerful imitation engines. Specifically the authors explored what AI models can tell us about imitation and innovation by testing whether they can be used to discover new tools and novel causal structures, contrasting their responses with those of human children. They argue that: "Children do learn through cultural transmission and statistical generalizations from data. But human children also learn in very different ways. Although we do not know the details of children's learning algorithms or data, we do know that, unlike large language and language-and-vision models, children are curious, active, self-supervised, and intrinsically motivated.

They are capable of extracting novel and abstract structures from the environment beyond statistical patterns, spontaneously making over hypotheses, and generalizations and applying these insights to new situations". (Yiu et al. 2024:7).

The important question here is whether these features of curiosity, active exploration, self-supervised and intrinsic motivation will be modified in the future generations of human children that are raised with and by artificial caregivers or caregivers who are already immersed in AIs environments from the outset of their lives. It remains an open question to what extent humans' cognitive abilities and sensory embodiment will be affected by regular interactions that drastically change not only the dynamics of these daily exchanges, but also their multisensory nature.

As we have mentioned earlier, there is significant evidence that human learning is more robust within embodied interactive contexts. Moreover, there is growing interest in highlighting the inherent multisensory and proximal nature of perceptual experiences as they unfold throughout the lifespan (Ciaunica and Fotopoulou, 2017; Faivre et al., 2017; Fotopoulou and Tsakiris, 2017; Seth and Tsakiris, 2018; Ciaunica and Crucianelli, 2019). Humans have proximal and "direct sensible acquaintance" (James, 1890) with others' bodies (via skin-to-skin interactions) well before they meet others' minds (via face-to-face and eye gaze interactions) or screen based visual interactions (Ciaunica and Fotopoulou, 2017; Ciaunica et al., 2021). Importantly, in regard to the latter, for example, a study of 622 participants found that increased use of digital distal media-based activities and online social e-meetings correlated with higher feelings of depersonalisation (Ciaunica et al. 2022). Depersonalisation is a condition that makes people feel detached from their self and body, and importantly, emotionally numb (Sierra & Berrios 1998; Sierra 2019). 5 The experience of a "split" between the self and the body-strikingly described as feeling trapped in one's head (mind) and outside of one's body (Ciaunica et al., 2020)-is one of the most frequently cited symptoms in DPD (Sierra and David, 2011). The study by Ciaunica and colleagues points to potential risks related to overly visual, sedentary, and hyper-digitalised lifestyle habits that may induce feelings of living in one's 'head' (mind), disconnected from one's body, self and the world.

Researchers have considered gradually introducing developmental and interactive aspects into the design of their artificial agents. In short, they have started to consider seriously the hypothesis that in order to understand how Geppetto's mind functions one may need to go back to baby Geppetto's embodiment and its proximal social and physical environment (Pfeifer & Iida, 2004; Pfeifer & Bongard, 2006; Reynolds & Roth, 2018; Ciaunica 2019).

It has been suggested that artificial intelligences and autonomous robots grounded in neuroscientific and physiological principles may be less prone to antisocial behaviour, as they may share embodied value systems analogous to those of humans (Christov-Moore et al. 2023). For example, Kuniyoshi's (2019) approach in cognitive developmental robotics proposed that human morality is fundamentally rooted in the body itself, including bodily morphology and internal dynamics. Another study revealed that even though evidence-based algorithms more accurately predict the future than do human forecasters, when humans are deciding whether to use a human forecaster or a statistical algorithm, they often choose the human forecaster ( Dietvorst et al. 2015).

More recently, a study looked at the relationship between expert-fine-tuned Gen-AI-powered chatbot, Therabot and mental health treatment of adults with major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, or at clinically One might think that the real interesting achievement would be to create an agent that can do stuff in the world without the constant invisible intervention of the puppeteer, pulling the strings from behind the scenes. After all, the real challenge for AI designers, industry and engineers is to make a puppet Pinocchio acting autonomously in the world, while Geppetto relaxes and drinks a coffee. But an even more important achievement would be to create artificial agents that complement human agency -systems that can work with Geppetto rather than instead of him. To achieve this task, it should be apparent that focusing on cognitive abilities and neural networks is not enough. One has to consider the body and its constant interactions with the environment. Indeed, the propensity to analyze minds based on purely abstract models unrelated to the life of the body, of the social/physical environment, has been vigorously challenged. To construct artificial others, one has thus to start with the developing body, with focus on its self-preservation mechanisms. Importantly and perhaps paradoxically, autonomous and adaptive interactions with an unpredictable world necessarily depend on the degree of openness and sharedness to the world and others.

6 Conclusion

In this paper we argued that the question of how to make artificial systems more human-like needs to be addressed in tandem, as two sides of the same coin, with the question: to what extent do interactions with artificial others make humans less human and more artificial? We built upon the observation that the distinction between mind and body permeates the modern history of thought and by extension the creation of artificial systems that think and perform actions like us in the world. Humans build artificial minds and bodies by taking as inspirational muse their own minds and bodies. Hence, in this sense, all human-initiated and human-made artificial systems are hybrid systems and thus hybrid agents, precisely because they depend upon humans for their ontological existence. Pinocchio may disobey and "act out" capriciously by following his own mind and agency, yet Geppettolike abilities may still partly and tacitly be present in Pinocchio in the form of underlying ontology and cognitive make up. Perhaps more importantly, we also argued that we ourselves become hybrid through our interactions with our artificial creations. Indeed, it is not only the passive absorption of the cognitive content that matters, nor the mere sophistication of the conversational exchanges with AI agents or embodied interactions with robots. The very nature of interaction itself matters too, which highly depends on the development and the situated embodiment of the co-actors (human or artificial).
