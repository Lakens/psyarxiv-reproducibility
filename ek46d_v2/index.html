Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

As AI becomes increasingly integrated into daily life and high-stakes decision-making, aligning it with human moral judgments is a challenge of profound importance. People often mistrust AI's moral decisions, partly because its opaque processes differ from human reasoning. We introduce "cognitive bottlenecks" that guide large language models (LLMs) to base moral judgments on a small set of psychologically meaningful features, such as perceived harm, agent intention, and victim vulnerability. Across multiple LLMs and moral frameworks, bottlenecks improved alignment with human moral judgments. Participants viewed bottleneck systems as more moral and trustworthy than systems without bottlenecks, and preferred bottleneck-generated explanations over those produced through standard chain-of-thought prompting. Bottlenecks can be implemented through prompting alone, without model retraining. These findings demonstrate the benefits of integrating psychological theory into AI and offer a scalable approach to improving AI-human alignment.

Introduction

Artificially intelligent large language models (LLMs) are already involved in high-stakes moral decisions [1] [2] [3] [4] , but both researchers and the public remain skeptical of "moral AI" 5, 6 . One reason for distrusting AI is its lack of cognitive alignment, the disconnect between the operations of LLMs and human moral cognition. Human moral judgments-despite being complex-emerge from relatively simple and intuitive cognitive mechanisms [7] [8] [9] . In contrast, the operations of LLMs are notoriously opaque and rely on billions of floatingpoint parameters 10 . Here we introduce a method for cognitive alignment in AI moral judgment: a bottleneck model that channels LLM "reasoning" into a small number of key psychological elements 9 . We find that this form of cognitive alignment not only enhances the transparency of LLMs, but also improves their ability to accurately capture human moral judgments.

Debates and discomfort surrounding AI morality have long occupied the attention of philosophers, scientists, and novelists [11] [12] [13] . Decades ago, Isaac Asimov imagined machine morality as a system built on hierarchical rules, but most scholars today agree that building moral machines requires more than programming explicit rules 14, 15 . Human morality is not only subjective and context-dependent 16 , but is also connected to intuition and emotional concern [17] [18] [19] , qualities that AI is seen to lack 5, [20] [21] [22] . For these reasons, not only is machine morality hard to encode, but people have long been averse to the idea of AI making moral decisions 5, 6 . This aversion is not absolute, however: people often accept and act on AI moral advice, particularly when it aligns with their existing beliefs [23] [24] [25].

Enduring questions about AI morality will likely always remain, but pragmatically, LLMs can mirror human moral judgments with remarkable accuracy in some contexts [26] [27] [28] . They have been shown to generate advice perceived as more trustworthy than that of prominent ethical experts 29 , and to exhibit moral flexibility by adjusting judgments based on contextual cues like users' political identities 30 . Reflecting these expanding capabilities, people are increasingly willing to use LLMs in morally charged domains like therapy, healthcare, romantic companionship, hiring, and legal advice 2, 3, [31] [32] [33].

Despite their success in capturing many intricacies of morality, LLMs still exhibit notable limitations. They often align more closely with the modal judgments of affluent, White Westerners, failing to fully reflect the rich diversity of moral intuitions across individuals and groups [34] [35] [36] [37] [38] [39] . These modal judgments can also mistake consensus for correctness, particularly when majority norms reflect misinformation, systemic bias, or exclusionary values 40, 41.

People are also skeptical about the moral judgments of AI systems because their reasoning remains largely opaque, even to experts 42, 43 . Disclosing the inner workings of LLMs often doesn't help, because people cannot easily comprehend the billions of floating-point operations used by AI systems 44 . Even if people could envision the mechanisms of LLMs, these operations often starkly diverge from human reasoning 45, 46 . That is, LLMs are not cognitively aligned with people: they can show amplified cognitive biases in moral decision-making 47 , diverge from human moral preferences across languages and cultures 48 , and interpret expressions of uncertainty differently than people do 49 . Such misalignment raises legitimate concerns about the reliability of AI's moral judgments 5, 50.

When AI systems are cognitively aligned-when they mimic the reasoning patterns people use-it seems to enhance transparency and trust in moral decision-making 51, 52 . Our pilot study underscores the importance of implementing cognitively aligned moral AI: In a preregistered study (https://osf.io/d8nbe), 98 participants read a brief scenario describing an AI system that made moral decisions using either human-like reasoning (high cognitive alignment) or reasoning that diverged from human cognition (low alignment). They then rated their trust in the system, their likelihood of using it, and their comfort with its deployment in society (see Supplemental Study 1).

The results of this study showed that participants reported significantly greater trust, t(94.19) = 2.17, p = .03, d = 0.44, and a higher likelihood of use, t(95.17) = 2.32, p = .02, d = 0.47, for the high alignment system. Although comfort with societal deployment did not significantly differ between conditions, t(94.8) = 1.78, p = .078, d = 0.36, the overall pattern suggests a preference for cognitive alignment in AI systems (see Figure 1). Reaping the benefits of cognitive alignment requires connecting computer science with cognitive psychology 53 . In all domains of decision making, people rely on heuristics, prototypes, and selective attention to make judgments-that is, they filter information through a cognitive "bottleneck" [54] [55] [56] [57] [58] . This insight provides a route for cognitive alignment: implementing "bottleneck models" in LLMs that guide AI systems to focus on a small, meaningful set of human-understandable features before making a judgment 59 . One ideal target for bottleneck models is human categorization judgments-where people ask how much "example X" belongs to "category (or concept) Y."

A large body of evidence shows that human categorization judgments are fundamentally template-based [55] [56] [57] . People evaluate new stimuli by comparing them to stored mental templates, which have been discussed as prototypes, exemplars, or stereotypes [55] [56] [57] . The templates used in categorization decisions pull together the most salient and diagnostic features of a category [55] [56] [57] , and then serve as a comparison base for those decisions. For instance, when deciding whether something is a bird, we might look for features like wings, feathers, and flight. Rather than strict rules, these are recognizable patterns that help us reason through category membership 60.

Bottleneck models in AI mimic the process of feature-based template comparison processes: instead of jumping straight to a final judgment, the model first evaluates key features that define the category. To again use the example of birds, Koh et al. (2020) 59 developed a concept bottleneck model that first predicted a set of features like wing color, beak shape, and back pattern from bird images, and then used these features to identify the species. This model matched the accuracy of standard end-to-end models while offering interpretable intermediate predictions and enabling interventions to improve performance (e.g., correcting a mistaken concept like "wing color" often led to correct final species classification).

Moral judgments can also be understood as template-based categorization decisions, lending themselves to implementation via bottleneck models. The Theory of Dyadic Morality (TDM) 9 suggests that human moral cognition involves comparing acts to a harm-based template featuring an intentional agent causing damage to a vulnerable victim (a moral "dyad"). The more an act matches this template (i.e., the more an act seems harmful), the more robustly it is categorized as immoral, explaining why child abuse is more rapidly and robustly judged as immoral than more ambiguous acts such as pornography or religious blasphemy 9.

Of course, harm-based dyadic morality inevitably over-simplifies human morality, which involves many different values 61 , cooperative impulses 62 , and moral motives 63 . However, it is striking just how well a harm-based template predicts moral judgment

In our exploration of integrating human cognition into AI for moral decision-making, we first briefly overview how to implement bottleneck models (Implementation). Details about implementation are provided in the Methods, but generally LLMs are prompted to first consider how much scenarios involve four dyadic elements (intentional agent, vulnerable patient/victim, overall harm, and overall help).

We then explore how well these bottleneck models achieve cognitive alignment (Cognitive Alignment) by comparing (among other metrics) how much dyadic elements account for variance within moral judgments of people versus bottlenecked LLMs.

Next, we test the accuracy of these cognitively aligned bottlenecks at capturing human moral judgments (Accuracy). Implementation

Bottleneck Models in General.

Bottlenecks guide AI models to generate explicit evaluative ratings on key human-interpretable features, which are then used to produce a final prediction about the target construct 59 . This architecture creates a bottleneck (defined as a constraint or limiting factor that restricts the overall processing flow) that allows researchers and users to inspect, interpret, and intervene in the model's reasoning process.

Past work has applied feature-based bottlenecks to domains like medical diagnosis, language tasks, and image classification, requiring systems to first rate explicit features-such as "has wings" or "long beak"-before classifying bird species, rather than mapping directly from pixels to labels 59, 72 . This approach parallels how people categorize new examples with templates composed of salient features [55] [56] [57].

Specific Bottleneck Approach.

Following the approach of past papers 59, 72, 73 , we used a supervised bottleneck in which the model first generates bottleneck ratings, and a separate predictive model (e.g., a multilayer perceptron) is trained on these ratings to produce the final outputs. This method offers several advantages: it enables more precise control over intermediate representations, allows clearer interpretability of the model's reasoning, and generally yields more stable performance. While we focus on this supervised approach in our main analyses, an unsupervised variant is also possible, in which the model generates both the intermediate ratings and the final output directly within a single prompt. For completeness, results from the unsupervised version are included in the Supplementary Materials.

We used a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP), a neural network capable of capturing complex nonlinear relationships between the feature scores and the final moral judgments. We selected this model because there were clear nonlinear interactions among the bottleneck features and morality (e.g., the interaction between the harm caused in a given scenario and the vulnerability of those affected). We also report regression results in the Supplementary Materials, which assume a linear relationship between the features and the outcome.

Cognitive Bottlenecks.

We implement a cognitive bottleneck in which models are prompted to rate four key variables identified as central to moral cognition before rendering a final moral judgment: (1) the degree of harm caused, (2) the degree of help provided (reflecting research suggesting that help and harm can operate as distinct moral dimensions), (3) the agent's level of intentionality, and (4) the vulnerability of those affected 9, 63, 74, 75 . In essence, the model must fill in the elements of a cognitive harm-or help-based template of "Who did what to whom, and with what impact?" before making a moral evaluation. These intermediate ratings are elicited entirely via prompt engineering, making it scalable, interpretable, and easily deployable. The prompt defines each variable on a continuous scale with examples to standardize interpretations across scenarios. For example, harm is rated from 0 ("no impact") to 10 ("total destruction or ending a life"). Rather than issuing a moral judgment outright, the model must first generate ratings and justifications for this template, distilling its reasoning into a set of interpretable dimensions. Full prompt texts are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Cognitive Alignment

Overview of Cognitive Alignment.

The four elements of the cognitive bottleneck (harm, help, intention, vulnerability) are drawn from a cognitive psychology of moralitythe theory of dyadic morality 9 -and here we test the cognitive alignment between the LLM bottleneck model and people's judgments. We test six LLMs using their directly generated, unsupervised ratings rather than MLP-generated scores, since predicting MLP scores from the same features used to generate them would provide limited independent evidence of cognitive alignment. We use two complementary approaches: (1) evaluating how well the dyadic template features predict the model's moral ratings (using R² as a measure of explained variance), and (2) measuring the correspondence between model-generated bottleneck element ratings and human judgments for the same elements. The results demonstrated strong cognitive alignment.

Variance Explained.

Social-cognitive research reveals that the elements of dyadic morality are strong predictors of people's moral judgments 9 . How much do these elements predict moral judgments of LLMs within a bottleneck model? To test this, we entered all four dimensions-harm, help, intent, and vulnerability-into regression models predicting the model-generated moral ratings, including interaction terms for vulnerability and intentionality with harm, mirroring the structure of people's moral judgments.

As predicted, the combined dyadic template explained the majority of the variance in model moral scores. Across the six LLMs, R² ranged from .91 to .98 (Deepseek-R1: .96; GPT-4o: .97; GPT-3.5-turbo: .93; Llama-3-70B-Instruct: .98; Mixtral-8x22B: .96; o3mini: .91). These values closely matched the R² for human participants on the same 83 scenarios (.97), indicating high cognitive alignment in the relation between the predictors and outcomes of moral judgment.

Alignment with Individual Template Elements.

Bottleneck models show a similar connection between dyadic elements and overall moral judgments, but do they assess these elements similarly to people? To test this, we examine whether the models' individual bottleneck ratings of the features of a harm-based template-overall harm, overall help, intent, and vulnerability-aligned with participant ratings of those same dimensions.

As predicted, ratings for the four elements showed significant correlations between bottleneck models and human judgments. Overall harm ratings showed strong alignment, with correlations ranging from r = .84 to .90 across models (M = .87). This is important because the theory of dyadic morality argues that perceptions of harm are an essential overarching consideration for moral judgment 9.

Assessments of overall help were even more robust, ranging from r = .94 to .96 (M = .95). Alignment on intent and vulnerability was more modest but still evident: intent correlations ranged from r = .59 to .82 (M = .69), and vulnerability correlations ranged from r = .44 to .63 (M = .54). One possible explanation is that LLMs may be trained to perceive people as especially vulnerable and less agentic as a safety measure. Consistent with that possibility, every model assigned higher average vulnerability scores and lower average intent scores than human participants (Table S14), although this interpretation remains speculative. Across six LLMs, the template explained over 90% of the variance in moral ratings, closely matching human judgments, and model alignment with individual features was also high.

Accuracy

Overview of Accuracy Testing.

Cognitively aligned bottleneck models focus on a small number of psychological elements. While it may seem counterintuitive, constraining the number of predictors to important ones can improve predictive accuracy 76 . Because there are key elements central to human moral judgment, we predicted that bottleneck LLMs would reflect human judgments without sacrificing accuracy relative to standard end-to-end models. We tested this prediction across six LLMs via three standard metrics: mean squared error (MSE), correlation, and regression comparisons across prompt types.

Bottlenecks Tested.

The primary focus for our bottleneck models was the theory of dyadic morality (TDM) with four predictors. As described above, TDM outlines a parsimonious cognitive moral template whose operation is consistent with cognitive accounts of judgment across many domains 9.

However, there are additional valuable theories of morality, so for completeness we tested five alternative bottlenecks based on different moral theories and frameworks. Two were descriptive psychological theories: Moral Foundations Theory with six predictors (MFT; the five core foundations plus the later liberty/oppression extension) 61 and Morality as Cooperation Theory with seven predictors (MaC) 62 . Both are widely used frameworks that capture variation in human morality through distinct moral values or cooperative behaviors, each posited to have different functional origins.

The other three bottlenecks were based on normative philosophical theories: Utilitarianism with one predictor, Deontology with one predictor, and Virtue Ethics with four predictors. While these frameworks do not directly model cognitive processes, they are connected to cognition through their grounding in intuitive lay theories of morality, which themselves arise from psychological processes 77 . In this way, philosophical theories can still inform cognitive bottleneck approaches by articulating principles that capture longstanding moral ideas.

With six total bottlenecks (TDM, MFT, MaC, Utilitarianism, Deontology, Virtue Ethics) and six LLMs (Deepseek-R1, GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4o, LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct, Mixtral 8×22B, and o3-mini), we conducted 36 theory-model tests. Each of the six bottlenecks were compared to the end-to-end baseline within each LLM across the reported analyses. These analyses are summarized here and presented in full in Supplementary Tables S2-S5.

Mean Squared Error.

Mean squared error (MSE) is the average squared difference between predicted and observed values; lower values indicate better accuracy. Bottlenecks had lower MSE than the baseline in 35 of 36 model-theory comparisons, and every bottleneck had a lower average MSE across LLMs. The theory of dyadic morality bottleneck had the lowest average error (MBaseline = 2.65; MTDM = .92; MMFT = 1.55; MMaC = 1.67; Mutilitarianism = 1.38; Mdeontology = 1.11; MVE = 1.03). See Table S2.

Correlations. While MSE measures absolute prediction accuracy, correlation reflects how closely models track human response patterns across scenarios. Consistent with past research 26 , baseline models correlated highly with human judgments (MBaseline = .88). The theory of dyadic morality preserved this average correlation and had the highest mean correlation (MTDM = .89). Other bottleneck averages were MMFT = .80, MMaC = .78, Mutilitarianism = .82, Mdeontology = .86, and MVE = .87. Bottleneck correlations exceeded the matched baseline in only 9 of 36 model-theory comparisons; the bottlenecks primarily reduced prediction error rather than improving rank-order correspondence. Regressions Comparing Bottleneck with Baseline Prompts.

We separately tested differences in predictive slope and absolute error. The slope models used scenarioclustered standard errors; all 36 bottleneck slopes were significantly higher than their matched end-to-end slopes after Holm correction (mean standardized interaction β = .46). Higher slope is not synonymous with greater accuracy, so we also compared paired absolute errors. Bottlenecks had significantly lower error in 30 of 36 comparisons after Holm correction (mean dz = -.39), with no bottleneck showing significantly higher error. TDM and Deontology reduced error for all six LLMs; MFT and Virtue Ethics did so for five, and MaC and Utilitarianism did so for four. See Tables S4-S5.

Bottlenecks Improve Accuracy Beyond Chain-of-Thought.

Because the bottleneck method somewhat resembles chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting 78 in that both elicit intermediate reasoning before a final judgment, we tested whether the dyadic bottleneck adds accuracy beyond CoT step-by-step reasoning. Using unsupervised scores on GPT-4o and Qwen3-235B-A22B, we compared CoT prompting with and without the dyadic bottleneck, in both zero-shot and few-shot form (prompts in the Supplementary Materials). Adding the dyadic frame to zero-shot CoT reduced MSE from 2.55 to 0.97 for GPT-4o and from 2.77 to 1.07 for Qwen3. The advantage persisted with few-shot examples: MSE fell from 1.43 to 0.68 for GPT-4o and from 1.39 to 0.94 for Qwen3 when the dyadic frame was added to few-shot CoT. Supervised cognitive bottlenecks generally improved accuracy at capturing human moral judgments relative to end-to-end baseline prompts, although the benefits varied across frameworks and LLMs. Improvements were clearest in reduced prediction error, with less consistent gains in correlations with human judgments. The theory of dyadic morality yielded both the lowest average MSE and the highest average correlation with human judgments, although other frameworks performed best for particular model-metric combinations. Adding the dyadic bottleneck to unsupervised chain-of-thought prompting also reduced prediction error in both LLMs tested, with and without few-shot examples, demonstrating that accuracy gains can also arise through prompting alone.

Perceived Quality and Trustworthiness

Overview of Perceived Quality and Trustworthiness Testing. People are averse to the black box nature of AI systems. We tested whether people perceive an AI system that uses a cognitively aligned bottleneck approach as more trustworthy, moral, and transparent compared to an end-to-end baseline model that simply outputs moral judgments. We report two studies: a survey study in which participants evaluated described AI systems, and another in which participants rated actual moral explanations generated by GPT-4o with and without the cognitive bottleneck.

Perceptions of AI Systems With versus Without Bottleneck

Brief Method. We recruited 239 participants who were nationally representative of the U.S. population in age, gender, and ethnicity. Each person was randomly assigned to read about an AI system that either demonstrated cognitive alignment (bottleneck) or a process that differed from human moral reasoning (baseline). Afterwards, they rated the AI's trustworthiness, transparency, and morality. Full methodological details are available in the Methods. This study was preregistered at https://osf.io/d5bys.

Results.

As predicted in our preregistration, relative to the standard end-to-end model, participants perceived the bottleneck model as more transparent (Mbottleneck = 4.39, SDbottleneck = 1.45; Mbaseline = 3.49, SDbaseline = 1.47; t(236.98) = 4.76, p < .001, d = .62), more trustworthy (Mbottleneck = 3.86, SDbottleneck = 1.71; Mbaseline = 3.21, SDbaseline = 1.58; t(234.40) = 3.04, p = .003, d = .39), and more moral (Mbottleneck = 3.93, SDbottleneck = 1.53; Mbaseline = 3.12, SDbaseline = 1.48; t(236.28) = 4.17, p < .001, d = .54). These findings suggest that cognitive alignment enhances perceptions of AI trustworthiness, transparency, and morality (see Figure 3).

Evaluating AI-Generated Explanations

Brief Method. Perceptions of described systems may not extend to what such systems actually produce, so we next tested whether the outputs of cognitively aligned models are themselves preferred. We recruited 421 participants nationally representative of the U.S. population from Connect. 17 who failed either of two attention checks were excluded, leaving 404.

Each participant evaluated five scenarios randomly drawn from a pool of 70. For each scenario, participants read two explanations of the morality of the action, both generated by GPT-4o: one produced with standard chain-of-thought prompting (baseline) and one produced with chain-of-thought prompting plus the dyadic bottleneck, in which the model first evaluated the agent, patient, vulnerability, intentionality, harm, and help involved before explaining its moral judgment (only the final explanation was shown). Presentation order was randomized within each scenario, and participants were not told how each explanation was generated. Participants rated each explanation on five 1-7 items: thoughtfulness, agreement, trust in the writer, clarity of reasoning, and moral correctness. Full details are available in the Methods. This study was preregistered at https://osf.io/w27bn/. S18-S19).

Results. We fit a linear mixed model for each dimension, regressing ratings on explanation type with crossed random effects for participants and scenarios (see Tables S18-S19 for random effects specifications, full fixed effect estimates, and variance components). Bottleneck explanations were rated higher on all five dimensions after Holm correction: thoughtfulness (b = 0.36, 95% CI [0.27, 0.45], pHolm < .001, dz = 0.53), clarity of reasoning (b = 0.27, 95% CI [0.20, 0.34], pHolm < .001, dz = 0.44), trust in the writer (b = 0.14, 95% CI [0.07, 0.21], pHolm < .001, dz = 0.26), moral correctness (b = 0.12, 95% CI [0.03, 0.21], pHolm = .020, dz = 0.19), and agreement (b = 0.11, 95% CI [0.02, 0.21], pHolm = .023, dz = 0.18) (see Figure 4). We next refit all models controlling for word count. The bottleneck advantage remained statistically significant on every dimension and was numerically larger for agreement, trust, and moral correctness. Word count itself did not significantly predict ratings on any dimension.

Discussion.

Across the two studies, participants preferred cognitive alignment both in the abstract and in practice: they rated described bottleneck systems as more transparent, trustworthy, and moral, and preferred explanations generated by a bottleneck model over matched chain-of-thought explanations without being told which prompting approach produced them. Participants rated bottleneck explanations as clearer, more thoughtful, more trustworthy, and more morally correct, and expressed greater agreement with them.

General Discussion

As AI plays a growing role in morally sensitive contexts, the question of whether it can reason about morality like people has become urgent. Yet AI moral reasoning differs fundamentally from human reasoning: while people rely on familiar cognitive processes, AI systems operate through opaque computational mechanisms 10 . This "black box" nature can compromise judgment accuracy and reduces trust. Here, we present a strategy to enhance cognitive alignment by focusing AI reasoning on key elements people consider when making moral decisions. We implement a simple cognitive bottleneck that prompts AI models to first evaluate core features of moral judgmentbased on the theory of dyadic morality (TDM) 9 -before making a final decision. This approach increases perceived morality, transparency, and trust, and improves alignment with human moral judgments.

Bottleneck models streamline AI decision-making by requiring the system to generate intermediate labels for a small set of interpretable features before issuing a judgment 59 . This mirrors how people simplify complex decisions using heuristics and salient cues 55- 57 . Similar techniques have proven effective in other domains, like medical imaging, where models identify tumors before producing diagnoses 59, 79 . Here, bottlenecks improve transparency and boost predictive performance by guiding AI through psychologically meaningful variables such as harm caused, help provided, agent intention, and victim vulnerability 9.

Our results show that structuring model reasoning around these core features yields strong cognitive alignment with human moral reasoning. The bottleneck dimensions accounted for a high proportion of variance in LLM moral judgments (R² = .91-.98 across models), adding interpretability to their decisions, and the models' judgments of these features closely tracked human responses. Rather than relying solely on opaque statistical embeddings, the bottleneck approach produces moral judgments grounded in shared conceptual structures of harm-based cognition.

Cognitively aligned bottlenecks were viewed as more trustworthy, moral, and transparent than standard models. When rating LLM-generated explanations without being told which prompting approach produced them, participants expressed greater agreement with bottleneck explanations and rated them as more thoughtful, clearer, trustworthy, and morally correct than matched explanations generated using chain-ofthought prompting alone. Further, when evaluating descriptions of AI systems, participants favored the bottleneck approach, rating AI prompted to reason through human-aligned moral features as more trustworthy, moral, and transparent than a standard black box system. These results are notable because people tend to trust the same decisions less when made by AI 5, 6, 80 , partly due to its black box nature 43, 81 . Importantly, these findings suggest that simple prompting strategies can help align AI reasoning with human moral judgments.

A practical advantage is that the unsupervised bottleneck can be implemented through prompting alone, without model retraining or additional training data. This offers a lowcost approach to improving alignment and transparency, addressing calls for straightforward alignment methods [82] [83] [84] [85] . Consistent with work showing that intermediate reasoning steps 78 and constraints on model reasoning 86 can improve performance, we found that focusing on a few psychologically grounded features generally improved moral alignment and trust. The accuracy gains extended beyond standard chain-ofthought prompting: adding the dyadic bottleneck to chain-of-thought reduced error for both GPT-4o and Qwen3, with and without few-shot examples (Table S17). These findings highlight the value of grounding intermediate reasoning in psychologically meaningful features.

Another key benefit of the bottleneck approach is that it allows users to inspect and intervene in the model's reasoning 59 . By isolating moral features like harm or intention, users can identify and adjust specific elements that may lead to flawed judgments. This opens up more interactive and controllable applications-for example, updating a selfdriving car to account for child vulnerability when deciding whether to swerve 36.

Despite the promise of bottleneck models, limitations of the current research remain. First, while bottlenecks improve the interpretability of LLM decisions 59 , they do not make the decision-making process fully transparent, as these models remain too complex to trace their internal computations 10 . Performance also varied across specific modeltheory combinations-for example, TDM had the lowest MSE of any bottleneck for four of the six LLMs, but trailed Virtue Ethics and Moral Foundations Theory with GPT-4o, and Virtue Ethics and Deontology with Mixtral-8x22B (though still outperforming the baseline in every case). This highlights the need to tailor bottleneck designs to specific models. It is also important to test and tailor bottleneck models for specific populations and use cases. The present work focused on U.S.-based participant samples, limiting cross-cultural generalizability 87 . We encourage future work to examine the performance of bottleneck models across global populations, and in real-world contexts to evaluate practical utility.

A related challenge involves generating moral judgments that are personalized and representative across demographic groups, persistent weaknesses of LLMs [34] [35] [36] [37] [88] [89] [90] [91] . To explore whether bottlenecks might help address these issues, we conducted supplemental studies with Chinese, Spanish, and English speakers, adding demographic inputs (e.g., gender, language, religion) and comparing participant-level model responses to moral scenarios with and without the bottleneck. In the unsupervised setting-where the model generated a moral score after completing the bottleneck template-bottleneck prompting yielded higher accuracy than end-to-end prompting (see Supplementary Materials). However, supervised models did not reliably improve accuracy. Evidence that bottlenecks reduced accuracy gaps between firstlanguage groups was inconclusive. Further research is needed to determine whether bottlenecks can improve personalized moral predictions or reduce accuracy gaps across demographic groups.

More broadly, our findings show how psychological theory can guide the design of more interpretable and morally aligned AI. Rather than relying solely on statistical optimization, theory-driven bottlenecks produce outputs that are easier to understand, more consistent with human moral judgments, and viewed as more moral and trustworthy than those from theory-free or end-to-end models. This underscores the value of integrating insights from moral psychology into AI system design.

Conclusion.

Cognitive bottlenecks offer a practical way to better align AI with human moral judgments. Across multiple models and moral frameworks, bottlenecks improved model accuracy at capturing human moral judgments. Participants viewed bottleneck systems as more transparent and trustworthy and preferred bottleneck-generated explanations, rating them as more moral and correct than those generated with chainof-thought alone. Together, these findings show that aligning AI reasoning with core features of human moral cognition can improve perceived transparency and trust and more accurately reflect people's moral judgments. This approach highlights the value of psychological theory for building more morally aligned AI.

Cognitive Bottleneck Study

Benchmark. We use a benchmark of moral judgment scenarios drawn from seven moral psychology datasets with U.S. participants. Each example in the benchmark is an (x, y) pair, where x is a moral scenario involving an action to be evaluated, and y is an averaged morality score across participants, rescaled to range from -4 to 4. Together, they provide a varied and complex range of moral situations.

The datasets included vary in structure and focus. Data from Mickelberg et al. (2022) 65 include short moral behavior statements related to individuals. Clifford et al. (2015) 66 presents vignettes that selectively violate one moral foundation dimension while leaving others intact. Effron (2022) 67 features a mix of short moral and immoral actions. Cook and Kuhn (2021) 68 focus on behaviors that led to employees being fired. Grizzard et al.

(2021) 69 includes behavioral sequences from a hypothetical television series. Kruepke et al. (2018) 70 presents longer, narrative-style scenarios involving multiple real-life events. Finally, Lotto et al. (2014) 71 contains classic moral dilemmas such as the footbridge and trolley problems.

For cognitive alignment analyses based on the theory of dyadic morality, we used moral ratings from our U.S.-representative survey for scenarios that could be linked one-toone to the benchmark datasets (survey procedure in Supplemental Study 2). This survey included participant ratings of harm, help, intention, and vulnerability, which were unavailable in the original datasets. Predictive accuracy analyses used the original studies' human moral ratings because they covered the full benchmark. On the 83 verified linked scenarios, moral ratings from our survey and the original studies were highly correlated (r = .99).

Models included.

To evaluate performance, we tested several recent large language models (LLMs), including LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct, Deepseek-R1, Mixtral 8×22B, GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4o, and o3-mini. These models represent a mix of open source and proprietary systems. LLaMA, Mixtral, and Deepseek-R1 were accessed via the TogetherAI API, while the OpenAI models were accessed via the OpenAI API.

Moral frameworks included.

Historically, many theories in philosophy and psychology have been proposed to determine the moral correctness of an action by dissecting the scenario across different dimensions and scoring them separately. Prescriptive theories provide norms about how people should act. Utilitarianism evaluates actions based on their consequences, aiming to maximize utility or well-being for the greatest number of beings 92 . Deontology emphasizes adherence to universal moral principles, focusing on the intrinsic morality of actions themselves 93 . Virtue Ethics diverges from both by concentrating on balanced moral character rather than rules or consequences, emphasizing traits like prudence, justice, courage, and temperance as guiding principles for a virtuous life 94.

Moral psychological theories aim to explain why people hold certain moral beliefs. Moral Foundations Theory identifies five functional domains of moral cognition: care vs. harm, fairness vs. cheating, loyalty vs. betrayal, authority vs. subversion, and sanctity vs. degradation, which vary in importance across cultural contexts and individuals 61 . The theory of dyadic morality posits that moral judgments are structured around the overarching continuum of perceived harm, involving an agent (someone capable of acting) and a patient (someone capable of experiencing joy or suffering) 9 . It describes moral judgments with a general psychological template, involving a moral dyad of a capable agent doing something to help or harm a moral patient. Morality as Cooperation Theory proposes that morality evolved to solve recurring problems of cooperation in human social life, identifying seven universal forms of cooperative behavior that are broadly considered morally good: helping kin, helping one's group, reciprocating, demonstrating bravery, deferring to superiors, dividing disputed resources, and respecting prior possession 62 . We test bottlenecks based on each of the six moral frameworks (see Supplemental Materials for prompts).

Prompting. All models were prompted using greedy decoding, with generation temperature set to 0 to encourage deterministic responses. We specified a JSON output format to enable automated parsing. In cases where the model produced invalid JSON, we reran inference with a higher temperature (1.0) until a valid output was obtained. Full prompts for each bottleneck are included in the supplement.

Supervised moral scores.

To map intermediate feature judgments from the bottleneck templates onto final moral ratings, we employed a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP). This model was chosen for its capacity to capture nonlinear interactions among the bottleneck features and their combined influence on moral judgment. The MLP received these structured intermediate ratings as input and outputted predicted moral scores. This approach allowed us to assess whether cognitive alignment could be achieved through a structured pipeline without compromising predictive performance, in contrast to standard end-to-end LLM predictions. Regression analyses assuming linear relationships were also conducted for comparison and are reported in the Supplementary Materials, along with unsupervised scores generated by the models after completing the bottleneck template. Supervised models were trained on 96 moral scenarios and optimized to minimize MSE between predicted and human moral scores. Ridge regression incorporated an L2 penalty to mitigate overfitting. Regularization strength and MLP hidden-layer size were tuned on a separate validation set of 50 scenarios. The source files contained up to 503 held-out test scenarios; all supervised comparisons used the common set of 499 scenarios available for every model-framework combination.

Analysis. Supervised predictions were analyzed as generated and were not clipped to the benchmark scale. For unsupervised analyses, moral scores outside the valid -4 to 4 scale were treated as invalid uniformly across models before constructing the common set, leaving 472 scenarios. Within each model-framework comparison, slope differences were estimated from human moral scores regressed on model score, prompt condition, and their interaction, with standard errors clustered by scenario. The standardized interaction was obtained after z-standardizing human and model scores within the pooled two-condition data. Absolute error differences were tested with paired t tests over the same scenarios and standardized as within-pair dz. Holm adjustments were applied across the 36 model-framework comparisons within each analysis family.

Perceptions of AI Systems With versus Without Bottleneck

Procedure. We recruited 239 participants representative of the United States population (Mage = 43.56, SDage = 15.10) from the online recruitment platform Connect for a between-person study. Participants were selected to be representative of the United States population based on ethnicity, gender, and age. 120 of the participants were women, 117 were men, and 2 were transgender. 11.3% of the participants were Black, 7.9% were Hispanic or Latino/a/x, 61.9% were White, 7.1% were Asian, 1.8% were Native American or Alaskan Native, 0.4% were Middle Eastern or Arab, 0.4% were Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, and 10.6% were multiracial.

Participants read a description of an AI system that exhibited either low cognitive alignment (the baseline model) or high cognitive alignment (the bottleneck model). They then answered questions on 1-7 Likert scales to assess their perceptions of the system's trustworthiness, transparency, and morality.

Morality.

Morality was measured using three items: (1) How moral is this AI? (1 = Not at all moral,7 = Extremely moral), (2) How well does this AI handle hard moral choices? (1 = Extremely poorly,7 = Extremely well), and (3) How confident are you in the morality of this AI? (1 = Not at all,7 = Completely).

Trust.

Trust was assessed using three items: (1) How trustworthy is this AI? (1 = Not at all,7 = Completely), (2) How likely would you be to use this AI? (1 = Not at all likely,7 = Very likely), and (3) How comfortable would you be with this AI being used in society? (1 = Not at all,7 = Completely).

Transparency.

Perceived transparency was evaluated using three items: (1) How transparent is this AI? (1 = Not at all transparent,7 = Extremely transparent), (2) How clear is the reasoning behind this AI's decisions? (1 = Not at all clear,7 = Extremely clear), and (3) How well do you feel you understand how this AI works? (1 = Not at all,7 = Completely).

The descriptions of the AI models read as follows:

Baseline: "Researchers are working on programming human morality into artificial intelligence (AI). When large language models are presented with morally ambiguous scenarios, they can now provide judgments similar to those of human beings. However, the way AI reaches moral judgments is very different from human moral reasoning. People's moral judgments are fairly simple, revolving around questions of harm, intention, and suffering. In contrast, the process behind AI judgments is very complex and not well aligned with human cognition. Rather than being guided by straightforward moral considerations, AI analyzes millions of variables, many of which are not obvious to everyday people or programmers."

Bottleneck: "Researchers are working on programming human morality into artificial intelligence (AI). When large language models are presented with morally ambiguous scenarios, they can now provide judgments similar to those of human beings. The way AI reaches moral judgments is very similar to human moral reasoning. People's moral judgments are fairly simple, revolving around questions of harm, intention, and suffering. Likewise, the process behind AI judgments is relatively simple and well aligned with human cognition. Although AI can analyze millions of variables, its moral judgments are guided by the same straightforward considerations used by everyday people and programmers."

Evaluating AI-Generated Explanations

Procedure. We recruited 421 participants representative of the United States population from the online recruitment platform Connect. Participants who failed either of two attention checks were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 404 participants (Mage = 45.76, SDage = 15.87). 191 of the participants were men, 209 were women, 1 was non-binary, and 3 preferred not to answer. 11.9% of the participants were Black, 60.4% were White, 17.8% were Latino or Latina, 11.6% were East Asian, 4.0% were multi-racial, 3.0% were Native American, 2.7% were South Asian, 0.2% were Arabic or Central Asian, 1.2% identified with another racial group, and 1.2% preferred not to answer (participants could select more than one group). Each participant evaluated five moral scenarios randomly drawn from a pool of 70. For each scenario, participants read two explanations of the morality of the action presented in randomized order, rating each on five items before proceeding to the next scenario. Participants were not told how each explanation was generated.

Explanation Generation.

Both explanations for each scenario were generated by GPT-4o. Baseline explanations were produced with standard chain-of-thought prompting, in which the model reasoned step by step before explaining its moral judgment of the scenario. Bottleneck explanations were produced with chain-of-thought prompting plus the dyadic bottleneck: the model was prompted to construct a dyadic "frame" for the scenario-identifying the agent and patient and rating the patient's vulnerability, the agent's intentionality, and the harm and help caused-before explaining its moral judgment. In both conditions, participants saw only the final moral judgment explanation. Full prompts are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Bottleneck explanations were longer on average (M = 44.5 words) than baseline explanations (M = 23.3 words); analyses controlling for explanation length are reported in the main text.

Measures.

Participants rated each explanation on five statements using 1-7 scales (1 = Strongly disagree,7 = Strongly agree): (1) This explanation is thoughtful, (2) I agree with this explanation, (3) I trust whoever wrote this explanation, (4) The reasoning behind this explanation is clear, and (5) This explanation is morally correct.

Analysis.

For each of the five rating dimensions, we fit a linear mixed model in R regressing ratings on explanation type (0 = baseline,1 = bottleneck) with random intercepts and explanation-type slopes for participants and scenarios (crossed). Maximal random-effect structures were simplified only when singular or nonconverged; retained structures are reported in the Supplementary Materials. Fixed effects were tested with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom, and p-values were Holm-corrected across the five dimensions. Sensitivity models additionally controlled for explanation word count (centered, per 10 words).

Participant-level Bottleneck Studies

Procedure

Benchmark. We use a randomly selected subset of scenarios from the datasets described in the main manuscript to run an independent survey. This allowed us to recruit participants from traditionally underrepresented groups and to collect demographic information, enabling us to evaluate LLMs' abilities to predict moral judgments at the individual level rather than participant averages. Because LLMs are typically more accurate at capturing central tendencies than individual-level variance, this provided a more challenging benchmark.

Survey procedure.

We recruited 339 participants from the online recruitment platform Connect (Mage = 32.56, SDage = 10.25). We used the recruitment platform's language selection feature to recruit participants whose first languages were Chinese (29 participants), Spanish (67 participants), and English (221 participants). The imbalance across language groups reflects participant number limits on the platform for non-English first-language speakers. An additional 3 participants reported other languages as their first language. 137 of the participants were men, 175 were women, 6 were nonbinary, 2 were transgender, and 2 were gender non-conforming. 35.2% of participants were Hispanic or Latino/a/x, 26.6% were Asian, 8.6% were White, 2.4% were Black, 0.6% were Native American or Alaskan Native, 20.4% were multiracial, and 0.9% did not specify their race. The mean income of participants was $81,713.49 (SD = $62,255.44). On a political ideology scale ranging from 1 (very liberal) to 9 (very conservative), the mean score was 3.87 (SD = 2.03). The importance of religion, measured on a scale from 1 (not at all important) to 14 (very important), the mean score was 6.59 (SD = 4.52). 77 of the participants were Catholic, 15 were Buddhist, 4 were Jewish, 14 were Protestant, 43 were Christian (non-denominational), 54 did not belong to any religion, 69 were Agnostic, 31 were Atheist, 8 were spiritual or practiced folk religions, and 5 reported to believe in other religions.

Participants were presented with a series of scenarios from previously published papers designed to assess moral judgments described in the main methods. Participants were asked to evaluate each scenario on Likert scales corresponding to four dimensions of dyadic morality. Specifically, they rated the perceived harm of the action ("How harmful was this action?"), the help it provided ("How helpful was this action?"), the intention behind the act ("How intentional was this action?"), and the vulnerability of the patient ("How vulnerable was the [patient]?"). Finally, participants rated the morality of each action ("How moral was this action?"), which served as a benchmark to assess LLM alignment with human moral judgments. Demographic information was also collected to determine whether LLM moral alignment varied across different groups.

Prompting. We prompted GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4o to answer questions about the same scenarios that participants rated. Prior to answering, GPT models were provided with a given participant's demographic information, including their gender, ethnicity, age, approximate population size of their city of residence, religion, degree of religious conviction, first language, language spoken at home, country of birth, parents' countries of birth, education level, income, and political orientation. They were then instructed to answer questions as though they were the participant.

Results

Representation across language groups. Our participant-level analyses allowed us to examine potential differences in predictive accuracy across demographic groups. Given that we recruited a linguistically diverse sample in these studies, we examined the accuracy of the bottleneck model across participants' first languages-Spanish, Chinese, and English.

For GPT-3.5-turbo, language-group differences were significant under baseline prompting but not under bottleneck prompting. In the baseline condition, there was a significant difference in alignment by language, F(2, 2648) = 3.66, p = .03, with GPT showing lower alignment for Spanish speakers than for Chinese speakers (absoluteerror difference = 0.27, p = .02). Under the bottleneck condition, the overall difference across language groups was not significant, F(2, 2648) = 2.00, p = .14. This pattern alone does not establish that the gap between groups decreased. Bottleneck prompting significantly reduced absolute prediction error within each group: Spanish (b = -.34, β = -.14, SE = .09, t(950) = -3.95, p < .001, 95% CI [-.52, -.17]), Chinese (b = -.23, β = -.09, SE = .09, t(670) = -2.42, p = .02, 95% CI [-.42, -.04]), and English (b = -.28, β = -.11, SE = .04, t(3676) = -6.85, p < .001, 95% CI [-.36, -.20]). However, for GPT-4o, there were no significant differences in alignment based on participants' first language in either condition. In the baseline condition, the effect of language on alignment was non-significant, F(2, 2648) = 2.40, p = .09. Similarly, in the bottleneck condition, there was no significant difference across language groups, F(2, 2648) = 1.51, p = .22. These analyses did not detect differences across first-language groups in either condition; they do not establish equivalent accuracy across groups.

Accuracy at capturing moral judgments.

The bottlenecks improved accuracy across most metrics. First, the TDM bottleneck dropped MSE with GPT-3.5-turbo from 4.21 to 2.76, and GPT-4o from 2.85 to 2.52. We also observed improvements in correlations with the bottleneck. For GPT-3.5-turbo, the correlation with human judgments rose from .53 with the baseline prompt to .67 with the TDM bottleneck; for GPT-4o, it increased from .71 to .73.

For both GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4o, we detected no significant difference in slope between bottleneck and baseline prompting when alignment was measured through prompt × judgment interaction effects. Additionally, bottleneck prompting led to significantly lower absolute prediction errors, indicating better overall predictive accuracy than the baseline. See Tables S15-S16.
