Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

This research was conducted on behalf of the Nationaal Huidfonds as part of the 2024-2025 skin cancer prevention project, funded by the Netherlands Ministry of Health, Welfare, and Sports (VWS; subsidy 90001168).

INTRODUCTION

The global rise in skin cancer rates presents a pressing public health concern [1]. In the Netherlands, approximately 75,000 individuals are diagnosed with skin cancer yearly, making it the most common type of cancer nationally. Incidence rates are expected to increase even further [2]. Not only does this threaten quality of life, but it also strains the healthcare system [3, 4]. The primary cause of skin cancer is exposure to ultraviolet radiation (UVR), particularly cumulative unprotected exposure and sunburns [5]. Outdoor workers, such as construction workers, roofers, and agricultural workers, are at increased risk of developing basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) due to sustained exposure to UVR by working during UVR peak moments and near reflective surfaces like water or metal [6] [7] [8] [9].

Estimates suggest that their UVR exposure is three times higher than that of indoor workers and exceeds safety limits across Europe [7, 10]. This leads to an increased risk of developing skin cancer [11, 12].

Sunburns are a preventable yet persistent risk factor among outdoor workers in Europe [13] [14] [15]. For example, nearly one in five outdoor workers in Germany reported at least one occupational sunburn in the past year, whereas in Denmark 89% of outdoor workers reported ever having had an occupational sunburn [14, 15]. Occupational sun protection behaviours among outdoor workers are often insufficient and their prevalence varies between sun protection methods (i.e., shade-seeking, covering up, and applying sunscreen) and countries [16] [17] [18] [19] [20]. These behaviours are hindered by individual determinants (e.g., low risk perceptions) and environmental determinants (e.g., workplace culture, time pressure, and a lack of shade) [19, [21] [22] [23] [24]. Workplace interventions, including role modelling, provision of personal protective equipment (PPE), and employee engagement, have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing sunburn frequency and improving sun protection behaviours and determinants [24] [25] [26].

Additionally, efforts to regulate occupational UVR exposure are made, such as the German official recognition of squamous cell carcinoma and multiple actinic keratoses as occupational diseases, which obliges employers and insurers to take preventive measures against UVR exposure [27].

While international studies provide valuable insights, their transferability to the Dutch context may be limited by differences in regulatory frameworks and occupational health policies. In the Netherlands, UVR constitutes the largest contributor to cancer cases related to occupational exposure [28], yet it is not formally recognised as an occupational risk. In 2025, the Collective Employment Agreement for the Roofing Companies mandated employers to provide personal protective equipment (UVR-protective sunglasses, (sun)caps with neck flaps, UVR-protective work clothing, and sunscreen) [29, 30], and the Netherlands Center for Occupational Diseases published a registration guideline providing practical advice for the identification, reporting, and prevention of work-related skin cancer [31]. Although these initiatives signal growing recognition of this risk, efforts remain fragmented and sector-specific.

While some employers have incorporated UVR-related guidelines within broader heat protocols and informational toolboxes, UVR protection is not systematically embedded in occupational risk assessments or national prevention strategies [31, 32]. Accordingly, there is insufficient understanding of how occupational sun protection is experienced within the Dutch outdoor work context. Such insights are essential to inform the development and implementation of effective occupational sun protection interventions.

There is a lack of data on the prevalence of sunburns and sun protection behaviours among the at-risk population of outdoor workers in the Netherlands. One study reported on sunscreen use, with only 7% of outdoor workers often or always applying sunscreen in the past month, increasing to 12% following an intervention [33]. There is limited understanding of how such behaviours are shaped within this Dutch context, specifically considering individual and environmental determinants of occupational sun protection. This constrains the development of effective prevention strategies, as interventions that do not align with community needs are unlikely to be sustainable [34].

More insights into understanding the health problem, risk population, and risk behaviours are needed [35]. Accordingly, this study aims to provide insights into the prevalence of sunburns, occupational sun protection behaviours, and the individual and environmental determinants underlying sun protection among outdoor workers in the Netherlands. A comprehensive mixed-methods needs assessment was conducted through a quantitative survey and qualitative interviews. Insights resulting from this needs assessment contribute to a better understanding of the at-risk population and build the evidence base for developing interventions that address the needs of outdoor workers within the Dutch context.

METHODS

This study conducts a systematic needs assessment aimed at understanding of a health problem, the behaviours contributing to it, and the underlying determinants among a priority population [36]. Intervention Mapping (IM) provides a stepwise approach for developing health interventions, including a needs assessment that is informed through epidemiological data that describe the impact and size of the health problem, and qualitative data that capture how the health problem is experienced by the at-risk population [36] [37] [38]. Guided by IM, the current needs assessment employed two complementary data sources. A quantitative survey is used to assess the health problem (sunburns and their consequences), the behaviours that contribute to this problem, and the underlying determinants. Qualitative semi-structured interviews add insights into the behaviours and determinants via qualitative interviews with outdoor workers. The survey enabled large-scale assessments of the health problem and behavioural patterns, while qualitative interviews allowed for deeper insights into underlying beliefs and contexts [39]. As within IM, the obtained data from the needs assessment is integrated into a Logic Model of the Problem that demonstrates the key determinants and pathways following the PRECEDE model [40]. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring voluntary participation, informed consent, and data confidentially [41]. The study was reviewed by the Research and Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health, Medicine and Life Sciences of Maastricht University (FHML-REC/2026/081/Audit).

Survey

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional study design using data from a single time point of a longitudinal survey that monitors sunburns, sun protection behaviours, and its determinants in the Netherlands. The current study involved secondary analyses on respondents who selfidentified as outdoor workers, using the most recent survey data from September 2025. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies was adhered to for reporting [42].

Sample and recruitment

The sample was randomly selected by the market research agency Motivaction B.V., using a national online panel of approximately 65,000 active members of the Dutch population (StemPunt) and complying with relevant data protection regulations [43, 44]. Panel members were recruited from eligible respondents living in the Netherlands aged between 16 and 70 years old. This was done by mail using advanced propensity methodologies to ensure inclusion of a representative sample in terms of education, age, sex, region, and socio-cultural orientations. Additionally, Dutch people with a Turkish, Moroccan, Surinamese, or Caribbean background were recruited to a number that is representative of their share within Dutch society. In total, 10,405 invitations were sent out, of which 2,726 respondents completed the questionnaire between 3 and 18 September 2025. Data cleaning procedures excluded respondents in case of deviant completion times (i.e., insufficient engagement with the questionnaire) or straightlining (i.e., selecting the same response option across multiple items without variation). Respondents were included in the current study when they self-identified as outdoor workers, based on reporting having a job that involves working outdoors between April and October.

Data collection

Demographic characteristics of the panel members were already available, including age, sex, and education. The survey was developed within a larger longitudinal project on skin cancer prevention, using constructs from the Reasoned Action Approach (RAA) [45], components of the sun exposure questionnaire by Køster et al. [46], and input from a Dutch expert panel. The questionnaire contained retrospective questions on 1) respondents' self-identification as outdoor workers; 2) sunburn frequency; 3) sun protection behaviour: extent to which sun protection measures were taken; and 4) individual determinants of behaviour: knowledge, risk perception, attitude, perceived norms, perceived behavioural control. The current study focused on the questions on outdoor workers' occupational characteristics, sunburn frequency, occupational sun protection behaviour, and individual determinants of behaviour (see Table, SDC 1, which presents the questionnaire constructs and response categories).

Occupational sun protection behaviour was assessed by the frequency of engaging in eight distinct behaviours: 1) sun avoidance between 12 and 3 PM; 2) shade-seeking; 3) wearing sun-protective clothing; 4) wearing sunglasses; 5) wearing a hat or cap; 6) application of sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher; 7) sunscreen application at least 30 minutes before sun exposure; and 8) sunscreen reapplication every 2 hours. Items were measured using 5-point Likert scales, ranging from '(almost) never' (1) to 'always' (5). Behavioural determinants were assessed through fourteen items measuring knowledge, eight items measuring risk perception (i.e., susceptibility and severity), eight items measuring attitude (i.e., importance, effort, pleasantness, appearance, and health and environmental consequences), two items measuring perceived norm (i.e., injunctive and prescriptive norm), and seven items measuring perceived behavioural control (i.e., capability and difficulty). Items were measured using 5point Likert and semantic differential scales. The full questionnaire can be found on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/3ps49/files/osfstorage/6a97c4ada6c25383078fc960).

Data analyses

Descriptive analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics [47]. Frequency tables were generated to assess sunburn frequency, occupational sun protection behaviours, and behavioural determinants. Some reversed items were included and recoded into the same (positive) direction. The analyses, beyond participant characteristics, used weighted percentages to adjust for the under-or overrepresentation of sex and age groups, aligning with the population distribution in the Netherlands.

Interviews

Design

This qualitative study employed a thematic analysis approach to provide insights into occupational sun protection behaviours and the underlying socio-cognitive beliefs. The analysis was guided by the RAA and Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), and subsequently extended with inductively derived insights [45, 48]. Individual, semi-structured interviews were conducted between November 2024 and February 2025. The Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) were adhered to for reporting [49].

Participants and recruitment

A purposive sampling technique was used to recruit participants from industries with high occupational sun exposure in the Netherlands to ensure a relevant and diverse sample. Three key sectors were identified: construction, port operations, and municipal services (e.g., parks maintenance and waste management), in line with typical outdoor jobs in the Netherlands [31].

In collaboration with the Nationaal Huidfonds [43], contact persons at relevant organisations employing outdoor workers were approached, who then further facilitated recruitment by sharing the study invitation with eligible employees. Eligible for inclusion within this study were adults aged ≥ 18 years who spoke Dutch or English, and were working outdoors for their job between April and October or had been doing this for a minimum of 5 years. The researcher contacted the individuals who were willing to take part in the study by e-mail or telephone, answered any questions about the study, and made an appointment for the interview at the participant's workplace at a suitable time. Data collection lasted until data saturation was reached.

Data collection

An interview route was developed (see Table, SDC 2, which presents the interview route translated in English), using evidence regarding behaviour change models and previous qualitative research on outdoor workers' perceptions towards skin cancer risks and prevention strategies [22, 23, 50]. The current interview route was structured around the IM needs assessment, exploring outdoor workers' behaviours related to sun protection at work, and determinants of these behaviours. These included constructs from the RAA [45] (i.e., attitude, perceived norms and perceived behavioural control) and PMT [48] (i.e., knowledge, risk perception, self-efficacy, and coping mechanisms). A pilot interview was conducted with an outdoor worker with personal experience of skin cancer, after which the interview route was improved and finalised. After providing written informed consent, all interviews were conducted in Dutch by one researcher (YS), followed by field notes describing the context and general impression. The interviewer did not know any of the participants prior to the study and there were no repeat interviews. All interviews were audio-recorded and, transcribed verbatim, after which the recordings were removed.

Data analysis

Any personal data was removed from the transcripts. A thematic analysis was performed in Atlas.ti to generate key themes, combining deductive and inductive coding to align with established behavioural determinants while allowing for the identification of new themes [51].

The principal investigator (YS) developed a code system based on iterative coding of fifteen interviews. The code system was initially informed by determinants derived from the constructs from the RAA and PMT, but during analyses extended with additional themes that were generated from the data. These generated themes related to environmental determinants, and could be interpreted according to the socioecological approach that is frequently used in behaviour change, including interpersonal, organisational, community, and societal levels in the environment of the at-risk population [52]. Two researchers (YS and ND) independently coded the remaining 25% of the interviews (n=5) to enhance consistent interpretation of the data. The assigned codes were discussed after one interview, and no substantial modifications were required. Coding of the following interview yielded satisfactory intercoder-agreement (Krippendorff's alpha = 0.8), calculated in Atlas.ti based on code application and code location (i.e., unitizing agreement), assuring mutual understanding of the codes and consistent identification [53]. Additionally, patterns in sun protection behaviours and beliefs were explored across surveys and interviews to identify potential subgroups of outdoor workers.

RESULTS

Survey

Study respondents

The survey was completed by 348 individuals working outdoors for their job between April and October. Mean age was 41 years (SD=12.6). The mean daily duration of their outdoor work was five hours and 13 minutes, including one hour and 52 minutes between the UVR peak from 12:00 to 15:00. Participant characteristics are provided in Table 1.

Sunburns

Outdoor workers reported the prevalence of sunburns in the past summer (Table 2). The majority of outdoor workers experienced one or more light (71.7%) or moderate sunburns (52.4%). One or more severe sunburns occurred in approximately one-third of the respondents.

Occupational sun protection behaviour

Outdoor workers reported the frequency of sun protection behaviours during an outdoor workday. They mostly engaged in wearing sunglasses (33.6% often or always), a hat or a cap (31.0%), and applying sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher (28.1%). In contrast, avoiding the sun between 12 and 3PM (49.2% (almost) never or sometimes), applying sunscreen at least 30 minutes before sun exposure (51.6%), and reapplying sunscreen every 2 hours (55.9%) were infrequently reported.

Behavioural determinants

Outdoor workers reported highest knowledge levels regarding the risk of skin cancer due to skin damage caused by the sun (79.3% answered correctly), but relatively low knowledge regarding the statement that sun is not stronger on a warm day than on a cool day (44.3% answered correctly). While outdoor workers perceived skin cancer as moderate to severe (M=3.90, SD=1.05), they reported low perceived severity of a single sunburn (M=2.45, SD=1.12), severity of skin ageing (MD=2.86, SD=1.09), and low vulnerability to developing skin cancer (M=3.13, SD=1.03). Outdoor workers found it moderately to highly important to protect themselves against the sun (M=3.54, SD=0.89), but reported neutral to negative attitudes regarding unpleasantness of applying sunscreen (M=2.69, SD=1.03), unpleasantness of wearing sun-protective clothing (M=2.72, SD=0.97), and the time it takes to apply sunscreen (M=2.76, SD=0.90). Perceived behavioural control towards protecting against the sun was moderate to high (M=3.51, SD=0.93), but predominantly limited by forgetting to protect against the sun (M=2.94, SD=0.92).

Interviews

Study participants

Of the 23 outdoor workers who expressed interest in participating, three dropped out due to time constraints. A total of 20 outdoor workers from eight organisations were interviewed, with interviews lasting from 20 to 60 minutes. Participants were working in construction (n=10), municipal public services (n=8), and harbours (n=2). The majority of participants were men (n=19), consistent with the gender composition of these industries in the Netherlands [56]. On average, they were 41 years old (range 27-61), had been working outdoors for 20 years (range 3-38) and spent 6.5 hours per day working outdoors (range 1-10).

Main themes

The analysis of transcripts resulted in ten main themes: sun protection behaviour, knowledge, risk perception, attitude, perceived norm, and perceived behavioural control (derived from the RAA and PMT), as well as the interpersonal, organisational, community, and societal environment (identified inductively from the data). The subthemes within these themes are presented below.

Sun protection behaviour

Participants lacked engagement in several occupational sun protection behaviours, indicating they often wear short sleeves (n=15), do not apply sunscreen (n=7), or if they do, they tend to refrain from repeated application (n=14). They engaged in some occupational sun protection behaviours; primarily shade-seeking (n=17), wearing long trousers (n=16), and wearing sunglasses (n=15). Participants' sun protection behaviours revealed three subgroups of engagement: minimal occupational sun protection (i.e., almost no use of sun protection), inconsistent occupational sun protection (i.e., some use of sun protection, but often constrained by barriers), and regular occupational sun protection (i.e., proactive use of sun protection).

Knowledge

In general, the majority of outdoor workers expressed knowing about the harmful effects of sun exposure (e.g., skin cancer risk). However, when they described their beliefs regarding sun protection behaviours, it became evident that their expressed awareness was partly based on incorrect or incomplete knowledge. This particularly included knowledge regarding sunburns (i.e., inability to recognize what a sunburn is or what its causes are), UVR exposure (i.e., inability to explain the meaning of the UVR index or the relationship between UVR exposure and skin cancer), and sun protection behaviours (i.e., inability to explain what effective sun protection methods are). It was a common misconception that sun protection was linked to temperature rather than UVR exposure: "I only use sunscreen when it is necessary; above 30 degrees Celcius." (P18)

Knowledge acquisition at the workplace appeared inconsistent; although some outdoor workers reported that information about sun protection and available protective resources was provided, interviews revealed that other outdoor workers at the same workplace did not always know about this information. Some mentioned that they do not always pay attention to such messages.

Risk perception

Most outdoor workers showed low perceived vulnerability (i.e., feeling personally at risk) and susceptibility (i.e., cognitive assessment of the risk) of experiencing sunburn and skin cancer.

On the personal level, many perceived that their skin could tolerate sun exposure and therefore felt at low risk for sunburns and skin cancer, which led to the belief that individual sun protection is unnecessary. On the occupational level, many outdoor workers did not consider their outdoor work as a factor that heightens the risk of sunburns and skin cancer as compared to other jobs or activities.

"I think that when you go shopping outside, you are as much at risk as I am when working in the sun." (P9)

Additionally, most outdoor workers reported low perceived severity of sunburns, due to the limited discomfort that sunburns cause, especially in comparison to other physical risks encountered on the job such as equipment-related accidents. They also experienced difficulties in estimating the severity of skin cancer, expressing that the condition and its consequences remained highly abstract, for example because they occur on the long-term and are often treatable. Finally, there was a high level of risk acceptance, as outdoor workers perceived occupational sun exposure as an inevitable part of their job, which they feel is also financially compensated and therefore accepted: "I love my salary, so I will take that bit of sunshine for granted."

(P13).

In general, the majority of outdoor workers expressed low perceived importance of occupational sun protection, particularly as many outdoor workers weighed occupational sun protection against competing priorities. Covering up was hindered by physical discomfort (i.e., long sleeves are hot, headwear would lead to excessive sweating), impracticalities (i.e., sunglasses reduce visibility), and the perception that protective clothing does not fit with personal or professional appearance, due to both personal preferences (i.e., certain clothing does not look good on oneself) and occupational considerations (i.e., some clothing is inappropriate for certain roles). Sunscreen use was deterred by time constraints, especially with sunscreen reapplication and early work starts, physical discomfort (i.e., dust and sand on the skin), and personal preferences of never using sunscreen in their lives, applying sunscreen only at home, or using specific types that did not always align with the availability at work: "SPF 20 is perfect for me. I don't need SPF 50, that is so thick, it doesn't work." (P7)

Perceived norm

Outdoor workers reported a collegial norm emphasising individual autonomy. This reflects a shared idea that sun protection is a matter of individual responsibility and one should not interfere with one another.

"We are all adult men; we all know what we should and shouldn't do, so it really is everyone's own responsibility to deal with it." (P18)

Perceived behavioural control

Outdoor workers experienced perceived behavioural control over their occupational sun protection behaviours in situations with job flexibility, when outdoor workers had autonomy over when, where, and how work tasks were performed, allowing them to anticipate on sun protection. Interpersonal environment

The outdoor work environment reflects shared norms emphasising resilience, in which people work hard and do not complain, including about health risks: "That is what people in construction are like: working hard, prioritizing productivity, so they don't always look out for themselves properly."

(P17)

Yet, efforts for social reinforcement of sun protection at the workplace were reported. These included occasional reminders to use sun protection, peer modelling by colleagues who demonstrated sun protection behaviours, and encouragement from employers to use or purchase sun protection equipment.

Organisational environment

Most outdoor workers indicated that there were protocols at the workplace, which encouraged safe working conditions at various temperatures and stimulated sun protection behaviours. Yet, the workplace environment did not always allow for sun protection due to the lack of sun protection equipment, for example shade structures or appropriate sunprotective clothing. Sun protection behaviours often unintentionally resulted from safety measures that prompted outdoor workers towards intentional wearing of protective clothing to protect against occupational hazards.

Community environment

Financial constraints in outdoor work sectors limited organisations' capacity to support sun protection, because of insufficient budgets for protective equipment and health education, and little flexibility to allow employees time to engage in sun protection behaviours.

Societal environment

The engagement in sun protection behaviours was hindered by societal aesthetic norms that value having a tanned skin, which contributes to the normalisation of UVR exposure. Several outdoor workers recognized this desire among themselves and others: "I think it's quite common that people prefer having a tan rather than not."

(P13)

Subgroups of sun protection behaviour

The interviews showed variation of occupational sun protection behaviours and beliefs within the at-risk population of outdoor workers, revealing three subgroups. These corresponded closely to the stages described in the transtheoretical model of behaviour change [57]. The first subgroup of outdoor workers reflected characteristics commonly associated with the precontemplation stage, including minimal engagement with occupational sun protection.

Most had low perceptions of the severity and susceptibility of occupational UVR exposure, while some accepted the risks as an inherent trade-off of their profession. Highly valuing personal autonomy, they expressed resistance to sun protection measures at the workplace and had no intention to change their behaviours. The second subgroup corresponded to the contemplation and preparation stages with outdoor workers recognizing the importance of sun protection but lacking consistent behavioural actions. Moderate risk perceptions resulted in intentions to protect themselves, but these were often hindered by contextual factors. The third subgroup of outdoor workers reflected the action and maintenance stages, with respondents accurately perceiving risks of occupational UVR exposure and proactively applying sun protection strategies, despite still encountering barriers. They used the job flexibility and autonomy in task planning, as well as employer support, to anticipate on UVR exposure and establish sun protection routines. Across all subgroups, the perceived need for sun protection was primarily linked to high temperatures, reflecting a persistent and non-discriminative belief that heat rather than UVR exposure drives sun protection.

DISCUSSION

This study provides a comprehensive needs assessment of occupational sun protection among outdoor workers in the Netherlands, including sunburns, sun protection behaviour, and the underlying individual and environmental determinants. The results indicate a high prevalence of sunburns: 71.7% of outdoor workers experienced at least one light sunburn and 52.4% at least one moderate sunburn during the past summer. This sunburn prevalence is notably high, even in comparison to other countries, and emphasises the cumulative burden of UVR-related risks related to sunburns during both outdoor work and leisure [14, 15].

Accordingly, outdoor workers face an increased risk of developing skin cancer; a health outcome associated with substantial mortality, morbidity and disease-related quality of life burden [6, 8, 9, 58]. However, despite the high incidence of sunburn, perceived susceptibility and severity of sunburn remained low. This is in line with previous research in other countries [22, 23, 59]. While outdoor workers in our qualitative study did not feel at (increased) risk of sunburn and underestimated its consequences, they also accepted risks as inherent to their job. Immediate physical hazards appeared as more urgent and relevant than UVR-related harm, contributing to the reluctance to take sun protection measures.

Moreover, outdoor workers reported suboptimal sun protection behaviours. While some engaged in wearing sunglasses, hats or caps, and applying sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher, this may partially reflect behaviours reinforced by existing safety regulations for exposure risks other than UVR (e.g., protective clothing against corrosive substances like wet cement). There was limited engagement in other relevant sun protection behaviours, particularly sun avoidance during 12 and 3 PM, sunscreen reapplication, and sunscreen application 30 minutes before sun exposure. This low level of occupational sun protection is comparable to previous findings from countries in Europe [14, 15, 18, 60], while studies from South Africa and Australia reported higher levels of occupational sun protection [21, 25]. These differences may be shaped by climatic factors, as cooler temperatures and cloud cover have been shown to be negatively associated with sun protection [61].

Current results also revealed individual and environmental determinants that underpin occupational sun protection behaviour. Sun protection was often deprioritised in regards to immediate occupational demands such as high productivity demands, more immediate occupational hazards, or financial considerations. Besides, a perceived norm that emphasised individual responsibility of sun protection seemed to overrule the efforts for social reinforcements that employers made. Sun protection was also experienced as unpleasant, impractical, or physically uncomfortable, for example due to occupational factors like dust and sand that hinder sunscreen application. These negative attitudes are common and previous studies also demonstrated the need for sun protection measures that are compatible with and feasible in the outdoor work context [22, 23, 62].

Although the majority of outdoor workers were aware of the harmful effects of sun exposure, their knowledge was often incorrect or incomplete. This is similar to previous findings and emphasises that knowledge alone is insufficient to facilitate sun protection behaviours [20, 22, 63]. While knowledge is often considered as a determinant of health behaviour, recent literature distinguishes between broad and narrow definitions of knowledge, and proposed that what is typically labeled as knowledge often reflects components of other, more proximal determinants of behaviour [64]. Using its broad definition, knowledge in our study encompasses several underlying determinants. Specifically, outdoor workers' reported knowledge about skin damage caused by sun exposure may reflect adequate risk perceptions, while their misperceptions regarding UVR exposure and temperature represent lower risk perceptions. The identified knowledge gaps regarding effective sun protection methods may reflect outdoor workers' limited perceived behavioural control. Considering these underlying determinants allows for a coherent understanding of occupational sun protection behaviours.

Besides individual determinants, the environments in which outdoor workers operate seemed to simultaneously enable and constrain sun protection behaviour. Expectations of resilience and societal norms of a tanned skin discouraged sun protection, but social reinforcement of sun protection was shown through reminders, peer modeling, and encouragement from employers. Most workplaces support occupational sun protection via protocols and safety measures, but structural changes in policies often turn out too costly. Comparable patterns have been reported in Australia, where role models and the integration of sun safe messages at the workplace supported sun protection, while the workplace culture and budgeting constraints hindered sun protection [24, 26, 65].

Importantly, perceived heat served as an important cue for outdoor workers to engage in occupational sun protection. This creates the opportunity to integrate sun safety measures into existing heat stress policies, emphasising its risks that prompt sun protection behaviour [24, 32]. However, a threat arises that if occupational sun protection remains only linked to high temperature and heat, outdoor workers may underestimate the need for sun protection under cooler conditions despite potentially harmful UVR exposure. It is therefore key to address the misconception that sun protection is only necessary at high temperatures, for example through workplace interventions that focus on understanding UVR exposure beyond warm conditions [61].

The study results have to be considered in light of several potential biases. Self-reported and retrospective data collection in the quantitative survey study may have introduced the risk of recall and social desirability bias [66]. The convenience sampling method in the qualitative interviews may have resulted in a positively biased sample. Voluntary recruitment of outdoor workers via workplace contacts limited insight into the number of individuals approached or who declined participation, and may have attracted outdoor workers with greater interest in health or sun protection, as is common in health research [67]. These biases suggest that sun protection behaviours may have been overestimated and sunburns underestimated.

Moreover, the lack of a consistent definition of what constitutes an outdoor worker reflects a conceptual challenge in the field and has implications for how this professional group is represented in research. Our study included individuals who self-identified as outdoor workers without a specific threshold for time spent outdoors, reflecting the lack of consensus in literature regarding the definition of outdoor work. Definitions range from working outdoors at least three hours of the day [68] to spending at least 75% of working time outdoors, according to the European Agency for Safety and Health at Work [6]. Importantly, the health impact of outdoor work depends not only on time spent outdoors, but also on the duration and intensity of UVR exposure, which are influenced by environmental factors including the time of day, surface reflectance, and cloud cover [6]. The current findings therefore represent the health problem across diverse outdoor work contexts and UVR exposure levels. Future research could refine exposure assessment by incorporating sensor-based data of UVR exposure alongside self-reported data, as demonstrated in one study combining data from self-reported measures with UVR dosimeters to account for both personal and environmental determinants [18].

Yet, as the first study to assess sun protection among outdoor workers in the Netherlands, it provides valuable insights into a context that is both relevant and underexplored. Building upon those insights, further research is needed to deepen understanding of the outdoor work context, particularly as many outdoor occupations are physically demanding yet autonomous, with workers often independently planning and executing tasks, including decisions about sun protection. While quantitative studies are recommended to examine the association between determinants of outdoor work environments and occupational sun protection behaviour, qualitative studies can provide deeper insights into the contextual conditions in which these behaviours occur, particularly capturing the diversity across outdoor occupations, and the specific opportunities and constraints for sun protection within each unique occupational environment. Despite existing occupational guidelines, sun protection is rarely addressed in a sector-specific manner [31]. Greater insight into these contextual differences would support the development of tailored occupational sun protection strategies. Together, this provides a strong evidence for progressing to the subsequent IM steps, aiming to translate the findings into change objectives for intervention development [34].

The observed prevalence of sunburn and the limited engagement in occupational sun protection behaviours call for immediate action in occupational sun safety. The recognition of skin cancer as an occupational disease in Germany offers an aspirational benchmark for more comprehensive occupational sun safety in other countries like the Netherlands [27]. A critical step in the Netherlands is taken with the recent registration guideline for work-related skin cancer that may contribute to increased recognition and provide an important opportunity to place occupational sun protection higher on the agenda of policymakers, employers, occupational health professionals, and outdoor workers [31]. The current study serves as the next step towards awareness-raising and intervention development. The identified behavioural determinants inform educational strategies to address perceived behavioural control and reduce barriers, specifically when tailored to the different outdoor work contexts and individuals' stage of behavioural change [24, 69].

In conclusion, this mixed-methods study offered a comprehensive understanding of sunburns, sun protection behaviours, and the underlying individual and environmental determinants in this unique priority population. Findings underscored that occupational sun protection is influenced not only by individual determinants such as attitudes, risk perceptions, and perceived behavioural control, but also by the work context, including job roles, situational tasks, and perceived practicality. Interventions need to be developed that address individual behaviours and create workplace environments that support consistent sun protection.
