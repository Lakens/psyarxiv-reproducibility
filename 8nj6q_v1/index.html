Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Trust in artificial intelligence (AI) is crucial for ensuring productive human-AI interactions across domains ranging from medicine to finance. As AI systems become central in the digital society, understanding how people decide when to rely on AI advice becomes increasingly important. This paper proposes that a decision-theoretic perspective is needed to explain how well-calibrated trust in AI can be achieved. We emphasize that an important component of trust in AI involves a decision to delegate control to an artificial agent, balancing potential rewards and risks of following AI advice versus relying on one's own judgment. Framed this way, established decision-making theories such as Prospect Theory, Evidence Accumulation Models, and Reinforcement Learning offer valuable insights into the cognitive processes that underlie trust in AI. These theories offer formal, computational models for explaining how individuals evaluate AI-generated advice, weigh risks and rewards, form biases, and update trust based on feedback, enabling precise hypotheses about when and why people accept or reject AI advice. We offer several examples illustrating that grounding trust in cognitive theory provides a conceptual framework for empirical research that moves beyond surface-level behaviors and towards mechanistic understanding. We conclude that a decision-theoretic approach can enhance psychological insight and guide the responsible design of AI systems within the digital society that are better aligned with human cognition.

Trust in AI: A Decision-Theoretic framework for Human-AI Interactions in the Digital Society

The digital society we live in is infused with Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies, which are capable of interacting with the environment and aim to simulate human intelligence (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). Understanding the ways AI and the digital society can benefit or threat us requires complementary scientific perspectives that highlight the different ways in which society, humans and technology are entangled (e.g., Bonnefon & Rahwan, 2020; Rahwan et al., 2019). One of the most fundamental aspects of this complex dynamic is to understand the decisions a human makes when interacting with an AI system. AI systems now perform tasks that once depended on human judgment, such as diagnosing illness, screening job applicants, or curating news. Close and frequent interactions between humans and AI are therefore inevitable (Dellermann et al., 2019) and require a careful balance: Enjoying the full benefits of AI requires having faith in its support while remaining critical to its future contributions and implications. As a result, people must continually decide whether to delegate control to an AI system (Fügener et al., 2021; Spitzer et al., 2026; Tsamados et al., 2025) or not. A key component of this decision is trust in AI (Everett et al., 2026; Mehrotra et al., 2024).

The need to consider trust (in AI) as a cognitive process

Definitions of trust often touch upon the common idea that trust involves a psychological state underlying the intention to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of another's intentions or behavior (e.g., Mayer et al., 1995; Rousseau et al., 1998). However, although accepting risk or vulnerability constitutes an important aspect of trust in many contexts and interactions, it is not a necessarily prerequisite for trust (see PytlikZillig & Kimbrough, 2016 for a meta-review), nor does it fully capture the construct of trust. Rather, trust is multi-faceted. For instance, trust is inherently relational, arising from the dependency between a trustor and a trustee (Castaldo et al., 2010; Castelfranchi & Falcone, 2010; Mayer et al., 1995). It also reflects stable individual differences, with some trustors generally being more inclined to trust than others (e.g., Hoff & Bashir, 2015; Rotter, 1967). Furthermore, trust is shaped by beliefs, evaluations, or expectancies regarding the trustee's characteristics and future behavior (Fink et al., 2010; Hoff & Bashir, 2015; Lee & See, 2004; Li, 2007). As a result, the relative importance and interplay of these components depend on the context in which trust is considered (PytlikZillig & Kimbrough, 2016). Taken together, trust is a multilayered construct that has been studies across numerous disciplines (e.g., Alós-Ferrer & Farolfi, 2019; Baier, 1986; Chen et al., 2023; Hardin, 2002; Hupcey et al., 2001; Luhmann, 1979; Mayer et al., 1995; PytlikZillig & Kimbrough, 2016), each emphasizing different components and levels of analysis.

Trust in AI is no less of an intricate concept as trust in general. Initially, trust in AI was mainly considered in terms of technology acceptance. Davis (1989) assumes that the use of (new) technology is mainly determined by two variables: the perceived usefulness of technology and the perceived ease of use of technology. These variables can further influence our attitudes towards technology, the intention to use and the actual use of technology. In general, technology acceptance is heavily moderated by trust ( see Wu et al., 2011 for a metaanalysis) and the use of AI forms no exception (e.g., Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Mehrotra et al., 2024). Glikson and Woolley (2020) argue that trust in AI involves multiple components, including dispositional, cognitive and emotional aspects of trust (see also Hoff & Bashir, 2015; McAllister, 1995), while also being shaped by characteristics of AI (e.g. virtual, embedded, embodied), the task-context and the perceived trustworthiness of the AI system. Mehrotra et al. (2024) consider trust in AI in terms of the 'appropriateness' of this trust.

Sustainable interactions with AI require that people balance their expectations, being neither too optimistic nor too skeptical but aligned with what an AI system can actually do. This balance requires a certain degree of trust in the system, so to make optimal use of its potential, while also preventing over-trust that may lead to potentially devastating outcomes when errors are not detected in time. Trust is appropriate when the user's trust formed by beliefs about the AI's trustworthiness is in line with the AI's actual trustworthiness. To this end, trust needs to be calibrated or aligned to correct for over-and under-trust over the course of time and repeated interactions. In line with technology acceptance, Mehrotra et al. (2024) map different trust constructs in terms of beliefs, intentions, and behavior, which results in a complex interplay of trust elements. For instance, part of the appropriate trust of towards AI is warranted and depends on AI's trustworthiness featured (i.e., warranted trust). At the same time, users expect AI to uphold certain functionalities and trust is endorsed without any proof that AI system actually does what it is intended to do (i.e., contractual trust). Of course, trust calibration will also depend on the perceived capability of AI.

The need for appropriate trust and trust calibration is especially important in view of the common observation that human agents are often preferred over AI agents in decision support systems (DSS), a class of AI systems designed to provide predictive or prescriptive outputs in structured tasks (Wang et al., 2019). These systems are designed to assist usersboth professionals, such as medical doctors or financial experts, and end users, in search of new holiday destinations or tv shows (Elhaddad & Hamam, 2024; Gupta et al., 2022; Soori et al., 2024; R. T. Sutton et al., 2020). AI support is typically dismissed in DSS, a phenomenon known as algorithm aversion (Burton et al., 2020; Dietvorst et al., 2015; Jussupow et al., 2020 Jussupow et al., , 2024;; Mahmud et al., 2022), which occurs despite the fact that algorithms outperform their human counterparts in forecasts (Meehl, 1954) and has been considered as a behavioral anomaly (Filiz et al., 2023) that extends even in social settings requiring human-AI cooperation (e.g., Liefooghe et al., 2023; Y. Zhang et al., 2026). Mahmud et al. (2022) propose that algorithm aversion can be related to characteristics of algorithms and users, as well as, task and contextual factors. For instance, algorithm characteristics such as the lack of transparency results in algorithm aversion (Dzindolet et al., 2002; Kayande et al., 2009). Individual differences and user characteristics further influence algorithm aversion. Familiarity with algorithms generally increases acceptance (Fenneman et al., 2021; Lim & O'Connor, 1996), whereas high levels of task expertise reduce reliance on algorithmic advice (Logg et al., 2019).

Task factors contribute to algorithm aversion, particularly when tasks are subjective or intuition-based rather than objective (Castelo et al., 2019). Similarly, algorithm aversion is more pronounced in moral or ethical domains (Bigman & Gray, 2018; Gogoll & Uhl, 2018).

The above overview indicates that trust in AI is conceptually versatile, as it is the case for the trust concept in generalfoot_0 . Research on algorithm aversion, which constitutes an important instance of trust in AI, also consists of a complex interplay of moderators. Here, we propose that research on trust in AI and interventions aimed at optimizing trust in AI may benefit a lot by considering that trust in AI also involves a decision process. Considering trust from a cognitive psychological perspective is not new (e.g., Chen et al., 2023; Delgado et al., 2023; McEvily, 2011) and its heuristic value in organizing the different trust constructs is acknowledged (PytlikZillig & Kimbrough, 2016). With respect to trust in AI, the model of Mehrotra et al. (2024) also outlines a process in which trust beliefs and trust intentions lead to trust behavior. However, at a deeper level of analysis the question remains what cognitive mechanisms support these processes? Whereas research on Human-AI interaction often acknowledges that trust is mediated by human cognition, the exact cognitive mechanisms underlying acceptance behavior are often not specified and regarded as topics for more 'fundamental' cognitive neuroscientific research (e.g., Chang et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2023; Delgado et al., 2023). However, and in contrast, Proctor and Vu (2010) Importantly, we do not claim that trust can be reduced to decision-making alone. Rather, our focus is on isolating one specific component of trust in AI: the decision process by which individuals choose whether, when, and to what extent to rely on AI systems. We argue that this decision component is especially central in decision support systems, where trust is often expressed behaviorally through reliance or delegation.

In order to create a pathway for specifying and formalizing the cognitive processes underlying trust in AI, we propose to endorse a decision-theoretic perspective. Such an approach relates, for instance, to earlier work on trust by McEvily (2011), who considered trust as form of social decision-making closely related to decision-making under risk that could either be analytical in nature or more automatic on the basis of heuristics formed by the trustor.

Here, we illustrate that a decision-making perspective enables us to relate trust in AI to three well-studied paradigms of decision making that can be formalized with computational models.

To this end, we focus on DSS. The use of DSS is central in the domain of AI (see above) and is supported by a class of AI systems designed to provide predictive or prescriptive outputs in structured tasks (Wang et al., 2019). In DSS -contrary to more exploratory or co-creative human-AI interactions -the outcomes of a decision can be readily evaluated, and the stakes are well-defined. In particular, DSS allow individual to either accept the advice that is provided by the DSS, or reject it. Although this offers a well-delineated framework for the present purposes, we acknowledge that trust in AI takes different forms and goes beyond such interactions. We consider the benefit of our approach for broader types of Human-AI interactions and trust research in general later on.

Defining the Decision-Making Process in Trust in AI

In order to build a strong decision-theoretic framework on trust in AI, we focus on a specific aspect of trust that is especially relevant in human-AI interaction: the decision to accept or reject AI advice. Rather than redefining trust itself, we isolate the decision component of trust that governs reliance behavior in situations involving AI-supported decision making.

To this end, we define the acceptance or rejection of AI advice as a decision-making process.

More specifically, we conceptualize this reliance decision as the decision to outsource some degree of control in a particular situation to AI in order to achieve a particular outcome that has more value than when no control is outsourced, but with the risk of getting a higher penalty than when control is not outsourced (cf. PytlikZillig & Kimbrough, 2016). That is, someone may choose to trust AI and for example accept decision support. They do so expecting a better outcome than when making a decision alone. At the same time, they release control over the action that will be decided upon. The critical reader may argue that our definition of trust is not entirely new and contains elements that were iterated before. The brief overview provided in the previous section also indicates that our definition misses elements. Instead, we aim to isolate the component of trust that manifests in observable reliance decisions and that can be modeled using decision-theoretic tools. A decision-theoretic framework applies the core idea that trust includes the intention to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of the intentions or behavior of another (e.g., Mayer et al., 1995; Rousseau et al., 1998) to Human-AI interactions and stressed that accepting AI advice contains the typical ingredients of a decisionmaking process: The decision-maker has to balance potential risks with the value of the different outcomes, while considering the inherent uncertainty of future events. Such a decision-making process applies to many contexts in which we use AI, from AI-driven systems recommending TV shows (Goyani & Chaurasiya, 2020) or holiday destinations (Hamid et al., 2021), to systems providing medical diagnoses (R. T. Sutton et al., 2020) or stock market advice (Devan et al., 2023). In each case, users choose to either delegate control to AI or ignore its recommendation and make their own judgment. By outsourcing, we can save time (e.g., avoiding the need to search for series or holiday destinations ourselves) or gain certainty (e.g., receiving a 'second opinion' in medical diagnoses). However, there is also the risk that the AI's advice may not meet our expectations (e.g., suggesting unwatchable series, bad hotels, or an incorrect diagnosis). Similarly, for more socially-geared interactions with AI, sharing personal information with a mental-health bot involves outsourcing control of this information to AI in order to achieve help. However, a potential risk is that this personal information could be misused.

The prime advantage of our definition is that an information-processing strategy can be adopted by studying trust in AI through the lens of well-established research programs in psychology that focus on the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying decision making (Mulder et al., 2014; Proctor & Vu, 2010; Rangel et al., 2008).

The dispositional aspects of trust refer to an individual's overall tendency to trust, independent of context or specific systems, and is based on both biological and environmental influences. Situational aspects vary depending on the context of the interaction and the characteristics of the trustor. Finally, the learned aspect of trust is based on the system's characteristics rather than the context, reflecting the trustor's evaluations of a system learned from past experience or the current interactions with the system.

When considering accepting AI advice as a decision-making process, we can consider at least three paradigms that have proven valuable in the study of decision-making and can advance our understanding of the neurocognitive mediators underlying trust in AI: Prospect Theory (Fox & Poldrack, 2009; Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992), Evidence Accumulation (Mulder et al., 2014; Ratcliff et al., 2016), and Reinforcement Learning (R. S. Sutton & Barto, 2018). Importantly, these paradigms employ formal models of human cognition, which further strengthen a tight framework with clear hypotheses and predictions (see e.g., Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019 for a discussion of the advantage of formal models in psychological research).

Here, we illustrate how Prospect Theory, Evidence Accumulation Models (EAMs), and Reinforcement Learning (RL) -offer hypotheses and concrete predictions about trust in AI by making specific assumptions about the cognitive mechanisms underlying trust in AI and trust in general. We briefly introduce each framework and consider it in view of DSS. Next, we formulate some predictions -some of which are already tested. However, we emphasize that we do not provide an exhaustive list of predictions. Other hypotheses could be entertained based on different literature, yielding different predictions. This follows from an important distinction between hypotheses and theoretical frameworks. The framework enables formulation of the specific hypothesis, but there may be others as well. In the context of cognitive models, this distinction is well-known (Cooper, 2007): A framework allows for the coherent study of certain fields (in this case, decision-making, including decisions about following AI advice) and it allows for the derivation of explicit hypotheses that can be subsequently tested. Rejection of specific hypotheses should not lead to the dismissal of the complete framework however, but only to the dismissal of the specific hypothesis.

Prospect theory

The decision to accept AI advice can be viewed as a choice between risky prospects. The most well-known paradigm in which risky decision making has been studied is Prospect Theory (Fox & Poldrack, 2009; Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992). Prospect Theory postulates that decisions are based on the perceived utility of each option, weighted by their perceived probabilities. This can result in individuals choosing a less desirable but more likely option over a more desirable, less likely option, avoiding the risk of ending up empty handed.

In addition, Prospect Theory assumes that individuals differ in how they value certain alternatives, and that individuals have no ideal insight into probabilities. These assumptions help to explain why people often deviate from a normatively optimal choice. In particular, people tend to be risk-seeking in the loss domain -that is, when there is a chance of a negative outcome -and risk-averse in the gain domain, when there is a chance of a positive outcome (Fox & Poldrack, 2009; Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Ruggeri et al., 2020).

Prospect Theory and the impact of risk and loss aversion in advice acceptance in DSS are starting to gain attraction and offer a promising pathway for obtaining new insights on trust in AI. For example, following Prospect Theory, users may be more likely to reject AI advice in gain-framed contexts due to risk aversion, but may accept AI advice more readily when outcomes are framed as losses. This hypothesis is based on similar observations in non-AI contexts, where participants have to make judgments framed as gains or losses respectively (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Ruggeri et al., 2020). In line with this hypothesis, Bockstedt and Buckman (2026) observed that human advice is preferred over algorithmic advice when accurate task performance leads to reward. However, when the task was framed in terms of losses, i.e., poor performance is penalized, both types of support were used to a similar extent.

Other studies observed that algorithms are relied upon more when decisions lead to gain, but not when the outcome is a loss (Toma et al., 2020) or did not obtain any evidence that outcome framing influences the acceptance of algorithmic advice (Liefooghe et al., 2026).

Moreover, Dietvorst and Bharti (2020) consider outcome probability and risk taking in the context of algorithm aversion by proposing that the sensitivity to a DSS error decreases when these errors are more frequent. This entails that DSS are evaluated on the basis of the perceived likelihood of producing a near-perfect outcome rather than on the DSS's average level of performance. They found that for uncertain decision domains human support is preferred over algorithms, because the higher variability associated with human performance is perceived as offering a greater opportunity of achieving a near-perfect outcome by chance.

The corresponding disadvantage of being more likely to be very inaccurate, receives less weight, in line with the asymmetric weighting of gains and losses that is predicted by Prospect Theory. In contrast, algorithms are perceived as being more consistent and less variable, which makes it unlikely that a near-perfect outcome can be obtained by chance. However, these differences in expectations toward human and algorithmic forecasters were found to be mitigated when the uncertainty of the decision domain increases. Under such conditions the outcome uncertainty becomes so high that the effect of forecast variability is negligible and preferences for human and algorithmic forecasters become similar. Whereas initial evidence supports this account (Dietvorst & Bharti, 2020), additional research indicates that when an outcome is very unlikely algorithms are even preferred over human support (Liefooghe et al., 2026). Finally, framing effects can extend beyond gains or losses and relate to how the decision context is framed in general (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Castelo et al. (2019) demonstrated a preference for human support for tasks that were perceived as being subjective compared to tasks that were likely to be considered as more objective. Renier et al. (2021) manipulated task contexts and the severity of decision outcomes in these contexts. Participants either received human or algorithmic support. The context and outcome severity did not seem to moderate algorithm aversion. Filiz et al. (2023) systematically manipulated the decision context to investigate the influence of context severity in algorithm aversion. These authors reasoned that in more severe contexts decision makers put more effort and make a more rational decision, thus counteracting the aversion of algorithms. In contrast to their hypothesis, Filiz et al. (2023) found that human support was preferred over algorithms when the decision context and the consequences of a decision were perceived to be more severe ( see also Liefooghe et al., 2026).

Taken together, viewing advice taking from AI through the lens of Prospect Theory suggests that trust in AI is fundamentally shaped by how decision makers perceive and evaluate risk and uncertainty rather than by objective system performance alone. Whether AI advice is accepted depends not only on expected accuracy, but also on how potential gains and losses are framed, how uncertain outcomes are perceived, and how people weigh the risks of relying on AI versus relying on their own judgment. Consequently, trust in AI can be viewed as a context-dependent decision process in which subjective evaluations of risk, uncertainty, and outcome framing jointly determine whether control is delegated to an AI system. Furthermore, Prospect Theory generates new hypotheses regarding trust in AI. For instance, the subjective value of accepting advice (one's own, another human expert, or an AI system) may vary, influencing the acceptance rate of AI advice (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992). Such a hypothesis is supported by observations that experienced and informed probabilities of a certain outcome have differential effects on choice (Abdellaoui et al., 2011). It seems reasonable to assume that decisions based on informed probabilities, coming from AI advice, also differ from decisions based on one's own judgment.

Evidence accumulation modeling

A second important paradigm when considering trust in AI as a decision-making process is the Evidence Accumulation Model (EAM). The dynamics of decision-making processes have been extensively investigated using these models (Mulder et al., 2014; Ratcliff et al., 2016). EAMs assume that, in order to select a choice alternative, individuals accumulate evidence over time for the choice alternatives, reaching a threshold that supports one alternative over the other.

The alternative that first reaches this threshold is selected. The speed of evidence accumulation is determined by the difficulty of the decision (e.g., Heekeren et al., 2004; Mulder et al., 2013; Van Maanen et al., 2016). When evidence takes the form of the difference in expected utilities between two options, a choice becomes more difficult when the expected utilities are more comparable (Summerfield & Tsetsos, 2012). In contrast, when the expected utilities of the alternatives are clearly dissimilar, decision makers are quick to select the most desirable choice alternative.

The effectiveness and relevance of EAMs in investigating trust in AI lie in their ability to identify and quantify the cognitive mechanisms that control the speed and accuracy of decisions. Two main processes drive these dynamics. First, decision makers are believed to have control over the decision threshold, when they feel this is appropriate in a given situation (Bogacz et al., 2010; Van Maanen et al., 2011). The consequence of a higher threshold is that the likelihood of selecting the best option increases, but decisions take longer (Boehm et al., 2020; Bogacz et al., 2006). Inversely, a lower threshold allows for faster decisions, but results in more variable -and possibly incorrect -choices. This mechanism provides a natural explanation of the often observed speed-accuracy trade-off in decision making (Heitz, 2014), in which individuals in daily life or professional environments such as medical diagnoses or financial trading, must balance the need for quick decisions with the importance of a correct choice. A second process involves decision makers having a prior preference for one choice alternative over the others, which according to the EAM paradigm yields asymmetric decision behavior. A prior preference means that less additional evidence is required to reach the decision threshold for the favored alternative, accelerating the decision-making process for that option while delaying decisions for the other options (Cerracchio et al., 2023; Leite & Ratcliff, 2011; Mulder et al., 2012; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; White & Poldrack, 2014).

These mechanisms potentially explain the decision to trust AI across different contexts, where factors influence the need for speed or accuracy, or when initial preferences for a certain outcome affect the decision-making process. With respect to algorithm aversion, a hypothesis could be that shifted starting points may reflect a discrepancy in trust, where individuals have a bias to disagree with AI. Such a mechanism would result in rapid, habitual rejections of AI advice even when evidence supports accepting it. Such a mechanisms would be in line with findings that show that expectations of differential reward lead to choice biases (Bogacz et al., 2006; Mulder et al., 2012). For example, Mulder et al. (2012) showed that a cue indicating the most likely response option in a binary choice task lead to a choice bias, reflecting by a shift in the starting point in an EAM. However, an alternative EAM explanation of algorithm aversion could be that the choice to agree with AI simply requires more evidence, for example because individuals find it less trustworthy. In a medical decision-making experiment, we indeed found evidence for this second hypothesis (Van Maanen et al., 2025). In two experiments, we asked participants to indicate whether they agreed or disagreed with the assessment of x-ray images by a medical advisor. In one experimental condition, the advisor was framed as a human, whereas in the other condition, the advisor was framed as an algorithm. We found that in the algorithmic-advice condition, participants indeed had a higher threshold, resulting in slower choices, and fewer incorrect choices. This pattern was independent of whether the human advisor was framed as an expert radiologist or a naïve fellow participant. This study this shows that measuring decision times alongside choices can help disentangle accumulation rates from starting point bias, shedding light on the decision mechanisms related to algorithm aversion.

A novel prediction that immediately follows from the EAM paradigm is that the difference between human and AI advice is mediated by time pressure. That is, time pressure is believed to affect the decision threshold (Bogacz et al., 2010; Heitz, 2014; Van Maanen et al., 2011). Therefore, it could be hypothesized that strong time pressure reduces the effect of human vs AI advisor because a reduction in threshold leads to an even larger reduction in response time, mitigating the difference between human and AI advice. This hypothesis is indirectly supported by recent work by Liang and colleagues (2026) who us EAMs not to understand the mechanisms underlying differences in endorsing human vs. algorithmic advice (e.g., Van Maanen et al., 2025), but the decision proper to outsource a task completely to a DSS. Participants had to make a perceptual decision, but were given the option to outsource each decision to a DSS. The proportion and timing of both decision making and outsourcing was well-explained by an EAM, and indicated that outsourcing to AI is indeed driven by a sense of urgency in terminating a difficult decision in view of time constraints.

Reinforcement learning theory

A third paradigm that helps to understand accepting AI advice as a decision-making process is Reinforcement Learning (RL, Rescorla & Wagner, 1972; R. S. Sutton & Barto, 2018), where decision-makers learn the value of actions by comparing observed outcomeseither rewards or punishments -with expected outcomes. In this paradigm, after each decision, the value of an action or choice is updated, based on the mismatch between the expected and actual outcome. A large mismatch between the expected and observed outcomes indicates that the value of an action has not yet been fully learned, resulting in an update of the expected outcome in the direction of the observed outcome. Indeed, evidence from trust research suggests that trust develops through iterative feedback-based learning. Chang et al. (2010) demonstrated that trustworthiness judgments in repeated social interactions can be captured using computational learning models, showing that trust is updated dynamically as a function of prediction errors. Applied to human-AI interaction, this finding suggests that trust in AI may be similarly shaped by repeated experiences with algorithmic performance, where prediction errors drive adjustments in beliefs about AI competence and reliability.

The RL paradigm thus provides an important perspective on trust in AI as it helps to explain how individuals learn to trust AI over time. That is, it explains the underlying processes that results in a learned preference for one option over another, based on the outcomes of previous decisions. As such, in the context of trust in AI, Reinforcement Learning helps us understand how individuals adjust their expectations about the reliability of algorithmic advice over time. This is achieved by updating their trust as they observe whether the algorithm's recommendations lead to (un)expected or (un)preferred outcomes, which in turn can result in algorithm appreciation or aversion. The speed at which new information is integrated into the existing value representation, called the learning rate, determines how quickly individuals adjust their beliefs in response to new information. As such, this learning rate reflects how certain factors can affect the speed with which users adapt their trust, based on the performance of a DDS. In addition to the learning rate, RL models include mechanisms that manage the trade-off between exploring new options and exploiting known ones (R. S. Sutton & Barto, 2018; van Maanen & Miletić, 2021). These mechanisms influence how likely different choices are, based on their expected value. A higher sensitivity to value differences promotes consistent exploitation of the best option, while lower sensitivity encourages more exploration, increasing variability in choices.

RL models suggest that reliance on actions evolves through outcome-based learning (R. S. Sutton & Barto, 2018). This predicts that trust in AI also develops through repeated successful interactions with AI advice. A lower learning rate may predict persistent algorithm aversion even after repeated successful AI advice, while value asymmetries in updating may favor human over AI-generated outcomes. Interestingly, algorithm aversion seems relatively insensitive to the actual performance of an algorithm. Evidence for this comes from research using iterated trust games (Berg et al., 1995). For instance, Ishowo-Oloko et al. (2019) used an online iterated prisoner's dilemma game in which participants were instructed to be coupled with another human coplayer or an algorithm. Over the course of the game, the algorithm adapted to the responses of the participants such that it maximized trust and cooperation.

However, the mere awareness that the coplayer was an algorithm reduced players' willingness to cooperate. Miwa and Terai (2012) used a similar set-up in which participants were led to believe that they were interacting with either human or computer coplayers. Across three experiments, they consistently observed that participants were more cooperative when they were instructed that the coplayer was a human. This difference in cooperation was present regardless of the coplayer's cooperation rate. Liefooghe, Min, et al. (2023) also observed more trust behavior when participants believed they were playing against a human rather than an algorithm. Again, this difference in cooperation did not depend on the coplayer's actual performance. In view of such findings, Zhang et al. (2026) proposed that because human agents are perceived as being more intentional and adaptive (Rand et al., 2012), more emphasis is placed on the actual behavior of a human coplayer compared to that of an algorithm.

The above discussion indicates that much can be learned to consider Human-AI interactions and DSS from the perspective of RL models as they can possibly explain the complex dynamics underlying the repeated use of AI in different contexts. Discussion and Conclusion

The present discussion paper examines trust in AI through the lens of decision-making.

Specifically, we focused on the decision component of trust: the cognitive process through which individuals decide whether and to what extent to rely on AI advice. This perspective is parsimonious and offers a common working ground for the multi-disciplinary field of AI. More importantly, it offers the opportunity to further integrate psychological theory in the optimization of Human-AI interaction (see also Brysbaert, in press for the importance of doing so). Specifically, a decision-theoretic approach can be developed to investigate and calibrate trust in AI, using three established paradigms: Prospect Theory, Evidence Accumulation Models and Reinforcement Learning. These paradigms go beyond more general conceptualizations of human cognition that are often considered and offer formal, computational models that capture cognitive processes in a straightforward way that underly the decision to accept or reject AI advice. As such, they can provide precise insights into how users evaluate and adjust their appreciation or aversion against AI systems in general, and DSS in particular. After understanding these processes, a next step is to understand which moderating factors affect these processes (Proctor & Vu, 2010), and thereby mitigate the adverse effects of algorithm aversion or over-appreciation. One approach to achieve this may be to study how the cognitive processes involved in trust in AI relate to the dispositional, situational, and learned aspects of trust (Hoff & Bashir, 2015). Individual differences and personality traits can be considered with respect to the value assigned to a particular outcome and the perceived risk when outsourcing control to AI. As such, AI may be appraised differently across individuals. In addition, situational aspects can influence the decision to outsource control as well. For instance, tasks and situations may vary in difficulty or urgency, which may influence the trustor's inclination to outsource. Finally, the decision to outsource control evolves over repeated interactions with the AI system, which may facilitate learning that AI advice leads to a better outcome, resulting in an increased reliance on its advice.

Presumably, most cognitive mediators reflect all three elements. For example, while it seems straightforward to assume that the learning rate of RL models is associated with learned aspects of trusts, individual differences in learning rate may reflect dispositional aspects, and different learning rates between different instances of a decision support system may reflect situational aspects.

The current paper focuses on trust in AI in the context of DSS. These systems are characterized by structured tasks, evaluable outcomes, and well-defined stakes, which makes the application of decision theories and models relatively straightforward. Unlike exploratory or co-creative human-AI interactions, DSS contexts more readily allow researchers to isolate decision variables, compute expected utilities, and apply normative benchmarks. Moreover, the consequences of mis-calibrated trust such as over-reliance on faulty diagnostic tools or unwarranted rejection of accurate recommendations are particularly consequential in these domains. As such, DSS provide both theoretical tractability and practical relevance for modeling accepting AI advice from a decision-theoretic perspective. However, more complex interactions can also be considered, such as, in a joint-decision making setting. Understanding these kinds of human-AI interactions may benefit from theories rooted in social psychology or sociology that address how individuals behave in the presence of others (e.g., with respect to the feeling of autonomy, Sankaran et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). We deem such studies also important future work, since decisions are rarely taken in isolation (Tump et al., 2020).

Relatedly, in many decision-making scenarios, the decision may be influenced by real-world constraints such as institutional protocols or liability concerns (Alowais et al., 2023). In fact, potentially all subfields of Psychology could contribute to our understanding of Human-AI interactions.

An additional benefit of grounding the research in trust in AI in a decision-theoretic framework is the potential to predict behavior from computational models. Liefooghe and Van Maanen (2023) argue that Human-AI interactions can be optimized by endowing AI with user models that not only represent user behavior but also the mental processes underlying that behavior. The step from a computational model intended to understand the role of trust in AI to a generative model aimed at predicting user behavior is relatively small, and has been taken in other domains (Klinkenberg et al., 2011; Koedinger & Anderson, 1997; Van Rijn et al., 2009 , see also Steyvers & Kumar, 2023). However, reviewing the literature makes clear that much work needs to be done. Indeed, whereas EAMs have found their way in research on DSS, the formal application of Prospect Theory still needs to be tested in this domain. Likewise, RL models have been used to formalize trust in general (Chen et al., 2023; Delgado et al., 2023), but their application in the context of trust in AI remain scarce (but see Y. Zhang et al., in preparation for an example).

In a more general sense, a decision-theoretic approach to understanding AI behavior has been advocated before (e.g., Hong & Lee, 2025; Webster, 2025). For example, Hong and Lee (2025) aim to understand choices of human and AI Go-players through the lens of Prospect Theory, and found that human players are more risk averse. In a similar vein, Webster (2025; see also Bonnefon & Rahwan, 2020) argues that we should understand current AI reasoning capabilities as system-1 reasoning, a term stemming from the influential Dual Process Theory of cognition (Kahneman, 2011). Such an approach provides important insights in machine behavior (Rahwan et al., 2019), but is markedly different from our endeavor: We advocate that it is important to study human cognition in interaction with AI, rather than AI itself.

Although the present paper focuses on developing a conceptual framework rather than prescribing specific experimental paradigms, the proposed models naturally lend themselves to empirical testing. Prospect Theory predictions can be evaluated through manipulations of gain/loss framing and perceived AI reliability. EAMs require joint analysis of choices and response times to distinguish bias from caution. Reinforcement Learning models are particularly suited to repeated interaction paradigms in which trust evolves as a function of feedback. Together, these approaches provide multiple avenues for empirically testing the cognitive mechanisms underlying trust in AI. Nevertheless, the current work is not to argue in favor of Prospect Theory, the EAM paradigm, or RL modeling. Rather, we advocate to consider the strong theoretical basis in the field of decision-making -including computational modeling -when considering trust decisions in the context of AI. That is, one could also aim to study trust decisions using the heuristics and biases framework (Gigerenzer & Todd, 1999), or from the perspective of bounded rationality (Simon, 1997), and make use of the theoretical insights that are established with those frameworks. Ultimately, the goal is to characterize trust decisions in a unifying framework (Newell, 1994; O'Doherty et al., 2017; Ruff & Fehr, 2014) that explains which elements of the decision relate to general decision-making mechanisms, and which elements are specific to trust in AI.

Of course, our decision-theoretic approach of trust in AI only constitutes a very small piece in a far more complex puzzle. This is because trust in AI unfolds within sociotechnical environments that also define what counts as trustworthy. Trust in AI is thus not only a psychological construct as we treat it here, but also a social construct that allows individuals to act under uncertainty and complexity (Luhmann, 1979). As we noted in the Introduction, trust goes beyond decision making and is an intricate interplay of constructs that operate and are defined at different levels (Baier, 1986; Chen et al., 2023; Hardin, 2002; Luhmann, 1979; Mayer et al., 1995). With our approach we do not wish to apply a reductionist approach to trust. In line with the work of McEvely (2011) and others (e.g., Chang et al., 2010; Delgado et al., 2023) we emphasize the importance of considering trust as a (cognitive) process. At the core of this process, we propose a decision-making mechanism that can possibly be moderated by the different constructs associated with trust. Here, we considered the case of trust in AI, but future work may also try to apply our decision-theoretic approach to trust research more generally. Our framework should therefore be understood as addressing one analytically tractable layer of trust-namely, reliance decisions under uncertainty-rather than the full phenomenon of trust in a digital society.
