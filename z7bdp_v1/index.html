What we found

We found 12 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

AI-literacy training may facilitate workplace adoption of GenAI, yet little is known about how employees' motivational beliefs change during training. Drawing on expectancyvalue theory, we examined motivation before and after Hazlo con IA, a national adaptive AI-literacy program in Chile. The baseline sample included 9,835 working adults across public, healthcare, and small and medium-sized enterprise sectors (SME); 1,864 provided matched pretest-posttest responses. At both time points, attainment value (B = .231-.234), intrinsic value (B = .156-.173), utility value (B = .113-.185), and perceived workplace support (B = .153-.184) positively predicted intentions to use GenAI (all ps < .001). Self-efficacy was positively associated with intentions only at posttest (B = .086, p = .004), whereas perceived costs and anxiety were not significant independent predictors. Following training, all measured constructs except perceived workplace support declined significantly, although effects were small; the largest standardized decline was in intention to use AI (ΔM = -.193, dz = -.29, p < .001). Age (V = .016, p < .001) and employment sector (V = .020, p = .005) showed small multivariate associations with change: older participants reported slightly greater declines in workplace support (B = -.0054), and public-sector employees showed larger declines in intentions than SME employees (ΔM = -.240 vs. -.061, p = .004). Other demographic and experience variables were not significant. These findings highlight the central role of subjective value in workplace GenAI intentions and the relative stability of motivational beliefs across demographic and occupational contexts.

1. Introduction

Generative Artificial intelligence (GenAI) is reshaping how organizations function and how employees carry out their daily work, at a pace that outstrips most institutions' capacity to adapt (Rosa et al., 2025; Söllner et al., 2025). In Latin America, a recent World Bank-ILO analysis estimates that 30-40% of jobs in the region have some exposure to GenAI, with 8-12% positioned to gain productivity benefits from it (Gmyrek et al., 2024). However, the same analysis warns that "up to half, some 17 million jobs, won't be able to leverage the potential benefits because of a lack of digital infrastructure" (World Bank, 2025, p. 1). It also identifies, 2-5% of jobs as at risk of automation, a risk that falls unevenly on younger, more educated urban workers, and especially women, who are twice as likely as men to be at risk. GenAI also departs from previous waves of automation. Indeed, earlier technologies mainly replaced routine, manual tasks, whereas GenAI can perform tasks involving higher levels of cognition (e.g., analyzing, evaluating, and creating) with the potential to augment and accelerate these processes. As a result, professionals in higher-skilled, more highly educated occupations are now among the most exposed (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Eloundou et al., 2024; Zheng, 2026). Consequently, it is highly important to also understand how the demographic characteristics of individuals shape their perceptions of and experiences with AI in the workplace.

The integration and potential of GenAI in the workplace have been described as a doubleedged technology (Liu & Li, 2025). On the one hand, it can increase productivity and create new opportunities for employees, but on the other hand, it can also generate anxiety, insecurity, and disadvantages among employees who may be left unprepared (Sapru, 2026; Yoon et al., 2026), potentially widening employment inequalities across sectors and national economies (Fernandez & Uriarte, 2026; Uddin et al., 2026). This is highly important in the context of Latin America, which is one of the most unequal regions in the world (World Bank, 2016; Gmyrek, et al., 2024), making it especially important to consider how this inequality could increase. Furthermore, understanding the impact of GenAI in the workplace is not simply a matter of technical training or AI literacy among workers. An employee can be provided with professional development and the capacities needed to integrate GenAI into their work, but still choose not to use it, use it only reluctantly, or abandon it once the initial curiosity fades. Indeed, familiarity does not guarantee acceptance. For instance, a 2023 survey in Switzerland found that employees who used GenAI were more concerned about it than those who used it less (Grampp et al., 2023). Nor can productivity gains be taken as given. Experimental studies show that GenAI increases productivity (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Noy & Zhang, 2023), but these findings cannot simply be extrapolated to Latin America and the Caribbean (Gmyrek et al., 2024).

The public debate is also shaping how workers may position themselves toward AI, with greater attention being paid to perceptions rather than literacies. In fact, much of the current public discourse on AI centers on banning the technology, its possible long-term effects on critical thinking, and the prospect of GenAI replacing workers. In this climate, employees may refuse to integrate GenAI even when they possess the necessary competencies.Whether workers engage with GenAI tools at all, and whether they sustain that engagement once training ends, is a motivational question as much as a competency one. Motivation determines whether an employee chooses to take up a training opportunity in the first place and whether they persist once it demands sustained effort. It is shaped by a combination of perceived relevance to one's job, personal interest, employer encouragement, and anticipated career benefits (Bhargava et al., 2021; Dwivedi et al., 2021). Understanding workforce readiness for GenAI therefore requires attention not only to what employees know, but also whether, and why, they are motivated to use it.

2. Conceptual Framework

>Conceptual Framework

2.1. Expectancy-Value Theory

Eccles' expectancy-value theory (EVT; Eccles, 1983 Eccles, , 2005;; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000) is a well-established framework for explaining why individuals engage in some tasks but not others (Hattie et al., 2020).

It defines motivation in terms of two positive components: expectancies for success and subjective task value (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; Wigfield et al., 2014). Expectancy for success refers to individuals' beliefs about their ability to perform a task successfully (Loh, 2019). In empirical research, this component is often operationalized through individual's perceived self-efficacy (e.g. Collie et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026; Yin & Goh, 2025). Subjective task value refers to the perceived reasons for engaging in a task and includes three components: attainment value (the personal importance of performing a task well), intrinsic value (the interest or enjoyment of the task), and utility value (the perceived usefulness of a task) (Eccles, 2005).

In addition, EVT considers cost, which represents the perceived negative consequences of engaging in a task. Cost includes the time and effort required to perform the task, and emotional costs, such as stress and anxiety (Flake et al., 2015; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Contextual factors, such as perceived organizational support, are also often considered within the EVT framework, as they may influence individuals' expectations of success, perceived task values, and costs. (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024). The motivational components of the EVT are related but may play different roles: expectancies for success tend to be more strongly associated with performance, whereas subjective task values are more closely associated with task choice and engagement (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; Jacobs et al., 2002).

Although EVT has traditionally been developed in school settings, it has also been applied to other contexts, including adult and continuing education. For example, it was shown that value beliefs predict participation in non-formal learning, particularly among less formally educated adults, and motivation becomes especially important where access to training is constrained (Gorges & Hollmann, 2015; Gorges, 2018; Heuer et al., 2024). Related studies show that positive attitudes toward digital learning predict adoption of elearning and digital learning platforms (Tsai et al., 2018; Annamalai et al., 2021; Zobeidi et al., 2023). More directly, Lyu and Salam (2025) found that AI-personalized instruction increased digital literacy, self-efficacy, and motivation among adult learners.

2.2 Expectancy-Value Factors in Workplace GenAI Use

Motivation to use AI is increasingly studied through EVT and its related constructs (selfefficacy, value, cost, affective, and contextual factors).

From the self-efficacy has consistently been found to predict intentions to learn about AI (Cheng, 2026) and to use AI in educational or future professional settings (Abuadas & Albikawi, 2026). Similar findings have been reported among university faculty in China (Sun et al., 2026). In addition, Collie et al. (2024) found that self-efficacy was associated with teachers' use of GenAI in their professional activities and that organizational support played a role in shaping these beliefs.

More evidence comes from healthcare professionals, among whom AI self-efficacy was positively associated with intentions to use AI technologies (Zeng et al., 2025). Similarly, a study of banking employees found that self-efficacy helped explain the relationship between organizational support, training opportunities, and intentions to adopt GenAI (Ahmad et al., 2026). However, findings are not entirely consistent, as some studies have reported mixed or contradictory relationships between technology self-efficacy and intentions to adopt AI (Cecil et al., 2025).

Different value components may also influence intentions to use GenAI, although it remains unclear which components are most important. For example, Collie et al. (2024) found a positive relationship between the perceived value of GenAI and its use in teachers' professional activities. However, they combined attainment, utility, and intrinsic values into a single construct rather than examining them separately. Sun et al. (2026) found that utility value positively predicted university faculty members' intentions to use GenAI, while intrinsic and attainment values did not have significant independent effects. In contrast, Kilat et al. (2026) found significant effects of utility and attainment values, but not intrinsic value. Overall, these findings suggest that the perceived utility of AI is an important factor, while the role of other value components remains less clear. The importance of these components may also depend on the type of work, as GenAI has different utility and application across professional tasks and occupations (Dorta-González et al., 2024; Çetindamar Kozanoglu & Abedin, 2021).

Cost components, in contrast, may reduce motivation to use AI, although the findings are less consistent. For example, Sun et al. (2026) found no significant independent effect of perceived cost on GenAI use among university faculty, whereas Yurt and Kaşarcı (2025) found a negative relationship between perceived cost and intentions to use AI among preservice teachers. Importantly, both studies measured perceived cost as a single construct, without examining the separate effects of different cost components, such as effort cost, time cost, and emotional cost. However, individual cost components may play different roles in motivation. For example, Yin and Goh (2025) found that effort cost was negatively associated with intentions to learn about and use AI, although emotional cost was positively associated with intentions to gain AI-related knowledge. At the same time, Abuadas and Albikawi (2026) found that anxiety, which can be considered a form of emotional cost (e.g., Flake et al., 2015; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), negativly predicted intention to use AI. These mixed findings may partly reflect the limited range of occupations studied, as perceived barriers and costs of using GenAI can vary across professional settings (Dorta-González et al., 2024).

Overall, existing research suggests that self-efficacy and utility value are generally positively associated with intentions to use AI, while the roles of intrinsic value, attainment value, and different cost components remain less clear, particularly across professional contexts. Moreover, most existing studies measure motivation at a single point in time and do not examine how different motivational components change together. As a result, we still know relatively little about how these beliefs change as people gain experience with AI, particularly through training programs aimed at improving AI literacy. The limited evidence available suggests that these changes are not always positive. For example, experimental studies indicate that GenAI use may reduce intrinsic motivation (Wu et al., 2025), while passive reliance on AI may lower self-efficacy (Lee et al., 2026). On the other hand, longitudinal evidence suggests that negative emotional attitudes toward AI may become less pronounced as people gain more experience using the technology (Cao & Wen, 2025).

2.3 Sociodemographic and Contextual Factors in Motivation to Use AI at Work

EVT components should not be considered separately from the background and characteristics of the individual, as well as the context in which the new practice takes place . A meta-analysis by Gegenfurtner and Vauras (2012) found a positive relationship between age and motivation to learn, with this relationship varying depending on training design, education level, and work context. Expectations of success, subjective task values, and perceived costs are shaped by individuals' previous experiences, personal characteristics, and social environments (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).

In the context of workplace AI use, this suggests that motivational beliefs may differ depending on various factors including age, gender, education level, previous experience with the technology, occupational sector, and organizational support. For example, research on workplace learning highlights the importance of individual characteristics. Yamashita et al. (2022) found that among working-age adults in the United States, younger individuals and those with higher levels of education and literacy showed greater motivation to learn. However, no consistent gender differences were found. At the same time, younger employees tend to use GenAI more frequently at work while reporting lower levels of work engagement (Marienhagen, 2026). Humlum and Vestergaard (2024) showed that younger and less experienced employees were more likely to use ChatGPT for work, while women were less likely to use it than men. Similar findings were reported by Dorta-González et al. (2024), who found that female researchers used GenAI less frequently than male researchers. However, these studies measured actual technology use rather than motivation, so it remains unclear whether these differences are related to selfefficacy, perceived utility, or other motivational components More direct evidence concerning intentions to use AI comes from healthcare settings. Dai et al. (2025) found that employees younger than 45, those with higher education levels, and those working in larger hospitals reported stronger intentions to use medical AI. In addition, higher levels of facilitating conditions, reflecting the availability of resources and support, were positively associated with intentions to use AI among both physicians and nurses.

Previous experience with AI may also play an important role. For example, Choe and Woo (2025) found that among nurses who had not previously used GenAI at work, higher performance expectancy and AI literacy were associated with stronger intentions to use the technology. However, among nurses who already had experience using GenAI, these relationships were not statistically significant. Moreover, previous GenAI use itself was not significantly associated with intentions when other predictors were taken into account. These findings suggest that previous experience may be related not so much to the overall level of motivation as to the relative importance of different motivational factors.

Beyond individual characteristics, the organizational environment may also affect the motivation and behavioural intentions. Collie and Martin (2024) found that among schoolteachers, greater perceived autonomy support from school leaders was associated with higher perceived value of GenAI. In addition, motivation for professional development and stress related to changes at work also positively predicted GenAI valuing. In another study, Collie et al. (2024) found that greater support for GenAI use at school was associated with both higher self-efficacy and higher perceived value of the technology. Similarly, Cioffi et al. (2026) found that employees who received organizational AI training reported higher performance expectancy and greater AI use.

Finally, occupational sector may also matter. Dorta-González et al. (2024) found that researchers working in private companies used GenAI approximately 19% more frequently, while those working in medical research organizations used it 16% more frequently than researchers in government institutions. These differences may be related to the nature of professional tasks, access to technology, and organizational conditions. However, little is known about whether they are also associated with differences in specific EVT components.

Overall, existing research shows that age, gender, education, previous experience, and the professional environment are associated with intentions to use AI and its actual use. Some studies also provide more direct evidence of positive relationships between organizational support and both self-efficacy and subjective value. However, we still know relatively little about how these factors relate to individual EVT components and, especially, how they relate to changes in these components following AI literacy training. Examining these changes among employees from different occupational sectors may help clarify how motivational patterns depend on individual characteristics and the context in which AI is used.

3. Present Study

The present study addresses the gaps in the literature using Hazlo con IA ("Do It with AI"), a free, national-scale, adaptive AI-literacy program launched in Chile in 2025 in response to estimates that nearly half of work tasks in the country could be accelerated using GenAI (Weintraub et al., 2025). The program was developed with institutional backing from Chile's Ministries of Economy, Science, and Labor, the National Training and Employment Service (SENCE), and SOFOFA (more details about the program can be found in Díaz et al., 2026). It is free and open to all workers in the country. The program uses an adaptive algorithm to tailor each learner's pathway to their self-identified skill needs across the SME, public, and healthcare sectors. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile (ID#250305004).

Using a pretest-posttest design, we examine how participants' expectancy-, value-, and cost-related motivational beliefs about AI change as they move through a structured adaptive training experience, as well as how these changes vary across participants and how motivational factors relate to their intention to use AI at work. In doing so, the study extends expectancy-value research to a workplace AI-training context and in a Latin American context, a region that remains comparatively underrepresented in research on workers' responses and adaptation to AI (Savolainen et al., 2026). Thus, the current study aims to answer three research questions: RQ1. How are expectancy, value, cost, and related motivational factors associated with workers' intention to use AI before and after the course? RQ2. How do workers' expectancy, value, cost, and related motivational beliefs about AI change through participation in an online adaptive AI-literacy course? RQ3. How are sociodemographic characteristics, employment sector, and prior experience with AI associated with changes in workers' motivation?

4. Methods

>Methods

4.1. Instrument development

To measure the constructs of interest, we developed a questionnaire following four steps: (1) literature review, (2) item generation, (3) expert review, and (4) cognitive pilot testing.

First, we reviewed prior research on motivation and technology acceptance, with particular emphasis on EVT, to identify the relevant EVT constructs and their relationships with the intention to use AI. The resulting theoretical constructs were selfefficacy, intrinsic value, utility value, attainment value, cost (time and effort), anxiety, work support, and intention to use AI. Appendix A provides an overview of the constructs and the specific justifications drawn from the literature.

Second, in addition to reviewing the literature to identify the constructs, we examined existing instruments and items that had demonstrated empirical evidence of measuring each construct. As discussed in the literature review, given the lack of instruments in our specific context (Latin America), as well as instruments specifically addressing generative AI in the workplace, we focused on identifying relevant constructs and items, even when they originated from different contexts or were originally developed in English. We drafted an initial set of items, which were then adapted, translated into Spanish, and reworded to refer specifically to AI use in workplace tasks.

Third, the draft items were reviewed for content validity by a panel comprising the lead academic, a postdoctoral researcher, and a doctoral student with expertise in AI and education. Items were assessed for clarity, relevance, and construct representation, leading to revisions of seven items. Fourth, the instrument was pilot-tested with 14 Chilean adults to assess clarity and intended interpretation. We conducted think-aloud interviews to gather in-depth participant feedback and identify ambiguous or difficult wording. The research team collectively incorporated the feedback and made the final adjustments to the instrument. Appendix B presents the complete final version of the survey.

4.2 Participants

Participants in the study were users enrolled in Hazlo con IA, a free, online, adaptive AI literacy program. All participants provided informed consent. They were invited to participate voluntarily in the study at two time points: before (PRE) and after (POST) completing the program. Participation involved completing the final version of the instrument at both time points. Additionally, participants were asked to voluntarily provide complementary demographic information, including their gender, age, baseline frequency of AI use, employment sector, education level, and previous experience with AI-related training. Gender was recorded as female, male, not specified, or other. Age was measured in years. Baseline AI-use frequency was assessed using five categories: never, 1 day per week, 2-3 days per week, 4-5 days per week, and 6-7 days per week. Employment sector was classified into three groups: public sector, small and mediumsized enterprises, and healthcare sector. Education level was classified as secondary education, technical education, university undergraduate degree, university undergraduate degree with a diploma, master's degree, doctoral degree/PhD, or other. Previous AI-training experience indicated whether participants had previously completed at least one AI-related course or training program.

A total of 10,764 responses were received in the PRE survey. We initially conducted data cleaning, during which we excluded participants younger than 18 years of age, those who did not consent to the use of their responses for research purposes, and those who provided the same response to all questionnaire items. Cases with missing responses were also removed.

After data cleaning, the final PRE dataset (N = 9,835) included participants aged 18 to 82 years (M = 43.74, SD = 10.60). The sample comprised 55.3% female participants, 44.0% male participants, and 0.7% who selected another gender category or did not specify their gender. The final paired PRE-POST dataset (N = 1,864) included participants aged 18 to 80 years (M = 43.60, SD = 10.20), of whom 55.0% were female, 44.5% were male, and 0.5% selected another gender category or did not specify their gender. 4.3 Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026). Confirmatory factor analysis was performed using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012). Attrition analysis was conducted using functions from the base R stats package, including independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests.

For RQ1, multiple linear regression models were fitted using the stats package. Regression diagnostics were conducted using the car package (Fox & Weisberg, 2019). HC3 robust standard errors were calculated using the sandwich package (Zeileis, 2004), and statistical significance was assessed using the lmtest package (Zeileis & Hothorn, 2002). For RQ2, PRE-POST changes were examined using paired-samples t-tests from the stats package. For RQ3, Type II MANCOVA and follow-up univariate tests were conducted using the car package (Fox & Weisberg, 2019). Estimated marginal means and pairwise comparisons were calculated using the emmeans package (Lenth & Piaskowski, 2026).

4.4 Confirmatory Factor Analysis

To assess the instrument's hypothesized factor structure and provide evidence of construct validity, we conducted a factor analysis using participants' pretest responses.

Since the questionnaire was based on an a priori theoretically specified factor structure, we used CFA for direct testing of whether the observed data are consistent with the hypothesized measurement model. Because the questionnaire items were treated as ordinal, it was conducted using the WLSMV estimator. A nine-factor correlated model was estimated on the full PRE sample (N = 9,835). The scaled model test was statistically significant, χ²(288) = 14,840.11, p < .001. However, given the large sample, the χ² test was not treated as a standalone criterion of model adequacy (Goretzko et al., 2024). The scaled fit indices were CFI = .960, TLI = .952, RMSEA = .072, 90% CI [.071, .073], and SRMR = .044. Robust counterparts were CFI = .939, TLI = .926, and RMSEA = .068, 90% CI [.067, .070]. All standardized factor loadings were substantial, ranging from .643 to .939 (see Table 2). Overall, the model showed acceptable fit.

Internal consistency was assessed using McDonald's omega. Specifically, ω was .887 for self-efficacy, .856 for intrinsic value, .854 for utility value, .856 for attainment value, .804 for time cost, .822 for effort cost, .785 for anxiety, .816 for work support, and .878 for intention to use AI.

Discriminant validity was evaluated using confidence intervals around the standardized latent-factor correlations (Rönkkö & Cho, 2022). Under this CFA-based criterion, two constructs are empirically distinguishable when the confidence interval around their latent correlation excludes unity. The largest latent correlation was between intrinsic value and Utility, r = .871, 95% CI [.862, .879], followed by Utility and Attainment, r = .843, 95% CI [.834, .852]. Thus, even the most strongly related pair remained clearly below unity.

According to Rönkkö and Cho (2022), this falls within marginal range, under which the scales can reasonably be interpreted as measuring distinct constructs. These results indicate that the instrument adequately captured the constructs of interest and was suitable for the subsequent analysis of PRE-POST changes. 5.2 RQ2. How do workers' expectancy, value, cost, and related motivational beliefs about AI change through participation in an online adaptive AI-literacy course?

Before examining PRE-POST changes in workers' motivational beliefs about AI, we assessed potential attrition bias by comparing participants who completed both assessments with those who completed only the pretest. This analysis examined whether the paired sample differed systematically from the pretest-only sample in demographic characteristics and baseline motivational beliefs, providing context for interpreting the subsequent longitudinal findings.

Of the 9,835 participants included in the attrition analysis, 1,864 (18.95%) completed both the pretest and posttest, while 7,971 (81.05%) completed only the pretest. To assess potential attrition bias, we compared the baseline characteristics of these two groups. No significant differences were found in age, t(2896.80) = 0.63, p = .526, gender, χ²(2) = 0.89, p = .641, or educational level, χ²(5) = 9.76, p = .082. However, employment sector differed significantly between groups, χ²(2) = 240.26, p < .001. Public-sector employees were more strongly represented in the paired sample (62.7%) than in the PRE-only sample (48.1%), whereas employees of SMEs were less represented (9.5% vs. 25.8%).

Comparisons of baseline motivational constructs revealed statistically significant differences in intrinsic value (Cohen's d = -0.100, p < .001), utility value (d = -0.137, p < .001), attainment value (d = -0.092, p = .002), and intention to use AI (d = -0.123, p < .001), with all p-values adjusted using the Holm method. Participants in the paired sample reported slightly lower baseline scores on all four constructs, although effect sizes were very small. No significant differences were found for the remaining five constructs.

To examine changes in the study constructs following participation in the AI literacy course, we compared participants' mean PRE and POST scores using paired-samples ttests. To account for multiple comparisons across the nine constructs, p-values were adjusted using the Holm correction. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen's dz, with 95% confidence intervals for both mean changes and effect sizes. Descriptive statistics and the results of the PRE-POST comparisons are presented in Table 3. Overall, scores decreased from PRE to POST across all constructs. These decreases were significant for all variables except work support. However, the effect sizes were small, with the largest decrease observed for intention to use GenAI.

5.3 RQ3. How are sociodemographic characteristics, employment sector, and prior experience with AI associated with changes in workers' motivation?

A MANCOVA was conducted to examine whether changes in the nine outcomes varied as a function of gender, age, baseline frequency of AI use, employment sector, educational level, and previous course experience. Due to the small number of participants who did not identify as either male or female, these participants were excluded from the MANCOVA. The final analytical sample included 1,854 participants. Participants with a doctoral degree were combined with those holding a master's degree into a single postgraduate category.

Type II MANCOVA using Pillai's trace revealed significant multivariate effects of age, V = .016, F(9, 1831) = 3.27, p < .001, and employment sector, V = .020, F(18, 3664) = 2.06, p = .005. No significant multivariate effects were found for gender, baseline frequency of AI use, educational level, or previous course experience (see Table 4).

Follow-up Type II univariate tests were conducted for the nine change scores for age and employment sector, with Holm adjustments applied separately across the nine outcomes for each predictor. After adjustment, only the effect of age on change in perceived work support, F(1, 1839) = 10.70, p = .010, ηp² = .006, and the effect of employment sector on change in intention to use AI, F(2, 1839) = 5.70, p = .031, ηp² = .006, remained statistically significant. A follow-up linear model indicated that age was negatively associated with change in perceived work support, B = -0.0054, SE = 0.0016, t(1839) = -3.27, p = .001.

To clarify the effect of employment sector on change in intention to use AI, we examined estimated marginal means and Holm-adjusted pairwise contrasts . The adjusted mean change was -0.240 (SE = 0.027) for the public-sector group, -0.061 (SE = 0.052) for the SME group, and -0.177 (SE = 0.036) for the healthcare group. Participants in the publicsector group showed a significantly greater decrease in intention to use AI than participants in the SME group (p = .004). The healthcare group did not differ significantly from either the public-sector group (p = .098) or the SME group (p = .098).

6. Discussion

The irruption of GenAI has challenged individuals to develop the skills to operate AI tools and develop the capabilities to assess their outputs critically, recognize their limitations, and make informed decisions about their appropriate use (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021; Benlian & Pinski, 2025). However, multiple limitations remain in the existing research.

The expansion of AI literacy research has not been accompanied by equivalent attention to people who are already in employment. Much of the educational literature has concentrated on school and university students, emphasizing preparation for future participation in an AI-enabled society and labor market (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Adamakis & Rachiotis, 2025). Comparatively less attention has been directed toward evaluating learning opportunities for current workers, who must develop these capabilities while responding to immediate professional demands (Laupichler et al., 2022; Bewersdorff et al., 2025). Additionally, when considering the integration of AI into actual workplace practices, it is important to account not only for employees' knowledge and skills but also for their subjective willingness to restructure established routines and learn new tools. Indeed, much of the research focuses on AI literacy, paying considerably less attention to motivation and its underlying factors. From an EVT perspective (Eccles, 1983 (Eccles, , 2005)), these factors reflect employees' expectations of success, as well as their evaluations of the benefits, personal value, and costs associated with AI use. Finally, much of the existing research has concentrated on specific groups and relatively small or non-representative samples. Additionally, studies have generally assessed motivation at only one point in time, without examining how it may change over time or how these changes may relate to increases in AI knowledge.

Our study aims to address these gaps and limitations in the current literature by studying 9,835 working adults across the public, healthcare, and small and SME sectors. Next, we organize our discussion around our research questions.

6.1. How are motivational factors associated with workers' intentions to use AI?

Our study showed that intentions to use GenAI at work were positively predicted by motivational constructs such as attainment value, intrinsic value, and utility value, as well as perceived work support. This pattern was observed at both measurement points (PRE and POST), suggesting that value components consistently contribute to intentions to use AI. Our findings partially align with Kilat et al. (2026), who found that attainment value and utility value positively predicted intentions to use GenAI among pre-service teachers, whereas intrinsic value was not a significant predictor. Similarly, Sun et al. (2026) found a positive association between utility value and intentions to use GenAI among university faculty, but reported no significant associations for attainment or intrinsic value. Thus, in our sample, all three value components were significantly and positively associated with intentions to use GenAI, whereas previous studies have often demonstrated positive associations for only some of these components.

These differences may be partly explained by sample characteristics, our study included participants from a wide range of occupational sectors, whereas the aforementioned studies focused on workers in education, who may hold conflicting attitudes toward AI due to ongoing debates surrounding its use in educational settings (Lee et al., 2024).

The significant positive contribution of perceived work support further highlights the importance of contextual workplace factors in shaping intentions to use GenAI for workrelated tasks. This is in line with research showing that organizational support is positively related to GenAI valuing and self-efficacy among teachers (Collie et al., 2024), as well as studies linking organizational training to expectations about the usefulness of AI (Cioffi et al., 2026). The present findings extend this evidence to a broader working population and support the idea that motivation to use GenAI should be understood in relation to the professional environment.

We obtained mixed results regarding the contribution of self-efficacy to intentions to use GenAI. Previous research has identified self-efficacy as a positive predictor of intentions to use AI (Cheng., 2026; Sun et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2025). However, we found no significant independent association at PRE when other motivational factors were taken into account, and only a very weak positive association at POST. Thus, our findings suggest that value components may be more important for intentions to use GenAI than employees' beliefs about their ability to successfully perform work-related tasks with its assistance. Although these results do not support the prominent role of self-efficacy described in previous research on AI learning and use, they are broadly consistent with classical expectancy-value theory research, in which expectancies for success was more closely associated with actual performance, whereas value components were more strongly related to willingness to engage in tasks (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Accordingly, our findings suggest that promoting AI adoption in the workplace may benefit more from communicating the value of these technologies to employees than from focusing primarily on increasing their confidence in their ability to use them effectively. However, this assumption should be tested in future intervention studies.

We found no significant independent contribution of perceived costs to intentions to use GenAI at either measurement point. These results support the findings by Sun et al. (2026), but contrast with studies showing negative associations between perceived costs and intentions to use AI (Abuadas & Albikawi, 2026; Yin & Goh, 2025; Yurt & Kaşarcı, 2025). These differences may also be explained by sample characteristics, such as greater awareness of the potential consequences of AI use among employees in educational settings and students, who constitute the main populations examined in these studies. It is worth noting, however, that Yin and Goh (2025) found a negative association between effort cost and intentions to learn about and use AI, but a positive association for emotional cost, suggesting that the role of different cost components is not straightforward. We therefore interpret these findings as suggesting that perceived costs may not be robust predictors of intentions to use GenAI and that their effects are likely to be highly sensitive to task characteristics and individual differences.

6.2. How do participants' motivations change during the AI-literacy intervention?

Examining PRE-POST changes among participants, we observed small decreases in both variables reflecting perceived values and those reflecting perceived costs. At the same time, participants' intention to use AI at work also declined. These results should be interpreted cautiously, as the decreases were statistically significant but very small in magnitude. One possibility is that they partly reflect regression to the mean. Conceptually, however, this interpretation is somewhat challenged by the pattern observed for work support, which did not show a statistically significant decline. This is expected, since, whereas subjective evaluations of motivational components may change relatively fast, characteristics of the work environment should be more stable (Caesens et al., 2023).

On the other hand, a potential response shift could also explain this pattern, suggesting that AI-literacy training may have altered participants' internal standards for evaluating their competencies and motivational beliefs.

Overall, the pattern appears consistent with the possibility that the AI-literacy course contributed to a more calibrated view of AI among employees, reducing both perceptions of the difficulty involved in learning to use it and expectations regarding its actual capabilities. Moreover, these findings are broadly consistent with previous work showing declines in self-efficacy (Lee et al., 2026) and intrinsic motivation (Wu et al., 2025), as well as a reduction in negative attitudes toward the technology as users gain more experience with it (Cao & Wen, 2025). Thus, our findings contribute to the limited evidence on changes in workplace-related motivational beliefs about GenAI, showing that although most measured constructs and behavioral intentions declined significantly, the magnitude of these changes was generally small. At the same time, we can hypothesize that participation in the AI literacy course slightly shifted participants' beliefs toward greater scepticism, encouraging a somewhat more critical assessment of the usefulness and value of this technology, although the study design does not allow us to draw causal conclusions.

6.3. How are participants' sociodemographic characteristics and contextual factors associated with their motivation?

We found that sociodemographic characteristics, previous AI experience, and employment sector showed limited associations with changes in the measured motivational constructs. Previous research has documented substantial demographic differences in GenAI adoption. For example, Humlum and Vestergaard (2024) found that younger workers were more likely to use ChatGPT for professional tasks, and women were significantly less likely to use it than men. Dorta-González et al. (2024) reported lower GenAI use among female researchers and those at more advanced career stages. However, these studies mainly examined differences in actual AI use at a single point in time. Our findings suggest that the same characteristics may be less strongly associated with changes in motivational beliefs and behavioral intentions over time. In particular, gender, education level, baseline frequency of AI use, and previous course experience were not significantly associated with the overall pattern of motivational changes. One possible explanation is related to the design of the Hazlo con IA course, which adapted its content to participants' professional needs. Such adaptation may have made the training relevant to employees with different backgrounds and levels of AI experience, potentially reducing differences in their motivational responses. However, these findings may also suggest that demographic differences in AI adoption do not necessarily translate into equally strong differences in how motivation changes during AI-literacy training.

Among sociodemographic and contextual factors, only age and employment sector showed statistically significant multivariate associations with motivational changes. Older participants reported slightly greater decreases in perceived work support, although the association was very weak (B = -0.0054). Previous research has also identified agerelated differences in GenAI engagement, with older employees reporting less frequent use of GenAI (Marienhagen, 2026). Similarly, Collie and Martin (2024) found that age was negatively correlated with GenAI valuing among school teachers. Our findings extend this evidence by suggesting that age may also be weakly associated with changes in motivational beliefs following AI literacy training. However, the very small effect size and the absence of significant age-related differences in changes in other constructs suggest that age plays only a limited role in shaping employees' motivational responses to training.

Employment sector was associated with changes in intentions to use GenAI: public-sector employees showed a significantly greater decrease than employees of SMEs. Although this sector effect was also small, it suggests that professional context may play a role in how workers' intentions change after training. Previous research has shown that workers in private-sector organizations tend to use GenAI more frequently than those employed in government organizations (e.g., Dorta-González et al., 2024). Our findings further demonstrate that public-sector employees also experienced a greater decline in intentions to use GenAI after AI literacy training. We could hypothesize that it is related to the particular challenges faced by public-sector organizations in terms of data security, accountability, transparency, and the ethical use of AI (OECD, 2026). These concerns may make employees more cautious about integrating GenAI into their professional activities, especially after learning more about its capabilities and limitations. However, a more detailed investigation of the possible reasons for this difference would require an empirical comparison of the two sectors. From an EVT perspective, these findings highlight the importance of considering the professional context in which motivational beliefs develop (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).

7. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the large sample size increases the likelihood of detecting statistically significant effects that may nevertheless be trivial in practical terms; for this reason, we have repeatedly noted that several findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, there was substantial attrition among participants from SMEs between the two measurement points. This raises the possibility of selective retention, when more motivated SME participants were more likely to remain in the study, potentially contributing to the comparatively smaller decline in intention to use GenAI observed in this sector. The role of employment sector should therefore be replicated in other populations and examined using additional variables that can help explain sectoral differences in motivation, including differences in job characteristics, organizational context, and individual differences of employees. Third, although this study provides repeated measurements of the same participants at two time points, it does not provide a fully longitudinal model capable of investigating the dynamics of change across all EVT components. The inclusion of a control group might be also necessary to demonstrate the effect of the AI literacy course on motivation in comparison with the participants who did not take a course. Future research could examine these processes within an integrated longitudinal model and relate changes in motivational beliefs to changes in independently measured AI literacy. Finally, this study did not measure improvements in AI literacy or their relationship with motivation. Therefore, any hypotheses regarding the relationship between AI literacy and motivation should be tested separately.

8. Conclusion

This study showed that employees participating in an AI-literacy course changed their motivation to use GenAI for work-related tasks. These changes appeared to reflect calibration, with participants reporting lower perceived value as well as lower perceived costs associated with integrating GenAI into their work. Changes in motivational factors were also largely consistent across participants: sociodemographic characteristics showed little association with these changes, while the employment sector accounted for only modest differences. At the same time, motivational factors predicted employees' subjective willingness to use GenAI at work. In particular, intrinsic value, utility value, attainment value, and perceived workplace support for AI use were consistently associated with stronger intentions to use GenAI, whereas perceived time and effort costs and anxiety showed no unique associations. These findings suggest that AI-literacy courses aimed at supporting the integration of GenAI into workplace practices should not focus solely on developing technical skills, but also on communicating the practical value of these technologies and strengthening their perceived relevance to employees' professional identity.

Appendix A. Motivation instrument constructs

Through a literature review, we identified the following constructs related to motivation to use GenAI, which provided the conceptual foundation for developing our scale.

Self-Efficacy.

Self-efficacy refers to the belief that one can successfully perform a particular behavioral task in order to achieve a desired outcome (Bandura, 1997). Within the expectancy-value framework, such competence-related beliefs are closely related to expectations of success and are expected to shape individuals' motivation to engage in a task (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). In the context of learning to use AI technologies, selfefficacy refers to confidence in one's ability to successfully learn and apply new AI tools.

In the present study, this subscale consisted of three items adapted and translated from a broader subscale developed by Ng et al. (2024).

Intrinsic value.

Intrinsic value refers to the enjoyment an individual experiences when performing a task and their interest in it (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). In the context of the present study, it reflects workers' enjoyment of and interest in using GenAI in their work. It was measured with three items: one developed for this study and two adapted and translated from the Intrinsic Motivation subscale of Ng et al. (2024).

Utility value.

It refers to the perceived usefulness of a task for achieving current or future goals (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). In the context of AI technologies, utility reflects the extent to which individuals perceive learning and using AI tools as beneficial for their work, productivity, or professional development. In the present study, utility was assessed using three items adapted from the Perceived Usefulness subscale developed by Sohn and Kwon (2020).

Attainment value. This construct refers to the personal importance of doing well on a task, since it is relevant to one's identity or self-concept (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). In the context of AI technologies, attainment reflects the extent to which becoming proficient in the use of AI is personally important and consistent with how individuals view themselves or the kind of professional they want to be. All three items of the subscale were developed specifically for the present study.

Cost (time). This subscale assesses the perceived time-related costs associated with completing a particular task. Three items were adapted from Flake et al. (2015) and slightly reworded to emphasize their relevance to learning and using AI technologies.

Cost (effort). This subscale assesses the perceived difficulty of a task and the effort required to complete it. Two items were adapted from Flake et al. (2015) and slightly expanded so that they directly referred to the use of AI technologies in work-related tasks.

Anxiety. According to EVT, emotional cost can influence decisions about whether to engage in an activity (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Thus, we included an Anxiety subscale to capture the affective component of perceived costs. Three items were selected from the Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale developed by Wang and Wang (2022).

Work Support. Some approaches within the broader expectancy-value framework emphasize the role of social and contextual factors in shaping individuals' expectations of benefits and perceptions of costs (e.g. Mackenzie et al., 2024). Accordingly, we included several items assessing participants' perceptions of the extent to which the use of AI is encouraged and supported in their workplace. The subscale consisted of four items developed specifically for the present study.

Intention to use AI. This variable reflects participants' intention to use AI for workrelated tasks. It was assessed using two items from the Behavioral Intention subscale developed by Sohn and Kwon (2020) and one item developed specifically for the present study.
