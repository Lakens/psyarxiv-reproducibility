Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The brief visual window-often only hundreds of milliseconds-in which drivers predict the arrival of oncoming vehicles and decide whether to give way is critical for safe and efficient manoeuvring. E-mobility devices (EMDs) are a relatively new form of transportation and may challenge these decisions due to their small visual profile and relatively high operating speeds.

Here, we examined whether observers show systematic biases when judging EMDs relative to other common vehicles, potentially contributing to their vulnerability in intersection crashes.

Using naturalistic video stimuli spanning seven vehicle categories, we tested whether observers would overestimate the time-to-arrival (TTA) of EMDs and, consequently, accept smaller gaps for them to pass through an intersection. Consistent with this prediction, EMDs elicited greater biases in both TTA and gap judgements (N = 31 and N = 59), with these effects further amplified on a visually complex road. By comparing across vehicle categories under naturalistic viewing conditions, the results provide evidence that the amplified biases observed for EMDs arise from differences in the underlying visual signals available to observers-specifically, their smaller retinal size and potentially less reliable motion cues at higher speeds. Importantly, these biases were not explained by speed misperception: in a separate two-alternative forced-choice task (N = 49), EMDs were perceived as faster, not slower, than other vehicles at matched speeds. This dissociation indicates that TTA overestimation cannot be attributed to speed underestimation alone. Together, these findings link controlled psychophysical processes to real-world traffic behaviour, with implications for future experimental design and traffic safety policy.

E-mobility devices (EMDs), such as e-scooters and e-bikes, are increasingly popular as alternatives to traditional transport in urban and suburban environments (Field & Jon, 2021; Kathiresh et al., 2022; Yannis et al., 2024). These devices are often praised for their ecofriendliness, accessibility, and convenience, offering a promising solution to the challenges of modern urban traffic (Kathiresh et al., 2022; Yannis et al., 2024). However, their rapid integration into existing transport systems worldwide has also raised significant safety concerns.

Reports frequently highlight risky practices such as riding without helmets, exceeding speed limits, and operating devices while intoxicated (Bai et al., 2015; Che et al., 2021; Field & Jon, 2021; Useche et al., 2022; Ssi Yan Kai et al., 2024). In Queensland, Australia alone, more than six thousand injuries were recorded in connection with EMD usage in a single year, including a dozen fatalities and cases of severe lifelong trauma (Inquiry into e-mobility safety and use in Queensland, 2026).

However, rider behaviour represents only one aspect of the safety problem. A second, less-explored factor concerns how other road users visually perceive these devices. Compared to larger vehicles, EMDs are smaller, quieter, and less perceptually salient, which may influence how their speed, distance, and arrival time are judged in traffic (Kathiresh et al., 2022; Petzoldt et al., 2017; Schleinitz et al., 2016). Errors in perceiving this visual information may contribute to unsafe decisions during traffic interactions (Petzoldt, 2016; Petzoldt et al., 2017; Schleinitz et al., 2016). The current study, therefore, focuses on the perceptual side of the problem by examining how a sample of Australian drivers perform traffic-related visual judgements involving EMDs.

Specifically, we are interested in time-to-arrival (TTA) and gap acceptance tasks. TTA measures how accurately individuals can predict the arrival time of an approaching vehicle (Lee, 1976; Schiff & Oldak, 1990), whereas gap acceptance measures how much space individuals are comfortable leaving between themselves and oncoming traffic to avoid potential collisions (Alexander et al., 2002; Petzoldt et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2007). TTA judgements are thought to provide an important perceptual basis for gap acceptance behaviour, because inaccurate timing (i.e., believing a vehicle will arrive later than it actually does) can lead drivers to leave insufficient space when giving way (Petzoldt, 2014). This overestimation of TTA has been associated with a greater likelihood of accepting shorter gaps when yielding to motorbikes (Lee & Sheppard, 2017; Robbins et al., 2018), and has been proposed as a key explanation for their disproportionately high crash rates (Pai, 2011). EMDs might also be prone to such perceptual errors, as they share similar visual characteristics with motorbikes, such as a small visual profile and relatively high-speed operation. The following sections review the existing literature supporting this account.

Size is an important cue to pictorial distance in human vision: objects closer to the observer are typically perceived as larger, whereas smaller objects are often perceived as farther away. This can produce the illusion that smaller objects will arrive later than larger ones (Caird & Hancock, 1994; DeLucia et al., 2003; DeLucia & Warren, 1994; Hosking & Crassini, 2011), a phenomenon known as the size-to-arrival effect. In traffic contexts, this bias is reflected in systematically longer, more overestimated TTA judgments for smaller vehicles. For example, Caird and Hancock (1994) showed that smaller vehicles, such as motorbikes and compact cars, are judged to arrive later than full-size cars and large vans, despite being at the same distance from the observer.

Additionally, participants often accept shorter, less-safe gaps for smaller vehicles than larger vehicles when crossing or turning. This suggests that the size-based perceptual bias translates to biases in decision-making in traffic contexts. Crucially, this bias is not tied to the vehicle's categorical label per se. When a large truck is digitally rescaled to match the retinal size of an oncoming motorbike, the difference in gap acceptance disappears (Levulis et al., 2015). Therefore, vehicle size can systematically shape both TTA estimation and gap acceptance, with important implications for road safety.

Vehicle Speed and Related Effects

Higher vehicle speeds are generally associated with longer perceived TTAs and smaller accepted gaps, highlighting the importance of speed-limit control (Alexander et al., 2002; Petzoldt et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2007). This issue may be especially relevant for EMDs, which often operate at speeds above those typical of similarly sized vehicles (Schleinitz et al., 2017).

Intuitively, higher EMD speeds could further amplify the size-to-arrival biases, leading observers to overestimate TTA, accept smaller gaps, and potentially make riskier crossing decisions.

However, there is mixed evidence for how approach speed influences EMD perception.

One possibility is that observers use prior knowledge about the high-speed profile of motorised devices to make more cautious decisions (Davies, 2009; Dey et al., 2019; Schleinitz & Petzoldt, 2019). Under this account, EMDs may be given larger, rather than smaller, gaps when their motorisation is clearly signalled. For example, one study found that observers allowed larger gaps when helmet colour indicated that an oncoming vehicle was an e-bike rather than a conventional bike (Schleinitz & Petzoldt, 2019). Because e-bikes and conventional bikes are similar in size, this finding suggests that cues to motorisation (i.e., helmet colour) rather than vehicle type might inform gap judgements. Note that this account predicts a different pattern in gap acceptance from the size-to-arrival account. If observers recognise an approaching vehicle as motorised, they may treat it as faster or more threatening and therefore allow a larger safety margin. Category-based expectations may therefore potentially reduce risk when motorisation is clearly signalled, rather than explaining why EMDs are sometimes given smaller gaps (Petzoldt et al., 2017).

Thus, EMDs may be vulnerable precisely because their motorisation is not always visually obvious. For example, observers may use visible effort cues, such as pedalling, to infer arrival time (Schleinitz et al., 2016). E-bikes may be particularly prone to misjudgements because they can travel at relatively high speeds, with less visible pedalling effort than conventional bikes. As a result, observers may underestimate how quickly they are approaching, overestimate TTA, and accept smaller gaps. Consistent with this account, research has found that e-bikes are consistently judged to arrive later than conventional bikes, and controlling for pedalling frequency seems to reduce their perceived TTA difference (Schleinitz et al., 2016).

However, these effects may depend on the speed range tested. A follow-up study by Schleinitz et al. (2020) found no reliable difference in TTA estimates or gap judgements between e-bikes and conventional bikes when the tested speed range was 25 to 35 km/h, rather than 10 to 25 km/h in their previous research (Schleinitz et al., 2016). E-scooters, however, were given larger gaps than both e-bikes and conventional bicycles, which the authors suggested may reflect greater novelty associated with these vehicles.

Together, these findings suggest that although their high-speed operation may contribute to perceptual biases specific to EMDs, these effects appear to depend on the cues available to observers and the speed range tested. This leaves open the question of exactly how the different speed and size profiles associated with these vehicles in everyday traffic might affect drivers' perception, a question the current research addresses.

The Current Study

The first aim of the current study was to examine whether EMDs' small size influences perceived arrival time and gap acceptance. To our knowledge, no previous research has compared EMDs across a range of everyday vehicle types that differ in visual size. Because EMDs are among the smallest vehicle categories encountered in traffic, we expected observers to exhibit a larger perceptual bias in their responses to these vehicles. Specifically, EMDs would be judged to arrive later (i.e., with greater TTA overestimation) and would be given smaller gaps than larger vehicles, but to a similar extent as other small vehicles (e.g., motorbikes). To test this, we compared two EMDs (e-scooter and e-bike) with three larger vehicle types (cars, SUVs, and trucks) and included other small vehicles (bikes and motorbikes) for comparison. To enhance ecological validity, we used recorded video stimuli rather than the computer-generated simulations used in prior research. This allowed observers to respond under more natural visual conditions, in which cues such as vehicle appearance, lighting, and motion vary as they do in real traffic environments.

Second, as discussed above, speed-related EMD biases depend on the cues observers use and on the speed ranges tested across studies. Because the current study examines drivers' perception under naturalistic conditions, we do not make specific assumptions about which cues participants will rely on. Instead, we allow participants to make judgments as they would in real traffic contexts, and then examine whether the resulting pattern is more consistent with one explanation or another.

To address the inconsistent findings across speed ranges identified in previous studies (Schleinitz et al., 2016 (Schleinitz et al., , 2020)), we examined a broader operating range, from 5 to 35 km/h, in finer 5 km/h increments. This allowed us to test whether EMD bias changed systematically with speed, rather than comparing its influence across discrete speed categories, as in previous research. Given that motion estimation is often less reliable at higher speeds, we expected higher vehicle approach speeds to increase the TTA overestimation, a result typically reported in traffic and perception literature (Petzoldt et al., 2017; Schleinitz et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2001).

Note that we do not make specific predictions about the direction of the gap bias as a function of speed for these vehicles. If observers judge the EMDs as arriving later but nevertheless accept larger gaps, this suggests that higher speeds increase caution toward these vehicles. In contrast, if observers judge the EMDs as arriving later and accept smaller gaps, this will indicate a potentially hazardous perceptual misjudgement: observers may behave as though less space is needed because the arrival of the vehicle appears less imminent than it actually is.

General Method Participants

A simulation-based power analysis was conducted in RStudio using the simr package (Green & MacLeod, 2016). Effect sizes for the EMDs and non-EMD comparisons were parameterised based on those reported by Schleinitz et al. (2020). Data were simulated under a GLMM with a subject-level random intercept, and power to detect the vehicle group × speed interaction was assessed using likelihood-ratio tests. Power was estimated using 50 simulations per sample size, with sample size varied from N = 10 to N = 50 and α = .05. The simulations indicated that at least 40 participants were required to achieve adequate power.

All participants held Australian or international driver's licences (full or provisional) and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Participants had held a driver's licence for between 0.5 and 24 years (M = 4.77, SD = 4.97). Participation was voluntary, and those who took part were compensated with course credits or AUD$15. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The project was approved by the Queensland University of Technology Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number 9745).

Video Stimuli

To maximise ecological validity while maintaining experimental control, we recorded stimuli on a closed-circuit track at the Royal Automobile Club of Queensland (RACQ) Mobility Centre using a Sony AX53 camera (1080p, 60fps). Videos were recorded at two locations on the track: (1) a downhill, long-distance approach and (2) a short-distance approach around a corner.

Examples of the two locations are provided in Supplementary Material E. The camera was positioned from a driver's point of view, in the left-hand lane of a two-way road. In each video, a vehicle travelled toward the camera in the right-hand lane at a controlled speed. Videos were trimmed from the vehicle's first full visibility to its complete exit from frame. The approaching vehicle was one of seven types: a bike, e-scooter, e-bike, motorbike, car, SUV, or truck.

Approach speeds varied in 5km/h increments: 5-30km/h for the bike; 5-35km/h for the e-scooters and e-bike; and from 10-60km/h for the motorbike, car, SUV, and truck.

For the e-scooter, two videos were available at every speed, each featuring a different rider. The riders were similarly dressed in black and were comparable in height and body size. In the main experiment, e-scooter trials were randomly drawn from these two videos and therefore featured either rider. Two riders were also used for the bike stimuli, although this variation was limited to the 10 km/h condition because filming at that speed required additional assistance.

Thus, rider identity was not systematically associated with vehicle type or varied across the full range of bike speeds.

All vehicle-speed combinations were recorded twice at each location (i.e., two videos per vehicle x speed x location) with the following exceptions: the bike was only recorded at Location 1 and only up to 30km/hr due to a tyre failure, the e-scooters at Location 2 could only reach 30km/hr without the steep downhill approach, and for all vehicle speeds above 50km/hr could not be achieved at Location 2 due to the corner approach. Since the videos were filmed outdoors, lighting conditions could not be experimentally controlled. To minimise variability, all videos were recorded on the same day between mid-morning and mid-afternoon. Camera settings were kept consistent across recordings, and for each vehicle x speed x location combination, the clip with the most consistent lighting was selected. This resulted in a total of 124 videos to be used in the experiments.

Speed was measured with a Bushnell Velocity Speed Gun (Model 101911) and crossvalidated against each vehicle's speedometer. Due to the 15km/hr detection limit of the speed gun, 5 and 10km/hr speeds were recorded from the speedometer only. Across overlapping speeds, the recording methods differed by ≤3km/hr.

Apparatus

The experiments were programmed in MATLAB R2024b using Psychtoolbox-3 (v3.0.20; Brainard, 1997) Stimuli were presented full screen on a Dell P2419H monitor (1920 x 1080, 60Hz) at a viewing distance of approximately 55cm. A standard Dell keyboard was provided to participants for Experiment 1 and 3, in which they indicated their responses using the left and right arrows or the space bar, respectively.

Procedure

For all experiments, participants were first presented with information about the task and provided informed consent. They then completed a questionnaire on demographics (age, gender, vision), licence status, and how often they used different modes of transport. Exploratory results on the frequency of EMD usage are provided in Supplementary Material D. After completing the questionnaire, participants viewed an instructional video that presented example screenshots of the experiment. Next, they completed practice trials to familiarise themselves with the task.

After the practice, participants moved on to the respective experiment, throughout which there were five evenly spaced breaks.

Experiment 1 (Time-to-Arrival)

Our first experiment examined the influence of vehicle size and speed on time-to-arrival (TTA) estimation. Based on the size-arrival effect, we hypothesised that e-mobility devices (EMDs; e-scooters and e-bikes) would show greater TTA overestimation than larger vehicles, meaning that observers would judge EMDs as arriving later than larger vehicles. However, we expected this bias to be comparable in magnitude to that observed for other small vehicles. We also hypothesised that higher approach speeds would increase TTA estimates.

Method

Participants

A total of 45 participants completed Experiment 1. However, five were removed due to unexpected technical difficulties (e.g., experiments stopped due to software updates), and another five were removed due to performance deviations from the mean (more than 2 standard deviations). This resulted in a final sample of 35 participants (25 women, 10 men, aged 18-40, Mage = 21.29) for the statistical analysis. They took, on average, 1 hour and 26 minutes to complete the experiment.

Stimuli and Experimental Design

An 'arrival point' (yellow line) was digitally superimposed over the road surface of each video. The original videos were cut to end when the leading edge of the vehicle reached the arrival point. The experiment was a within-subjects design with prediction windows (3 levels: 1, 2, and 3 seconds) x vehicle type (7 levels: bike, e-scooter, e-bike, motorbike, car, SUV, truck) x speed. Speed levels varied by vehicle type (see General Method), resulting in a non-factorial design.

Participants completed a time-to-arrival (TTA) estimation task as per previous research (Horswill et al., 2005; Schleinitz et al., 2016; Sidaway et al., 1996). As shown in Figure 1, each trial started with a fixation period (500ms) that was followed by a 3000ms video of a vehicle approaching the arrival point. When the vehicle was either 1000ms, 2000ms, or 3000ms from reaching the arrival point, the screen changed to a grey background indicating the start of the response window. Participants pressed the spacebar when they estimated the front of the vehicle would have reached the arrival point. The response time (ms) was recorded as the perceived TTA estimation. The design yielded 192 unique trials. Each unique trial was repeated three times for a total of 576 trials. Trial order and the location where the clip was recorded were randomised. Results

One perceptual explanation that has been put forth to explain the rising number of crashes involving EMDs is that their time-to-arrival (TTA) is overestimated, or perceived as arriving later, relative to other vehicle types (Schleinitz et al., 2016). Here, participants (N = 35) with a valid driver's license estimated the arrival time of seven common vehicle types, including e-scooters and e-bikes, relative to an arrival point, with visual information blanked for 1, 2, or 3 seconds. These prediction windows, while substantially shorter than those typically tested in the literature (around 4 to 8 seconds), were selected to ensure that all cropped videos, including the fastest, were able to be shown for a full 3 seconds before reaching the arrival point.

LMM Results Revealed Vehicle-specific Interactions

The linear mixed-effects model (LMM) included vehicle type, prediction window, speed, and their interactions (vehicle × speed; vehicle × prediction window; vehicle × speed × prediction window) as fixed effects, with participant IDs included as a random intercept to account for the within-subjects design. The dependent variable was re-expressed as the ratio of perceived to veridical time-to-arrival (Rdiff), consistent with prior research (Schiff & Oldak, 1990; Schleinitz et al., 2020). Values greater than 1 (Rdiff > 1) indicate overestimation, whereas values less than 1 indicate underestimation of TTA. This ratio was log-transformed prior to the LMM analysis to account for its strictly positive distribution, then back-transformed for visualisation and interpretation.

We compared this model to a baseline model without vehicle effects and found that including vehicle type improved model fit (AIC: 22580 < 22860), supporting our hypothesis that TTA estimation differs across vehicles. An ANOVA summary of the model (Supplementary Material A) indicated that all predictors contributed significantly to participants' responses (all ps < .01). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were all below 3, indicating no problematic multicollinearity among predictors.

Shorter Prediction Windows Increase TTA Overestimation for EMDs.

There was a significant two-way interaction between vehicle type and prediction window, F(12, 17784) = 3.82, p < 0.01. Results are illustrated in Figure 3b. Motorbike TTA was consistently overestimated relative to larger vehicles across all prediction windows (all Tukey-adjusted ps < 0.05), replicating previous findings and indicating a stable bias that is relatively insensitive to time remaining (Horswill et al., 2005).

In contrast, EMDs showed a temporally contingent pattern. The e-scooter TTA was significantly more overestimated than cars, SUVs, and trucks in the 1-s condition (M = 1.96 vs. 1.77-1.85; Tukey-adjusted ps < 0.05), but not at longer prediction windows (all Tukey-adjusted ps > 0.05). E-bikes showed a similar but weaker effect: they were more overestimated than cars at 1 s (p = 0.01), but not SUVs or trucks. Notably, at the longest window (3 s), e-bike overestimation again exceeded all large vehicles (M = 1.45 vs. 1.33-1.37; all Tukey-adjusted ps < 0.05). These findings indicate that, unlike the stable overestimation observed for motorbikes, EMD overestimation may be more time-dependent and most pronounced when prediction windows are the shortest.

Discussion

Consistent with the size-arrival effect (DeLucia & Warren, 1994), we found that EMDs were judged as arriving later than larger vehicles. Building on prior findings (Schleinitz et al., 2020), this effect was moderated by prediction window and speed, with greatest TTA overestimation for e-scooters and e-bikes at higher speeds and shorter prediction windows. This pattern suggests a potential mechanism underlying risky decisions at intersections: when drivers must make rapid judgements based on briefly unfolding approach information, they may overestimate the time available before an approaching EMD arrives, increasing the likelihood of accepting less safe gaps. Experiment 2 builds on this account by directly examining gap acceptance behaviour.

Experiment 2 (Gap Acceptance)

Experiment 1 showed that participants overestimated the time-to-arrival (TTA) of EMDs relative to larger vehicles, consistent with the size-to-arrival effect. Notably, this overestimation increased with speed, and did so more strongly for EMDs than for other vehicle types. This raises an important question: do these perceptual biases influence decision-making? To address this, Experiment 2 examined how vehicle type and speed influence gap acceptance judgments. If the perceptual biases observed in Experiment 1 carry over into behaviour, participants should accept smaller gaps for EMDs compared to other vehicles.

Method

Participants

A total of 59 participants completed Experiment 2. Four were excluded due to technical difficulties. This resulted in a final sample of 55 participants (41 women, 11 men, 3 non-binary people) aged 18-40 years (M = 22.13, SD = 4.92). They took an average of 1 hour and 16 minutes to complete the experiment.

Apparatus and Stimuli

Participants responded using a Logitech G29 Driving Force Racing Wheel and pedals.

The brake pedal was used to register responses, and participants maintained a 'ten and two' position on the steering wheel for a more realistic driving experience. A circular indicator was digitally superimposed in the lower left corner of each video. The circle was green at the onset of the trial and turned red when the participant pressed the brake pedal (Figure 6).

Experimental Design

The study adopted a within-subjects design including vehicle type (7 levels: bike, escooter, e-bike, motorbike, car, SUV, truck), speed, and road type (2 levels: straight vs curved roads) as repeated factors. As speed ranges were not equivalent across vehicle types (see General Methods), the full factorial combination of factors was not possible, yielding a non-factorial design. Participants viewed an oncoming vehicle and pressed the brake pedal when they judged that it was no longer safe to initiate a right-hand turn in front of the vehicle. An example trial is illustrated in Figure 5. Each trial began with a grey background displayed for 500ms, followed by the onset of the stimulus video. The response window commenced at video onset. When a response was made, the video paused, and the green indicator circle turned red for 150ms to confirm the response had been registered. If no response was made by the end of the video clip, the clip replayed until the participant responded. There were 64 unique vehicle x speed x location trials. Each unique trial was presented four times for a total of 256 trials. The trial order was randomised.

Results

Do people behave dangerously by failing to give EMDs sufficient space at intersections?

We showed participants (N = 55) video clips of seven vehicle types, including the EMDs, and asked them to signal stop when they deemed it no longer safe to initiate a crossing of the intersection in front of the oncoming vehicle. The dependent variable, gap acceptance, was defined relative to the time the vehicle reached the intersection, with positive values indicating that participants accepted a gap before the vehicle arrived and negative values indicating a late decision.

On average, participants accepted gaps that allowed vehicles to pass safely (M = 5.67 s, SD = 5.22), confirming that the task was performed as intended. However, gap acceptance varied systematically across vehicle types. Discussion

Consistent with prior research, participants accepted shorter gaps when the vehicle size was smaller (Lee & Sheppard, 2017; Levulis et al., 2015; Robbins et al., 2018). EMDs also received larger gaps overall than bicycles, which is broadly consistent with previous findings. However, because bicycle trials were not available on Road Two, it remains difficult to determine whether this difference reflects motorisation per se or the specific road contexts in which these vehicles were tested. Importantly, we found that EMDs showed the steepest decline in the gap selected as speed increased, indicating that the combined effects of speed and small size might disproportionately influence perceptual judgements involving these devices, similarly to our Experiment 1. Notably, this effect was most pronounced on the curved road (Road 2), suggesting that EMDs are particularly susceptible to miscalibrated gap selection under increased environmental uncertainty.

Importantly, the reduced gap acceptance observed here aligns with the increased overestimation of time-to-arrival for EMDs at higher speeds in Experiment 1, suggesting that speed-related perceptual biases may underlie EMD misjudgements. To test this possibility, Experiment 3 examined speed perception directly, with the hypothesis that EMDs would be underestimated in speed, given the inverse relationship between speed and time-to-arrival.

Experiment 3 (Speed)

Experiments 1 and 2 showed that EMDs are systematically misjudged in both time-toarrival (TTA) and gap acceptance tasks, with these biases strongly modulated by speed. This raises the possibility that speed perception itself may be distorted for EMDs. Because TTA is inversely related to perceived speed, the overestimation of TTA for EMDs that we observed in Experiment 1 would predict that EMDs are perceived as travelling more slowly than they actually are. Accordingly, we hypothesised that, in a two-alternative forced-choice task (2AFC), participants would underestimate the speed of EMDs relative to other vehicles. To keep the design manageable and limit the number of pairwise comparisons, the e-scooter was used as the reference vehicle.

Method

Participants

Forty-one participants (33 women, 8 men) aged 18-49 years (M = 22.88, SD = 7.44) completed Experiment 3. On average, participants took 1 hour and 7 minutes to complete the experiment. All participants' data were retained to ensure adequate statistical power.

Stimuli and Experimental Design

The stimuli were 2000ms snippets extracted from each video. To prevent the vehicle's starting position from cuing its speed, the snippet began at a randomly selected point 6.5, 7, or 7.5 seconds before the end of the original video. If a video was too short to accommodate this window, the snippet began at the start of the original video.

Participants completed a 2AFC speed-discrimination task, indicating whether the vehicle in the first or second clip was travelling faster (using the left or right arrow keys). Each trial paired an e-scooter (reference vehicle) with a comparison vehicle selected from six types: bike, e-bike, motorbike, car, SUV, or truck. The speed difference ratio between the two vehicles ranged from one (identical) to three (one vehicle travelled at three times the speed of the other).

Trials in which the comparison speed exceeded this threefold range were excluded to avoid extreme and potentially uninformative comparisons. This design yielded 252 unique vehicle pairing x e-scooter speed x comparison vehicle speed combinations. Each combination was presented twice for a total of 504 trials. An example trial is illustrated in Figure 11. Each trial started with a grey background (500ms) that was followed by the first clip (2000ms), an inter-stimulus interval (ISI; 500ms), the second clip (2000ms), and the response window. Clip order (i.e., which clip depicted the e-scooter), which vehicle was faster, and whether the clip was shot at Location 1 or 2 were randomised across trials.

Data Processing and Analysis

Raw participant responses were recoded as binary outcomes (1 = e-scooter judged faster,0 = comparison vehicle judged faster). This transformation allowed us to visualise the probability of choosing the reference vehicle (e-scooter) across all vehicle comparisons (Figure 12). Then, to formally quantify bias toward the reference vehicle, we fitted a generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) with a probit link function under a binomial distribution. The model included fixed effects of speed difference (Δspeed = e-scooter -comparison vehicle), vehicle type (categorical), and their interaction. Random intercepts were included to account for subject-level variability.

The probit link maps the linear predictor onto the cumulative Gaussian distribution, allowing model predictions to be interpreted as cumulative normal psychometric functions. Bias was quantified using the point of subjective equality (PSE), defined as the speed at which the predicted probability of judging the e-scooter as faster equalled 0.5. Because the model used a probit link, this corresponds to the value of speed at which the linear predictor equals zero. For each vehicle type, the PSE was thus computed analytically from the model's fixed-effect intercept and slope parameters. The full model equation is provided in the Supplementary Material B.

Results

Our third experiment examined participants' speed judgments of e-scooters relative to six other common road vehicles (e-bike, bike, motorbike, car, SUV, truck) in a two-alternative forced-choice (2-AFC) task. On average, participants achieved 70.73% accuracy (N = 41), indicating they could determine above chance whether the e-scooter or the comparison vehicle was travelling faster. We calculated the proportion of trials in which participants selected the escooter as the faster vehicle, which served as the dependent variable. Descriptive results were visualised in a heatmap (Figure 12), with brighter yellow indicating a higher likelihood of selecting the e-scooter and darker magenta/purple indicating a lower likelihood. The dense cluster of yellow pixels in the top-left corner of the heatmaps suggests that when e-scooters were travelling faster than their comparison vehicles (as indicated by the speed difference between the x-and y-axes), participants were more likely to choose the e-scooter, confirming that they performed the task as instructed.

To address our main hypothesis, we examined whether participants overestimated or underestimated the speed of e-scooters relative to other vehicles. In a 2AFC task, bias is reflected in a systematic preference for one option when both vehicles are objectively moving at the same speed. In Figure 12, the diagonal dashed line represents objective equality (e.g., e-scooter at 10 km/h vs. car at 10 km/h). The irregular line connects points of subjective equality, indicating the speed combinations participants perceived as equally fast. The separation between these two lines reflects perceptual bias. In the car-e-scooter comparison, the subjective equality line was consistently shifted to the right of the objective equality line. This indicates that the car had to be physically faster than the e-scooter to be judged as equally fast, demonstrating a systematic overestimation of e-scooter speed, contrary to our initial hypothesis.

Other vehicle comparisons showed a similar tendency to overestimate the e-scooter's speed, although these effects were less consistent. In these cases, the subjective equality lines fluctuated around the objective equality line rather than remaining cleanly on one side. GLMM Reveals Size-based Speed Judgement

To formally quantify bias in speed judgement in a within-subjects experiment, we generated psychometric functions for each vehicle comparison by fitting a generalised linear mixed-effects model (GLMM). Responses were recoded as binary (1 = e-scooter judged faster,0 = comparison vehicle judged faster). Fixed effects included vehicle type and speed difference, and individual differences were modelled with a nested random-effects structure. The results of this model yielded significant coefficients for speed and for all vehicle comparisons (Supplementary Material B). We adopted a probit link function corresponding to the cumulative Gaussian distribution under a binomial structure, allowing model predictions to be interpreted as psychometric functions (Figure 13a). Two properties were of key interest: the slopes of these functions, which indicate discriminability, and the horizontal shifts of the functions, which indicate bias.

Better Speed Discriminability for Smaller Vehicles.

To quantify the slopes of the psychometric functions for each vehicle comparison, we extracted the model coefficients for vehicle effects as a function of speed difference. Higher values indicate steeper slopes and greater sensitivity in discriminating speed differences between the e-scooter and the comparison vehicle.

Across vehicle types, the average slope of speed difference was β = 0.85, p < .01, indicating that a one-unit increase in speed difference (equivalent to 14 km/h in raw units due to rescaling of speed) produced a substantial increase in the latent decision variable favouring the escooter. Under a probit link, this slope corresponds to a just-noticeable difference of approximately 11 km/h (50%-75% criterion). That is, participants required a speed difference of about 11 km/h to discriminate the speeds of the two vehicles with 75% accuracy. Each vehicle's slope differed significantly from the grand mean reported above (all p < 0.01). Among the comparison vehicles, those of similar size to the e-scooter showed greater discriminability. The bike had the steepest slope, with a positive deviation from the grand mean (β = 0.44), indicating greater-than-average discriminability. This was followed by the e-bike (β = 0.24). In contrast, larger vehicles showed reduced discriminability, as reflected in negative deviations from the grand mean (-0.12 to-0.26). The car had the shallowest slope (β = -0.26), indicating the weakest discrimination performance. Overall, these results roughly follow a sizebased pattern in discriminability: participants were more sensitive to speed differences between the e-scooter and smaller vehicles than between the e-scooter and larger vehicles.

PSE Reveals Consistent Overestimation of E-Scooter Speed.

The model coefficients were then used to derive the point of subjective equality (PSE), providing a quantitative measure of participants' speed bias (Methods). The PSE was defined as the difference between the objectively matched speed level (Sdiff = 0) and the speed level at which participants responded at chance (50%). We summarised the results in the bar graph in Figure 13b. PSE values were consistently negative, indicating that the psychometric functions were shifted leftward relative to objective equality. This pattern suggests that participants judged comparison vehicles to be travelling at the same speed as the e-scooter, when the e-scooter was objectively slower, reflecting an overall overestimation of e-scooter speed. This bias was more pronounced for larger vehicles than for smaller ones (Mlarge = -5.40 km/h vs. Msmall = -0.87 km/h), indicating a clear influence of vehicle size, consistent with the sensitivity results reported above. Among all planned comparisons, the car exhibited the largest bias relative to the escooter, followed by the SUV and truck, whereas smaller vehicles showed comparatively minimal shifts (Figure 13b). Linear mixed-effects tests against zero confirmed that these biases were more reliable for larger vehicles; all showed significant deviations from zero (ps < 0.05). In contrast, among smaller comparison vehicles, only the e-bike was significantly biased relative to the e-scooter (t = -2.14, p = 0.04), whereas the bike and motorbike were not (ps > 0.05). The full table of results is reported in Supplementary Material B.

Discussion

Contrary to our expectations, the 2AFC speed-judgement experiment revealed that escooters were perceived as faster, not slower, than most vehicles, even when their actual speeds were matched. This finding suggests that speed perception may not directly align with the mechanisms underlying time-to-arrival and gap judgement observed in the earlier experiments, given that overestimating time-to-arrival would imply underestimating speed. Instead, these results are consistent with the size-speed illusion literature (Clark et al., 2016; Lee & Sheppard, 2017; Yong & Hsieh, 2017), which suggests that smaller vehicles are perceived as moving faster than larger ones at the same physical speed. General Discussion

Safe road use depends on how well observers can use available visual information to judge and respond to approaching traffic (Hills, 1980). This reliance becomes especially important with the rise of e-mobility devices (EMDs), which may introduce novel visual and motion characteristics into the traffic environment (Petzoldt et al., 2017; Schleinitz et al., 2016).

A key question, therefore, is how safely these devices can be integrated into existing traffic systems. Using naturalistic video stimuli, the present study investigated whether basic visual cues associated with e-scooters and e-bikes influence observers' perception of time-to-arrival and, consequently, their gap decision-making. A follow-up experiment then explored whether differences between vehicles' TTA estimates and gap acceptance may be explained by differences in speed perception.

Small Size Increases EMD Biases

Smaller vehicles are often perceived as farther away, leading drivers to overestimate their time-to-arrival and select shorter gaps to allow them to pass an intersection (Caird & Hancock, 1994; DeLucia et al., 2003; DeLucia & Warren, 1994; Horswill et al., 2005). Consistent with this size-arrival effect, EMDs were systematically misjudged to arrive later than larger vehicles and were assigned smaller gaps. This pattern aligns with our hypothesis and prior findings (Caird & Hancock, 1994; DeLucia et al., 2003; DeLucia & Warren, 1994; Horswill et al., 2005), suggesting that the smaller visual angles subtended by these vehicles may increase their susceptibility to timing misjudgements and, in turn, reduce the safety margins allocated to them.

Importantly, these effects were observed when viewing naturalistic stimuli, supporting the ecological validity of vehicle size effects beyond those observed in driving simulations (Caird & Hancock, 1994; Levulis et al., 2015; Sidaway et al., 1996).

EMD Biases Increase Under High Speeds

Both time-to-arrival (TTA) and gap selection errors increased disproportionately for EMDs at higher speeds, with greater TTA overestimation and reduced gap acceptance, rising more steeply than for larger vehicles and even for similarly sized smaller vehicles such as bikes and motorbikes. This suggests that higher speeds amplify the existing biases associated with EMDs, beyond the contribution of size alone. This finding broadly aligns with previous research highlighting the potential risks of higher speeds associated with EMDs (Petzoldt et al., 2017; Schleinitz et al., 2016).

Our findings extend this work in two important ways: first, by testing a wider, more finely sampled range of approach speeds; and second, by comparing EMDs against multiple everyday vehicles of different physical sizes. This allowed us to show that the speed-related reduction in accepted gaps was not only present for e-bikes, as in Petzoldt et al. (2017), but also extended to e-scooters, and was especially pronounced for EMDs relative to the other common road vehicles. Importantly, we also found that higher speeds were associated with greater TTA overestimation for EMDs relative to other vehicles. This suggests that the speed-related reduction in accepted gaps has a perceptual basis: riskier crossing decisions were accompanied by greater overestimation of the vehicles' TTA. This interpretation is consistent with Petzoldt et al. (2014), who showed that pedestrian crossing decisions were predicted by biases in participants' TTA estimates. Overall, these results suggest that high-speed operation may make EMDs especially vulnerable to risky road-user decisions.

Why Does High-Speed Lead to More EMD Biases?

Two accounts have been proposed to explain why EMDs may be perceived or judged differently at higher speeds. One account suggests that EMDs may be treated as motorised, highspeed vehicles, leading to more cautious gap decisions. For example, Schleinitz and Petzoldt (2019) found that observers gave larger gaps to riders when cues indicated the vehicle was an ebike than when cues indicated it was a conventional bike, even though the same vehicle was used, suggesting that recognising a vehicle as motorised may encourage more cautious decisions.

Our findings are consistent with this interpretation: EMDs were generally associated with larger accepted gaps than conventional bikes. This suggests that, even without explicit labelling, observers may use motorisation to adjust their gap judgements and compensate for the potential risks associated with these vehicles.

However, our results also showed that this behavioural adjustment may not be consistent across speeds. Although EMDs received relatively cautious gap responses at lower speeds, this benefit appeared to decrease more sharply with increasing speed than conventional bikes. This suggests that attempts to give more gap for these devices might not sufficiently scale up as speed increases. Our study, therefore, expands on past studies (Schleinitz et al., 2020; Schleinitz & Petzoldt, 2019), showing that while observers' gap judgement may reflect considering motorisation, this does not necessarily make EMDs safer on the road. In fact, this may be especially problematic because observers may not be able to maintain a consistent gap for these vehicles as speed increases compared to others.

A separate account is that visible pedalling provides a cue to approach speed. Under this account, pedalling may help observers estimate the speed of conventional bikes, but may provide a weaker or less reliable cue for e-bikes when motorisation is not obvious (Schleinitz et al., 2016). Nevertheless, we did not find evidence that e-bikes were systematically overestimated relative to conventional bicycles. Although the difference in perceived TTA between the two vehicle types appeared to increase at higher speeds, the three-way interaction was largely driven by a single prediction-window condition (1 s), in which perceived TTA for bicycles decreased as speed increased. This pattern may partly reflect uncontrolled stimulus-level variation arising from our decision to preserve natural variability across the filmed approaches. Further research using more tightly controlled stimuli is needed to determine whether the observed pattern reflects a genuine vehicle-specific effect or incidental differences in stimulus properties.

Speed Misjudgement: A Possible Mechanism?

Could this speed-dependent effect be driven by a misjudgement of vehicle speed specific to EMDs? In our third experiment, we initially hypothesised that the speed-dependent biases for EMDs in TTA and gap judgements might arise from underestimating their speed. This prediction follows the inverse relationship between time and speed (i.e., time-to-arrival = distance/speed).

Under this account, observers would perceive EMDs as travelling more slowly than they actually are, leading to inflated time-to-arrival estimates and smaller gaps. However, we found the opposite pattern: e-scooters were overestimated in speed relative to most larger vehicles, with this effect attenuated compared with similarly sized small vehicles. This result again highlights the influence of size and is consistent with previous findings on the size-speed illusion (Clark et al., 2016; Petzoldt, 2016; Yong & Hsieh, 2017), whereby smaller objects are perceived as moving faster than larger ones. Nevertheless, if our observers had relied on this bias when making time-to-arrival judgements, they should have underestimated, rather than overestimated, the time-to-arrival of EMDs relative to larger vehicles (Petzoldt, 2016). Moreover, in our experiment, the overestimation of EMD TTA increased with speed, regardless of relative vehicle size, suggesting that a simple size-based account may be insufficient. The observed dissociation, therefore, suggests that time-to-arrival errors for EMDs cannot be fully explained by speed misjudgement alone.

Road Complexity Amplifies EMD Biases

Previous work has shown that environmental factors can influence gap selection, with steeper road gradients leading to smaller accepted gaps for EMDs (Petzoldt et al., 2017).

Extending this work, we found that, compared to steep straight-road contexts, curved-road contexts elicited the most pronounced reduction in accepted gaps for EMDs. In this condition, vehicles emerged from a bend rather than remaining continuously visible, likely increasing visual uncertainty about their approach dynamics. This sensitivity to environmental uncertainty may have important implications for realworld traffic contexts. Detecting and tracking EMDs may become more difficult, for example, when they are partially occluded by larger vehicles or under low-visibility conditions such as at night (Gould et al., 2012; Kennon et al., 2025; Lacherez et al., 2014). Future experiments could therefore examine how occlusion affects EMD perception, particularly because these vehicles may appear unexpectedly from peripheral locations or around corners in dense urban areas (Inquiry into e-mobility safety and use in Queensland, 2026). More broadly, examining how visibility, occlusion, and road complexity interact with EMD motion processing could help identify strategies for reducing risk. Potential interventions include warning signage, restrictions on EMD use in certain road environments, or design changes that improve visual conspicuity.

Research on motorbike safety, for instance, shows that adding auxiliary side headlights can significantly reduce unsafe gap-selection behaviour (Cavallo et al., 2021). The present study contributes to understanding how environmental factors shape perceptual biases in EMD-related driving decisions.

Summary, Limitations, and Policy Implications

We showed that people overestimate the time-to-arrival and may consequently afford smaller gaps to EMDs. Two factors underpinned this effect: size and speed. Examining them in real-world traffic videos, our findings support the notion that the small size of EMDs and their relatively high-speed profiles increase their susceptibility to perceptual and decisional biases, which, in turn, could contribute to their on-road vulnerability. We also found evidence that road complexity modulates these effects, indicating that environmental factors further amplify their existing vulnerabilities.

Several limitations should be considered. First, although we found that overestimation of time-to-arrival coincided with smaller accepted gaps for EMDs, and that both effects were strongly moderated by speed, these results were obtained from separate participant groups.

Establishing a direct link between perception and behaviour would require a repeated-measures design that relates individual performance across both tasks (Petzoldt, 2014). Nevertheless, the current study provides empirical evidence of similar patterns of time-to-arrival bias and gap selection for EMDs (Petzoldt, 2016 (Petzoldt, , 2016;; Schleinitz et al., 2016 Schleinitz et al., , 2020)).

A second limitation concerns stimulus properties. Smaller vehicles in our dataset (ebikes, e-scooters, and bikes) were generally darker in colour than larger vehicles, which were predominantly white. Because higher contrast is associated with faster perceived speed, this difference may have contributed to some of the observed size-based effects (Rideaux & Welchman, 2020; Sudkamp & Souto, 2023; Yong & Hsieh, 2017). However, the aim of the present study was not to control for such variability, but to examine how naturally occurring visual differences in real-world driving environments shape perception and behaviour. The darker appearance of EMDs in our stimuli reflects the typical characteristics of privately owned devices encountered in everyday traffic. Future work could extend this approach by including other commonly encountered EMDs, such as rental devices, which often feature brighter colours to enhance visibility. Comparing these conditions would help determine the extent to which the biases observed here generalise.

From a policy perspective, our findings highlight that the misperception of EMDs is disproportionately affected by speed, with judgements becoming increasingly dangerous compared to other vehicles as speed increases. These results provide empirical support for policies that limit the speed of EMDs to improve safety. Additionally, our results suggest this effect of speed is further exaggerated in environments where the visibility of an approaching EMD is limited (e.g., Road 2). In real-world road environments, approaching vehicles are often only partially visible, such as when they are travelling in blind spots, emerging from behind parked cars, or occluded by other road users or roadside infrastructure. Taken together, these findings suggest that EMDs may be disproportionately vulnerable on the road.

Our findings highlight the importance of designing interventions that account for perceptual limitations in judging EMDs. For instance, improving the visual conspicuity of EMDs, regulating their speeds in high-risk environments, and adapting road infrastructure to enhance visibility and predictability may help mitigate these biases and improve safety outcomes. With the increasing presence of autonomous systems, it is also important to consider how human perceptual biases interact with machine decision-making (Hubmann et al., 2017).

Systems that account for the greater uncertainty associated with EMDs-for example, by adopting more conservative gap-acceptance thresholds-may reduce the risk of unsafe interactions with vulnerable road users.

Road Complexity Changes How People Treat EMDs at Intersections

Road type also significantly influenced spatial buffering, F(1, 25714) = 287.17, p < 0.01. Participants allocated less space on the curved road (M = 33) than on the straight road (M = 41), indicating reduced spatial margins under increased roadway complexity. Further, a significant two-way interaction between vehicle and road showed that this influence varied across vehicle types (F(5, 25714) = 12.18, p < 0.01), suggesting that people allocate space for vehicles flexibly depending on road context. For instance, the E-bike showed a 14.84-metre reduction in the gap on Road Two (t = 15.34, p < 0.01), whereas the E-scooter showed a reduction of around 10.20 metres (t = 9.71, p < 0.01). The remaining vehicle types ranged from a reduction of 1.24 to 11.67, with the SUV being the only type that did not show a significant change (t = 1.83, p = 0.06). These results corroborate the time-based analysis, suggesting that driving behaviours observed on a straight road might not always translate to more complex road structures, such as those with bends.

Finally, a significant three-way interaction among vehicle type, road, and speed emerged, F (5, 25714) = 16.97, p < 0.01. Here, we focus on contrasts between EMDs and other vehicles. Model predictions were generated at low and high speeds (10 km/h to 25 km/h), corresponding to the range in which all vehicle types had empirical observations the larger vehicles at the low speed (MEMDs = 18.26 vs Mlarge = 25.79, ts = -7.28, ps < 0.05), treating them equivalent to bicycles (Mbike = 15.06, ts = 3.15, ps > 0.05).

This pattern suggests that at higher speeds (~25km/h), EMDs may be perceived as carrying a risk profile more similar to that of motorised two-wheelers.

On Road 2 (a curved road), both EMDs (the E-bike and E-scooter) seemed to be given a similar amount of space as motorbikes, even at lower speeds (MEMDs = 24.05 vs Mmotorbike = 26.17; ts = -1,88, ps > 0.05), consistent with the idea that curved-road contexts elevate perceived uncertainty. Supplementary Material C: Gap Judgement in Spatial Units

Using the same fixed-and random-effects structure, we fitted an additional model to predict participants' accepted spatial distance. Spatial distance was derived by multiplying the reported temporal gap by vehicle speed and converting the result to meters. This transformation does not assume veridical speed perception; rather, it provides a mathematical conversion that expresses time-based judgements in spatial units, enabling direct comparison with physical clearance distances that would otherwise be difficult to estimate from naturalistic scene videos. Full model coefficients are reported in the Supplementary Material A.

The distance gap increased systematically with speed (Figure 1a). This is notable because, in the time-based model, accepted temporal gaps decreased as speed increased. If temporal reductions fully compensated for higher speeds, spatial distance would not increase systematically. Instead, speed remained a strong positive predictor of spatial gap acceptance, F(1, 25714) = 2292.88, p < 0.01, indicating that spatial margins expanded as speed increased, despite shorter reported time gaps. Because spatial distance was derived from temporal reports rather than directly measured, we cannot conclusively determine whether this expansion reflects deliberate increases in safety buffering or misestimation of vehicle speed.

Nevertheless, the systematic speed effect indicates that participants' responses did not collapse spatial clearance at higher speeds but instead resulted in larger accepted physical gaps.
