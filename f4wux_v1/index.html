Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

As healthcare systems increasingly emphasize value-based care, evidence regarding the economic implications of nonpharmacologic interventions is needed to inform implementation and reimbursement decisions. This study aimed to assess the economic value of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, identify clinical populations in which economic evaluations have been conducted, and assess the strength of the available economic evidence.

All clinical populations and healthcare settings.

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Metaanalyses 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered (CRD420250648516). Nine databases, including MEDLINE, Embase and Scopus, were searched from inception through March 2026 without language restriction, with reference-list screening and citation tracking. Eligible studies evaluated Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction and at least one economic outcome. Two reviewers independently screened records, extracted data, and assessed quality using the Quality of Health Economic Studies instrument and the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 checklist.

Among 468 identified records, 12 economic evaluations met inclusion criteria. Studies included 8 randomized clinical trials, 2 Markov models, and 2 prospective cohort studies, conducted in North America (6) and Europe (6). Chronic pain conditions were most frequent (6), followed by mental health disorders (3), breast cancer survivorship (2), and pulmonary arterial hypertension (1). Across these populations, reductions in healthcare utilization and productivity losses generally offset delivery costs, resulting in lower overall costs with equivalent or improved health outcomes for Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction compared with treatment-as-usual. Results were also favorable or comparable relative to active comparators including cognitive behavioral therapy. Despite intervention costs of $195 to $866 per participant (mean, $461), all cost-utility analyses comparing Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction with treatment-as-usual over at least 6 months reported lower costs with equivalent or improved outcomes. Methodological quality was high, with 10 of 12 studies meeting above 80% quality criteria and 5 achieving 100% adherence. Reporting transparency was generally strong.

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction was associated with favorable economic outcomes across chronic pain, mental health, and breast cancer survivorship populations. Current evidence suggests that MBSR may represent a high-value nonpharmacologic intervention, with reductions in healthcare utilization and productivity losses frequently offsetting intervention costs. Additional economic evaluations are needed in underrepresented populations, healthcare settings, and delivery models.

 This is the first systematic review focused exclusively on the economic value of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) independent of other mindfulness-based interventions.

 A comprehensive search strategy was developed with librarian support, peer reviewed using the PRESS checklist, and applied across nine databases without language restrictions.  Economic evidence was evaluated using established frameworks for both methodological quality (QHES) and reporting transparency (CHEERS 2022), with assessments conducted independently by multiple reviewers.

 Included studies represented diverse clinical populations, healthcare settings, economic perspectives, and evaluation types, allowing assessment of economic outcomes across a broad range of contexts.

 Substantial heterogeneity in populations, comparators, cost inputs, perspectives, and time horizons precluded quantitative pooling of results, and available evidence was largely limited to North America and Europe.

Introduction

Healthcare systems worldwide face growing challenges from chronic pain, mental health disorders, stressrelated illness, and cancer survivorship, conditions associated with high healthcare utilization, disability, and escalating costs. 1 2 Chronic pain affects approximately 25% of US adults with annual costs of $722.8 billion, 3 while major depressive disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder carry estimated annual economic burdens of $382.4 billion and $232.2 billion, respectively. 4 5 Anxiety and other stress-related conditions are similarly linked to lost productivity and diminished quality of life. 6 7 These challenges, alongside provider shortages and concerns regarding opioid use and long-term pharmacologic management, have increased interest in effective, scalable nonpharmacologic care approaches. [8] [9] [10] [11] Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn in 1979, is among the most widely disseminated and standardized mindfulness-based interventions. 12 Delivered primarily in groups, MBSR integrates meditation, gentle yoga, and psychoeducation to cultivate awareness and adaptive responses to physical and emotional distress. 13 14 Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and metaanalyses demonstrate benefits for psychological distress, health-related quality of life, pain-related outcomes, and stress symptoms across diverse populations. [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] Despite this evidence base, MBSR integration into healthcare delivery and reimbursement systems remains limited outside programs such as the Veterans Health Administration Whole Health System. [21] [22] [23] Broader implementation requires evidence of both clinical benefit and economic value. 24 25 Previous systematic reviews suggest mindfulness-based interventions may be cost-effective; however, most combined MBSR with other third-wave interventions, including Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, limiting MBSR-specific conclusions. [26] [27] [28] Furthermore, MBSR economic evaluations have been published since the latest comprehensive reviews. 29 30 Therefore, an updated synthesis focused specifically on MBSR is warranted. The recommendations in this systematic review were informed by integrated knowledge translation research (IKT) involving health insurers, policymakers, healthcare administrators, clinicians, MBSR teacher-trainers and teachers, and patients. 31 These groups identified cost-effectiveness of MBSR as needed to understand in order to advance access to MBSR. 31 The objective of this systematic review was to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of MBSR across clinical populations and healthcare settings, and the quality of the available economic evidence.

Methods

This review was registered with PROSPERO in March, 2025 (CRD420250648516) and reported following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. 32 A US-based advisory panel informed review priorities and scope early in the project. 22

METHODOLOGICAL QUALITY AND REPORTING TRANSPARENCY

Methodological quality of economic evaluations was assessed using the Quality of Health Economic Studies (QHES) instrument, 33 34 and reporting transparency using the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022 checklist. 35 Quality assessments were conducted in duplicate (RLA, RG, NAL). For details, see eAppendix 1.

ELIGIBILITY CRITERIA

Eligibility criteria used the TOPICS+M framework (Time, Outcomes, Population, Intervention, Comparison, Setting, and Moderators). 36 Eligible studies evaluated standard MBSR, including minor pragmatic adaptations (e.g., modestly shorter sessions, fewer sessions, or retreat removal, detailed in eAppendix 2), provided MBSR remained the primary intervention component, and reported at least 1 economic outcome. No restrictions were placed on population, setting, study design, follow-up duration, comparator, or moderator. Results were classified by type of economic evaluation and health condition.

SEARCH STRATEGY

The research librarian (LH), along with experts in mindfulness and health economics, developed an Ovid MEDLINE search using MeSH terms and keywords for MBSR and economic evaluation. A second librarian peer-reviewed it using the PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies) checklist. 37 The final search was run in Ovid MEDLINE and translated for Embase, Scopus, APA PsycINFO, the NHS Economic Evaluation Database (NHS EED), the International HTA Database, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses, the Tufts Cost-Effectiveness Analysis Registry, and CINAHL. Additional databases searched yielded no new citations. All searches appear in eTable 1.

Searches covered database inception through March 2026, without language restrictions, and were supplemented by reference-list screening and reverse citation tracking. Records were uploaded into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for deduplication, screening, and review management.

STUDY SELECTION AND DATA EXTRACTION

Two scientists (RLA, RG) independently screened all titles/abstracts and full-text articles. Inter-rater reliability at the full-text review stage was good (κ=0.63) according to the rating scale proposed by Cicchetti (1994). 38 Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with input from a senior scientist when necessary (EBL). Two reviewers (RLA, RG) independently extracted study characteristics, intervention and comparator details, economic perspectives, costing methods, time horizons, uncertainty analyses, and reported economic outcomes.

ECONOMIC OUTCOMES AND EVIDENCE SYNTHESIS

Because this review sought to synthesize all forms of economic evaluation, no single primary type of economic evaluation was prespecified. Included studies reported a broad range of economic components and evaluation types, including intervention delivery costs, healthcare utilization costs, direct and indirect costs, implementation costs, cost-effectiveness outcomes, and cost-utility outcomes. Cost-utility analyses (CUAs) reporting incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) in terms of cost per increase in qualityadjusted life-years (QALYs) were considered the most rigorous and policy-relevant analyses, thus were prioritized in the synthesis when available. Economic findings were synthesized narratively across clinical populations, analytic perspectives, comparator types, and economic evaluation types. Meta-analysis of economic outcomes was not performed because economic evaluations often differ in the cost components included and other context-and setting-specific factors, including healthcare delivery, resource use, implementation, and local cost structures, limiting the transferability and interpretability of pooled economic estimates. [39] [40] [41] Accordingly, interpretation focused on the overall direction and consistency of economic findings rather than pooled quantitative estimates.

Comparator interventions were categorized as treatment-as-usual (TAU) when MBSR was compared to any form of usual standard care. Economic perspectives were classified according to cost inputs rather than author-reported terminology and grouped into 3 categories: payer, healthcare sector, and societal. The payer perspective included direct healthcare costs reimbursed by a healthcare payer; the healthcare sector perspective included all direct healthcare costs regardless of who pays; and the societal perspective included both healthcare and non-healthcare costs, including productivity losses, no matter who pays. 42 43 To facilitate interpretation of economic findings across studies, CUA results were mapped onto a cost-effectiveness decision matrix categorizing interventions according to relative differences in costs and health outcomes (e.g., lower cost with improved outcomes). Separate matrices are shown for studies comparing to TAU and to active comparators.

COST STANDARDIZATION

Intervention delivery costs were extracted as reported in individual studies. Costs reported in non-U.S. currencies were converted to U.S. dollars using average exchange rates for the reported price year and subsequently inflated to 2025 U.S. dollars using the medical care component of the Consumer Price Index. 44

Results

STUDY SELECTION

The literature search identified 468 records across databases, with one additional study identified through citation searching (eFigure 1 -PRISMA Flow Diagram). After removal of duplicates, 287 records underwent title and abstract screening, and 67 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. In total, 12 economic evaluations met the inclusion criteria (eTable 4).

STUDY CHARACTERISTICS

Study characteristics, cost inputs and analytic perspectives, and economic outcomes are summarized in Tables 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Five economic evaluations were conducted in the United States, six from four European countries (Spain, Denmark, Sweden, and the United Kingdom), and one in Canada. Eight were full economic evaluations using CUA, including six primary trial-based evaluations and two simulation modeling studies. Three studies were partial economic evaluations, and one was a pilot feasibility study assessing intervention delivery costs and the feasibility of collecting healthcare utilization data for a future economic evaluation. Study design included 8 RCTs, 2 prospective cohort trials, and 2 mathematical modeling studies using decision-analytic (Markov) models that synthesized evidence from multiple sources and simulated transitions between health states to estimate costs and health outcomes. Chronic pain-related conditions were the most studied population (6), followed by mental health disorders (3), breast cancer survivorship (2), and pulmonary arterial hypertension (1). Cost inputs varied across studies.

METHODOLOGICAL QUALITY

Using the QHES instrument to evaluate methodological quality of the included economic evaluations (eTable 2), 33 five studies achieved a perfect QHES score, meeting 100% of assessed criteria. Five additional studies adhered to 81-89% of the criteria, while one non-randomized study 45 met 78% and the feasibility study 46 met 61% (Table 3).

Common methodological limitations included limited discussion of the direction and magnitude of potential biases (6 studies), incomplete handling of uncertainty through sensitivity analyses (5 studies), and time horizons that were deemed insufficient to capture all relevant outcomes (3 studies). Partial economic evaluations were additionally limited by the absence of QALY-based outcomes and reduced ability to provide a formal assessment of cost-effectiveness relative to alternative interventions.

REPORTING TRANSPARENCY

Across the 12 studies, reporting was strong for the descriptive CHEERS items (eTable 3), and all but one study disclosed funding sources. Notable methodological gaps were observed. Only 2 studies reported a formal health economic analysis plan, use of discount rates, or patient/stakeholder engagement. Only 1 study described how patient/stakeholder engagement influenced findings, and only 1 considered equity.

Four studies did not include the currency/price year.

ECONOMIC OUTCOMES BY HEALTH CONDITION

Economic findings were generally favorable across health conditions (Table 3 and Figure 1; details in eAppendix 3). Overall, the most consistent results were observed in chronic pain-related conditions, where multiple trial-based and model-based economic evaluations (k=6) demonstrated dominance of MBSR over TAU (health improvements and cost savings) from the payer, healthcare, and societal perspectives in a variety of settings. The two modeling studies predicted greater economic value for older adults (aged ≥80 years) 47 and those with more severe pain. 47 48 Studies in mental health conditions (k=3) similarly reported lower costs with comparable or improved health outcomes relative to usual care from all perspectives, and in several settings. Evidence among breast cancer survivors was more limited (k=2) but included one recent CUA demonstrating lower costs and improved outcomes from the payer and societal perspectives compared with an active control intervention. A single feasibility-focused study in pulmonary arterial hypertension evaluated implementation costs but did not assess comparative cost-effectiveness.

COMPARATOR EFFECTS

As illustrated in the cost-effectiveness matrix (Figure 1), economic findings varied by comparator type. When compared with TAU, MBSR ultimately saved costs despite the upfront cost of intervention delivery (see Table 4 for intervention costs), with all full CUA studies with sufficient time horizons reporting lower costs together with improved or equivalent health outcomes. TAU reflected heterogeneous standard care approaches varying by population and level of care.

Relative to evidence-based behavioral therapies, MBSR demonstrated economically favorable or comparable outcomes. Compared with a frontline behavioral treatment, specifically CBT, MBSR was associated with lower costs and comparable health outcomes for those with chronic low back pain. 49 Similarly, compared with attachment-based compassion therapy (ABCT), MBSR produced marginally greater health benefits at slightly higher cost for mental health patients. 29 MBSR also performed favorably when compared with other active comparators, including educational, attention-matched, and self-management interventions designed to control nonspecific treatment effects. MBSR was dominant relative to a breast cancer education support group (BCES), producing both lower costs and improved health outcomes, 30 and dominant relative to home exercise and advice in a model-based analysis of chronic pain. 47 In contrast, MBSR demonstrated comparable costs and health outcomes relative to FibroQoL, a multicomponent intervention developed specifically for individuals with fibromyalgia. 50

ECONOMIC PERSPECTIVE

Studies that evaluated more than one perspective reported similar findings across perspectives. Use of the payer perspective was most prevalent (9 studies). A healthcare sector perspective was used in two studies, and a societal perspective was used in seven; several studies reported more than one perspective.

DRIVERS OF COST DIFFERENCES

From the healthcare and payer perspectives, across studies conducted in various health systems in North America and Europe, the cost savings associated with MBSR were driven by reduced healthcare utilization. Common contributors included decreases in outpatient visits, primary care encounters, hospital admissions, emergency department utilization, diagnostic testing, and laboratory costs.

Specifically, studies reported fewer emergency room visits, hospitalizations, and diagnostic tests among individuals with breast cancer, 30 fewer primary care visits among individuals with mental health conditions, 29 and reductions in both condition-related and unrelated costs among individuals with chronic low back pain. 49 From the societal perspective, cost savings reflected both reduced healthcare use and improvements in workplace functioning, including reduced absenteeism and productivity losses among individuals with fibromyalgia, 50 and reductions in disability pension use in pain populations. 51

UNCERTAINTY AND SENSITIVITY ANALYSES

Uncertainty and sensitivity analyses were conducted in seven studies using methods such as nonparametric bootstrapping, probabilistic sensitivity analyses, scenario analyses, and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves. Overall, these analyses generally supported the robustness of the primary findings, with economic conclusions remaining favorable across a range of assumptions despite uncertainty in some incremental cost estimates. Detailed descriptions and results of the uncertainty and sensitivity analyses are provided in eAppendix 4.

TIME HORIZON

Time horizons ranged from 8 weeks to 5 years, with one study projecting 20 years. 52 Most findings used a 1-year time horizon. Two studies were rated as having insufficient time horizons on QHES item 8 because follow-up was limited to 8 and 12 weeks. 30 46 53 Details appear in eAppendix 5.

INTERVENTION DELIVERY COSTS

Reported per-participant intervention delivery costs varied substantially across studies. After standardization to 2025 USD, estimated intervention costs ranged from $195 to $866, with a mean cost of $461. This estimate excludes two studies reporting higher than typical costs due to low attendance and unclear cost inputs (Table 4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this systematic review is the first to focus exclusively on the economic value of MBSR independent of other mindfulness-based interventions. Although economic evaluations are inherently context-dependent, studies conducted across diverse healthcare settings and analytic perspectives reached similar conclusions regarding the economic value of MBSR. Across chronic pain, mental health, and breast cancer survivorship populations, MBSR was associated with favorable economic outcomes, with reductions in healthcare utilization and productivity losses generally offsetting intervention costs and often resulting in overall cost savings (Figure 1). Findings were consistent across heterogeneous settings and study designs, including randomized clinical trials, prospective cohort studies, and decision-analytic models. Methodological quality was generally rated high, with 10 of 12 studies meeting more than 80% of QHES criteria, and reporting transparency generally strong according to CHEERS 2022.

The largest number of studies were observed in chronic pain-related populations, which accounted for half of the 12 included studies. Across low back pain, fibromyalgia, and mixed chronic pain-related conditions, MBSR consistently demonstrated cost savings, favorable cost-effectiveness, or both. Given the burden of chronic pain, these findings suggest it may be among the strongest candidates for broader implementation and reimbursement of MBSR. Modeling simulations further suggest that economic benefits may be even greater among older adults and those with more severe pain, highlighting the importance of future research aimed at identifying the patient subgroups most likely to derive clinical and economic benefit.

As expected, results were more favorable when MBSR was compared to TAU rather than active controls. Importantly, however, MBSR remained economically competitive with established psychotherapies, producing outcomes similar to CBT at lower cost and comparable economic value compared with other active behavioral interventions. Because MBSR was cost-effective at conventional thresholds and compared favorably with CBT, a therapy already reimbursed and guideline-recommended for chronic pain, these findings support consideration of MBSR as a reimbursable option in routine care. 54 Although MBSR requires an upfront investment (mean intervention cost, approximately $461 per participant), these costs were frequently offset by fewer primary care visits, emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and diagnostic tests. Societal-perspective analyses also identified reductions in absenteeism, productivity losses, and disability-related costs. 50 51 These findings suggest that MBSR may yield benefits for healthcare systems, employers, and society.

Most evaluations examined costs and outcomes no longer than 6 or 12 months. Although this timeframe captures many relevant costs and outcomes, it may not fully reflect longer-term economic consequences. The favorable findings observed within these follow-up periods suggest that MBSR can generate economic value in the near to medium term. Future research should evaluate whether these benefits are sustained over longer periods and whether strategies such as booster sessions or digital supports are needed to maintain long-term clinical and economic gains.

Despite favorable economic evidence, integration of MBSR into routine care remains limited. This gap may reflect reimbursement barriers, implementation challenges, workforce constraints, and limited awareness of the growing economic evidence base. Notably, mindfulness-based interventions are recommended within contemporary nonpharmacologic approaches to chronic pain management, yet coverage and adoption remain inconsistent across healthcare settings. 55 56 A 2026 multisite RCT (n=451) demonstrated that MBSR delivered through health insurance-reimbursable group medical visits resulted in significant reductions in pain intensity and pain interference, with benefits maintained for up to one year. 57 In that trial, MBSR was delivered in 2-hour sessions (consistent with the original MBSR format), to groups of 4 to 14 participants, without a retreat day, to align with existing insurance reimbursement structures. 58 In 2026, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, stated that group medical visits typically occur within 60-to 120-minute time blocks and further proposed limiting reimbursement to a maximum session duration of 60 minutes. 59 Because current evidence for mindfulness-based programs is derived primarily from 120-to 150-minute sessions, the effectiveness of shorter reimbursable formats remains uncertain. Reimbursement policies should therefore consider aligning covered session durations with the formats evaluated in clinical and economic studies.

LIMITATIONS

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Included studies demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in economic perspectives, cost inputs, healthcare settings, currencies, time horizons, comparators, and patient populations. Despite this variability, findings were consistent across studies. Evaluations were conducted exclusively in North America and Europe. Most focused on chronic pain, mental health conditions, or breast cancer survivorship, and used follow-up periods of approximately 1 year or less, limiting generalizability to other regions, settings, health conditions, and longer-term economic effects. In addition, none of the identified studies evaluated online or hybrid MBSR delivery models, representing an important evidence gap given increasing interest in scalable implementation approaches. Economic evaluations also remain absent across several conditions for which evidence of MBSR's clinical benefit continues to accumulate, including posttraumatic stress disorder, sleep disorders, clinician burnout, substance use disorders, migraine, arthritis, and other chronic health conditions.

Conclusions

This systematic review found that MBSR was associated with favorable economic outcomes, particularly in chronic pain populations. Across healthcare settings, analytic perspectives, and study designs, MBSR was associated with similar or improved outcomes at comparable or lower costs, with savings driven by reductions in healthcare utilization and, where examined, improvements in worker productivity. eAppendix 1: Details on Methodological Quality and Reporting Transparency

Quality assessments were conducted independently by two reviewers (RLA and RG). Following RG's departure from the study team, a third reviewer (NAL) independently reviewed the assessments and completed additional evaluations as needed. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus between RLA and NAL.

The Quality of Health Economic Studies (QHES) instrument is a 16-item tool that evaluates the methodological quality of economic analyses, including study design, data sources, analytic methods, handling of uncertainty, and transparency of reporting. Studies were assigned an overall QHES quality score on a standardized 0-100 scale, with higher scores indicating greater methodological rigor. The QHES criteria evaluate whether studies clearly define objectives and analytic perspective, use high-quality data sources, and apply appropriate methods for measuring and valuing costs and outcomes. It also assesses critical analytic components, including incremental analysis, handling of uncertainty (e.g., statistical and sensitivity analyses), appropriate time horizons and discounting, and the validity and reliability of outcome measures. In addition, the QHES criteria emphasize transparency in reporting, requiring clear description of model structure, assumptions, data abstraction methods, and potential sources of bias, as well as justification of conclusions and disclosure of funding sources.

Evidence level was classified using a modified Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine hierarchy consistent with JAMA Health Forum recommendations: Level 1, Properly powered and conducted randomized clinical trial; systematic review with meta-analysis; Level 2, Well-designed controlled trial without randomization; prospective comparative cohort trial; Level 3, Case-control studies; retrospective cohort study; Level 4, Case series with or without intervention; cross-sectional study; Level 5, Opinion of respected authorities; case reports. Quality rating did not include economic outcome-related statistical power into evidence level assignments because studies are typically powered for clinical outcomes rather than economic outcomes.

CHEERS 2022 is a 28-item checklist developed by the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research to promote transparency and consistency in economic evaluation reporting. Adherence to CHEERS 2022 reporting standards was summarized descriptively as the proportion of applicable items reported per study. Consistent with guidance for reporting checklists, aggregate CHEERS scores were not interpreted as measures of methodological quality.

eAppendix 2: Details on Study Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible if they evaluated standard MBSR delivered according to the intervention manual (e.g., in a guided group-based format, including in-person or synchronous online delivery). Minor protocol modifications consistent with pragmatic clinical implementation were permitted (acceptable modifications include reducing or removing the all-day retreat, shortening the weekly sessions by up to 30% from the standard 2.5 hours, shortening the number of weekly sessions up to 25% from the standard 8 weekly sessions). Studies combining MBSR with usual clinical care or closely related mindfulness-based interventions were eligible, provided that MBSR remained a core intervention component.

eAppendix 3: Economic Outcomes by Clinical Population

Chronic Pain and Related Persistent Somatic Symptom Conditions

Chronic pain-related conditions represented the largest evidence base identified in this review (Tables 1 2 3). Two RCT-based CUAs, 1,2 two model-based CUAs, 3, 4 and two partial economic evaluations 5, 6 evaluated MBSR among chronic low back pain, chronic neck/back pain, fibromyalgia, bodily distress syndrome, and medically unexplained symptom populations. Sample sizes ranged from 93 to 342 participants, and all but one study incorporated time horizons of at least one year. Although medically unexplained symptoms and bodily distress syndrome are not defined solely by pain, they commonly involve persistent pain and other somatic symptoms and were therefore included within the chronic pain and related persistent somatic symptom category.

Across these studies, MBSR was consistently associated with economically favorable findings when compared with treatment as usual and economically comparable findings when compared with active behavioral comparators. Both model-based studies projected greater economic value among individuals with high-impact chronic pain 3, 4 , and one additionally projected greater value among older adults. 4 Partial economic evaluations additionally suggested reductions in healthcare utilization, outpatient utilization, and disability-related costs among patients with bodily distress syndrome and medically unexplained symptoms. Herman et al. (2017) conducted the largest and most comprehensive trial-based CUA among adults with chronic low back pain. 1 Relative to usual care, MBSR was associated with lower total costs and improved health-related quality of life from both healthcare/payer and societal perspectives. Incremental QALY gain was 0.034 (95% CI: 0.008 to 0.060), with approximately 90% probability of cost-effectiveness at a willingness-to-pay threshold of $50,000/QALY.

Herman (2020) used a Markov model to extend these findings; 3 the model facilitates comparisons across interventions not directly compared in trials, using consistent outcome measures after balancing the baseline mix of patients. Model results predicted that the costeffectiveness of MBSR is similar to CBT for a typical patient mix, but MBSR is twice as effective for those with high-impact chronic pain. Outcomes also differed by pain impact level, emphasizing the need to measure CLBP subgroups.

Leininger (2023; thesis), 4 a similar modeling study using different comparators and subgroups, predicted MBSR to be dominant over the base case of home exercise and advice, and the value of MBSR treatment was increased for hypothetical populations of high-impact pain and advanced age (80-year-old population). This model also included adverse effects and found costeffectiveness of medications was lower in older vs younger (80yrs vs 40 yrs), and the risk of adverse events from medication was higher for older vs younger. Authors note that in this model, the cost-effectiveness of MBSR is comparable to therapies that are often covered by insurance for chronic pain conditions: massage, CBT, and spinal manipulation therapy.

Pérez-Aranda et al.

(2019) evaluated adults with fibromyalgia over a 12-month time horizon and similarly reported lower costs and improved QALYs relative to treatment as usual from both healthcare and governmental perspectives. 2 In contrast, comparisons against FibroQoL, an active multicomponent educational comparator, demonstrated substantially smaller differences in both costs and outcomes.

Mental Health Disorders

Mental health disorders represented a smaller but generally favorable evidence base identified in this review (Tables 1 2 3). Two RCT-based CUAs evaluated MBSR or mindfulness-based group therapy among adults with depression, anxiety, stress-related disorders, and adjustment disorders. 7, 8 Across these studies, MBSR-based interventions were associated with economically favorable findings when compared with treatment as usual and economically comparable findings when compared with active behavioral interventions.

D'Amico et al. (2024) reported favorable cost-utility findings for both MBSR and attachment-based compassion therapy (ABCT) compared with TAU, with lower healthcare costs and modest QALY gains. 7 When MBSR was compared directly with ABCT, MBSR was associated with a small increase in costs (€53.69, 95% CI [-571.27 to 513.14]) and a slightly greater improvement in health outcomes (0.004 QALYs, 95% CI [-0.031 to 0.049]), corresponding to an ICER of €13,422.50 per QALY gained. Both interventions significantly reduced visits to general practitioners compared with TAU.

Saha et al. (2020) found that mindfulness group therapy was cost-saving from both healthcare and societal perspectives with comparable short-term health outcomes over a short 8week horizon, notably against a comparator in which 76% of controls received CBT, so savings were realized relative to active psychotherapy rather than passive treatment as usual. 8 Knight et al. (2015) conducted a large matched-control healthcare utilization analysis among 1,730 high healthcare-utilizing patients with mixed chronic mental and physical health conditions using administrative claims data. 9 Relative to matched controls, participants in the MBSR program demonstrated approximately $244 -$279 lower physician and laboratory costs per person over one year.

Cancer Survivorship

Cancer survivorship studies represented a smaller but evolving evidence base identified in this review (Tables 1 2 3). Two RCT-based CUAs evaluated MBSR among women with breast cancer. 10, 11 The earlier study used a relatively brief time horizon and narrow economic framework focused primarily on intervention delivery and participant-related costs, whereas the later study incorporated a larger sample, longer follow-up period, and broader healthcare utilization measures. Lengacher et al. (2015) counted only MBSR delivery costs and participant time costs and did not measure healthcare utilization. 10 MBSR improved health-related quality of life (0.03 QALYs gained at 12 weeks) at an added cost that remained cost-effective, with a provider cost of approximately $22,200 per QALY at 12 weeks, falling to ~$5,000 per QALY when projected to one year. In contrast, Meng et al. (2025) demonstrated dominant findings for MBSR relative to a breast cancer education support comparator from both societal and healthcare/payer perspectives. 11 Probability of cost-effectiveness at a willingness-to-pay threshold of $50,000/QALY was 83.6%, with cost differences driven by reductions in emergency department utilization, hospital admissions, outpatient utilization, diagnostic testing, and productivity-related costs.

Cardiovascular/Pulmonary Conditions

Tulloh et al. (2018) conducted a pilot feasibility study of MBSR among patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension that included an assessment of intervention delivery costs and collection of healthcare resource-use data to inform a future economic evaluation. 12 Rather than evaluating cost-effectiveness, the study focused on the feasibility of delivering a group-based MBSR program in a geographically dispersed rare disease population. Delivery costs ranged from £2,234 to £4,128 per group (approximately $3,515 -$6,495 in 2025 USD) and were highly sensitive to actual session attendance. Although groups were intended to include approximately seven participants, average attendance ranged from only 1.75 to 2.75 participants per session, resulting in relatively high per-participant delivery costs of £1,211 to £1,538 ($1905-$2419). The authors concluded that alternative delivery formats, such as remote or online programs, may improve participation and reduce per-participant costs.

eAppendix 4: Uncertainty and Sensitivity Analyses

Seven studies incorporated formal uncertainty analyses to address the substantial variability and skew commonly observed in healthcare expenditure data (QHES item #5). Methods included nonparametric bootstrap resampling, probabilistic sensitivity analyses, scenario analyses, threshold analyses, and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves.

Despite wide confidence intervals for incremental cost estimates that crossed zero, economic findings remained directionally favorable across many sensitivity analyses and modeled assumptions. Herman et al. (2017) reported approximately 90% probability of costeffectiveness at a willingness-to-pay threshold of $50,000/QALY despite broad incremental cost confidence intervals. 1 Similarly, Meng et al. (2025) reported an 83.6% probability of costeffectiveness at the same willingness-to-pay threshold. 11 Leininger (2023) additionally demonstrated economically favorable findings across multiple sensitivity and scenario analyses, with larger and more statistically robust cost differences observed from a governmental perspective and per-protocol analyses, 2 and stronger cost-effectiveness estimates projected among patients with high-impact chronic pain and older adults. 3, 4 Saha et al. (2020) conducted uncertainty analyses using nonparametric bootstrapping with 5,000 replications, cost-effectiveness planes, and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves. 8 Although QALY differences between groups were small and statistically nonsignificant (incremental QALY gain: -0.003, 95% CI [-0.008 to 0.001]), incremental costs consistently favored mindfulness group therapy from both healthcare and societal perspectives. Organizing the bootstrapped results into cost-effectiveness acceptability curves demonstrated that the mindfulness intervention had a 67% probability of being cost-effective from the societal perspective and a 70% probability from the healthcare perspective at the willingness-to-pay threshold of €24 691 per QALY. Additional sensitivity analyses generally upheld the base case finding that mindfulness group therapy was a cost-saving alternative to treatment as usual.

eAppendix 5: Time Horizon

Time horizons ranged from 8 weeks to 5 years, with one study projecting outcomes over 20 years. Most analyses used a 1-year time horizon. Under QHES criterion 8 (appropriate time horizon), two studies were judged to have insufficient follow-up because outcomes and costs were assessed over only 8 weeks (Saha) 8 and 12 weeks (Lengacher); 10 given that intervention costs are incurred immediately while many economic benefits may accrue over months to years, these follow-up periods were considered insufficient to adequately capture the economic consequences of MBSR.

The study with the longest observational follow-up among the empirical studies, using a 2-year pre-post administrative claims window (Knight; Canada), 9 found reductions in healthcare utilization (e.g., claims, labs) for the MBSR group at 1-year follow-up, amounting to savings of CAD 244 -279 (as of 2015) per participant, while costs increased slightly among controls. However, these differences were no longer observed when utilization and costs were assessed over a 2-year follow-up period, suggesting that the short-term reductions in healthcare use were not maintained over time. The authors speculated that ongoing meditation practice and continued support may be necessary to sustain longer-term benefits.

For breast cancer survivors, Lengacher et al. projected outcomes over a 20-year survival horizon, showing MBSR value accruing over time. 10 They used a 3% annual discount rate on QALY gains and no additional costs, yielding a discounted provider cost per QALY gained of $457 (≈$595 in 2025 USD) and a discounted patient opportunity cost per QALY gained of $407 (≈$530 in 2025 USD), evaluating the direct and opportunity costs of MBSR to providers and patients per QALY gained. In this analysis, patient opportunity costs were calculated from participant-reported annual income, which was converted to an hourly wage and multiplied by the total time spent participating in the six-week MBSR(BC) program, including class attendance and travel to and from the intervention site. Additional participant-incurred costs associated with program participation, including transportation (e.g., gas or bus fare), childcare, meals, and lodging for those traveling longer distances, were also included in the opportunity cost estimate.

A modeling study (Leininger, 2023) used a 5-year time horizon, applying a 3% annual discount rate; 4 across analyses, MBSR versus home exercise and advice produced ICERs consistently below $100,000/QALY and was often dominant.
