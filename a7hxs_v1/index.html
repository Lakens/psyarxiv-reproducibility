Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Autism spectrum disorder is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in social-cognitive functioning, yet individual differences contributing to this variability remain poorly understood. Personality provides a normative framework for characterizing such differences across diagnostic boundaries. Here, we investigated whether personality, particularly agreeableness, relates to behavioral and neural variability in social cognition in autistic young adults without intellectual and language impairment. Thirty-eight autistic participants were compared with a large neurotypical sample for personality and with neurotypical individuals selected with high (HA) or low (LA) agreeableness during three EEG paradigms examining social-interaction perception, basic emotion recognition, and complex mental-state decoding.

Autistic participants showed lower agreeableness, extraversion, and conscientiousness and higher neuroticism than neurotypical participants, with substantial interindividual variability. Their agreeableness scores were closer to those of LA than HA participants. Behavioral differences were selective: autistic participants showed lower accuracy than HA participants during complex mental-state decoding, whereas no significant difference was detected relative to LA participants or during the other social-cognitive tasks. Neural activity revealed a more nuanced pattern. No significant group differences emerged during social-interaction perception or basic emotion recognition. In contrast, decoding of complex mental states from the face revealed group differences in the ERP component N170 and a smaller Late Slow Wave (LSW) response in autistic participants. The LSW difference was more extensive relative to HA than to LA neurotypical participants. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering individual variability at both behavioral and neural levels, as similar behavioral outcomes may reflect distinct neural processing.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a clinical label encompassing substantial variability in social, cognitive, linguistic, and adaptive functioning (Lai et al., 2013; Lord et al., 2020; Warrier et al., 2018). With the introduction of the DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013), previous diagnostic categories, including autistic disorder, Asperger syndrome, and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified, were merged into a single spectrum. While this conceptualization acknowledges the heterogeneous and dimensional nature of autism, it also brings together individuals with considerably different profiles. Such heterogeneity represents a major challenge for understanding the neurobiological and cognitive variability associated with autism and may partly account for the inconsistent findings reported across studies (Bertelsen et al., 2021; Mottron, 2020; Vincent et al., 2020).

Recent research has therefore emphasized the importance of considering individual variability alongside group-level differences in autism (Wang, 2022; Floris et al., 2025). This issue is especially relevant when considering autistic adults without intellectual disability and with preserved language abilities (a group that, before DSM-5, often received a diagnosis of Asperger syndrome). Intellectual and language abilities alone may not adequately capture their social-cognitive and adaptive profiles, and individuals who are relatively independent in some areas of everyday functioning may nevertheless have specific needs that become particularly relevant in adulthood (Kamp-Becker et al., 2010; Liss et al., 2001; Renty et al., 2006). Research on autistic adults without intellectual disability also indicates that available post-diagnostic services do not always correspond to the support they report as useful, further highlighting the importance of considering variability within this population rather than assuming that relatively preserved intellectual and language abilities correspond to homogeneous functioning or support needs (Norris et al., 2025). Characterizing factors that contribute to individual variability within this group may therefore provide information that is not captured by the diagnostic label alone.

Social cognition represents a particularly relevant domain in which to examine this variability.

Theory of Mind (ToM), broadly defined as the ability to represent and understand others' mental states, plays a central role in social interactions (Frith & Frith, 2010; Schurz et al., 2014). However, social cognition is not a unitary process and cannot be adequately characterized by a single measure. It comprises partially dissociable processes, including social perception, emotion recognition, and mentalizing, which differ in the type and complexity of information that needs to be processed. Recent work has further emphasized the multifaceted nature of social cognition in autism, including differences across its components and between explicit and implicit processing (Bölte, 2025). A more informative approach may therefore be to determine which components of social cognition vary across autistic individuals, at which stages of processing this variability emerges, and which individual characteristics contribute to it.

One potential source of this individual variability is personality. The Five-Factor Model (FFM; Costa & McCrae, 1992; Goldberg, 1990) describes personality along five continuous dimensions (i.e., extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism, and openness) which capture individual differences in emotional, motivational, cognitive, and behavioral tendencies. The role of personality and individual differences in autism has received considerably less attention than core diagnostic features.

Previous studies have reported, on average, lower extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, openness and emotional stability in autistic individuals, although the magnitude and consistency of these differences vary across studies (Austin, 2005; Kanai et al., 2011; Lodi-Smith et al., 2019; Schwartzman et al., 2016; Strunz et al., 2015; Wakabayashi et al., 2006). Personality traits have also been associated with individual differences in internalizing and externalizing behaviors in autistic children and with the quality of life and the adaptive outcomes in autistic adults (De Pauw et al., 2011; Ratner & Burrow, 2017; Rodgers et al., 2018; Schriber et al., 2014; Schwartzman et al., 2016). Importantly, however, average group differences do not imply the existence of a uniform personality profile associated with autism. The variable effect sizes reported in previous studies and the substantial variability across samples suggest instead that broad personality dimensions may provide a complementary way of characterizing individual differences within the spectrum (Lodi-Smith et al., 2019; Schwartzman et al., 2016).

Interpretation of the existing literature is further complicated by the heterogeneity of the samples considered. Personality studies have included participants differing considerably in age, intellectual and language abilities, and clinical characteristics, and have mainly relied on self-or other-report measures (Lodi-Smith et al., 2019; Robinson et al., 2020; Schwartzman et al., 2016). Thus, whether personality differences are also associated with differences in the processing of social information in autistic adults remains largely unexplored. This question is particularly relevant in individuals without intellectual disability and with preserved language abilities, for whom performance in explicit behavioral tasks may not fully characterize the processes through which social information is analyzed. Electrophysiological measures can complement behavioral performance by providing information about the temporal dynamics of these processes. Previous EEG studies in autism have indeed reported differences in the timing and pattern of neural responses to social and emotional information compared with neurotypical individuals, particularly during face perception and the processing of socially relevant information (e.g., Benning et al., 2016; Fan et al., 2014; Hileman et al., 2011; Kang et al., 2018; Lerner et al., 2013).

Together, these findings highlight the value of examining neural differences between autistic and neurotypical individuals across different stages of social-cognitive processing, while also considering how individual characteristics may contribute to the observed patterns.

Among the five personality dimensions, agreeableness is particularly relevant to social cognition.

Agreeableness reflects individual differences in prosocial tendencies, cooperation, empathy, and interpersonal sympathy (Costa & McCrae, 1992; Goldberg, 1990), and has been associated with social cognition and ToM abilities (Allen & DeYoung, 2017; DeYoung & Blain, 2020; Nettle & Liddle, 2008; Udochi et al., 2022). Our previous studies on neurotypical individuals showed that individual differences in agreeableness are associated with how social and emotional information is processed at both behavioral and neural levels. Using fMRI, we found that individuals with higher agreeableness showed a greater differentiation between neural representations of social and non-social information in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, a region consistently involved in mentalizing (Arbula et al., 2021). We subsequently used EEG to investigate when during social information processing the influence of agreeableness emerges. During the perception of social interactions, higher agreeableness was associated with a greater neural differentiation between social and non-social scenes, with effects emerging at relatively early stages of processing (Pisanu et al., 2024a). In another study, we examined emotional information processing at different levels of complexity by comparing the recognition of basic emotions conveyed by biological motion with the decoding of complex mental states from the eye region.

Agreeableness modulated neural responses during complex mental-state decoding, but not during the recognition of basic emotional actions, with the effect emerging in a late time window, and source estimates pointing to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (Pisanu et al., 2024b). These findings suggest that the influence of agreeableness on social information processing is not equally evident across all social and emotional processes but depends on the type and stage of processing considered.

Together, these findings provided the rationale for using the same three experimental tasks to investigate distinct social-cognitive processes previously examined in relation to agreeableness, as well as for using EEG to determine whether differences in the temporal dynamics of neural processing may emerge even when behavioral outcomes do not differ. The three tasks range from the perception of social interactions to the recognition of basic emotional actions and the decoding of complex mental states. The Social Interaction Task (SIT) requires participants to discriminate interacting from non-interacting individuals; the Biological (e)Motion task (BM) probes basic emotion recognition from dynamic pointlight body movements; and the Reading the Mind in the Eyes task (RME) requires the decoding of complex mental states from static eye-region information. Together, these paradigms allowed us to examine whether the relationship between personality and social cognition extends across different social-cognitive processes or emerges selectively depending on the type of processing involved.

The present study extends our previous work to autistic adults without intellectual disability and with preserved language abilities. Specifically, we asked whether personality, and agreeableness in particular, could account for part of the variability in the social and emotional processes previously investigated in neurotypical adults. We addressed three research questions. First, we investigated whether autistic participants showed a characteristic personality profile compared with a large neurotypical sample and, importantly, whether substantial interindividual variability was preserved within the autistic group. Second, we asked whether autistic participants were more similar in agreeableness to neurotypical individuals with relatively low rather than high agreeableness. Third, we investigated whether this pattern extended to behavioral performance and neural responses during the SIT, BM, and RME tasks. Based on previous personality studies in autism (Lodi-Smith et al., 2019), we expected the autistic group to show lower mean scores on agreeableness, extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness, and higher neuroticism, while retaining substantial interindividual variability. Given the lower agreeableness reported on average in autism, we further expected autistic participants to be closer to neurotypical participants with lower than with higher agreeableness scores. We expected this relative proximity to be reflected, at least in part, in behavioral performance, although previous findings suggested that its expression might vary across social-cognitive processes. At the neural level, our previous EEG studies provided more process-and time-specific predictions: agreeableness modulated neural responses during social interaction perception and, at a later processing stage, during complex mental-state decoding, but not during basic emotion recognition. Accordingly, we expected group differences in neural activity to vary across tasks and processing stages, with a particularly clear difference between autistic and highly agreeable participants during the late stages of complex mental-state decoding. Whether comparable behavioral performance would be accompanied by similar temporal dynamics of neural processing remained an open question.

However, this difference did not survive the correction for multiple comparisons.

Discussion

Our previous studies showed that agreeableness is associated with some of the social-cognitive processes in neurotypical individuals. In our previous work, higher agreeableness was associated with greater neural differentiation between social and non-social information during the perception of social interactions (Pisanu et al., 2024a), as well as with later neural activity during the decoding of complex mental states, whereas no comparable association emerged during the recognition of basic emotional actions (Pisanu et al., 2024b). Together, these findings indicate that social-cognitive processing is modulated by individual differences in agreeableness, while also suggesting that this influence is not uniform but depends on the specific process involved and on the stage at which social information is processed. More broadly, they highlight the importance of considering individual characteristics when investigating the behavioral and neural mechanisms underlying social cognition. This perspective may be particularly relevant to autism, given the substantial heterogeneity in social-cognitive functioning within the spectrum.

In the present study, we focused specifically on autistic young adults without intellectual or language impairment, a population in which individual differences in social-cognitive processing may be particularly important to characterize, and investigated whether individual differences in personality, with a particular focus on agreeableness, could contribute to characterizing social-cognitive processing.

To this aim, we used the same three experimental paradigms employed in our previous EEG studies (Pisanu et al., 2024a,b), allowing us to examine processes ranging from the perception of social interactions and basic emotional actions to the decoding of complex mental states. In the following sections, we discuss the findings in relation to the three research questions outlined in the Introduction.

Regarding the first research question, we observed a personality profile tending towards lower scores in our autistic sample, in line with the meta-analysis by Lodi-Smith et al. (2019). Autistic participants showed significantly lower scores in agreeableness, extraversion, conscientiousness, and higher neuroticism than a large neurotypical sample. Contrary to our hypothesis, openness did not differ between groups. One possible explanation is sample composition: our participants had no intellectual disability and preserved language abilities, whereas previous samples varied considerably in these characteristics (Lodi-Smith et al., 2019). Importantly, relative to Italian normative values, autistic participants did not uniformly show extreme scores; rather, scores varied considerably within each trait, and only some individuals showed extreme values, as in the neurotypical sample. These findings underscore the variability within this population, and argue against equating autism with a uniformly low personality profile. They also suggest that personality assessment may help characterize individual strengths and difficulties, which could inform more tailored support. Having the same diagnosis does not necessarily imply uniformity among individuals with that diagnosis.

Moving to the second research question, agreeableness showed the largest difference between the autistic and neurotypical groups. As hypothesized, autistic participants showed agreeableness scores closer to those of neurotypical participants with lower agreeableness, while differentiating from those with higher agreeableness scores.

This result led us to pursue the third research question, asking whether the same pattern would also emerge across the social-cognitive processes investigated in our previous EEG studies (Pisanu et al., 2024a,b), at both the behavioral and neural levels. Our results indicated no significant behavioral group differences in the detection of social interaction and basic emotions, as measured by the SIT and BM tasks respectively. However, significant differences emerged in the RME task. Here, autistic participants exhibited lower accuracy in decoding complex mental states than HA participants, whereas no significant difference emerged relative to LA participants. Thus, at the behavioral level, the pattern observed for agreeableness, a difference from HA but not from LA participants, was found during complex mentalstate decoding, but did not emerge as a generalized pattern across all the social-cognitive processes examined.

The neural results provided a more differentiated picture. Consistent with the behavioral findings, no significant ERP differences emerged between groups in either the SIT or BM tasks. In the RME task, however, two temporally and spatially distinct patterns were observed. The first consisted of an early posterior effect, occurring approximately 120-170 ms after stimulus onset. Effects with a highly consistent temporal and posterior distribution emerged in the emotion, age, and observe conditions in comparisons between the autistic group and the neurotypical groups, although the ASD-HA comparison did not reach significance in the age condition. No corresponding difference emerged between HA and LA participants. The timing and parieto-occipital distribution of this effect are consistent with the N170, a face-sensitive ERP component associated with the early perceptual processing of facial information (Feuerriegel et al., 2015; Kappenman et al., 2021; Rossion et al., 2003), which is typically reduced and/or delayed in ASD individuals (see Kang et al., 2018 for a meta-analysis).

A second pattern, specific to the mental-state decoding, emerged at later stages of processing and paralleled the behavioral results. In the emotion condition, autistic participants showed a smaller positive amplitude over frontocentral sites than both neurotypical groups. Significant clusters spanned approximately 450-1000 ms for the ASD-HA comparison and 400-600 ms for the ASD-LA comparison. Importantly, no comparable late effect emerged in the age or observe conditions, suggesting this effect may be particularly relevant when participants are engaged in complex mental-state decoding, rather than reflecting a general response to visual stimuli. This later effect is particularly interesting in relation to our previous EEG findings. In neurotypical participants, agreeableness was associated with a late slow-wave response during complex mental-state decoding in the RME (Pisanu et al., 2024b). The current late frontocentral effect partly overlaps with the temporal and spatial characteristics of this previous effect and is similarly selective for the condition requiring complex mental-state decoding.

Descriptively, and in line with our previous findings, HA participants exhibited the largest late positive activity, autistic participants the smallest, while LA participants tended to fall between the two. This descriptive pattern should be interpreted cautiously, since no significant ERP differences emerged between HA and LA participants. Nevertheless, the finding that autistic participants differed from both neurotypical groups indicates that the neural pattern cannot be reduced to a simple correspondence between autism and lower agreeableness. This distinction becomes particularly relevant when behavioral and neural findings are considered together. At the behavioral level, autistic participants differed from HA participants during mental-state decoding, whereas no significant difference was detected between autistic and LA participants. At the neural level, however, autistic participants differed from both neurotypical groups. Therefore, the behavioral similarity observed between autistic and LA participants did not correspond to a similar pattern of neural activity. Agreeableness may contribute to characterizing individual variability in the absence of a significant behavioral difference between autistic and neurotypical participants. More generally, these findings suggest that comparable behavioral outcomes do not necessarily imply similar temporal dynamics of the neural processes supporting those outcomes.

To sum up, the early posterior effect distinguished autistic from neurotypical participants regardless of agreeableness and experimental condition, whereas the late frontocentral effect was specific to mental-state decoding and paralleled the behavioral difference from HA participants.

One possible interpretation is that autistic and LA participants may achieve comparable behavioral outcomes through partly different processing dynamics. In autistic individuals without intellectual and/or language impairment, such a dissociation could be compatible with the involvement of compensatory processes, whereby differences in neural processing do not necessarily result in corresponding differences in behavioral performance. However, the present study does not directly demonstrate compensation or identify the strategies through which comparable performance may be achieved. The current findings therefore provide evidence primarily for a dissociation between behavioral outcome and neural processing, while compensation remains one possible interpretation to be tested more directly in future studies.

Regarding the null results in the SIT and BM tasks, two considerations arise. First, autistic individuals may experience difficulties in social interactions and emotional expression, yet this does not imply an inability to recognize such stimuli. Difficulties in some of their social skills do not necessarily signify a comprehensive impairment in the realm of social cognition. This is the rationale behind our approach of dissecting this domain, evaluating social and emotional aspects individually, while also accounting for task complexity. This may be particularly true for autistic individuals without language and intellectual impairment who exhibit a certain degree of adaptability to environmental demands, including relational aspects (Kamp-Becker et al., 2010; Liss et al., 2001; Renty et al., 2006). This finding is consistent with the idea that social-cognitive profiles in autism can be heterogeneous and that relatively preserved performance in some processes may coexist with differences in others. Second, task characteristics should also be considered when interpreting the null findings. As regards the recognition of basic emotions through point-light bodily stimuli (BM task), the extended duration and dynamic nature of these stimuli may provide participants with more information over time and therefore facilitate recognition. It is possible that this additional processing time contributes to similar behavioral performance across groups, although the present design cannot establish this explanation. Future studies manipulating stimulus duration or the amount of dynamic information available could test this possibility more directly. Similarly, because the present EEG analyses of the BM focused on later processing stages, analyses specifically designed to characterize neural responses from stimulus onset could provide complementary information about earlier stages of biological-motion and emotion processing.

Notably, our tasks were not designed to emphasize response speed, which may explain the absence of group differences in reaction times. Tasks requiring speeded responses could provide an interesting avenue to uncover distinctions, possibly not in terms of accuracy, but rather in terms of delayed responses among autistic participants, which could provide further evidence relevant to compensatory mechanisms. Several limitations should be considered. First, the autistic sample was relatively small, particularly for investigating interindividual variability, and replication in larger samples will be important. Second, agreeableness is a continuous personality dimension, whereas the present study used HA and LA neurotypical groups to address the specific comparison with autistic participants. Although this approach allowed us to test whether autistic participants more closely resembled neurotypical individuals differing in agreeableness, categorization inevitably reduces information about continuous individual variability. This distinction is particularly important when comparing the present findings with our previous studies, in which agreeableness was examined as a continuous dimension. Third, nonsignificant differences between groups cannot be interpreted as evidence of equivalence. Finally, the three paradigms isolate specific components of social cognition under controlled experimental conditions and therefore cannot capture the full complexity of social functioning in everyday life. Future studies using larger samples and combining dimensional personality measures with more naturalistic assessments of social cognition could further clarify how personality contributes to individual variability within autism.

Overall, these findings underscore the importance of considering individual variability within autism rather than relying on diagnostic status alone. Recognizing individual strengths and difficulties may ultimately help inform more tailored interventions and support strategies aimed at promoting social participation and engagement in educational and occupational settings.

Materials and Methods

Participants recruitment

Forty-two autistic young adults without accompanying intellectual and language impairment (14 females; mean age = 26 years, SD = 8.98, range = 17-45 years) were recruited from a specialized clinical Four participants did not complete the study, resulting in a final sample of 38 participants (13 females; mean age = 25 years, SD = 8.14, range = 17-44 years). Diagnoses were established by clinicians according to DSM-5 criteria. Exclusion criteria included a history of head injury, epilepsy, neurological or motor disorders, and IQ below 70. All participants provided written informed consent, with parental consent and participant assent obtained for minors. The study was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee of Friuli Venezia Giulia and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Neurotypical participants were drawn from two previous EEG studies (Pisanu et al., 2024a,b).

Personality was assessed using the Big Five Inventory (BFI; Ubbiali et al., 2013), measuring agreeableness, extraversion, conscientiousness, neuroticism, and openness. For the personality-profile comparison, the autistic group was compared with the full neurotypical sample (N = 462). For the behavioral and EEG comparisons, we used a subset of 62 neurotypical participants previously selected to increase variability in agreeableness. This sample was divided by a median split into highagreeableness (HA; N = 29) and low-agreeableness (LA; N = 33) groups. Further details on recruitment, participant selection, and personality assessment are provided in the SI Appendix, Methods, Participants recruitment.

Procedure and experimental tasks

Data from autistic participants were collected in two experimental sessions on separate days.

During the first session, participants completed personality questionnaires, while the second session involved EEG recording during three experimental tasks presented in counterbalanced order.

The SIT assessed the perception of social interactions using images depicting two individuals either interacting with each other or acting independently (Isik et al., 2020). Participants were asked to Differences in personality traits between autistic participants and the full neurotypical sample were assessed using Welch's two-sample t tests. Agreeableness scores were subsequently compared across the autistic, HA, and LA groups using a linear model.

Behavioral performance was compared across groups using linear models. For the SIT, discrimination sensitivity was quantified using the signal detection index d′. For the BM and RME, accuracy and reaction times were analyzed separately for each experimental condition, excluding the RME observe condition, which did not require a response. Significant group effects were followed by Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons. Additional details on behavioral data processing, outcome measures, and statistical procedures are provided in the SI Appendix, Data analyses, Behavioral data analysis.

ERP analyses were performed separately for each task using the LIMO EEG toolbox (Pernet et al., 2011). A two-level mass-univariate approach was adopted to investigate group differences across electrodes and time points. At the first level, a general linear model was estimated independently for each participant and experimental condition, including both correct and incorrect trials. Analyses covered 0-1000 ms after target onset for the SIT and RME and 1000-2500 ms for the BM, focusing on later processing stages for the longer-duration biological-motion stimuli. At the second level, robust independent-samples tests compared neural responses between groups (ASD-HA, ASD-LA, HA-LA) separately within each experimental condition. Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using spatiotemporal cluster-based permutation procedures (Maris & Oostenveld, 2007; Pernet et al., 2015).
