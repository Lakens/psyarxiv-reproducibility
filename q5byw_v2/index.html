Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Code Check works

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective: Single-session interventions (SSIs) have been proposed as a scalable approach to reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms; however, evidence for adults remains limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating SSIs relative to inactive or minimal-control conditions.

Method: A systematic search identified adult RCTs. Outcomes were analyzed separately for anxiety and depression using three-level random-effects meta-analytic models. Effects were pooled across 1-13 weeks and within short-term (1-3 weeks) and medium-term (4-13 weeks) follow-up windows.

Results: Twenty-one RCTs (N = 7,035) contributed 53 outcome-level effect sizes. Across 1-13 weeks, SSIs were associated with significant reductions in anxiety symptoms (g = -0.66, 95% CI [-0.98, -0.34]) and depressive symptoms (g = -0.49, 95% CI [-0.84, -0.13]). At 1-3 weeks, effects were significant for anxiety symptoms (g = -0.80, 95% CI [-1.24, -0.36]), but not depressive symptoms (g = -0.78, 95% CI [-1.78, 0.22]). At 4-13 weeks, effects were significant for both depressive symptoms (g = -0.29, 95% CI [-0.55, -0.02]) and anxiety symptoms (g = -0.48, 95% CI [-0.93, -0.02]). Heterogeneity was high, prediction intervals were wide, publication bias was detected for anxiety outcomes at 1-13 and 1-3 weeks and for depression at 1-13 weeks, and most studies had some concerns or high risk of bias.

Conclusions: SSIs may reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms over the short-to-medium term in adults. Substantial heterogeneity, limited trial availability, publication bias, and risk of bias constrain confidence in the magnitude and generalizability of these effects. Rigorous trials with extended follow-up are needed to clarify effect durability and further inform the role within routine care.

Public Significance Statement

This meta-analysis suggests that psychological interventions designed to be delivered in a single session may reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms in adults over the short to medium term. These interventions may provide an additional brief option within mental health care, but future research is needed to determine how durable their effects are, for whom they work, and how they should be implemented in routine practice. The findings should be interpreted cautiously because the included studies differed substantially and many had methodological limitations.

Effectiveness of Single-Session Interventions for Adult Anxiety and Depression

Anxiety and depressive disorders are among the most prevalent and burdensome mental health conditions worldwide. Globally, they affect an estimated 301 million and 280 million people, respectively (Javaid et al., 2023; World Health Organization [WHO], 2023), together accounting for about 7-8% of all years lived with disability (YLDs) (Yan et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). These internalizing conditions cause substantial emotional distress and impair daily functioning, interpersonal relationships, and physical health (Kalin, 2020; WHO, 2023). Their burden has risen steadily over recent decades (Yan et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025) and global costs exceed US $1 trillion annually in lost productivity (WHO, 2023).

Given their high prevalence, disabling impact, and economic costs, reducing the global burden of anxiety and depression through scalable, evidence-based interventions has become an urgent public health priority (WHO, 2023).

Evidence-based psychological interventions (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy, behavioral activation, interpersonal psychotherapy, and third-wave approaches) are effective, producing moderate to large anxiety and depressive symptom reductions across populations and delivery formats (Cuijpers et al., 2023; De Ponti et al., 2024; Papola et al., 2024; Pauley et al., 2023). Yet most individuals with anxiety or depression remain untreated, revealing a striking discrepancy between the need for care and the receipt of adequate treatment (WHO, 2023; Bayetti et al., 2023).

Barriers sustaining the treatment gap span systemic (e.g., shortages of professionals, long waitlists, fragmented service pathways), financial (e.g., high out-of-pocket costs, limited insurance coverage); and attitudinal obstacles (e.g., fear of stigma, low perceived need) (Kazdin, 2017; Patel et al., 2018; WHO, 2023). They contribute to lack of access to care, or long delays. For instance, data from the World Mental Health Surveys indicate that only 27.6% of individuals with a 12-month anxiety disorder receive any mental health treatment (including psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, or general medical care) and only 9.8% receive possibly adequate treatment, with substantially lower coverage in low-andmiddle-income countries (LMICs) (Alonso et al., 2018). For depressive disorders, waiting times for routine depression treatment (psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, or combined care) can extend over multiple weeks and are associated with less favorable outcomes (Van Dijk et al., 2023).

Traditional service models cannot meet global population-level demand (WHO, 2023). Expanding specialist training or enrolling every patient in multi-month therapy is unrealistic (WHO, 2023). Therefore, recent global mental health initiatives have prioritized scalable innovations such as task-sharing and ultra-brief, adaptable interventions that can reach individuals who would otherwise remain untreated (Mills, 2018; Patel et al., 2018; WHO, 2023).

Single-session interventions (SSIs) are one such innovation (Schleider et al., 2022).

SSIs are commonly defined as structured psychological interventions intentionally designed to involve one therapeutic encounter with a program, provider, clinic, or platform (Schleider & Weisz, 2017; Schleider et al., 2025). SSIs involve the selection and targeted delivery of specific evidence-informed therapeutic components that can be meaningfully applied within a single encounter and are intended to produce measurable improvements in mental health outcomes (Schleider et al., 2025). The broader brief-intervention literature includes related but distinct formats, including single-session therapy (SST), one-at-a-time therapy, onesession treatment for specific phobias, and focused approaches such as Focused Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (FACT). These models differ in their assumptions about future contact: some are designed as one encounter only, whereas others treat each session as a complete therapeutic unit while allowing additional sessions, follow-up, or booster contact when needed (Dryden, 2020; Hoyt et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2023). One-session exposure for specific phobias (i.e., a manualized, intensive exposure-based treatment delivered in one extended session) has a comparatively strong evidence base and appears broadly comparable to multisession exposure while requiring less therapist time (Odgers et al., 2022; Zlomke & Davis, 2008). Evidence for adult SST (i.e., a therapist-delivered approach in which the current session is treated as potentially sufficient, although additional sessions remain possible) in common mental disorders is more limited but suggests potential benefits for depressive symptoms, with less consistent findings for anxiety (Kim et al., 2023). FACT (i.e., a brief ACT-based treatment model focused on increasing psychological flexibility that may involve one or more sessions) similarly illustrates how evidence-based therapeutic processes can be adapted for brief care, although existing FACT studies often involve more than one session and therefore remain adjacent rather than directly equivalent to strictly defined SSIs (Glover et al., 2016). In the present review, we use SSI in a narrower operational sense to refer to structured, evidence-informed psychological interventions designed and evaluated as single-session interventions for adult anxiety or depressive symptoms. For example, an SSI for depression may target behavioral activation or cognitive restructuring, whereas an SSI for anxiety may target exposure, anxiety sensitivity, or catastrophic appraisals. Delivery formats may range from clinician-led to digital self-guided programs or blended models with minimal non-therapeutic follow-up (i.e., any post-session contact limited to reminders, assessment completion, logistical communication, or non-clinical check-ins, without introducing new therapeutic content, individualized clinical feedback, skills training, or additional treatment sessions).

While not intended to replace longer-term psychotherapy in complex or chronic cases, SSIs can serve multiple roles within mental health care systems. They may function as standalone treatments for mild to moderate symptoms, as adjuncts to ongoing therapy, or as accessible entry points for individuals who might otherwise remain untreated (Hoyt et al., 2018; Kazdin, 2017; Schleider et al., 2022). Across these applications, the SSI literature suggests that treatment effects are most consistently observed in the short-to-medium term, whereas evidence regarding longer-term effects remains limited and less conclusive, and may be influenced by subsequent support or contextual factors (Schleider & Weisz, 2017).

Unlike multisession psychotherapies, SSIs are explicitly designed to deliver maximal therapeutic benefit within a single session (Schleider et al., 2022). Given that many patients attend only one therapy session, concentrating therapeutic input within that encounter may yield greater benefit than conventional multisession models when treatment is prematurely discontinued (Cuijpers et al., 2023; Schleider et al., 2022). By requiring fewer professional resources and substantially reducing total therapy time, SSIs are highly scalable and can be disseminated across schools, primary care, and community settings, while still aiming to produce measurable symptom improvements and minimize treatment dropout (Kazdin, 2017; Schleider & Weisz, 2017; Schleider et al., 2022). Their rapid deployability reduces waiting times and improves access to timely support at the point of need, which may in turn help prevent symptom escalation (Schleider et al., 2025).

Accumulating evidence supports the therapeutic potential of SSIs. Recent reviews further situate SSIs within a broader and rapidly expanding evidence base, including systematic work on initial therapy contacts in adult psychological services (Aafjes-van Doorn & Sweeney, 2019), bibliometric evidence showing substantial growth and diversification of single-session therapy research (Joseph & Rajan, 2024), and emerging condition-and approach-specific syntheses suggesting promise for eating disorders (Negi et al., 2026) and single-session Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (Rehman et al., 2026). A meta-analysis of 50 randomized trials in youth populations (N ≈ 10,500) found modest overall effects compared with no-treatment, waitlist, or placebo control conditions (Hedges' g = 0.32), with stronger outcomes for anxiety (g = 0.56) and conduct problems (g = 0.54), smaller effects for depression (g = 0.21), and negligible effects for substance use (g = 0.08) (Schleider & Weisz, 2017). Furthermore, a recent umbrella review synthesized 24 systematic reviews encompassing 415 trials across children, adolescents, and adults and concluded that SSIs yield beneficial effects across multiple domains, including anxiety, depression, behavioral problems, eating problems, substance use, and service engagement (Schleider et al., 2025). In the domain of specific phobias, a meta-analysis demonstrated that single-session exposure therapy produced large effects at both post-treatment and follow-up, equivalent to multisession exposure while requiring substantially less therapist time (Odgers et al., 2022).

Despite these encouraging findings for the general effectiveness of SSIs across populations, critical gaps persist in the literature. Most prior reviews evaluating SSI effectiveness have been narrative rather than quantitative, have concentrated on youth populations, or have combined heterogeneous outcomes across diverse symptom domains (Schleider & Weisz, 2017; Schleider et al., 2022). Moreover, although early post-intervention outcomes are frequently reported, the existing literature has not yet established a consistent or fine-grained temporal differentiation of SSI effects. Follow-up assessments vary widely in timing across studies, and prior syntheses have typically aggregated early outcomes rather than systematically comparing adjacent post-intervention windows. This distinction is relevant because broader SSI evidence suggests that effects may vary by follow-up length, with larger average effects observed at very short follow-up than at later assessments (Schleider et al., 2025). Meta-analytic work on brief psychological therapies has likewise noted that variation in follow-up intervals can contribute to heterogeneity, underscoring that timing of outcome assessment is not merely a reporting detail but part of how treatment effects are interpreted (Cape et al., 2010). As a result, post-intervention outcomes are often treated collectively as a pragmatic indicator of SSI impact, even though symptom trajectories within and beyond this window remain insufficiently resolved. Separating adjacent shortterm and more distal assessments may therefore offer additional insight into potential temporal patterns without presupposing their presence.

Against this backdrop, evidence regarding how SSI effects unfold over time in adults with anxiety and depression remains limited. Given the high prevalence and disabling burden of these conditions in adulthood, and the reality that many adults receive little or no care beyond a single encounter, strengthening the adult evidence base is particularly important. Doing so would help determine whether SSIs can be considered an evidence-based approach for anxiety and depression and clarify their potential role in expanding access to effective psychological interventions (Kazdin, 2017; Schleider et al., 2025).

Related brief-intervention formats were considered eligible only when they were explicitly designed as single-session interventions, targeted anxiety and/or depressive symptoms in adults, and met the review's randomized controlled trial and comparator criteria.

Accordingly, this systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effects of singlesession interventions (SSIs) on adult anxiety and depressive symptoms, relative to inactive or minimal-control conditions, operationalized as waitlist, no intervention, usual care, or minimal, nonspecific, or placebo-like comparators; structured psychological treatments evaluated as alternative interventions were excluded. Analyses were conducted separately for anxiety and depression. For each outcome domain, effect sizes were first pooled across all available post-intervention assessments within the short-to-medium term. Subsequently, domain-specific effects were estimated separately across adjacent follow-up windows to assess potential temporal variation in SSI effectiveness.

Based on prior evidence suggesting that SSI effects may be strongest shortly after intervention delivery and may attenuate over time (Cape et al., 2010; Schleider et al., 2025), we expected that SSIs would yield significant reductions in anxiety and depressive symptoms across the available short-to-medium-term post-intervention period, and that effect size estimates would be descriptively larger at earlier than later follow-up assessments.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al., 2021) and was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/5e87a).

After preregistration and prior to study identification and data extraction, several protocol refinements were implemented. A detailed rationale for all protocol refinements is provided in Supplement D.

Identification and Selection of Trials

A comprehensive literature search was conducted on May 5, 2026. To identify relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we first screened two curated databases maintained by the Meta-analytic Research Domains (MARDs) initiative: the Metapsy Depression Database and the Metapsy Anxiety Database (Cuijpers et al., 2023; www.metapsy.org). These living databases are systematically updated and include all published RCTs of evidence-based psychological interventions for depression (updated quarterly) and anxiety (updated annually). Recognizing that SSIs may be underrepresented or inconsistently labeled in these broader psychotherapy databases, we additionally developed a tailored search strategy using keyword combinations specifically related to SSIs and brief psychotherapies. This targeted search was run across four major electronic databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials). The full search string and syntax for each database are provided in the Supplementary Material.

In addition to database and register searches, records were identified through consultation with subject-matter experts, including researchers who had contributed to trials or data resources relevant to this review; records identified this way underwent the same eligibility screening as records identified through the search strategies above. For trials with published reports insufficient for effect-size calculation, deidentified individual participant data (IPD) were sought, either from publicly archived data repositories or by direct request to study authors.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) employed a randomized controlled trial (RCT) design; (2) included adult participants, defined as samples restricted to participants aged 18 years or older, with either a formal diagnosis of anxiety or depression based on DSM or ICD criteria or clinically significant symptoms as determined by standardized instruments; (3) evaluated an SSI derived from an evidence-informed psychotherapeutic approach, defined as a recognizable psychological treatment model with prior empirical support for anxiety, depression, or related internalizing symptoms, from which the SSI delivered at least one identifiable therapeutic component; (4) delivered the intervention in one primary therapeutic session, with optional non-therapeutic follow-up only when post-session contact was limited to reminders, assessment prompts, logistical communication, or brief non-clinical check-ins and did not introduce new therapeutic content, individualized clinical feedback, skills training, or booster intervention material; (5) compared the SSI with an inactive or minimal-control condition, operationalized as waitlist, no intervention, usual care, or a minimal, nonspecific, or placebo-like comparator, such as a reading task, study-skills module, physical-health education, or expressive writing.

Comparator conditions could be matched to the SSI in length, format, attention, or activity level, but studies were excluded when the comparator was a structured psychological treatment evaluated as an alternative intervention; (6) reported post-intervention anxiety and/or depressive symptom outcomes using psychometrically established instruments, defined as standardized symptom measures for which prior published psychometric evaluations demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in relevant or comparable populations, with sufficient data for effect-size computation.

For trials in which arm-level eligibility could not be determined from the published report, for example, trials evaluating several intervention variants against a shared comparator, eligibility was assessed separately for each arm against the criteria above, and study authors were contacted for clarification when necessary. Only arms independently judged eligible were retained; each eligible arm was treated as a separate intervention-control contrast for data extraction and analysis.

Two researchers independently screened all abstracts and full texts. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool ( Sterne et al., 2019), which evaluates potential bias across five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported results. Each domain was rated separately as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. An overall risk of bias judgment was then derived by aggregating these domain-level ratings following RoB 2 guidance (Sterne et al., 2019). Accordingly, studies were rated as low risk if all domains were rated as low risk; studies were rated as high risk if any domain was rated as high risk or if three or more domains were rated as some concerns; and, studies were rated as having some concerns if one or two domains were rated as some concerns. For trials contributing multiple eligible arms compared against a single shared control condition, risk of bias was assessed once at the trial level and applied to all contributing arms.

Two researchers independently assessed the risk of bias for each included study.

Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer.

Outcome Measures

We extracted means and standard deviations from intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses when available; otherwise, completer-only data were extracted.

For each study, we identified eligible intervention-control contrasts, defined as contrasts between a single-session intervention (SSI) and inactive or minimal-control conditions When studies included multiple eligible SSI or control arms, each qualifying SSIcontrol pairing was treated as a separate intervention-control contrast.

From each intervention-control contrast, outcome data were extracted for each eligible symptom instrument reported at post-intervention assessments. When multiple instruments were reported within the same contrast, data from each instrument were extracted separately and treated as distinct outcome-level effect sizes. Thus, effect sizes were derived at the outcome level, based on instrument-specific data within each intervention-control contrast.

Only total scores from psychometrically established instruments (i.e., standardized symptom measures with published evidence of reliability and validity for assessing anxiety or depressive symptoms, excluding ad hoc, single-item, author-created, or non-symptom measures without prior psychometric evaluation, e.g., BDI-II, PHQ-9, GAD-7) were included. Outcomes were classified by symptom domain (anxiety or depression).

For each outcome-level effect size derived from an intervention-control contrast, standardized mean differences (Hedges' g) were calculated as the difference in mean postintervention scores between intervention and control groups, divided by the pooled standard deviation and corrected for small-sample bias (Hedges, 1981). Negative values reflected lower symptom scores in the SSI group relative to controls; conventional benchmarks are approximately -0.20, -0.50, and -0.80 for small, moderate, and large effects, respectively (Cohen, 1977).

For studies that did not report arm-level post-intervention means and standard deviations in the format required for the standardization procedure above, but for which deidentified individual participant data (IPD) were available, effect sizes were derived by reproducing the study's primary analytic model (e.g., a longitudinal mixed-effects model of the relevant outcome) and extracting the model-estimated between-group coefficient and its standard error at the corresponding post-intervention assessment. This coefficient and its standard error were standardized using the pooled available-case standard deviation at that assessment, computed directly from the IPD, and corrected for small-sample bias (Hedges, 1981), consistent with the standardization approach applied to all other included effect sizes.

Where a study's primary model was reproduced in this way, the resulting coefficients were checked against those reported in the original publication or associated analysis materials before use. IPD reanalyses were conducted in R using mixed-effects modeling packages (e.g., lme4, lmerTest).

All eligible post-intervention follow-up assessments were extracted. The primary post-intervention window was defined as 1-13 weeks (hereafter: the short-to-medium-term window), reflecting the range of eligible follow-up assessments available in the included studies. Within this window, outcome-level effect sizes were grouped into two pragmatic, data-informed intervals: 1-3 weeks and 4-13 weeks. These intervals were used to distinguish more proximal from later assessments while preserving sufficient outcome-level effect sizes within each analytic window; they were not intended to represent established clinical thresholds. For studies reporting more than one eligible follow-up assessment within the 1-13 week post-intervention period, each time point was extracted separately and assigned to the corresponding analytic window. Studies with assessments in both windows could therefore contribute to both the 1-3 week and 4-13 week analyses; dependency among multiple effect sizes from the same study was handled using the three-level model. Studies reporting only one eligible post-intervention assessment within the 1-13 week window contributed that single effect size to the corresponding analytic interval.

Meta-analyses

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2) and RStudio, employing the metapsyTools package (Harrer et al., 2022), which builds on functionality from established meta-analysis packages, including metafor, meta, and dmetar. Because several studies contributed more than one outcome-level effect size, effect sizes from the same study or intervention-comparator contrast could not be treated as statistically independent. We therefore used three-level correlated-and-hierarchical-effects (CHE) random-effects metaanalytic models (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022), which estimate sampling variability at the effect-size level, dependency among effect sizes within studies or contrasts, and betweenstudy heterogeneity. Robust variance estimation was used to obtain standard errors and confidence intervals that account for this dependency structure. An intra-study correlation of ρ = .60 was specified for dependent outcome-level effect sizes derived from the same construct (e.g., repeated assessments of the same outcome), and an additional correlation of φ = .90 was specified for hierarchically nested effect sizes (e.g., multiple intervention arms compared against a single shared control condition). These parameters reflect the assumed correlation structure among effect sizes derived from the same study or contrast and primarily affects the estimation of standard errors and confidence intervals rather than the substantive direction of the pooled effect. Meta-analyses were conducted separately for anxiety and depression. For each outcome domain, effect sizes were pooled across the overall 1-13 week post-intervention window and separately within the 1-3 week and 4-13 week windows.

For any outcome domain and analytic window in which one included study contributed a substantially larger number of effect sizes, or effect sizes of substantially greater statistical precision, than the remaining included studies, the primary meta-analysis was additionally repeated with that study's contributed effect sizes excluded, to evaluate whether the pooled estimate and its statistical significance were disproportionately driven by that study.

Heterogeneity was assessed by calculating the I² statistic (Higgins & Thompson, 2002; Rücker et al., 2008) and its 95% confidence interval, which estimate the proportion of variance not attributable to sampling error. I² values of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% represent no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively (Higgins & Thompson, 2002). In line with the three-level model structure, we applied a multilevel extension of I² that partitions variance into heterogeneity arising from differences within studies (level 2) and from variability between studies (level 3). In addition, between-study heterogeneity variance (τ²) was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood.

Prediction intervals (PI) were calculated to indicate the range in which the true effect sizes of comparable future studies are expected to fall. Numbers needed to treat (NNTs) were calculated from Hedges' g, assuming a control group event rate of 20%, to convert standardized mean differences into NNTs for binary interpretation (Furukawa & Leucht, 2011).

Sensitivity analyses (i.e., outlier detection, publication bias tests, and bias adjustment methods) were conducted using the combined (two-level) random-effects model. These procedures were not applied to the three-level model because standard implementations of these diagnostics and bias-adjustment methods are more straightforward for two-level models and are less established for dependent, multilevel effect-size structures.

Outliers were defined as outcome-level effect sizes whose 95% confidence intervals did not overlap with the pooled effect size and were excluded in a subsequent effect-size recalculation.

Publication bias was assessed using Egger's test of the intercept (Egger et al., 1997), but only in meta-analyses including at least 10 studies, in line with recommended thresholds (Sterne et al., 2011). When evidence of publication bias was detected, adjusted effect sizes were estimated using the trim-and-fill method (Duval & Tweedie, 2000), limit metaanalysis (Rücker et al., 2011), and a step-function selection model (Vevea & Hedges, 1995).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized findings from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining single-session interventions (SSIs) for adult anxiety or depression compared with inactive or minimal-control conditions. In line with our expectations, significant SSI effects were observed for both anxiety (g = -0.66) and depression (g = -0.49) across the full follow-up period (1-13 weeks post-intervention).

Consistent with our expectation that effects would be descriptively larger at earlier than later follow-up assessments, point estimates were larger at 1-3 weeks than at 4-13 weeks for both anxiety (g = -0.80 vs. -0.48) and depression (g = -0.78 vs. -0.29). This pattern reached statistical significance at both windows for anxiety, but only at the 4-13 week window for depression; the 1-3 week depression estimate, while descriptively the largest observed, had a wide confidence interval spanning the null (95% CI [-1.78, 0.22]), likely reflecting the small number of contributing studies (k = 5) at that window.

Sensitivity analyses yielded broadly similar results across conditions and follow-up windows. The depression estimates showed comparatively greater sensitivity to the inclusion of one large trial, discussed further below. These follow-up windows should be interpreted as pragmatic analytic groupings rather than established clinical thresholds, reflecting the distribution of available assessment points in the included studies.

Overall, interpretation of our findings warrants caution. Although the non-significant estimates were in the expected direction, their confidence intervals included the null, i.e., the pooled estimate was statistically inconclusive and compatible with a range of possible effects.

Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of (in)effectiveness. Across most analyses, heterogeneity was substantial and prediction intervals were wide, indicating that pooled effects represent averages across interventions, populations, and trial designs that differ in potentially meaningful ways (e.g., intervention content, delivery mode, target populations, and control condition stringency). The small number of independent studies limited the extent to which these sources of heterogeneity could be examined statistically. Subgroup analyses based on small numbers of studies are often underpowered, and nonsignificant subgroup differences would not establish that effects are equivalent across categories (Harrer et al., 2021). Such heterogeneity implies that true effects vary considerably across studies, which reduces the precision of pooled estimates and may contribute to non-significant findings in specific follow-up windows despite non-zero point estimates. Although this diversity underscores the conceptual flexibility and adaptability of SSIs, it necessarily limits the extent to which any single pooled effect can be generalized to specific populations or implementation contexts.

In addition, evidence of publication bias was detected for anxiety outcomes at both the 1-13 week and 1-3 week windows, suggesting that studies with null or smaller effects may be underrepresented. Evidence of publication bias was also detected for depression at 1-13 weeks when Kaveladze et al. (2026) was included; no such evidence was found at 4-13 weeks. Because Egger's test requires at least 10 independent studies, publication-bias diagnostics could not be conducted for any depression subset excluding this trial, nor for the anxiety 4-13-week or depression 1-3-week windows, and the presence of bias in these narrower subsets cannot be excluded.

Moreover, only two included trials were judged to be at low risk of bias; most studies raised some concerns or were rated as high risk due to factors such as lack of preregistration or inadequate allocation concealment. Collectively, these factors may both inflate pooled effect estimates and obscure reliable detection of effects within narrower time windows.

A related consideration concerns the influence of a single large trial identified through consultation with subject-matter experts (Kaveladze et al., 2026). This trial had been completed as of our search date but was not captured by the database searches. It contributed a disproportionate share of the available depression effect sizes (40-53%, depending on window) and was markedly more statistically precise than the remaining depression trials.

When this trial was excluded, pooled depression effects were larger at both the 1-13 week window (g = -0.56 vs. -0.49) and the 4-13 week window (g = -0.42 vs. -0.29), though estimates remained statistically significant either way. This suggests the depression estimates reported here are, to some extent, attenuated by this trial relative to what the smaller, earlier trials alone would suggest. Because this trial was self-guided, delivered entirely online, and recruited a large community sample, its smaller observed effect may reflect differences in population, delivery format, or engagement rather than a true difference in intervention efficacy; conversely, its larger sample and low risk of bias may make it a more precise, less inflated estimate of the true effect than the smaller trials it is being compared against. Both interpretations remain speculative given that only one trial of this scale is currently available, and replication in similarly large, low-risk-of-bias adult SSI trials will be needed to adjudicate between them.

A post hoc analysis of within-control-group change offers a complementary perspective on this attenuation. Across the 10 depression trials with an eligible control arm, control conditions showed a statistically significant improvement from baseline to postintervention on average (g = -0.40, 95% CI [-0.67, -0.12]), indicating that participants in inactive or minimal-control conditions also tended to improve over time, independent of any SSI-specific benefit. This is consistent with the broader observation that between-group effect sizes partly reflect the extent to which control conditions themselves are associated with improvement (e.g., regression to the mean, natural symptom fluctuation, or nonspecific effects of trial participation), rather than solely reflecting intervention efficacy. A moderator analysis found no evidence that control-arm improvement in Kaveladze et al. (2026) differed from that of the other depression trials' control arms (QM(1) = 0.10, p = .755), suggesting its smaller between-group effect is not attributable to an unusually large improvement in its control condition specifically. The source of its attenuated effect therefore appears more likely related to sample composition, delivery format, or precision than to an atypical control condition.

Nevertheless, the present findings align with prior work supporting the therapeutic potential of single-session interventions in adult populations (e.g., Odgers et al., 2022; Schleider et al., 2025). The small-to-moderate effects of SSIs for adult anxiety and depression observed extend earlier meta-analytic evidence from youth SSI trials. However, effect sizes reported in youth samples have generally been smaller (e.g., anxiety g ≈ 0.56; depression g ≈ 0.21; Schleider & Weisz, 2017; Schleider et al., 2025). One plausible explanation, among other factors, may be that adult SSI trials more frequently enroll treatment-seeking individuals with clinically significant distress, whereas youth SSI studies often target preventive or subclinical populations, thereby limiting the magnitude of observable symptom change. This interpretation is consistent with broader psychotherapy research indicating larger effects in higher-severity samples compared with preventive or lower-intensity intervention contexts (e.g., Driessen et al., 2010).

Several strengths of the present review warrant consideration. First, to our knowledge, this meta-analysis provides the first quantitative synthesis explicitly focused on short-tomedium-term effects of single-session interventions for adult anxiety and depression, addressing a gap in the SSI literature that has primarily emphasized youth populations or aggregated outcomes across symptom domains in adults (e.g., Schleider & Weisz, 2017; Schleider et al., 2025). Second, by examining short-term and medium-term outcomes separately for each symptom domain, the review offers a more differentiated, domainspecific characterization of SSI effects across adjacent post-intervention windows.

Third, despite the modest number of available trials (k = 21), the included studies encompassed a range of therapeutic approaches, delivery formats, and adult age groups while adhering to a common SSI framework, enhancing the relevance of the findings to contemporary implementation contexts. Fourth, the use of three-level random-effects models constitutes a methodological strength, allowing for appropriate handling of dependency among multiple effect sizes within studies and more accurate estimation of uncertainty. Finally, situating the observed short-to-medium-term effects in relation to both youth SSI research and the broader psychotherapy literature helps contextualize adult SSI effects within established evidence on brief psychological interventions.

However, several limitations constrain the conclusions that can be drawn from this synthesis. The number of included randomized controlled trials remains small, with most studies conducted between 2012 and 2019 and relatively few published in recent years, indicating a slow accumulation of evidence in adult SSI research, though the total available sample size has grown substantially with the addition of one large 2026 trial. Moreover, the majority of trials were conducted in high-income countries and enrolled predominantly female, highly educated participants, which limits the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations and to low-and middle-income settings (WHO, 2023; Yan et al., 2024).

Comparator conditions also varied in stringency, ranging from waitlist or no-treatment controls to usual-care and placebo-like conditions. Although all met the prespecified definition of an inactive or minimal-control condition and none constituted a structured psychological treatment evaluated as an alternative intervention, they may still have differed in nonspecific factors such as contact, expectancy, symptom monitoring, or access to usual services. Formal interrater reliability statistics could not be reported because the preconsensus screening data required for retrospective calculation were unavailable. Finally, the within-control-group change analysis (see Meta-analyses and Results) was a post hoc addition prompted by an unexpected pattern in the depression findings rather than a preregistered analysis; its results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Beyond these constraints, important questions remain regarding the durability and longer-term implications of SSI effects. Although short-to-medium-term effects are commonly regarded as a primary therapeutic target of single-session interventions, longerterm outcomes remain essential for clarifying the role of SSIs within stepped-care and population-level mental health systems. In particular, evidence on whether a subset of individuals maintains clinically meaningful improvement over time would help determine the extent to which SSIs can reduce reliance on more intensive, multisession treatments by addressing needs amenable to brief care. This perspective aligns with emerging conceptualizations of SSIs as scalable components that complement, rather than replace, traditional services (Schleider et al., 2025). Future research should aim to address these gaps and strengthen the evidence base for SSIs targeting anxiety and depressive symptoms in adults across several key domains. First, additional preregistered and methodologically rigorous randomized controlled trials focused on adult anxiety and depression are needed to improve the precision and credibility of effect estimates and to reduce the risk of bias and publication-related inflation evident in the current literature. Second, trials should incorporate longer follow-up periods and multiple postintervention assessments to more clearly delineate the durability of short-to-medium-term SSI effects on anxiety and depressive symptoms and to characterize symptom trajectories beyond this time frame. Third, systematic investigation of moderators relevant to adult anxiety and depression will be important for clarifying for whom SSIs are most effective and under which conditions. Future meta-analyses should examine whether SSI effects vary by intervention and study characteristics, including therapeutic orientation, delivery format, session duration, comparator type, baseline severity, diagnostic status, age, and participant population. In the present evidence base, these characteristics were too sparsely represented and often closely intertwined to support reliable formal moderator analyses; for example, differences in session duration frequently reflected broader differences in intervention format, content, and intensity. As the adult SSI literature grows, larger evidence bases will be needed to disentangle these sources of heterogeneity and determine whether observed variation in effects reflects true moderation rather than study-level confounding. Fourth, future research should examine SSI effects in relation to active comparator conditions. The present review focused on whether SSIs show symptom effects relative to waitlist, no intervention, usual care, or minimal, nonspecific, or placebo-like comparators. Such comparators could be matched to the SSI in length, format, attention, or activity level, but structured psychological treatments evaluated as alternative interventions were excluded. By contrast, activecomparator trials address a distinct comparative question: whether SSIs are superior, equivalent, or noninferior to other active psychological interventions. This question is particularly relevant for implementation, because SSIs may be valuable not only if they produce larger symptom reductions, but also if they achieve comparable outcomes with lower time burden, lower cost, greater scalability, or easier delivery. Future meta-analyses should therefore examine active-comparator trials separately once a sufficiently large adult evidence base is available. Fifth, greater attention to mechanisms of change implicated in anxiety and depression (e.g., skill acquisition, cognitive restructuring, changes in hope or perceived control) would help elucidate how SSIs exert their effects in adult samples and guide optimization of intervention content and delivery. Sixth, future research should explicitly evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and scalability of SSIs for adult anxiety and depression, particularly in settings where the treatment gap for common mental disorders is most pronounced, such as low-and middle-income countries (Patel et al., 2018; WHO, 2023).

Seventh, the present review synthesized anxiety and depressive symptom outcomes, whereas secondary outcomes such as quality of life, functioning, wellbeing, impairment, and related transdiagnostic outcomes were not systematically examined. These outcomes are clinically important because SSIs may produce benefits that extend beyond symptom reduction, including improved coping, functioning, perceived control, or engagement with further care. Rationale for Deviations and Refinements from the Preregistered Protocol

This section documents and justifies refinements to the preregistered protocol that were implemented after registration but prior to study identification and data extraction, in accordance with PRISMA recommendations for transparent reporting of protocol deviations.

Refinement of Comparator Scope

In the preregistered protocol, we planned to include comparisons between single-session interventions (SSIs) and both inactive control conditions and multisession psychotherapies. Prior to study identification, this scope was refined to include only comparisons between SSIs and inactive control conditions (e.g., waitlist, no treatment, care-as-usual, or information-only controls). This refinement was motivated by conceptual and methodological considerations. First, the primary objective of the review was to establish whether SSIs produce measurable symptom reductions for adult anxiety and depression relative to minimal or no intervention, which represents the conventional first step in evaluating the effectiveness of a psychological intervention. Comparisons with inactive controls provide a clearer estimate of absolute intervention effects and avoid conflating SSI efficacy with differences in treatment intensity, duration, or therapist contact inherent in comparisons with multisession psychotherapies. Second, preliminary scoping indicated that trials directly comparing SSIs with multisession psychotherapies were rare limiting the interpretability of pooled estimates. Including such comparisons would likely have introduced substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity without meaningfully advancing the primary research question. Importantly, this refinement did not alter the core research objective, namely, to evaluate the effectiveness of SSIs for adult anxiety and depression, but rather clarified the comparator framework to align with best practices in intervention evaluation and meta-analytic methodology.

Operationalization of the Proximal Post-Intervention Window

The preregistered protocol specified post-intervention symptom outcomes but did not predefine specific follow-up intervals. Prior to data extraction, follow-up assessments were operationalized into short-term (1-3 weeks), medium-term (4-13 weeks), and overall (1-13 weeks) intervals, corresponding to an empirically supported proximal post-intervention window. This decision was informed by both theoretical considerations and the distribution of assessment timepoints across eligible trials. SSIs are explicitly conceptualized as ultra-brief interventions designed to exert their primary therapeutic effects shortly after intervention delivery, with longer-term outcomes often more variable and dependent on subsequent support or contextual factors. Consistent with prior SSI meta-analytic work, early postintervention assessments therefore represent the most theoretically appropriate window for evaluating SSI effects.

Refinement of the Statistical Analytic Model

The preregistered analytic plan specified conventional random-effects meta-analytic models. Prior to data extraction, this plan was refined to employ three-level random-effects models with robust variance estimation.This refinement was driven by the anticipated data structure of SSI trials, many of which report multiple effect sizes per study (e.g., multiple outcomes, time points, or instruments). Three-level models allow for appropriate handling of dependency among effect sizes nested within studies, thereby reducing bias in standard errors and improving the accuracy of uncertainty estimates.
