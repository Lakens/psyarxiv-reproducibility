research_questions Studies that assess children’s ability to deploy possibility concepts using a Y-shaped tube task – which require a child to catch a marble falling down a forked tube by covering both possible exits of tube — suggest that 3-year-olds generally deploy possibility concepts when choosing their actions (e.g., Andreuceolli et al., 2024; Redshaw & Suddendorf 2016). Others have argued that success in the Y-shaped tube task is better explained on the hypothesis that participants deploy “minimal representation of possibility” (Leahy & Carey 2020; Leahy et al. 2022). On this view, children use simulation to identify one location for a reward, and instead of marking that simulated location as merely possible, they take it to be the fact of the matter.
In one test of this idea, Leahy (2024) assessed children’s performance on a modified Y-shaped tube with two possible exits, where a third “impossible tube” was attached to the apparatus. Leahy reasoned that (1) a child who deploys minimal representations of possibility should cover one possible exit; (2) a child who is not evaluating the structure of the tube should often cover the impossible exit; and (3) a child who deploys possibility concepts should cover both possible exits. In this study, only 17% of 3-year-olds deployed possibility concepts (i.e., identified multiple incompatible possibilities while ruling out impossibilities by placing their hands under both ends of the Y-shaped tube).
In a further test of this hypothesis, the proposed study will examine whether children are minimal representers of possibility by making two modifications to Leahy’s (2024) procedure. First, instead of asking children to use their hands to catch dropping marbles, objects will fall into one of three opaque boxes. Two of these boxes will be connected to a Y-shaped tube, and a third box will be connected to a single (“impossible”) tube. On critical trials, children will be asked to identify two boxes they want to look inside by placing 1 sticker on each of them, and critically, will not be permitted to look inside any boxes until both choices are made (to preclude feedback that might allow a sequential simulation of possibilities). Second, the single tube will not be connected to the Y-shaped apparatus to account for potential failures in children’s knowledge of tube structure (i.e., to avoid the inference that the impossible arm is a viable option since it’s connected to the Y-shaped tube).
Our reasoning is that asking children to place stickers will force them to always make two choices, unlike studies that ask children to use their hands and allow them to choose their own strategy. One possibility, for example, is that modal reasoners understand that there are two possible exits for a ball, but prefer to place only one hand under their preferred “bet” of where it will emerge. We reasoned that when forced to make two choices (1) a child who deploys possibility concepts should cover both possible exits; and (2) a child who deploys minimal representations of possibility should choose one possible exit, and may choose randomly between the other two exits. Therefore, if children are minimal representers, and only succeed on Y-shaped tubes via sequential simulation, then they should fail in the present study since they do not have evidence that their initial choice is only merely possible. 		In Study 1, we examined whether children are minimal representers of possibility on a modified version of Leahy’s (2024) impossible tubes task. First, instead of asking children to use their hands to catch dropping marbles, objects were dropped into one of three opaque boxes. Two of these boxes were connected to a Y-shaped tube, and a third box was connected to a single (“impossible”) tube. On critical trials, children were asked to identify two boxes they want to look inside by placing 1 sticker on each of them, and critically, were not be permitted to look inside any boxes until both choices were made (to preclude feedback that might allow a sequential simulation of possibilities). We found that the older children in our study (i.e., children between 31 and 38 months) almost always covered both possible exits, whereas younger children (i.e., children between 24 and 30 months) chose randomly between possible and impossible exits. The results of Experiment 1 indicated not just that children’s representations of possibility emerge gradually between 2 and 3 years of age, but critically, that 3-year-olds do deploy possibility concepts, contradicting the findings of previous studies.

In this follow-up study, we seek to replicate this pattern of findings, while removing children’s ability to adapt a heuristic while completing critical trials. In particular, we will address the possibility that older children might always choose both ends of the y-shaped tube when it is implicated in a trial (i.e., both when it is the right answer and when it is not). To do so, we will introduce an equal number of trials in which the correct answer is (a) both ends of the y-shaped tube (critical; see 4a), and (b) the impossible tube and one of the two y-shaped tubes (two-ball; see 4d). Therefore, if children are minimal representers, and only succeed on Y-shaped tubes by adapting a heuristic, then they should always choose the y-shaped tube, regardless of the correct choice. However, if children are distinguishing merely possible and impossible events, then we should see (1) continued success on the critical trial, and (2) a higher proportion of correct responses on both (critical and two-ball) trials relative to incorrect responses on both trials.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

A prominent hypothesis in the literature on logical reasoning suggests that, unlike human adults and older children, 3-year-olds deploy a "minimal representation of possibility" when reasoning about the existence of a reward in one of two possible locations. On this view, children are unable to represent two mutually exclusive possibilities simultaneously, and instead commit to one location for the reward in a pair of possible locations, only considering the second location once they have evidence that their first choice is not correct. Although this hypothesis may explain children's struggles on various tests of modal reasoning, the idea that children may represent possible reward locations sequentially (rather than simultaneously) has not been directly tested. In two studies, we asked 24-to 38-month-olds to choose two of three reward locations, one of which was physically impossible. We found that children aged 31 to 38 months selected the two possible locations without feedback when required to make a simultaneous choice. We argue that these data are compatible with an ability to represent multiple mutually exclusive possibilities.

[div-01]

The capacity to represent possibilities is central to human reasoning (Carey et al., 2020).

People consider multiple outcomes when we make everyday decisions -e.g., whether to drive or take the bus to work, whether to cook dinner at home or order takeout, etc. On some views, the ability to represent possibilities is evolutionarily-ancient (Englemman et al., 2021; 2023; c.f. Redshaw et al., 2016; Suddendorf et al., 2017) and early emerging in ontogeny, and therefore detectable in the behaviors of young infants (e.g., Cesana-Arlotti, 2018; 2022; Téglás & Bonatti, 2016). However, others argue that this ability is gradually constructed in human development, and depends upon learning modal language like 'can', 'might', 'may', and 'have to' at around age 4 (e.g., Leahy, 2023; 2024; 2026; Leahy & Carey, 2020; Leahy & Zalnieriunas, 2022; Leahy et al., 2025; 2026; Ozturk & Papafragou, 2015). On this latter view, prior to acquiring modal language, children deploy a ''minimal representation" of possibility, which acts as a precursor to possibility concepts and only allows children to consider one possible outcome at a time (Leahy, 2024; 2026; Leahy & Carey, 2020; Leahy et al., 2022). More concretely, when children see a reward enter one of two mutually exclusive possible locations (e.g., two cups), rather than representing the two possibilities simultaneously, children may instead commit to one reward location (e.g., one cup), and only consider the second location once they have evidence that their first choice is not correct.

Consistent with the minimal representations hypothesis, non-verbal tests of children's modal representations reveal a failure to represent multiple simultaneous possibilities until age 4 (Beck et al., 2006; Leahy, 2023; 2024; 2026; Leahy et al. 2022; Mody & Carey, 2016). One prominent example of such failure comes from Mody and Carey's (2016) three cups task. In this task and subsequent variants (Grigoroglou et al., 2019; Leahy, 2023; Leahy and Zalnieriunas, 2022), children are presented with three containers organized into two sets: a pair on one side, and a singleton on the other (Figure 1a). Each set is occluded and children see one reward hidden in each set, such that the singleton set always has a reward, but only one of the two containers in the pair has a reward (Figure 1b). Finally, all occluders are removed, and children are asked to choose a container (Figure 1c). The logic of the task is that children who can distinguish necessary from merely possible outcomes should always choose the singleton set, since the singleton guarantees a reward. Surprisingly, however, older 2-year-olds only choose the singleton around 50% of the time and 3-year-olds do so around 60% of the time, whereas 4-year-olds choose the singleton around 70% of the time (Mody & Carey, 2016; also see Grigoroglou et al. 2019; Leahy 2023). Although 3-year-olds' performance could be interpreted as success on the task (since performance by chance would be around 33%), subsequent research suggests that these data may be better explained by the minimal representations hypothesis (Leahy et al., 2023; Leahy & Carey, 2020). (2016).

According to the minimal account, 3-year-olds commit to specific concrete beliefs about the location of each reward in the three cups tasks -e.g., hypothesizing that the singleton cup contains a reward, but also that one particular cup from the side with two options also contains one. What children cannot do, on this view, is entertain more than one mutually exclusive possibility at a time: that a reward could be located in either of the cups on the two-cup side.

Given this, when they make a choice, it is expected that they should search the singleton side 50% of the time and the two-cup side 50% of the time, just as one would if they knew the actual locations of each reward. By contrast, older children are argued to reason about possibility, favoring the sure bet of choosing the singleton cup (Leahy & Carey, 2020; Ozturk & Papafragou, 2015; Rafetseder et al., 2010; Redshaw & Suddendorf, 2020). Crucially, success on such tasks is associated with children's developing modal language. Although children begin to use words that express possibility and necessity, such as "maybe", "can", and "have to" by age 2 or 3 (Cournane, 2020), they only acquire adult-like meanings of these words around or after age 4 (Leahy et al., 2026; Leahy & Zalnieriunas, 2022). According to the authors, understanding the meanings of these lexical items may provide children a hypothesis space from which to construct such meanings (Grigoroglou & Ganea, 2022; Leahy et al., 2026; Leahy & Carey, 2020).

While some previous studies make a strong case for the minimal representations hypothesis, others have contested it, arguing that children can represent multiple mutually exclusive possibilities earlier in development (Alderete & Xu, 2023; Andreuccioli, Mazor, et al., 2026; Cesana-Arlotti et al. 2018; Cesana-Arlotti et al. 2022; Goddu et al., 2021; Gopnik et al. 2016; Téglás & Bonatti, 2016; Téglás et al., 2007; Turan-Küçük & Kibbe, 2024; 2025a; 2025b; Xu & Garcia, 2008; for some evidence of modal reasoning in non-human animals, see Engelmann et al., 2023a; 2021; Warren et al., 2025). Some suggest that possibility representations are deployed by pre-linguistic infants (e.g., Cesana-Arlotti et al. 2018; Cesana-Arlotti et al. 2022; for discussion, also see Brody et al., 2024; Turan-Küçük & Kibbe, 2026). Others suggest that by 3 years of age, children may be able to deploy possibility concepts in other, arguably simpler tests of knowledge (Redshaw & Suddendorf, 2016; 2019; Turan-Küçük & Kibbe, 2024; 2025a). On this latter view, children's possibility concepts could precede modal language, but their possibility representations may be masked by competing motivations or cognitive demands, such as a tendency to explore (Andreuccioli, Mazor et al., 2026), a compulsion to make one choice or avoid undesired actions (Xu & Alderete, 2023), or difficulty with action planning (Turan-Kucuk & Kibbe, 2024; also see Turan-Kucuk & Kibbe, 2025a , 2025b; for review, see Turan-Kucuk, Alderete, et al., 2026). As one example of this, Andreuccioli, Mazor, et al. (2026) suggest that 3-year-olds' inability to select necessary over merely possible outcomes in the three-cup tasks may stem from children's motivation to seek additional information about uncertain events.

Although several studies document earlier knowledge across tasks, perhaps the most compelling evidence that 3-year-olds deploy possibility concepts comes from studies using the Y-shaped tube task and its variations (Beck et al., 2006; Redshaw & Suddendorf, 2016; Redshaw et al., 2019; Turan-Kucuk & Kibbe, 2024). In this task, children are presented with a tube that is forked at the bottom, shaped like an upside-down ''Y" (Figure 2a). The experimenter demonstrates that when a marble is dropped into the tube, it may come out of either end of the tube, and can be caught by placing their hands below the openings of the tubes. These authors reasoned that if children are able to represent multiple possible outcomes, they should choose to cover both ends of the Y-shaped tube by placing one hand under each. However, if they fail to represent multiple possibilities, in keeping with the minimal representations hypothesis, they should place just one hand under the opening where they expect the marble to emerge. Using this method, Redshaw and Suddendorf found that although most 2-year-olds used one hand on every trial, many 3-year-olds used two hands on every trial (39%), or successfully switched to two-handed responses after using one hand for a few trials (33%), while most 4-year-olds (78%) used two hands spontaneously. Still, proponents of the minimal representations account (Leahy & Carey 2020; Leahy et al., 2023) argue that children's success on the Y-shaped tube task can also be explained by their deployment of minimal representations. For example, Leahy (2024) argued that children may not reason about the possibilities inherent to the structure of the Y-shaped tubes, but simply observe that there are two good places to put their hands, and therefore place one under each location. To demonstrate this, Leahy (2024) tested children using a modified apparatus that featured a Y-shaped tube with two possible exits, but also a third straight tube, grafted to the Y-shaped tube by a piece of solid wood, such that a ball placed in the top of the Y could not possibly exit from the third "impossible" tube (Figure 2b). Leahy reasoned that (1) a child who deploys possibility concepts should cover both exits of the Y-shaped tube, (2) a child using minimal representations of possibility should cover one possible exit of the Y-shaped tube; and (3) a child who is not reasoning about possibility in the task, and who fails to evaluate the structure of the apparatus should also frequently cover the impossible exit. However, in their study, only 17% of 3-year-olds placed a hand under both exits of the Y-shaped tube, suggesting that very few, if any, 3-year-olds deployed possibility concepts. Further, Leahy found that when children were tested with a "U-shaped tube" (Figure 2c), where only one of two openings was a possible candidate for the ball to exit, 3-year-olds deployed one hand to cover the single possible exit just as often (42%) as they used a two-hand strategy to cover both exits (37%), even though the second exit was physically impossible. Given these findings, Leahy argued that 3-year-olds' apparent success on previous iterations of the Y-shaped tubes may have overestimated children's modal reasoning abilities.

In the present study, we challenge two key assumptions of this past work to provide a stronger test of the minimal representations hypothesis. First, a critical assumption in Leahy's (2024) study was that the impossible tubes he presented were perceived by children as categorically impossible. However, it's unclear that this was the case, since in each of the reported studies the "impossible" distractor tubes were still physically connected to the target "possible" tubes, making it ambiguous whether balls might indeed enter them. For example, in Leahy's adapted Y-shaped tube, the child was required to infer that the distractor tube was impossible by reasoning that a ball placed into the apparatus would need to travel against gravity to enter it. Similarly, in the U-tube trials, it was crucial that children notice, as adults might, that for a ball to exit the impossible tube it would have to travel along a path that defies gravity.

However, if children did not reason like adults, or did not attend carefully to the precise location of the entry point, they may have failed to notice this physical impossibility, raising the possibility that their placement of two hands did indeed reflect modal reasoning.

Second, a key challenge to evaluating previous studies using the tubes method, including Leahy's, is that success requires not only modal reasoning, but also a shared understanding of the goals of the game. For example, rather than thinking that their goal is to maximize the number of objects that they can catch, children may instead understand the task as a test of whether they can correctly guess an object's location. These two goals should produce very different patterns of behavior, with the first predicting the use of two hands, but the second predicting the placement of one hand under the child's "best bet". By analogy, whereas one might place bets on all horses in a race to communicate that all outcomes are possible, to win at the races one ordinarily picks a winner and places a single bet. Similarly, some children may not choose to use two hands because they view their goal as correctly guessing each object's location, not maximizing the number of objects retrieved. More broadly, existing versions of the tube task do not clearly differentiate between the ability to represent multiple possibilities and the ability to infer the goals of the task and the strategies that are required to succeed. Given this, failures in previous paradigms may not reflect an inability to represent multiple possibilities, but instead uncertainty about how to act on those representations within the context of a task.

In the present study, we addressed these concerns by making two critical changes to the tube task. First, we reasoned that a stronger test of Leahy's claim requires making the impossible tubes obviously and categorically impossible -i.e., that there is no reason to make their impossibility ambiguous by connecting them to the possible tubes, as he did, and that introducing this ambiguity undermines the assumption that children perceive them as truly impossible.

Therefore in the two studies we present below, all impossible tubes were obviously impossible.

In particular, we presented children with a Y-shaped tube that was attached to a sheet of perforated pegboard, next to a singleton "impossible" tube that was connected to the apparatus, but not directly connected to the Y-shaped tube (contrary to Leahy, 2024). At the bottom end of each tube was a black box that was meant to catch falling objects, while also concealing their final landing sites (Figure 3). We reasoned that, to the extent that children actually viewed impossible tubes as impossible in Leahy (2024), children should behave as he predicted, and should show no preference for covering the second exit of Y-shaped tube vs. the single impossible tube. However, if children engage in possibility reasoning and are able to distinguish impossible vs. possible outcomes, then they should selectively prefer to place their hands beneath the two exits of the Y-shaped tube.

Second, and critical to our intervention, we required children to always make two choices at two distinct locations, in order to test which of the three locations they thought were most likely to contain objects. Specifically, instead of asking children to use their hands to catch dropping balls, they were asked to identify two boxes they wanted to look inside by placing one sticker on each of two locations prior to opening either. Children were trained to place both stickers at the same time, minimizing the likelihood of a sequential choice. 2 In this way, their choices depended on their assessment of which exits were possible, rather than on their ability to understand the goals of the game, or to conjure an effective strategy. We reasoned that, when forced to make two choices (by placing two stickers on 2 out of 3 boxes at the same time), a child who deploys mature possibility concepts should cover both exits of the Y-shaped tube, but never the exit of the physically impossible singleton tube. However, a child who deploys minimal representations of possibility should believe that only one exit of the Y-shaped tube is possible and that the other is impossible, and therefore should not make a conceptual differentiation between the second exit of the Y-shaped tube and the singleton impossible tube (since impossibility is not a graded notion, and should apply equally to both remaining options on the minimal representations hypothesis; see General Discussion). Materials and Procedure

Sticker Placement Training and Pretest

Participants were first taught to place stickers on opaque boxes to make choices and were required to pass a pretest before proceeding to the Tubes task. Specifically, participants were introduced to four boxes (Figure 4), and were told that they had to choose lunchboxes for a circles denote the stickers. The far side of the box (towards the experimenter / giraffe) was open, and the opening was repositioned for the child to explore and find fruit only once the child made their choices by placing both stickers.

The experimenter then gave the child either one or two stickers, and asked, "Which box[es] do you want to look inside? Can you put a sticker[s] on it/them?" Children were first given two practice trials, wherein they received one sticker to place on the first trial, and two stickers on the second trial. On the second trial, if a child chose only one box (e.g., by only placing one of the two stickers, or by placing two stickers on the same box), they were reminded that they could choose more than one box at a time ("Remember! You should put one sticker on every box you want to look inside. Can you choose another box? Let's do another!"). If children correctly placed both stickers on different boxes, they were given affirmative feedback ("That's right -just like that! Let's do another!"). In this way, children were taught to place two stickers on two boxes at the same time.

Following this training, children completed three additional trials in which they were given two stickers to place on every trial, without feedback. If children made two choices from the four options (i.e., by placing stickers on 2/4 boxes, they "passed" the trial). We used their performance on this task as a criterion for inclusion in the study. Only children who passed at least 2 of the 3 pretest trials were included. This ensured that children who were included understood that (a) placing a sticker meant making a choice, and (b) that they could only look inside the boxes on which they placed stickers. Based on these criteria, two children were not included in the study, because they did not place any stickers on the boxes.

Tubes Task

Once children passed the sticker training, they were introduced to the tube apparatus (Figure 3). Following Leahy (2024), the apparatus consisted of a Y-shaped tube and a separate, impossible singleton tube, though in the present study the impossible tube was obviously impossible by virtue of not being physically connected to the Y-shaped tube. Both the Y-shaped tube and the singleton tube were attached to a single sheet of white pegboard, and the ends of each tube were connected to a black box.

Familiarization Phase. In the familiarization phase, participants were introduced to the apparatus: "We're going to play a game! Let me show you how it works. Look, these tubes are connected to some boxes. I will drop some balls down these tubes, and your job is to guess where they went." The experimenter then dropped a ball down the singleton tube and said, "Look! When I drop a ball down this tube, it falls into this box". The experimenter removed the box from the apparatus and allowed the child to take the ball out of the box. Next, the experimenter dropped a ball down the Y-shaped tube and said, "Look! When I drop a ball down this tube, it didn't fall here, [E shows empty box], it's here! [E showed the box with a ball and allowed the child to retrieve it]. This demonstration was completed twice, with the ball falling down the left side once, and the right side once, counterbalanced between participants. A plastic divider, not visible to the child, was placed within the opening of the Y-shaped tube to control which side the ball dropped. Finally, the ball was dropped down the singleton tube once again.

Participants therefore saw the ball dropped in the following sequences: (1) singleton, (2) Y-shaped tube (left/right), (3) Y-shaped tube (right/left), (4) singleton.

Test Phase. At the beginning of each trial, the experimenter held the ball[s] on top of one entrance for the tube in which it was supposed to fall (the Y-shaped tube or the single tube), and drew the child's attention to it, "Look!" before dropping it. Once the ball dropped, the child was given either one or two stickers and asked, "Which box[es] do you want to look inside? Can you put [a] sticker[s] on it/them?". On each of the four critical trials, the experimenter dropped the ball down the Y-shaped tube, and children were given two stickers to place. On critical trials, the experimenter gave the child both stickers to place at the same time. There were four one-sticker filler trials, wherein the experimenter dropped a ball down the Y-shaped tube (Y-filler x 2 trials) or singleton tube (S-filler x 2 trials) and the child was given only one sticker to place. In addition, there was a two-ball filler trial, in which the experimenter dropped two balls simultaneously, one down the Y-shaped tube and one down the singleton tube, and the child was given two stickers to place (two-ball x 1 trial; see Table 1). We included these filler trials so that children could not easily adopt a heuristic for solving the critical trials. Participants were only allowed to look inside the boxes they chose once they had made both of their choices. Trials began either with a Y-filler or an S-filler, counterbalanced across participants. This meant that participants completed the test phase in one of two orders: (1) S-filler, critical, Y-filler, critical, two-ball, S-filler, critical, Y-filler, critical, or (2) Y-filler, critical, S-filler, critical, two-ball, Y-filler, critical, S-filler, critical.

Study 2

In Study 2, we sought to replicate the pattern of findings in Study 1, while addressing the possibility that older children adopted a heuristic of always choosing both ends of the Y-shaped tube when that response was expected on a large number of trials. To address this, in Study 2 we tested children with an equal number of trials in which the correct answer was (a) both ends of the Y-shaped tube (critical), or (b) the singleton tube plus one of the two Y-shaped tube locations (two-ball). We reasoned that if children are minimal representers and only succeed by adopting a heuristic of picking both exits of the Y-shaped tube on all trials with two stickers, then they should again show a preference for both ends of the Y-shaped tube regardless of which locations are the correct choice. However, if they reason about possibility, then given this balanced trial structure children should only select both exits of the Y-shaped tube when these are the correct choices.

General Discussion

We tested the hypothesis that 2-to 3-year-olds deploy "minimal representations" of possibility in the Tubes task using an adaptation of Leahy (2024). In two studies, we asked children to identify two locations for a reward. In Study 1, we found that older children (between 2;7 and 3;2 years of age) -but not younger children -chose the two possible locations most of the time. In Study 2, both older and younger children (between 2;0 and 2;6) performed better than chance when a more balanced trial structure was used. Thus, when required to make two choices, children preferred the two exits of the Y-shaped tube over the singleton tube. This suggests that children thought that both exits of the Y-shaped tube were possible in a way that the singleton tube was not. Crucially, on the minimal representations view, once a child commits to an object's location, they should consider all remaining locations to be impossible. The theory therefore predicts no differentiation between locations that are deemed impossible by inference (e.g., the other exit of the Y-shaped tube) and those that are physically impossible (e.g., the singleton). Although we found considerable variability in the performance of 24-to 30-month-olds, our data suggest that children as young as 2½ (31 months and older) treated physically possible outcomes differently from a physically impossible outcome, contrary to Leahy's (2024) findings, and consistent with the idea that children can reason about multiple distinct possibilities.

Was children's reasoning in our study based on modal reasoning, or did they differentiate possible from impossible outcomes using sequential simulation? As described in the Introduction, Leahy and colleagues (Leahy et al., 2022; Leahy, 2024) argued that children show greater success on past versions of the Tubes task because they sequentially simulated the possible locations of the reward -e.g., first imagining one possible location but not the other, and then, when given feedback that their choice was incorrect, imagining the second possible location. Given this, a key goal of the present study was to minimize the likelihood of sequential simulation by asking children to simultaneously identify two possible locations of a reward without knowing the outcome of either location. To accomplish this, on critical trials the experimenter handed the child two stickers together, and trained them to place both stickers on two boxes at the same time. On these trials, the child did not receive feedback from the experimenter regarding the actual object locations before both stickers were placed. Thus, although it remains possible that children engage in sequential simulation when they are forced to make two simultaneous choices, this would require that they do so quickly, either before making their choices or during the response process. Crucially, this would make sequential simulation difficult to empirically differentiate from a simultaneous process that reflects modal reasoning in any age group, including 4-year-olds, whose success on such tasks has been argued to reflect modal reasoning (e.g., Carey, 2020; Leahy et al., 2022; Leahy, 2026).

Another way to explain our findings is to argue that children assigned a different status to "physical" vs. "inferred" impossibility. We reasoned, following the minimal representation view, that once a child commits to a particular object location all other locations become impossible, and equally so, predicting no difference between them. However, could it be the case that children treat physical impossibility as more impossible than inferred impossibility? Two versions of this hypothesis might be proposed. First, children might completely ignore the singleton tube, and have a generalized bias to select exits of the Y-shaped tube regardless of the ball locations. Against this, however, children almost always chose the singleton tube exit when appropriate. Also, our main results remained significant when excluding children who showed any sign of a Y-shaped tube bias. A second possibility is that children might have a graded notion of impossibility, classifying physically impossible tubes as more impossible than tubes that are deemed impossible by inference. The problem with this view is that there is no clear sense in which something can be partially impossible without being partially possible, and in general, impossibility is treated as absolute in modal logic. For example, while previous accounts argue for graded notions of possibility (Kratzer, 1981; Lassiter, 2017), and recognize different varieties of necessity (Fine, 2005), they never assign gradations of possibility to impossible events.

Interestingly, some accounts posit gradations of similarity between possible and impossible worlds (Nolan, 1997), potentially explaining human judgments that levitating a feather might be easier than levitating a rock (e.g., Shtulman & Morgan, 2017; for related studies, see Hu et al., 2026; Khan & Starmans, 2025). However, even on these accounts, logically impossible worlds remain categorically impossible. Just as important, multiple empirical studies with older children suggest that rather than having a more fine-grained understanding of impossibility than adults (e.g., that might allow them to assign some impossible events a gradient of possibility), children instead have a tendency to categorize improbable events as categorically impossible. For example, many 4-to 5-year-olds judge that it's impossible to find a crocodile under their bed or to make pickle-flavoured ice-cream, though both are possible and differ only in probability (e.g., Goulding & Freidman, 2021; Shtulman & Carey, 2007; also see Hu et al., 2026; Khan & Starmans, 2025). 7 While the present study was restricted to testing modal reasoning with a tube task, our findings may also be relevant to understanding children's failures in other tasks. In the Introduction, we reasoned that young children may fail the Y-shaped tube task because they view it as a test of their ability to correctly guess an object's location -like betting on the winner of a race -rather than as a test of their understanding of which outcomes are possible (e.g., which would involve listing which racers are competing). The results of this study are relevant to the claim that children's ability to reason about multiple possibilities is tied to their acquisition of modal language. In past studies, proponents of the minimal representations hypothesis have argued that children begin to succeed on tests of modal reasoning at 4 years of age owing to their acquisition of modal language (see Leahy & Carey, 2026; Leahy & Zalnieriunas, 2022; for relevant discussion, also see: Dieuleveut & van Dooren, 2025), and 3-year-olds fail to reason about possibility because they have not yet acquired modal language (Mody & Carey, 2016; Leahy et al., 2020; 2022; Leahy, 2024; 2026; Leahy & Zalnieriunas, 2022). Our finding that children may succeed by age 2½ conflicts with this conclusion. In particular, our data suggest that many children can simultaneously represent multiple possible locations of a reward before they comprehend modal language. Therefore, it's possible that rather than driving the development of modal concepts, modal language may emerge only once non-verbal possibility concepts have emerged in development.

Although our findings suggest that 2½-year-olds may be able to represent multiple possibilities simultaneously, they also leave open a number of questions that should be addressed in future studies. First, although older children between 2;7 and 3;2 years were consistently successful across both studies, younger children between 2;0 and 2;6 were much more variable in their performance. One possibility is that younger children are more likely to perseverate over a specific strategy and gradually become better at understanding the goals of the task. Future research should further explore the mechanisms underlying younger children's behaviour using other, arguably more sensitive measures of children's reasoning. Second, although our study suggests that strategy choice and planning may obscure modal reasoning in the Y-shaped tube task, it's unclear whether a similar explanation can be deployed to explain failures on other tasks, or if distinct explanations are required. Future studies should consider constructing a cups task analogous to the task used here, but that makes clearer the goal of optimizing reward vs.

exploration. Finally, because we were unable to collect demographic data about our participants' socioeconomic status or ethnic backgrounds, it's possible that these demographic factors -or co-occurring differences in executive functioning or vocabulary -explain some variability in our data, and therefore limit the generalizability of these findings. Future research should explore these questions.

In the present studies, we tested the minimal representations hypothesis by requiring children to always make two choices from three available options, and found that children as young as 2½ were able to make possibility inferences when given a clear goal of maximizing their rewards. Children's ability to choose two merely possible locations for a reward while avoiding the impossible location is most compatible with simultaneous representations of possibility.
