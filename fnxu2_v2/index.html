How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Emotion is rooted in bodily physiology and socially constructed through language and culture.

We propose the Theory of Co-constructed Emotion (TCoE) via Collective Predictive Coding, integrating interoception into agents' generative models within an active-inference framework.

Emotion emerges through coupled loops of individual allostatic regulation and social communication: bodily evidence grounds shared emotion words, while negotiated words shape bodily inference and regulation. This framework bridges psychological and social constructivism, aligns with findings on emotional contagion and concept formation, and supports constructive verification in multi-agent robotic systems. It also offers a computational account of alexithymia as a disconnection between interoception and social symbols, linking individual embodiment with evolving social symbol systems.

1 Introduction: Bridging Minds, Bodies, and Societies

Our emotions are inextricably dual: psychologically constructed and rooted in the body, while simultaneously socially constructed and rooted in language and culture. Language, culture, and their value systems are often treated as static givens. Yet the term "social construction" itself signals otherwise: they are continuously co-produced by the agents who use them. In this sense, emotions are co-constructed.

Despite the emphasis on social construction in constructivist theories of emotion, the bottom-up pathways involved in this process have not been sufficiently theorized. Although current constructivist frameworks acknowledge reciprocal relations between bodily experience and social meaning, they have mostly formalized how "already constructed" language, culture, and norms exert a top-down influence on our emotions. This perspective remains incomplete. Indeed, Parkinson (2012) argues that the social construction of emotion is no longer in question, and that the outstanding task is to specify how these processes operate and what materials they use. A bottom-up computational account is precisely positioned to answer this call for mechanism. In this paper, we build on Collective Predictive Coding (CPC) (Taniguchi, 2024a) and Collective Active Inference (CAIF) (Taniguchi et al., 2026) to present a novel theoretical framework. We describe how people, through interaction, actively form emotion words and associated linguistic systems within a community, or semiosphere. These emergent systems, in turn, shape individuals' emotional understanding and bodily regulation.

Barrett's Theory of Constructed Emotion (TCE) (Barrett, 2017a (Barrett, , 2017b;; Barrett & Miller, 2026) has driven a paradigm shift in emotion research by redefining emotion not as a passive reaction to external stimuli, but as an active predictive process in which the brain constructs meaning from interoceptive fluctuations (Barrett & Simmons, 2015). In this view, the brain mobilizes prior concepts to infer the causes of uncertain bodily signals, thereby constructing emotional episodes suited to the current context. However, even this sophisticated framework typically treats the generative mechanisms by which emotional concepts and linguistic categories themselves are formed and shared as given: society and culture appear only as fixed top-down priors on individual processing, and their bottom-up co-construction is left unaddressed. Social constructivism (Harré, 1986; Reddy, 2001) has long emphasized that emotions are shaped by historical and cultural discourse. Interoceptive accounts (Craig, 2002; Seth, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016) use the Free Energy Principle (FEP) (K. Friston, 2010) and active inference (AIF) (Parr et al., 2022) to show how socially shared concepts function as top-down priors that regulate interoceptive prediction error, vividly demonstrating the link between social meaning and individual bodily experience. Yet even in these models, language and society serve as fixed environments external to the agent. What remains unaddressed is the cyclical process of co-construction itself (Ohira, 2020a): how the social predictive model that regulates individual emotion emerges bottom-up from affective interactions and continues to dynamically evolve. Symbol Emergence in Robotics (Taniguchi et al., 2016 (Taniguchi et al., , 2018)), a constructive approach to symbol emergence systems and developing minds, offers a constructive answer to this challenge: it reframes language not as a predefined symbolic system, but as a dynamic system that self-organizes bottom-up through physical and social interactions among agents. CPC (Taniguchi, 2024a) formalizes this symbol emergence as decentralized Bayesian inference and extends FEP (K. Friston, 2010 Friston, , 2019) ) to the social level. Building on CPC, we extend symbol emergence to the domain of emotion and propose the Theory of Co-constructed Emotion (TCoE) via CPC, which integrates it with active inference by incorporating interoception into each agent's generative model, alongside exteroception, internal and external representations, and symbolic information. Individual interoceptive regulation and social concept sharing thus become processes driven by the same mathematical principle: an internal loop of allostasis regulates individual bodily states, and a social loop of communication shares emotional concepts. Bodily experience grounds the negotiation of shared symbols, while those symbols shape individual bodily prediction and regulation.

The unique contribution of this study is the explicit incorporation of interoception (Barrett & Simmons, 2015; Craig, 2002; Seth, 2013) into the conventional CPC framework, which has primarily focused on exteroceptive symbol sharing. This extension is non-trivial because interoceptive states are private, low-resolution, and only indirectly accessible, whereas CPC has typically modeled symbol sharing through externally observable signals. Grounding this extension in FEP connects individual bodily inference and regulation to the collective perspective of CAIF.

Consequently, our model extends beyond mere passive shared perception to encompass bodily regulation aimed at minimizing interoceptive prediction errors-namely, mechanisms of homeostasis and allostasis-within the context of collective interaction. This extension makes it possible, for the first time, to describe within a single mathematical framework the dynamic process by which "private bodily sensations" and "public symbols" mutually constrain and co-construct one another.

The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows: 1.

Theoretical Integration: We propose TCoE via CPC, which unifies the bodily and social construction of emotion within a single mathematical framework, computationally bridging individual interoceptive processes and social symbolic processes.

2. Clinical Implications: We redefine alexithymia computationally as a failure of emotional co-construction-a disconnection between interoception and social symbols-offering a novel interpretation through the lens of predictive processing.

3. Path to Constructive Verification:

We show that TCoE provides a foundation verifiable through constructive experiments using multi-agent robotic systems and artificial intelligence (AI), opening a new frontier between computational neuroscience and robotics.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews psychological and neuroscientific evidence, Section 3 examines constructive models of embodied emotion, and Section 4 presents TCoE via CPC. Section 5 offers a computational account of alexithymia, Section 6 discusses empirical predictions, and Section 7 concludes.

2 Empirical Foundations: Psychological and Neuroscientific Evidence

2.1 Predictive Coding of Interoception

Predictive coding treats perception as inference through generative models, rather than passive registration of sensory input (K. Friston, 2010; Rao & Ballard, 1999). Applied to interoception, it concerns how bodily signals are interpreted and connected to regulation (Craig, 2002 (Craig, , 2009;; Seth, 2013). This provides a framework for relating physiological fluctuations to emotional experience without assuming that a bodily pattern uniquely specifies an emotion. Predictive processing has also been proposed as an integrative account of perception, action, affect, and subjective experience (Ohira, 2023).

Interoceptive inference connects expectations about bodily conditions to autonomic, endocrine, and immune regulation (Seth, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016). Allostasis emphasizes anticipated bodily needs rather than only correction of an existing deviation (Sterling, 2012). Barrett's body-budget account places predictive resource regulation at the center of constructed emo-tion (Barrett, 2017a). The related allostasis-first perspective treats mental phenomena as developing from the coordination of physiological resources (Theriault et al., 2025).

Large-scale neuroimaging supports an allostatic-interoceptive account of brain function.

Using the Embodied Predictive Interoception Coding (EPIC) model, Kleckner and colleagues identified a distributed system involving insular, anterior cingulate, and prefrontal regions, linking its connectivity to interoceptive ability (Kleckner et al., 2017). Connectivity studies further describe gradients related to allostatic and interoceptive functions across cortical and subcortical structures (Katsumi et al., 2022 (Katsumi et al., , 2023;; J. Zhang et al., 2025). These findings support the view that interoceptive predictive processing contributes broadly to emotion, decision-making, and cognitive control.

Because allostatic regulation acts on expectations about forthcoming bodily demands, contexts and acquired concepts can shape those expectations. TCoE extends this point to how the symbols entering such expectations are socially formed and revised.

2.2 Empirical Evidence for Interoceptive Predictive Coding

Direct evidence for interoceptive predictive coding remains limited, but converging findings support the account. Human studies show that anterior insular and anterior cingulate activity covaries with interoceptive attention and autonomic arousal, establishing these regions as key neural substrates of body-state representation (Craig, 2009; Critchley et al., 2004).

Animal studies provide complementary evidence about anticipated bodily conditions. In hungry or thirsty mice, cues associated with food or water shift insular activity toward patterns related to future satiety (Livneh et al., 2020; Namboodiri & Stuber, 2020). This links insular representations to anticipated as well as current physiological states and motivates examining how learned contextual cues enter bodily regulation. Immune regulation provides another example. Koren and colleagues identified insular neuronal ensembles active during colitis or peritonitis in mice; chemogenetic reactivation sub-sequently reinstated corresponding inflammatory responses (Koren et al., 2021). The authors interpreted this as immune memory. For an interoceptive predictive account, however, the important observation is that reactivating a neural representation can reproduce a peripheral bodily response, rather than merely recalling information. We interpret this as consistent with reinstated predictions recruiting bodily responses to reduce prediction error, even when the resulting inflammation is harmful (Koren et al., 2021; Rolls, 2023). This supports a predictive-processing interpretation in which reducing a mismatch need not benefit the body.

Recent glucose-regulation experiments provide further support for anticipatory control.

Litvak and colleagues report that insular activity shifts toward a future metabolic-state representation before meal onset. Suppressing anticipatory insular activity impaired anticipatory insulin release and subsequent metabolic homeostasis (Litvak et al., 2025). These findings support a feedforward contribution of insular predictions to bodily regulation, consistent with interoceptive predictive coding. Supplementary Section S3 retains procedural details and the extended empirical discussion.

2.3 Homeostatic Reinforcement Learning and Generated Emotion

Homeostatic reinforcement learning (HRL) grounds reward in the reduction of deviations from physiologically desirable states (Hulme et al., 2019; Keramati & Gutkin, 2014; Pezzulo et al., 2015). It therefore connects motivation and action selection to internal bodily needs rather than treating external rewards as the sole source of value. This makes HRL relevant to constructive accounts in which emotion depends on regulation of an embodied agent.

A related proposal links emotional valence to changes in prediction error or free energy (Joffily & Coricelli, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016). In this view, improving predictions and resolving uncertainty can contribute to positive affect, whereas worsening prediction can contribute to negative affect. These proposals concern the dynamics of inference, not a universal identification of any prediction-error decrease with physiological benefit. Body-budget and hi-erarchical regulation accounts likewise connect preferences and motivated behavior to anticipated bodily demands (Barrett, 2017a; Pezzulo et al., 2018).

Computational work makes aspects of this connection executable. Yoshida and colleagues model internal physiological variables and drives that shape reinforcement signals and policy learning, allowing agents to acquire behaviors that restore physiological balance (Yoshida et al., 2024 (Yoshida et al., , 2025)). Idei and collaborators connect homeostatic regulation with inference in hierarchical neurorobotic and Bayesian models, examining how bodily and environmental uncertainty influence action (Idei et al., 2025). These models provide mechanisms to investigate, rather than establishing a complete account of human emotion. A glucose-regulation example distinguishes the questions involved. Falling glucose can motivate anticipation of a future deficit and food-seeking. HRL asks how reducing a bodily deviation rewards learning such behavior; predictive accounts ask how evidence and expectations about bodily change shape inference and action; valence proposals add the temporal dynamics of prediction error. These objectives are connected but not interchangeable without model-specific assumptions, such as the cost and regularization assumptions under which Supplementary Section S2 compares HRL. For TCoE, HRL contributes a bodily basis for evaluating action; how that evaluation couples to socially negotiated symbols is a further modeling question.

2.4 Sharing of Interoceptive Predictive Coding in Social Interaction

Social interaction can influence emotional experience and bodily regulation. Parkinson's relationalignment account describes how emotions organize convergence, complementarity, or conflict between people's orientations to shared objects (Parkinson, 2021). TCoE asks how this relational process could be connected to private bodily evidence and communicable symbols, rather than assuming that interpersonal alignment requires identical internal states.

Social Baseline Theory treats proximity to others as a resource affecting estimates of risk and effort (Beckes & Coan, 2011; Coan & Sbarra, 2015). Reduced threat-related neural responses during spousal handholding illustrate how social relationships can modify responses to challenge (Coan et al., 2006). Interpersonal emotion regulation similarly includes support, attunement, and others' responses, beyond regulation performed in isolation (Zaki & Williams, 2013).

Communication studies associate speaker-listener neural coupling with shared understanding, while parent-child cooperative-task synchrony relates to interaction quality and emotion regulation (Hasson et al., 2012; Reindl et al., 2018; Stephens et al., 2010). Such observations motivate examining interaction, but synchrony alone does not identify a shared generative model or the acceptance computation proposed in TCoE. Active-inference accounts of communication offer a theoretical description of mutual model adjustment (K. J. Friston & Frith, 2015).

CPC extends this question to the symbols negotiated through interaction (Taniguchi, 2024a; Taniguchi et al., 2023). Work on predictive categorization motivates considering emotion words as category cues that organize heterogeneous bodily and situational information (Barrett & Miller, 2026). TCoE brings these directions together: bodily experience informs negotiation, and negotiated symbols can shape later interpretation and regulation. The findings reviewed here motivate that reciprocal hypothesis without independently demonstrating the complete cycle.

Labels such as anxiety or anger organize bodily and situational interpretations for interaction. Shared usage permits different experiences, complementarity, and conflict, motivating an account of how public labels remain grounded in private evidence.

3 Embodied Constructive Models of Emotion and Their Limits

3.1 Constructive Approaches to Emotion in Robotics

Constructive approaches complement human observation by building systems to test how emotion-like states and concepts emerge through bodily regulation, action, interaction, and interpretation.

Early affective robotics often recognized emotion from facial, vocal, or physiological features (Breazeal, 2003; Picard, 1997). Such systems supported human-robot interaction, but typically treated emotional categories as given. Cognitive developmental robotics instead asks how concepts can self-organize through an agent's sensorimotor and social experience (Asada et al., 2001 (Asada et al., , 2009;; Pfeifer & Bongard, 2006).

Horii and colleagues demonstrated bottom-up emotion-concept formation through a Multimodal Deep Belief Network (MDBN), with interpersonal tactile stimuli serving as the dominant modality (Horii et al., 2018). The model learned representations organizing multimodal experience without predefined emotion labels. Its largely passive treatment of supplied stimuli left open how those concepts acquire meaning through the agent's own action and social relationships.

The Deep Emotion model incorporates action generation into learning (Hieida et al., 2018). Its three-layer architecture combines innate reactions, experience-based learning, and predictive control to support abstract interoception and homeostatic regulation. Emotion is thereby approached as an internally regulated process rather than an externally imposed label. This connects constructive modeling to predictive body-budget management and embodied homeostasis (Barrett, 2017a; Man & Damasio, 2019; Sterling, 2012; Theriault et al., 2025).

Work by Kuniyoshi and colleagues further integrates physiological models, including musculoskeletal, visceral, and respiratory systems, with neural development. These models examine how respiration, emotion, and cognition become coupled through neuromodulatory mechanisms (Cueto et al., 2019; Yonekura et al., 2022). They show how bodily morphology can constrain and scaffold affective development, extending embodiment beyond neural information processing alone.

These models connect category formation to the bodily conditions that give actions value, and allow HRL to be studied in bodies whose morphology constrains regulation. Their constructive contribution is not only to reproduce emotional labels, but to examine how bodily organization supports affective states.

3.2 Active-Inference Models of Embodied Emotion

Predictive coding and active inference (AIF) provide a computational foundation for embodied emotion models. Smith and colleagues model emotion-concept learning through latent states ex-plaining interoceptive, exteroceptive, and proprioceptive observations alongside actions (Smith et al., 2019). Inconsistent caregiver labels for similar bodily states (e.g., anger, anxiety, or sadness) can impair these associations, represented as reduced precision between emotion-related latent states and linguistic labels (Smith et al., 2019). This links bodily experience, learned categories, and developmental input to the clinical account in Section 5.

Idei and colleagues' Multimodal Bayesian Homeostatic Recurrent Neural Network (MBH-RNN) implements anticipatory regulation, minimizing predicted future sensory entropy associated with deviations from homeostasis (Idei et al., 2025). Its predictive-coding-inspired variational recurrent neural network hierarchy (Ahmadi & Tani, 2019) separates sensory-mean and uncertainty predictions across perceptual, multimodal-associative, unexpected-uncertaintycause, and higher-cognitive modules, connecting sensorimotor and allostatic processing to insular and prefrontal-cingulate functional organization (Craig, 2009; Critchley et al., 2004; Kleckner et al., 2017). Past variational free energy (VFE P ) combines negative accuracy and complexity over observed sensations; future variational free energy (VFE F ) combines predicted conditional sensory entropy and predicted complexity over a future window (Idei et al., 2025). Their distinction concerns temporal windows, not a one-to-one correspondence with accuracy and complexity.

After random-exploration learning in a dynamic foraging environment, the agent autonomously alternated goal-directed feeding and sensory-focused rest. Increased VFE F strengthened intention-related beliefs; successful regulation allowed a return to recognition. It also increased food intake before anticipated scarcity and outlived a reactive setpoint-based control, illustrating predictive allostasis and connecting to hierarchical AIF accounts of motivated behavior (Pezzulo et al., 2015 (Pezzulo et al., , 2018)).

TCoE extends individual anticipatory regulation through a reciprocal body-symbol cycle:

bodily experience grounds shared emotion-word negotiation, while words shape bodily inference and regulation through the symbol-conditioned social allostatic prior (Section 4). This functional connection does not identify VFE F with complexity. Supplementary Section S4 retains extended architectural descriptions and model comparisons.

3.3 The Need for Co-constructed Emotion

These advances make the origin of social priors a central question. In the models reviewed here, caregiver labels and other environmental structures help determine what learned concepts mean.

Atzil and colleagues emphasize that caregiver-infant interaction shapes the developing allostatic and social brain: caregivers provide scaffolding through which bodily regulation and emotioncategory formation develop together (Atzil et al., 2018). The social prior is therefore not simply an input arriving from outside an otherwise isolated individual.

A model that treats caregiver input as fixed cannot explain how the relationship itself generates and revises the available concepts, or how those concepts change through interaction across a community. TCoE addresses this gap by treating public symbols as variables negotiated among agents with their own interoceptive models. The next section connects this collective symbol formation to individual bodily prediction and regulation.

4.2 Theoretical Foundation 1: Active Inference and Interoception

Interoceptive inference addresses a problem of partial access. An agent receives signals arising from bodily processes but must infer the states that produced them (Craig, 2002; Seth, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016).

The original state-space model specifies bodily dynamics, a sensory observation process, and their uncertainties. Its continuous-time and discrete-time forms are given in Supplementary Section S1.3. These equations separate the process generating bodily signals from the model used to interpret them. For the individual model, t indexes discrete bodily time steps and the agent superscript k is omitted. The latent internal state z t and interoceptive observation x int t are the time-indexed counterparts of the variables above. Here, w t is the emotion word socially supplied at step t: the bodily process is modeled conditional on that input, rather than generating it internally. Its social generation is addressed by CPC below. The word conditions the prior p(z t | w t ), and the approximate posterior q(z t | x int t , w t ) represents the agent's current state estimate. We use p for generative-model distributions, including priors and likelihoods, and q here for an approximate posterior; in the original formal specification, q also denotes policy and proposal distributions where explicitly indicated. The word therefore supplies probabilistic contextual information, not a universal physiological signature (Keramati & Gutkin, 2014; Ohira, 2020b; Smith et al., 2019).

Variational free energy F t evaluates this current estimate through sensory fit and divergence from the prior (K. Friston, 2010; Parr et al., 2022).

The accuracy term concerns how well an inferred state explains the observation. The regularization term concerns the relation between the estimate and the symbol-conditioned expectation. Updating the estimate changes what the agent believes is happening. Additional assumptions are needed to explain how a change in that belief produces an action or a bodily transition. The original model uses a Gaussian approximation to describe uncertainty in state transitions and observations. These uncertainties have different roles: bodily dynamics can vary even when observations are reliable, and sensory evidence can be uncertain even when the underlying state changes little. The emotion word conditions expectations within this model; it does not itself supply a Gaussian physiological state. Retaining these distinctions is important when relating a model parameter to an empirical measure of interoception.

Allostasis concerns anticipated bodily needs, so inference about the present must be connected to evaluation of possible futures (Pezzulo et al., 2015 (Pezzulo et al., , 2018;; Sterling, 2012). In active inference (AIF), a candidate policy π can be evaluated using expected free energy G(π), with an inverse-temperature parameter controlling the selectivity of policy choice. The original policy expression and its assumptions are retained in Supplementary Section S1.3. Supplementary Section S2 contains the separate HRL comparison.

This distinction between present inference and prospective action is important for TCoE.

A word may change an interpretation before any measurable physiological change occurs. To influence regulation, the interpretation must enter predictions or policy evaluation and be connected to actions that affect the body or its environment. Accurate sensory prediction alone does not specify which outcomes maintain bodily viability. The proposed connection therefore requires both an account of action-dependent consequences and an account of regulatory preferences. It also permits bodily evidence to challenge an available interpretation, rather than requiring the body to conform to a word.

4.3 Theoretical Foundation 2: Collective Predictive Coding (CPC)

CPC extends the individual inference problem by treating the shared symbol as something participants can negotiate (Taniguchi, 2024a (Taniguchi, , 2024b)). In the collective model, time indices are suppressed: w, z k , and x k abbreviate the symbol, state, and observation trajectories over the modeled interval, as specified in Supplementary Section S1.4. Each observation includes both modalities, x k = (x int,k , x ext,k ). There is one common latent symbol variable w, alongside the private internal states and observations of agents indexed by k. Each agent contributes through its own likelihood and symbol-conditioned state distribution. This common latent variable is a collectivemodel description, not information an individual can inspect directly.

Supplementary Section S1.4 specifies a structured variational distribution that retains dependence between the symbol and individual states. Its free energy separates into collective regularization, individual prediction error, and individual regularization through joint expected log ratios, rather than a sum of independent agentwise KL divergences. They allow shared meaning to be represented alongside different internal models. A stored word in a language-game algorithm is a current sample of the shared variable, whereas the distributions linking words to experience constitute the agent's learned model. The distinction matters because changing a current sample and changing the meaning associated with that sample are different operations.

The Metropolis-Hastings Naming Game (MHNG) offers one computational realization of locally grounded negotiation (Hoang et al., 2024; Taniguchi et al., 2023). In a two-agent exchange, a speaker proposes a word using its own information, and a listener evaluates it using its own evidence. When the proposal and target distributions are matched as described below, a word with greater listener-side support is accepted, while a less well-supported word can still be accepted probabilistically. This permits exploration rather than requiring every exchange to improve the current interpretation.

Symbol negotiation can be modeled at either of two levels. In a state-level approach, both the speaker's proposal and the listener's evaluation are based on inferred internal states z k . In an observation-level approach, both are based on observations x k , with latent states marginalized out. These observations include interoception and accessible external context. The approaches retain different information during a symbol update; the observation-level rule is not obtained by changing only the listener's evaluation while leaving an arbitrary state-level proposal unchanged.

Neither approach is asserted to be an exact account of human processing.

4.4 Formalizing the Co-construction of Emotion as Collective Active Inference

The symbol-conditioned prior p(z k | w) provides a possible connection from shared interpretation to individual processing. We call it a social allostatic prior, relating the computational account to work on socially organized bodily regulation (Saxbe et al., 2020; Schulkin, 2011). Its immediate meaning is an expectation about internal states. Its regulatory significance depends on how that expectation enters action selection and physical bodily dynamics.

The proposed cycle has three stages. First, individuals infer their bodily situation from private sensations and accessible context. Second, they propose and evaluate symbols using those interpretations or the associated sensory evidence. Third, an adopted expression informs subsequent expectations and potentially regulatory actions. The consequences of those actions provide new evidence that can support or undermine the expression in later exchanges. Thus, the public symbol links an upward contribution from individual experience with a downward contribution to individual prediction.

The stages can operate at different timescales. 1 Bodily regulation continues between communicative events. A conversation may change the currently selected word, while repeated experience changes its learned associations. Stability of a label need not imply identical or stable physiology, and a stable vocabulary need not prevent changes in how a word is understood.

The model can also represent asymmetric relationships, such as caregiver-infant interactions, in which participants have different information and different regulatory roles.

4.5 Constructive Illustrations of Emotion Symbol Emergence

5.1 Alexithymia as a Disconnection Between Interoception and Linguistic Concepts

Alexithymia is a common personality trait characterized by difficulty expressing one's emotions in words. Here, we reinterpret alexithymia not merely as altered emotional awareness, but as a disruption of the co-constructive process through which bodily states and social symbols are dynamically coupled to generate emotion.

In TCoE, language and emotion words are not mere labels but socially shared symbols that integrate heterogeneous interoceptive signals, exteroceptive context, and subjective evaluations into meaningful emotion concepts. Such symbols help segment bodily fluctuations into patterns that guide judgment and regulation and align predictions within a linguistic community.

Alexithymia is clinically relevant precisely because this coupling between bodily inference and social symbols is thought to break down.

Recall from Section 4.4 that the latent internal state z represents bodily conditions, accessed only indirectly through interoceptive observations x int (e.g., cardiac sensations, visceral feelings), while q(z | x int ) denotes the posterior estimate of that state and shared emotion concepts w guide its interpretation.

In typical emotional experience, the posterior belief q(z | x int ) is stably formed from interoceptive observations, and the social symbol w provides a frame for interpreting it: words such as "anxiety" or "anger" predict bodily patterns and guide how those states are understood and regulated.

In alexithymia, however, low interoceptive precision can leave the posterior belief q(z | x int ) weakly constrained by bodily evidence: bodily states are experienced in a vague and undifferentiated manner, making it difficult to interpret them by reference to the social symbol w. As a result, the social symbol fails to ground emotion word acceptance or revision, and the coordination between bodily interpretation and social meaning-making breaks down. This coupling failure propagates upward. When q(z | x int ) is unreliable, top-down social allostatic constraints cannot effectively regulate bodily states, and bodily signals that remain uncategorized persist as poorly resolved inputs, impeding free-energy minimization. The resulting chronic mismatch between bodily states and social expectations may give rise to the psychosomatic symptoms frequently reported in alexithymia.

Empirical evidence supports this account of failed coupling at the level of bodily representations. The disconnection between interoceptive signals and social symbols becomes most pronounced at the stage of mapping bodily states onto emotion concepts. Using body maps, Lloyd et al. (2021) demonstrated that individuals with high alexithymia show reduced differentiation of bodily representations across emotions.

This finding suggests a failure of Bayesian learning processes that associate specific physiological patterns with specific emotion categories-what has been termed affective abstraction (Fiedler et al., 2025; Wotschack & Klann-Delius, 2013). Local bodily sensations remain unabstracted and fail to acquire conceptual labels tied to shared emotion symbols.

These observations can be formalized within our framework by focusing first on social negotiation: whether a listener can accept or reject emotion words proposed by others on the basis of bodily evidence. The marginal likelihood of interoceptive observations given an emotion word integrates out the latent state (Supplementary Section S5.1). If interoceptive processing is inaccurate, this can be modeled as high variance in the likelihood. When the likelihood varies little over the states supported by the compared word-conditioned priors, the resulting marginal likelihoods become similar across words. This has a direct consequence for emotion word negotiation. In the observation-level MHNG formulation, with the matched proposal and target specified in Supplementary Section S1, acceptance depends on the listener's marginal-likelihood ratio. If that ratio approaches unity, acceptance also approaches unity, and bodily evidence contributes little to discrimination among the compared words (Supplementary Section S5.2). This predicts reduced bodily grounding of negotiation under those assumptions, rather than indiscriminate acceptance as a general consequence of low interoceptive precision. The latent-state formulation remains an alternative, with acceptance evaluated at that level as described in Section 4.3.

5.2 Imprecise Interoception and Instability of Interoceptive Beliefs

This subsection examines the complementary failure in the recognition direction-inferring an appropriate emotion word from one's own bodily evidence-and reviews empirical support for the underlying interoceptive imprecision. If emotion words and interoceptive sensations are interconnected in this manner, dysfunctions in interoceptive processing or in the formation of emotion concepts are likely to produce discrepancies between predicted and actual internal states. Such discrepancies have been reported across a range of psychiatric conditions (Khalsa et al., 2018) and can be conceptualized here as a failure to connect with the collective predictive processes underlying emotional co-construction.

Alexithymia provides a useful construct for examining this process. Characterized by difficulty identifying feelings (DIF), difficulty describing feelings (DDF), and externally-oriented thinking (EOT), alexithymia reflects a breakdown in the interaction between emotion, language, and interoception.

Recent studies suggest that alexithymia is associated with imprecision in interoceptive input itself (Anonymous, 2026). Using the task developed by Legrand et al. (2022), recent work has demonstrated that individuals with high alexithymia exhibit greater uncertainty (lower precision) in interoceptive judgment. Specifically, they showed a wider tolerance range when judging whether an externally presented heartbeat tempo matched their own, consistent with uncertain internal state estimation. At present, this behavioral tolerance should be interpreted as an empirical proxy for interoceptive uncertainty rather than a calibrated estimate of the model parameter Σ int.

Such imprecision is not limited to emotional domains but extends to interoceptive sensations more broadly, including hunger and heartbeat perception (Brewer et al., 2016). As a result, discordance arises between physiological arousal and subjective reports (Peasley-Miklus et al., 2016), undermining the formation of stable interoceptive beliefs. Conversely, the "language hypothesis of alexithymia" proposed by Lee et al. (2022) and Hobson et al. (2019) emphasizes that language-as a system of social symbols essential for translating interoceptive input into emotion-fails to function adequately in alexithymia. Emotional granularity, as discussed by Hoemann et al. (2021, 2023), likewise depends on the acquisition of rich linguistic categories.

Together, these findings suggest that alexithymia should not be framed as a simple dichotomy between impaired interoception and deficient language. Rather, it reflects a vicious cycle in which imprecise interoceptive input and impoverished symbolic abstraction mutually reinforce one another, resulting in a breakdown of the co-constructive coupling between interoceptive inference and social symbolic systems that gives rise to emotion in TCoE.

It is instructive to contrast this account with an alternative model. Smith et al. (2019) explain insufficient emotion-word learning through ambiguous mappings between bodily or situational cues and caregiver labels, with similar sensations and behaviors labeled as anger, anxiety, or sadness at different times. Their emphasis is on variability in linguistic labeling. In contrast, we emphasize reduced reliability at an earlier stage: interoceptive processing and the construction of emotion-related meanings from bodily signals. This distinction is empirically testable: manipulating interoceptive precision should change emotion-word acceptance even with label input held constant, whereas the label-ambiguity account predicts stronger sensitivity to linguisticfeedback consistency.

The flattening of the marginal likelihood described in Section 5.1 also affects recognition: inferring an emotion word from one's own bodily evidence. By Bayes' rule, the posterior over words depends on their marginal likelihoods and prior probabilities (Supplementary Section S5.3). When the likelihood is word-invariant, the posterior reduces to the prior. Bodily evidence then no longer distinguishes among words, but their probabilities need not be equal unless the prior is uniform. This corresponds to the clinical observation that people with alexithymia can retain emotion vocabulary yet struggle to use it to label their own bodily states (Hobson et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2022). Thus, impaired bodily grounding affects both acceptance during social negotiation and recognition in self-labeling. This computational account also suggests a principled basis for therapeutic interventions.

Strategies that target interoceptive precision (bottom-up, e.g., heartbeat discrimination training, Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA)-based body-state calibration) and strategies that target emotion vocabulary restructuring (top-down, e.g., guided re-labeling, context-rich emotional socialization) can both be understood as restoring the coupling between bodily evidence and emotion words in the acceptance and recognition directions formalized above. The framework predicts that integrated interventions addressing both sides simultaneously should be more effective than either alone.

At the same time, alexithymia is heterogeneous, and interoceptive precision alone cannot account for all clinical manifestations. Attention, working memory, developmental history, interpersonal experience, and culture can modulate bodily inference and emotion-word use. Quantitative links between model precision and behavioral measures, such as heartbeat-matching tolerance and physiological-subjective discordance, remain to be established. TCoE therefore provides a promising computational re-description rather than a complete clinical account. First, following the acceptance dynamics formalized in Section 5.1, TCoE predicts an asymmetry in lexical dominance within dyads that differ in interoceptive precision. When a highprecision individual and a low-precision individual interact repeatedly, the shared emotion vocabulary should converge toward the high-precision individual's word usage. The proposed mechanism is reduced bodily selectivity on one side and stronger bodily grounding on the other. In the matched observation-level MHNG formulation, approximately word-invariant listener likelihoods make acceptance approach unity (Supplementary Section S5.2), whereas informative likelihoods allow more selective evaluation. This asymmetric convergence is a prediction that no purely individual-level theory can generate. It also connects to development: mothers' understanding of emotion-interoception links is associated with children's emotion regulation (MacCormack et al., 2020), and emotion-concept acquisition requires categorizing bodily and contextual experiences (Hoemann et al., 2020). TCoE frames this acquisition as MHNG-like interaction coupling shared symbols with bodily inference.

Second, as testable predictions about repeated interaction, TCoE proposes that the rate of convergence of emotion vocabularies should depend on the interoceptive precision of both participants: high-precision dyads converge rapidly to body-grounded labels; low-precision dyads converge but to prior-driven labels with lower cross-dyad consistency; mixed dyads show intermediate speed with strong asymmetry in who drives the final vocabulary. These predictions can be tested in controlled experiments where pairs interact over emotion-labeling tasks, with interoceptive precision measured independently (e.g., heartbeat discrimination accuracy).

Third, TCoE makes falsifiable predictions about what counts as disconfirming evidence.

Symmetric convergence regardless of interoceptive precision, or convergence explained by social dominance or linguistic fluency rather than bodily-state coupling, would require revision of the current formulation. The strongest test is whether precision predicts asymmetric convergence after controlling for dominance, verbal ability, and prior label familiarity. Similarly, a lack of greater bodily selectivity in high-precision individuals would challenge the proposed marginallikelihood mechanism. These boundary conditions distinguish TCoE from competing accounts.

In typical development, TCoE reframes emotion-word acquisition as a co-constructive process in which children and caregivers iteratively align bodily experience and shared symbols, rather than passively internalizing labels. Caregivers and children co-create learning moments within networks of similarly valenced emotion words (Nencheva et al., 2023). TCoE proposes that this learning depends on the interactive alignment of shared symbols with bodily inference. This motivates longitudinal studies of how interaction histories shape concept boundaries, and early-life interventions combining interoceptive discrimination training with emotion-word scaffolding.

6.3 Constructive and Robotic Research

Constructive and robotic experiments provide the most direct test of the central claim of TCoE: that emotion concepts are not merely individually inferred but socially co-constructed through repeated interaction among embodied agents. Three predictions follow. First, when multiple agents equipped with interoceptive generative models engage in MHNG-style symbol negotiation, a shared emotion vocabulary should self-organize without pre-specifying fixed categories.

Second, under the bodily-grounding conditions discussed in Section 5.1, selectively reducing interoceptive precision should degrade both the differentiation of emotion words and their bodily grounding, providing a robotic analogue of the acceptance failure formalized in Section 5.1 (Eq. S5.2). Third, varying interaction topology or partner assignment should systematically shift the boundaries of emergent emotion categories, demonstrating that category structure is a function of collective dynamics rather than individual embodiment alone.

Constructive validation cannot rest on simulation alone. Ultimately, it is necessary to test, using real parent-child interaction data, how social interaction connects children's interoceptive states, behavioral expressions, and the formation of emotion words. Feldman's research has shown that parent-infant synchrony and children's emotion regulation mutually predict each other across development (2012)-a bidirectional, temporally structured dynamic that is precisely what TCoE's allostatic and social loops predict. Datasets of parent-child physiological synchrony (Bizzego et al., 2022; Capraz et al., 2023; Tamis-LeMonda, 2023) and reports of partner-specific differences in synchrony between mother-child and father-child dyads (Puglisi et al., 2023) offer rich targets for validating how social allostatic priors and dyadic coordination are established in actual developmental processes.

The deepest frontier lies at the intersection of these two directions: building robots with genuine homeostatic needs-systems whose continued operation depends on managing a real body budget-would introduce the physical constraints of embodied interoception into the social dynamics of collective symbol emergence via CPC. Under such dual constraints, where neither the body nor the social relationship is simulated, one can ask whether something struc-turally analogous to human emotion-concept formation emerges. If it does, this would constitute the strongest constructive evidence for Barrett's claim that the brain evolved primarily as a body-budget manager, and for TCoE's claim that this management is irreducibly social. The intersection of bodily and social constraint is, in this sense, where the constructive study of emotion becomes the constructive study of mind.

A related future direction is to extend HRL beyond individual homeostatic regulation toward collective body-budget management in social interaction. Such an extension would require augmenting individual homeostatic cost with social-allostatic and collective-symbolic terms, so that policies are evaluated not only by their effects on one's own bodily prediction errors but also by their contribution to alignment with shared emotion symbols and collective coordination. We leave this formal development for future work.

7 Conclusion

The central contribution of this article is to show that emotion is neither confined to the individual body nor imposed on the individual by society, but co-constructed in the cycle between interoceptive prediction and social symbol sharing. Grounded in CPC, TCoE connects the individual loop of interoceptive inference and predictive adjustment with the social loop of sharing and updating emotion words and concepts under a single principle of free energy minimization, bridging psychological and social constructivism within one formal framework. The key role of CPC is to treat the shared symbol w not as a fixed external condition but as a latent variable updated through collective interaction, thereby representing both how society shapes individuals and how their interactions transform shared emotional concepts.

In sum, TCoE extends the computational understanding of emotion from something made inside the body to something co-constructed between body and society. CPC formalizes this process through the dynamics of prediction and symbol generation, with implications for alexithymia, emotion formation and vocabulary development, and social simulation using artificial agents. The essence of emotion lies not in private experience enclosed within the individual, nor in fixed categories given by society, but in a co-constructive process that is continually rewoven in the reciprocal movement between bodily prediction and social symbols. This proposal is the first to describe this process systematically as a computational theory.

Several important scope conditions of TCoE should be noted. As a computational-level theory, TCoE specifies what is computed during emotion co-construction. Its neural implementation remains an open question: which brain regions support interoceptive precision weighting, emotion-word marginal likelihood computation, or the MHNG acceptance decision? The neuroscientific research program outlined above addresses this question. The current formulation also treats model parameters as stationary within each interaction episode, abstracts away from attention, working memory, and executive control, and does not yet accommodate social emotions such as shame, guilt, and jealousy, which presuppose recursive social comparison beyond the single shared symbol w. This single-symbol assumption is a simplifying entry point rather than a principled limit of CPC: the same framework can in principle be extended to hierarchical or simultaneous systems of shared symbols. S1.6 Constructive Illustrations of Emotion Symbol Emergence

This subsection expands the constructive illustrations of TCoE in the main article, describing routes toward implementation in multi-agent models. Within this formulation, CPC and MHNG provide mechanisms for coupling shared emotion symbols with individual interoceptive generative models: agents negotiate emotion words while their internal models are shaped by, and in turn constrain, the shared symbolic space (Inukai et al., 2023; Taniguchi, 2024a). The examples below should therefore be read not as direct implementations of Expected Collective Free Energy minimization, nor as definitive evidence for TCoE, but as preliminary testbeds for expressing parts of the co-constructive cycle of Section S1.5.3 in runnable systems.

The first line of work addresses emotion category alignment under embodied subjectivity.

Z. Zhang et al. (2025) employed the Inter-GMM+MVAE framework, a multimodal generativemodel architecture coupled through MHNG (Hoang et al., 2024) to model how two agents form and align emotion categories from their own multimodal and interoceptive generative models.

Each agent inferred its own latent representation and proposed or accepted emotion category signs on the basis of its own internal state, without direct access to the partner's latent space.

To probe whether communication could bridge bodily differences, the model introduced agents with divergent interoceptive dynamics. The important point for TCoE is not the particular sensory implementation, but the role of selective acceptance and rejection: indiscriminate acceptance of proposed signs is insufficient, whereas selective negotiation can form a shared symbolic scaffold over non-identical bodily representations, allowing category alignment without requiring agents to converge on identical internal states. Tsubamoto and Horii (2026) shift the focus from category alignment to homeostatic co-regulation. Their mother-infant model represents an asymmetric POMDP setting in which the parent agent has a regulatory model for maintaining the infant's body budget but must infer the infant's state from external cues, whereas the infant has direct interoceptive access to its own visceral state but must learn which coordinated actions maintain homeostasis. MHNG-style communication lets the two agents negotiate shared symbols on the basis of their own state estimates, and shared representation can emerge before the agents' generative model parameters fully converge. From the perspective of TCoE, this illustrates how a social symbolic loop may become coupled to an individual allostatic loop: communication does not merely transmit labels, but can help coordinate physiological regulation across agents with different access to bodily information.

Building on this homeostatic co-regulation setting, Nomura et al. (2026) address how the meaning of shared symbols may itself emerge and change through interaction. In the Tsubamoto model, the mapping from symbols to regulatory actions is fixed, so the content of symbols is largely predetermined. Nomura et al. relax this assumption by allowing both regulatory preferences and symbol-to-action mappings to be updated online. The resulting interaction produces a compromise in bodily norms and a change in how symbols are interpreted as regulation proceeds.

This provides a constructive approximation to a further TCoE claim: emotion-like symbols are not fixed labels attached to bodily states, but renegotiated meanings whose interpretants can coevolve with agents' allostatic histories.

Taken together, these studies do not yet constitute a full realization of the TCoE objective. Instead, they clarify three constructive tasks implied by the framework: aligning emotion categories across non-identical bodies, coupling symbolic communication to social allostatic regulation, and allowing symbol meaning itself to evolve through interaction. Because these studies remain preliminary and largely pre-publication, they should be treated as engineering testbeds rather than conclusive demonstrations.

S3 Extended Empirical Foundations

This section expands the empirical and theoretical background reviewed in Section 2 of the main article, with additional detail on interoceptive predictive coding, homeostatic reinforcement learning, and social interaction.

S3.1 Predictive Coding of Interoception

This subsection expands the predictive-coding background summarized in Section 2.1 of the main article. The theory of predictive coding suggests that the brain does not passively register sensory inputs but actively interprets them through hierarchical generative models, continuously minimizing prediction errors (K. Friston, 2010). While initially developed to account for exteroceptive modalities such as vision and audition, predictive coding has now been extended to interoception-the perception of internal bodily states-offering crucial insights into the emergence of emotion and bodily regulation (Craig, 2002 (Craig, , 2009;; Seth, 2013). This formulation resonates with a recent account (Ohira, 2023) of predictive processing as a unifying framework that integrates perception, action, interoception, affect, and the emergence of subjective mental experience. Seth (2013) introduced the concept of interoceptive inference, proposing that the brain predicts internal bodily conditions to regulate autonomic, endocrine, and immune systems. In this view, bodily regulation is fundamentally predictive rather than reactive, constituting allostasis rather than simple homeostasis (Sterling, 2012). Barrett (2017a) developed the notion of the body budget, arguing that emotions arise as the brain's predictive regulation of physiological resources.

From this psychological constructionist perspective, emotions are not fixed biological modules but context-dependent inferences grounded in prior experience (Barrett, 2017a; Seth & Friston, 2016). Theriault et al. (2025) further proposed an "allostasis-first" view in which mental phenomena such as emotion and thought are downstream consequences of the brain's primary function: coordinating physiological resource distribution. fied an allostatic-interoceptive network centered on the insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and prefrontal regions, suggesting that interoceptive predictive coding is fundamental not only to emotion but also to decision-making and cognitive control. Consistent with this notion, recent findings from analyses of large-scale functional connectivity have indicated that the cortex, cerebellum, hippocampus, and subcortical structures are extensively organized around gradients relevant to allostatic and interoceptive functions (Katsumi et al., 2022 (Katsumi et al., , 2023;; J. Zhang et al., 2025).

S3.2 Empirical Evidence for Interoceptive Predictive Coding

The studies described below provide additional empirical context for the account of interoceptive predictive coding reviewed in the main article. This account is largely hypothetical and still has limited direct empirical evidence; however, several converging findings now support it. Foundational human studies showed that activity in the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex covaries with interoceptive attention and autonomic arousal, establishing these regions as key neural substrates of body-state representation (Craig, 2009; Critchley et al., 2004). In mice, neurons in the insula, which is a core region of interoception, usually show activity patterns corresponding to current physiological states such as hunger or thirst. However, during hunger or thirst states, visual cues previously associated with food or water shift insular neuron activity to a future satietyrelated pattern (Livneh et al., 2020). This finding suggests that insula activity patterns represent both current states of the internal body and predictions of future bodily states. Such predictions help drive behaviors to acquire physiological rewards, such as food or water (Livneh et al., 2020; Namboodiri & Stuber, 2020).

The insula has also been associated with the prediction of interoceptive signals related to inflammation, which is important for immune function. Koren et al. (2021) showed that when two drugs were used to generate inflammation in the colon and peritoneum of mice, groups of neurons in the insula were activated specifically for each type of inflammation. These neuronal groups were activated after the inflammation was resolved, using designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADD). This technique uses viral vectors to express artificial receptors at specific locations in the brain, and then administers agonists that bind to these artificial receptors to enhance or inhibit neuronal excitation. Surprisingly, activation of insular neurons by this manipulation produced specific inflammation in the colon and peritoneum (Koren et al., 2021). The authors interpreted this phenomenon as a memory of inflammation. However, if it were just a memory, there would be no need to produce toxic inflammation in the body. These insular neurons may encode predictions of inflammation in peripheral body sites with high spatial resolution. The activation of these neurons by DREADD is consistent with a prediction-error account: artificially reactivating inflammation-related predictions could trigger bodily activation in the form of inflammation to reduce the prediction error. Despite this phenomenon being harmful to the body, the brain still prioritizes the reduction of prediction error. Hence, this finding supports interoceptive predictive processing as a powerful principle of the brain (Koren et al., 2021; Rolls, 2023).

Litvak and colleagues report a transient shift of insular population activity toward a refedlike state before and at meal onset. Chemogenetic inhibition attenuated anticipatory insulin release, while temporally restricted optogenetic inhibition during the anticipatory phase also impaired subsequent metabolic regulation (Litvak et al., 2025). These findings support feedforward physiological control based on insular predictions.

S3.3 Homeostatic Reinforcement Learning and Generated Emotion

Homeostatic Reinforcement Learning (HRL) is a normative theory that grounds the source of motivation, namely reward signals, in the internal dynamics of the body by defining rewards as reductions in deviations from physiologically desirable states (set points) (Hulme et al., 2019; Keramati & Gutkin, 2014; Pezzulo et al., 2015). It specifically addresses reinforcement learning problems in which the maintenance of homeostasis is the central regulatory objective, grounding motivational signals in reductions of physiological deviations rather than identifying them with sensory prediction error. Relatedly, Seth and Friston (2016) argued that emotion can be understood as the temporal change in free energy, or prediction error. They stated that pleasant emotions arise when the model updates in a direction that reduces prediction error, while unpleasant emotions occur when uncertainty increases. This idea is developed in Joffily and Coricelli's (2013) mathematical model, which hypothesizes that emotional valence corresponds to the sign of prediction-error dynamics. For example, considering fluctuations in blood glucose levels-a crucial interoceptive sensory event-the brain predicts the onset of hypoglycemia when glucose is decreasing. The discrepancy between this prediction and the actual physical state is encoded as a prediction error. As the error increases, eating behavior is prompted. At this point, the subjective experience of hunger is not merely a reflection of physiological state but the subjective experience of a prediction-error signal indicating that the predictive model should be revised (Joffily & Coricelli, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016).

Predictive accounts complement the physiological-deviation objective of HRL by considering expectations about future bodily changes and the temporal dynamics of inference. These quantities are related but are not interchangeable definitions of reward. Joffily and Coricelli (2013) proposed a model in which emotional valence is defined by the negative rate of change of free energy, or of prediction error under simplifying assumptions: decreasing free energy corresponds to positive valence and increasing free energy to negative valence. Similarly, preferences are not fixed traits but can be understood as learning processes that optimize the body budget (Barrett, 2017a; Pezzulo et al., 2018). Metabolic state can influence decision-making and value judgments, with energy-rich foods-options excellent for short-term energy replenishment-being more likely to be chosen under stress or homeostatic load. This occurs because when the body budget is strained, the predictive model updates to highly value choices that rapidly replenish energy.

Together, HRL and active-inference accounts provide a framework for connecting emotion, value, and decision-making to bodily regulation. Their objectives can be related under explicit modeling assumptions, but reducing sensory prediction error does not by itself guarantee physiological benefit. From this perspective, the ability to more accurately predict the body's in-ternal states and minimize bodily prediction errors can drive motivated behavior (Hulme et al., 2019; Keramati & Gutkin, 2014; Pezzulo et al., 2018). This perspective transcends traditional models centered on external rewards and provides a crucial framework for clarifying the computational basis of emotions.

Recent work has begun to implement these theoretical ideas in neurally grounded computational models of homeostatic regulation. Yoshida and colleagues developed a computational framework of homeostatically regulated reinforcement learning in which internal physiological variables are explicitly represented as latent states that shape reward prediction and policy learning. In their formulation, deviations from physiological set points generate internal drives that modulate reinforcement signals, enabling agents to learn behaviors that restore physiological balance. Simulation studies demonstrated that such agents can acquire adaptive policies-such as food-seeking or energy-conserving behaviors-through learning processes that directly incorporate internal bodily states into the value function (Yoshida et al., 2024 (Yoshida et al., , 2025)). These results provide a mechanistic account of how homeostatic variables can serve as the internal basis of value and motivation within reinforcement learning architectures. Complementary work by Idei and collaborators further integrates HRL with predictive coding. Using neurorobotic and Bayesian network models based on hierarchical generative architectures, they showed that agents can simultaneously infer external environmental states and internal bodily states while minimizing prediction errors across these domains. In these models, homeostatic regulation emerges from the dynamic interaction between action selection and the inference of interoceptive signals. Importantly, their simulations demonstrated that exploratory behaviors, context-sensitive decisionmaking, and mode switching between goal-directed and sensory-focused behavior can arise spontaneously as agents attempt to maintain stable internal states under uncertain environmental conditions (Idei et al., 2025).

These accounts of HRL motivate a formal connection between homeostatic regulation and active inference. In Supplementary Section S2, we show that, under the specified homeostaticcost and regularization assumptions, HRL policy selection can be recovered as the minimization of the pragmatic, interoceptive-cost component of expected free energy. Thus, HRL provides a useful computational bridge from bodily regulation to the allostatic component of the TCoE formalism developed in Section 4 of the main article.

S3.4 Sharing of Interoceptive Predictive Coding in Social Interaction

Predictive coding can extend beyond individual physiological regulation to encompass the understanding of social interactions. Social interaction can co-construct emotional experience by reducing uncertainty distributed across multiple agents. That is, emotion can be understood not merely as the result of reading each individual's interoceptive signals, but as a shared prediction generated through the alignment of generative models and the stabilization of predictions within interactions between multiple agents (K. J. Friston & Frith, 2015; Taniguchi, 2024a). At the psychological level, this alignment corresponds to what Parkinson (2021) terms relation alignment: emotions function as interpersonal processes that produce convergence, complementarity, or conflict between people's orientations to shared objects.

Our framework recasts this relational description in generative-model terms, where alignment is realized as the mutual reduction of prediction error over shared symbols. Beckes and Coan's (2011) Social Baseline Theory, which emphasizes that the human nervous system is inherently designed for social proximity, supports this view. The presence of others alters estimates of risk and effort, functioning as a resource that ultimately reduces physiological load (Coan & Sbarra, 2015). A finding showing that holding a spouse's hand during threat reduces brain threat-related responses suggests that social relationships function to optimize bodily budgets (Coan et al., 2006). Others serve not only as external stimuli but also as inferential scaffolding that can be utilized to reduce one's own prediction errors. K. J. Friston and Frith (2015) formalized communication as a process in which generative models predicting others synchronize through interaction, aligning "singing from the same hymn sheet." Here, interaction is understood not merely as information transfer, but as a regulation process in which models constrain each other's updates while adjusting prediction accuracy. tion errors and uncertainty among multiple agents. The interpersonal emotion-regulation framework described by Zaki and Williams (2013) demonstrates that emotion is not solely completed through intrapersonal reappraisal or regulation. It is established through others' responses, including empathy, assurance, attunement, and support. Furthermore, from the perspective of brain-to-brain coupling, neural activity coupling between speaker and listener in communication is implicated in shared understanding (Hasson et al., 2012; Stephens et al., 2010), and brain-tobrain synchrony during parent-child cooperative tasks correlates with emotional regulation and interaction quality (Reindl et al., 2018).

The CPC hypothesis provides a useful framework for formalizing this view. CPC (Taniguchi, 2024a) is formulated from the perspective that internal representations formed by individuals through interaction with the environment and meanings shared through social symbolic processes converge as distributed Bayesian inference. Crucially, CPC posits that what is shared extends beyond mere information or propositions. Through interaction, parts of generative models themselves-specifically, ways of partitioning the world, attention, and precision allocation-align at the group level (Taniguchi, 2024a; Taniguchi et al., 2023). From this perspective, emotion can be understood as both the subjective interpretation of an individual's interoceptive prediction error and, concurrently, as a shared gradient of valence that emerges as multiple agents' models align through interaction, collectively reducing uncertainty. This argument also clarifies why language is central to collective emotion construction. Barrett and Miller (2026) argue that categorization is not a late stage added after perception, but a core predictive operation embedded throughout neural signal processing. If categories are constructed through predictive feedback, then linguistic symbols can function as socially shared category cues: words such as "anxiety," "anger," or "loneliness" compress heterogeneous bodily and situational signals into communicable equivalence classes and thereby guide attention, precision allocation, and action. In social interaction, language therefore does more than report pre-existing emotions; it helps align the categories through which interoceptive prediction errors are inter-preted and shared. From this viewpoint, the sharing of interoceptive predictive coding is also a sharing of linguistic-conceptual categories that stabilize what bodily changes mean for a group or dyad (Barrett & Miller, 2026; Taniguchi, 2024a).

S4.1 Constructive Approaches to Emotion in Robotics

The robotics studies reviewed below illustrate how constructive approaches provide a synthetic method for studying emotional dynamics that are too complex to be fully understood through observation and analysis alone. Because emotion unfolds through closed loops among bodily regulation, action, interaction, and symbolic interpretation, it is crucial to build embodied systems in which these loops can be instantiated and tested. Robotics offers such a platform: constructing agents that regulate, express, and acquire emotion-like states makes it possible to examine how emotional processes may emerge from bodily and social dynamics.

Historically, however, early research on affective robotics predominantly relied on pattern recognition models that extracted emotion categories from facial expressions, vocal features, or discrete physiological signals (Breazeal, 2003; Picard, 1997). Representative systems in this tradition treated emotion as a fixed mapping from external stimuli to categorical outputs-valuable for human-robot interaction in controlled settings, but fundamentally limited: the agent's internal bodily state played no generative role in emotional processing, and the question of how emotion categories themselves come to be was never posed.

Cognitive developmental robotics (Asada et al., 2001 (Asada et al., , 2009;; Pfeifer & Bongard, 2006) offers a different starting point. Rather than pre-defining emotional categories and training a classifier to detect them, it asks how a robot-through autonomous sensorimotor interaction with its environment and others-can self-organize the concept of emotion from the ground up. This approach has developed in parallel with the theoretical frameworks reviewed in Section 2 of the main article, and provides a means to test their computational claims in physically embodied sys-tems.

An important early milestone in this tradition is the work of Horii et al. (2018), who demonstrated that emotion concepts can emerge through bottom-up integration of multimodal sensory stimuli using a Multimodal Deep Belief Network (MDBN), with tactile stimuli from others serving as the dominant modality. This study marked a significant departure from stimulusresponse classifiers: rather than labeling pre-defined categories, the model learned latent representations that organized multimodal experience without explicit supervision. However, the approach remained largely passive-a sophisticated interpretation of given stimuli-and did not address what meaning the acquired concepts hold in relation to the environment, others, or society as a whole.

Addressing this issue of passivity, the "Deep Emotion" model (Hieida et al., 2018) takes a more active approach by incorporating the agent's own action generation into the learning loop.

The model adopts a three-layer architecture encompassing innate reactions, experience-based learning, and predictive control, enabling the robot to learn action policies and internal representations aimed at maintaining its own bodily state through abstract interoception and homeostatic regulation. This constitutes a significant step toward the allostatic perspective elaborated in Section 2.1 of the main article-specifically, the view that emotion arises not from passive registration of physiological state but from the brain's predictive management of its body budget (Barrett, 2017a; Man & Damasio, 2019; Sterling, 2012; Theriault et al., 2025). The model thereby shifts the locus of emotion from an externally imposed label to an internally regulated process, consistent with Barrett's TCE. This demand for deeper embodiment has further driven modeling beyond neural information processing to include circulatory and metabolic systems. Kuniyoshi and colleagues have developed computational models integrating detailed physiological models-including the musculoskeletal, visceral, and respiratory systems-with neural developmental processes, examining how respiration, emotion, and cognition are coupled through neuromodulatory mechanisms (Cueto et al., 2019; Yonekura et al., 2022). Such work grounds the notion of HRL (Sec-tion 2.3 of the main article) in biologically realistic morphology, demonstrating that the body's physical architecture itself constrains and scaffolds the emergence of affective states.

S1.5.2 Prospective Collective Regulation

We use G CPC as a provisional name for a prospective collective action objective, not as an established extension of the static variational free energy above. Such an objective would compare possible bodily and communicative actions through their predicted consequences for individual experience and symbol negotiation. The action-conditioned model motivates this connection, but does not by itself define a complete prospective action model.

In individual active inference, expected free energy links policy evaluation to predicted observations, information gain, and prior preferences (Parr et al., 2022; Pezzulo et al., 2015).

A corresponding collective account would need to specify how actions affect subsequent bodily states, observations, and communication, and which outcomes are preferred. The collective and individual regularization terms in the static VFE cannot simply be relabeled as epistemic or pragmatic values. In particular, matching a symbol-conditioned prior is not by itself information seeking, and sensory likelihood is not by itself a measure of physiological desirability.

S1.5.3 Synthesis: The Cycle of Co-construction

TCoE proposes a reciprocal coupling between bodily inference and social symbol negotiation.

Private bodily evidence contributes to evaluating a public expression; an adopted expression can then inform individual expectations and potentially regulatory action. The consequences of those actions provide evidence for later interpretation. These are proposed connections between processes, rather than consequences guaranteed by a three-term objective.

The social allostatic prior p(z k | w) represents expectations about an agent's internal state conditional on a shared symbol. Changing that expectation need not change physiology. Bodily effects depend on the actions and dynamics through which expectations enter regulation. Similarly, acceptance of a symbol in MHNG concerns its fit to the listener's evidence at the specified modeling level; it is not sufficient evidence that the symbol has a beneficial regulatory effect.

S4.3 The Need for Co-constructed Emotion Despite these advances, a fundamental limitation is shared across all state-of-the-art single-agent emotion models: the question of where the social prior distribution comes from in the first place remains unanswered. Even in the experiments by Smith et al., learning the observation matrix that maps latent states to observable cues relies heavily on top-down labeling by caregivers alongside environmental interaction. This dependency is not merely a modeling convenience: Atzil et al. (2018) demonstrate that caregiver-infant interaction shapes the developing allostatic and social brain at a structural level, with caregivers serving as the external allostatic scaffolding through which the infant's physiological regulation and emotion category formation are co-constituted.

A single-agent model, by treating caregiver input as a fixed environmental variable, cannot represent this constitutively social character of emotion development-the prior does not merely come "from outside" but is itself generated through a developmentally embedded allostatic relationship. The grounding of the acquired latent variables' meanings and the boundaries of their interpretation depend on fixed environmental structures or external others, failing to account for the long-term dynamics of how emotion concepts transform within society. In other words, the prior is treated as given-precisely the theoretical gap identified in Section 1 of the main article with respect to social constructivism. This limitation points to a developmental reality: emotion concepts do not arise in isolation but are shaped from the earliest stages of life through social interaction. The key computational question, therefore, is how a group of agents-each with its own interoceptive generative model-can come to share emotion concepts through interaction, rather than having those concepts imposed from outside. Section 4 of the main article formalizes this problem as TCoE via CPC.

TCoE generates empirical predictions that are distinct from those of individual-level constructivist theories of emotion because it is explicitly a theory of dyadic co-construction. The central predictions concern not merely the relationship between interoception and emotion labeling within a single individual, but the dynamics of emotion word convergence through iterated social interaction.

p(x int | w) should correspond to neural processes that evaluate the compatibility between bodily state and an emotion word; the prior p(w) should correspond to socially and linguistically acquired expectations over available labels; the acceptance computation should correspond to neural mechanisms that compare a newly proposed word with the current shared state; and precision weighting should correspond to the modulation of how strongly interoceptive evidence constrains that comparison. Although the precise neural substrates remain open, TCoE predicts that these components cannot be fully understood within single-agent paradigms alone. The most direct neuroscientific tests should use interactive paradigms in which one participant proposes an emotion word and another accepts or rejects it during neural recording. Supporting evidence would include responses modulated jointly by bodily state and shared lexical context, with individual differences related to interoceptive precision. Weaker bodily-state coupling in high-alexithymia participants would support the clinical account in Section 5. Responses explained entirely by generic semantic processing, social attention, or linguistic dominance, without bodily-state coupling, would require revision. Such experiments would help clarify whether emotion co-construction has a distinct neural basis beyond existing individual-level accounts of constructed emotion.
