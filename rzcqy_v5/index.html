data_collection_procedures Data on parents of children with CCN (In Kaart)

This study is conducted as part of the project Ouders in Evenwicht (Balanced Parents). The data was collected through In Kaart, a register for people with neurodivergence and/or disabilities and their next of kin in the Netherlands (In Kaart | In Kaart - register voor en door mensen met ontwikkelingsproblemen.). Recruitment of participants was a collaborative effort between the project group of Ouders in Evenwicht and In Kaart. Participants were approached to participate in “a study regarding burnout and resilience among parents of children with complex care needs.”

Recruitment occurred through multiple channels: parental organizations and platforms, Facebook groups for parents of children with various illnesses/ disabilities, patient organizations, conferences for healthcare professionals and parents, and schools. We also used the our project group members’ personal networks via platforms such as Facebook, Instagram and LinkedIn. Interest groups, the network of the steering committee members of In Kaart, relevant healthcare institutions (e.g., general practitioners, child health clinics, mental health care services, Municipal Public Health Service, rehabilitation centers) were also instrumental in the recruitment process. Recruitment materials (e-mails, social media messages, and flyers) were prepared in collaboration with experts by experience, ensuring they were relevant and sensitive to the target audience.

Data collection started 5th of September 2022 and ended 30th of June 2023. The data collected for this study was the first measurement wave of ongoing data collections.

Inclusion criteria of parents of children with CCN

Parents (biological, legal guardians, adoptive, or stepparents) were eligible to participate if they had a child with CCN below 16 years. CCN was defined as “…those who have or are at increased risk of chronic physical, developmental, behavioral, or emotional conditions and who also require health and related services beyond what is typically required by children” (McPherson et al., 1998, p. 138). Parents were also encouraged to participate if there was a suspicion of a diagnosis or if they worried about the health or development of their child, this was to ensure that the study reflected the experiences of a diverse group of parents, making the findings more applicable to a wider group of parents and improving the relevance of the results for similar populations. Furthermore, the age limit of 16 years was chosen due to privacy regulations in the Netherlands, where parents of children aged 16 or above are not allowed to provide information about the child without the child’s consent.

Data for parents of children without CCN (LISS-panel)

The recruitment of participants and data collection were handled by the independent non-profit institute Centerdata using the LISS panel. The LISS panel is a household panel consisting of approximately 7500 panel members from 5,000 households. The panel is based on a probability sample drawn from the population register by Statistics Netherlands (CBS). Through this sample Centerdata tries to create a representative sample of the Dutch population. The members of the panel receive monthly invitations via email to participate in questionnaires. For the data collection such a standard mail was used.
All LISS data are available for non-commercial purposes through a freely accessible and advanced data archive. Centerdata is an official partner of Tilburg University. Before a household can participate in the LISS panel, they must give informed consent in three different steps. Monthly participants are invited to fill in surveys for which they get compensated (Home - LISS panel).

The data for this study was collected as part of a bigger data collection effort. This data collection took place from August 2024 to December 2024 through online surveys. Data was collected over four waves. Wave 1 took place in August and September, wave 2 in September and October, wave 3 in October and November, and wave 4 in November and December. Only parents who completed the previous wave were invited to participate in the next wave. measured_variables The Parental Burnout Assessment

The Dutch translated version of Parental Burnout Assessment (PBA) (Roskam et al., 2018) was used to measure parental burnout. This PBA consists of 23 items, covering four dimensions: ‘emotional exhaustion’ in one’s parental role (EE: 9 items; e.g., “I feel completely run down by my role as a parent”), ‘emotional distancing’ (ED: 3 items; e.g. “I’m no longer able to show my child(ren) how much I love them”), contrast with previous parental self (CO: 6 items; e.g., “I’m no longer proud of myself as a parent”), and ‘feelings of being fed up’ (FU: 5 items; e.g., “I can’t take being a parent any more”). Items are rated on a 7-point likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to a few times a year or less (1), once a month or less (2), a few times a month (3), once a week (4), a few times a week (5), every day (6). The total score is the sum of the individual items, a higher score reflects more burnout related thoughts and feelings.

The analysis will be conducted following the steps below:

Research question 1: To what extent does the PBA demonstrate measurement invariance across parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN?

1. Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omegas will be calculated over the total PBA and the 4 subscales for the total sample and both subgroups (parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN).

2. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) will be conducted for both subgroups, using the Lavaan package (Rossel 2012), to test if the four-dimensional structure is similar across groups and to ensure the measurement model fits well in both subgroups independently using the Diagonally Weighted Least Squared (DWLS) method. The model fit will be evaluated using fit indices: and where needed the model will be adjusted. The fit will be assessed through Comparative Fit index (CFI), Chi-Square Test of Model Fit, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error Of Approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). These fit indices will also be applied in the invariance analysis (see step 3).

3. Once the CFA shows good fit* in both subgroups, measurement invariance of the total sample (i.e., across parents of children with CCN and parents without children with CCN) will be conducted using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MG-CFA) . The invariance analysis will be conducted in a stepwise manner as outlined below (a-d), with each subsequent step performed only if the model demonstrates an acceptable fit at the preceding level.

a. Configural invariance will be tested with a MG-CFA with two groups (1 = parents of children with CCN, 2 = parents of children without CCN). Configural invariance tests if the factor structure – that is, the number of factors and factor loadings patterns - is the same across groups. This is done by not setting any constraints on the parameters (factor loadings, item threshold/ intercepts, residual variance). Good fit indicates that parents with children with CCN and those without conceptualize burnout as measured by the PBA similarly.

b. Metric (weak) invariance tests whether the factor loadings are equal across the groups. This is done by constraining the factor loadings - i.e., the strength of the relationship with the item and the factor loading- to be equal across the subgroups. A good model fit under metric invariance implies that the strength of the relationship between the items and the underlying factor is the same across groups. The model fit will be evaluated and compared to the configural model using delta Chi-Squares. A good fit means that parents of children with CCN and those without interpret the items in a similar way.

c. Scalar (strong) invariance tests whether the item intercepts/ thresholds are equal across groups, in addition to equal factor loadings. A good fit indicates that parents of children with CCN and those without who have the same level of burnout are equally likely to choose the same response options on each item.

d. Residual (strict) invariance will be tested, which tests if factor loadings, item thresholds/ intercepts, and residual variance are equal across groups. Residual invariance indicates that parents of children with CCN and those without have the same amount of measurement error or item-specific variance in their responses.

Research question 2: Taking the structure of the PBA in both samples into account, what are the differences between these two groups?

If the factor structure of the PBA is found to be invariant between the two subgroups at least at the configural invariance level:
An ANOVA will be conducted to examine whether there are statistically significant differences in the PBA total score and subscale scores between the two subgroups.

If the factor structure of the PBA is found not invariant between the two subgroups at least at the configural invariance level:
Include descriptives at the item level for each subgroup to examine the variability across items and identify items that may be less relevant or problematic for the CCN subgroup.

Research question 3: Which sociodemographic characteristics (such as, parental age, gender, education, occupation, ethnicity, child’s age, number of children and family composition) are associated with burnout among parents of children with CCN and parents without children without CCN?

If the factor structure of the PBA is found to be invariant between the two subgroups at least at the configural invariance level:
Descriptive statistics will be used to compare both subgroups at the subscale level, to provide insight into the distribution of scores and support the interpretation of the means. Socio-demographic factors related to the differences in burnout scores between both groups will be tested. The aim is to understand which socio-demographic factors are associated with burnout related thoughts and feelings given the differences between the two groups. Moderation analyses will be used to examine between-group differences.

If the factor structure of the PBA is found not invariant between the two subgroups at least at the configural invariance level:
Explore the relationship between socio-demographic characteristics and burnout separately for parents of children with CCN and those without. Within each subgroup, regression analysis will be conducted to investigate which socio-demographic characteristics are associated with burnout scores. Since the PBA is not invariant, the aim will not be to explain group differences, but rather to understand which socio-demographic characteristics are related to burnout within each subgroup independently

*If configural invariance is not achieved (step 2) across the four dimensions of the PBA, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) will be conducted separately for both groups. This will help determine whether an alternative factor structure may be more appropriate for parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN. The goal is to explore whether different dimensions emerge that may better reflect the underlying structure of parental burnout in each group and potentially show configural invariance across groups. If configural invariance can be achieved following a modified factor structure of the PBA, then invariance testing on that factor structure can continue (steps 3a-d).

Steps 1-3 will also be conducted among parents of children with CCN and the total sample of LISS (n = 450), hence also including those parents in the LISS sample who have a child with CCN. This will serve as supplementary analysis and will not be part of the scientific publication.

inference_criteria A p-value < .05 will be used to determine statistical significance of the results. To determine an acceptable model fit, Chi-Square test needs to be non-significant. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective. This study evaluated the measurement invariance of the Parental Burnout Assessment (PBA) across parents of children with complex care needs (CCN) and parents of children without CCN and studied the difference in burnout scores and their associations with sociodemographic factors. Design. The PBA was completed by 337 parents of children with CCN and 329 parents of children without CCN. A multigroup-confirmatory factor analysis was employed to test measurement invariance. Group differences in total and subscale scores were examined. Stepwise regression included sociodemographic variables (parental age, sex, ethnicity, educational attainment, single parent, minimum child age, maximum child age, and the number of children) and parental CCN status with its interaction term as a moderator. Results. Scalar invariance was established for a four-factor model and a second-order model of the PBA, indicating that the observed differences in PBA scores reflect true differences. Parents of children with CCN reported higher PBA scores than parents of children without CCN (d = 1.33, p < .001). Being a mother of a child with CCN was associated with substantially higher PBA scores compared to being a father. This difference was smaller among parents of children without CCN. Other sociodemographic characteristics were not associated with higher scores. Conclusions. Findings underscore the heightened vulnerability for parental burnout among parents of children with CCN compared to parents of children without CCN while also providing further evidence for the suitability of the PBA as a research instrument for assessing parental burnout across these caregiving contexts.

INTRODUCTION

While parenting can be deeply rewarding, it also comes with significant challenges, especially for parents of children with complex care needs (CCN). Children with CCN have a combination of enduring physical, developmental, behavioral, and/or emotional problems and needs, requiring care beyond what is typically required (McPherson et al., 1998). Parents of children with CCN face unique demands, such as extensive daily assistance, complex medical tasks, and uncertainty regarding the disability or illness and its progression (Hodapp et al., 2019; Power et al., 2019). The concept of "parental burnout" was introduced in the 1980s to describe how prolonged exposure to parentingrelated stress may have severe long-term psychosocial effects (Procaccini & Kiefaber, 1983). Parental burnout is often linked to the burden of prolonged caregiving demands without reprieve. The Parental Burnout Assessment (PBA) has emerged as a preferred questionnaire (Arikan et al., 2020). Research on parental burnout has grown rapidly (Mikolajczak et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2024), with several studies comparing burnout levels between parents of children with CCN to those without (for a review see Patty, van Meeteren, Willemen, et al., 2024). Parents of children with CCN tend to report higher levels of burnout compared to parents of children without CCN, with prevalence estimates ranging from 20-77% for parents of children with CCN (compared to 20-34% among parents of children without CCN). Several factors may contribute to the wide range in estimates, including the use of diverse instruments for measuring burnout, heterogeneity on sociodemographic factors across populations, and potential differences in the meaning of the construct. Moreover, some studies suggest that parental burnout may manifests itself differently among parents of children with CCN than those without (Abdoli et al., 2020; Patty, van Meeteren, Verdonk, et al., 2024). Consequently, the aim of the present study was to examine the measurement invariance of the PBA across parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN. Taking invariance into account, levels of burnout-related thoughts and feelings between these two groups were compared, and the associations between sociodemographic characteristics and burnout scores explored, given the caregiving context of having a child with CCN or not.

Developed by Roskam et al. (2018), the PBA has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across languages and cultural contexts (Roskam et al., 2021). The PBA consists of 23 items and conceptualizes parental burnout through four dimensions: emotional exhaustion in the parental role, emotional distancing from one's child, feelings of being fed up with the parental role, and contrast with previous parental self. To date, its invariance has been tested across parental education level, gender, and languages (see Aunola et al., 2020; Roskam et al., 2021) both for the four-factor structure and PBA as a higher-order factor, representing overall parental burnout. The PBA has also been used in parents of children with CCN (Aviad et al., 2024; Dzielińska et al., 2024; Hassanein et al., 2025; Roskam & Mikolajczak, 2023). However, it is unknown whether the four-factor structure of the PBA, developed from accounts of burned-out mothers in the general population, manifests in the same way among parents of children with CCN.

Qualitative evidence suggests that, for these parents, burnout stems from the double burden of simultaneously parenting and providing intensive, ongoing care (Patty, van Meeteren, Willemen, et al., 2024; Patty et al., 2025). This experience encompasses substantial physical, emotional, and organizational strain, compounded by the long-term and inescapable nature of caregiving responsibilities (Abdoli et al., 2020; Patty, van Meeteren, Verdonk, et al., 2024). Although the qualitative findings show conceptual overlap with the four dimensions of the PBA, parent's experiences were organized and emphasized somewhat differently. For example, in the study by Patty, van Meeteren, Verdonk, et al., (2024) survival mode emerged as a prominent theme in the conceptualization of burnout among parents of children with CCN, reflecting the need to continue functioning despite exhaustion and distancing, due to the ongoing caregiving responsibilities.

Although survival mode is not considered a dimension of the PBA, it illustrates how the intensive and ongoing caregiving context may shape how some aspects of burnout are experienced. Hence, this context adds to the possibility that, despite some conceptual overlap, the PBA may not capture these experiences in the same way across parents of children with CCN and those without children with CCN. On top of this, conceptual overlap by itself does not necessarily imply measurement invariance.

Even when the same dimensions are present across groups, individual items may relate differently to the dimensions. (Abdoli et al., 2020; Patty, van Meeteren, Verdonk, et al., 2024)In the light these considerations, we suggest that while the PBA captures important facets of parental burnout, its structure and content may only partially represent the lived experiences of parents of children with CCN, potentially limiting its applicability in this population. Additionally, the combined burden of parenting and intense caregiving required for a child with CCN may shape how sociodemographic factors influence parental burnout related thoughts and feelings (Patty, van Meeteren, Willemen, et al., 2024). This aligns with the findings from a meta-analysis which found that the effect of sociodemographic factors on parental burnout may vary depending on the caregiving context, with no consistent associations observed for factors such as age, gender, education, or single parenthood (Mikolajczak et al., 2023).

To date, only one study has investigated the associations between sociodemographic factors and parental burnout among parents with children with CCN compared to parents without children with CCN. Using the Parental Burnout Inventory (PBI; Roskam et al., 2018), Gérain and Zech (2018) found no significant associations between parental burnout and sociodemographic variablesincluding parental age, sex, education level, working part time, single parenting, and the number of children -when comparing parents of children with CCN to those without. Consequently, if measurement invariance is established, we can appropriately assess the associations between sociodemographic factors and parental burnout given the caregiving context of parenting a child with CCN or not, as it ensures that the comparison is not confounded by potential group differences in the interpretation of the PBA.

In sum, meaningful comparisons between parents with and without a child with CCN depend on whether the PBA can be considered invariant -that is, the degree to which the PBA measures the same construct in the same way across these two groups. When invariance cannot be established, that is when the PBA does not measure the concept of parental burnout in the same way across the two groups, caution is needed when drawing conclusions about group differences. Once invariance has been established, it becomes relevant to examine whether the caregiving context of having a child with CCN or not also affects the associations between sociodemographic factors and parental burnout.

Clarifying this helps detangle the unique psychological impact of intense caregiving from broader structural or contextual factors, thereby informing the design of more targeted support strategies and policy interventions aimed at reducing parental burnout.

The primary aim of this paper is to investigate measurement invariance of the PBA across parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN and the secondary aim is to investigate the differences in the PBA scores and the association with sociodemographic characteristics in both groups, given the caregiving context of having a child with CCN or not. First, we hypothesize that PBA may demonstrate only partial measurement invariance across parents of children with CCN and parents without children with CCN, based on the differences in the emphasis of certain aspects of burnout, despite substantial conceptual overlap with the PBA from the qualitative accounts of parents (Patty, van Meeteren, Verdonk, et al., 2024). (Patty, van Meeteren, Verdonk, et al., 2024)Second, to the extent that the PBA can be considered invariant, differences in burnout scores are expected, with parents of children with CCN reporting higher levels of parental burnout than parents of children without CCN. Third, we expect the associations between sociodemographic characteristics and the PBA to differ for parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN. Eight sociodemographic factors will be subjected to analysis: parental age, sex, ethnicity, educational attainment, single parent, minimum and maximum child age. Considering the limited and inconsistent evidence in the literature regarding sociodemographic correlates on parental burnout, these analyses were largely exploratory. The exception was our hypothesis regarding child age, for which differential associations with parental burnout were expected depending on both the age of the youngest and the age of the oldest child and the caregiving context (i.e., having a child with CCN or not). Among parents in general, having a child younger than 5 years was associated with higher burnout scores, likely due to caregiving demands being at their peak (Mikolajczak et al., 2018). In contrast, among parents of children with a CCN, a longer caregiving history may be associated with elevated burnoutrelated thoughts and feelings. Accordingly, we expect that, in this group, having an older child will be associated with higher levels of parental burnout. This assumption reflects the cumulative burden of long-term caregiving and has not yet been explored in the parental burnout literature.

METHODS

Participants

The samples consisted of n = 337 parents of children with CCN and n = 329 parents without a child with CCN (see Table 1 for the demographic characteristics). In the sample of parents of children with CCN, the majority were mothers (n = 307, 91.9%). In contrast, the sample of parents of children without CCN was more evenly distributed, with mothers comprising 50.5% of the group (n = 166). Furthermore, highly educated parents (university or college degree) were overrepresented in the sample, with 69.9% of parents of children with CCN and 55.3% of parents of children without CCN being highly educated. According to the Statistics Netherlands, approximately 37.0% of the Dutch population had completed higher education in 2024 (Statistics Netherlands, 2024), indicating that the respondents in the current study were more highly educated than the general population. Regarding ethnicity, 9.0% of the parents of children with CCN identified themselves as non-Dutch or bi-ethnic (i.e., Dutch in combination with another ethnicity or a non-Dutch background), compared to 21.4% of parents of children without a child with CCN. In 2023, Statistics Netherlands reported that 16.0% of residents in the Netherlands were born abroad or have a migration background. Furthermore, the diagnosis of the children varied widely in the group and 62% (n = 210) of parents of children with CCN reported that their child had behavioral problems. See Table 2 for further information of the characteristics of the children.

Procedures

This study was preregistered on OSF.io (see https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/ZUT3X). Minor adjustments to the data analysis plan were made based on assumption checks and were also documented on OSF.

Data Collection for Parents of Children with CCN.

This study is part of the project Ouders in Evenwicht (Parents in Balance). The data were collected online through In Kaart, a Dutch register for people with neurodivergence and/or disabilities and their families (InKaart.org). In Kaart is a noncommercial research register established to facilitate scientific research. Ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific and Ethical Review Board of the Faculty of Behavioural and Movement Sciences of the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam (registration number: VCWE-2022-103). Participants were recruited by the project group and In Kaart through parental organizations, social media, patient organizations, conferences, schools, healthcare institutions, and the personal networks of project members. Participation was voluntary, and no financial compensation was provided. Interested parents first registered to participate in the study, after which each participant received a unique code and individual link to access the online questionnaire. The questionnaire was therefore not accessible through an open survey link. Recruitment materials were co-developed with experts by experience to ensure sensitivity and relevance. Data collection took place between September 5, 2022, and June 30, 2023. Participants were approached to participate in "a study regarding burnout and resilience among parents of children with complex care needs". Parents were invited to participate if they had a child with CCN, suspected a diagnosis, or were concerned about their child's health or development.

Eligibility was based on parental reports and was not independently verified through medical records or clinical assessment, as this could exclude families of children with substantial care needs who were still undergoing diagnostic assessment. As an additional indication of the care needs within the sample, among the parents included in the PBA analyses who provided information on formal care and/ or support (n = 303), 80% (n = 241) reported that their child received formal care and/ or support. This broad recruitment strategy among parents of children with CCN was chosen to help capture the heterogeneity in children's conditions and care needs and to avoid restricting the sample to a specific diagnosis or clinical population, as is commonly done in research on burnout among parents of children with CCN (Mroskova et al., 2020; Patty, van Meeteren, Willemen, et al., 2024). Parents (biological, legal guardians, adoptive, or stepparents) were eligible to participate if they had a child with CCN below 16 years. CCN was defined as "…those who have or are at increased risk of chronic physical, developmental, behavioral, or emotional conditions and who also require health and related services beyond what is typically required by children" (McPherson et al., 1998, p. 138). Furthermore, the age limit of 16 years was chosen due to privacy regulations in the Netherlands, where parents of children aged 16 or above are not allowed to provide information about the child without the child's consent.

Data Collection for Parents of Children without CCN.

The data collection was handled by the non-profit research institute Centerdata (Tilburg University, the Netherlands) using the Longitudinal Internet Studies for the Social Sciences (LISS panel) (Scherpenzeel & Das, 2010) Centerdata was instructed to invite panel members to create the following sample: approximately 500 parents with children aged 0 to 18 years of whom at least one lived at home. Within this sample of 500 parents, at least 150 parents had to have a child between 5 to 12 years, with an ideal gender ratio of approximately 50% mothers and 50% fathers within this subsample. For the entire sample, no ideal gender ratio was defined, instead the aim was a minimum of 100 fathers and 100 mothers. Additionally, parents had to be part of different households. The original comparison sample provided by Centerdata also included parents who reported having a child with CCN. In the present study, these parents were excluded from the comparison sample to create a comparison group consisting of parents of children without CCN.

Data for parents of children with CCN were collected through Qualtrics and imported into IBM SPSS statistical software program (Version 29.0). Data for parents of children without CCN were obtained from Centerdata in a CSV and SPSS file. The data harmonization and exploration of the data occurred in SPSS.

Measures Parental Burnout Assessment (PBA).

The Dutch translation of the PBA (Roskam et al., 2018 (Roskam et al., , 2021) ) was used to measure parental burnout. The PBA consists of 23 items, covering four dimensions: "emotional exhaustion" in one's parental role (EE: 9 items; e.g., "I feel completely run down by my role as a parent"), "emotional distancing" (ED: 3 items; e.g. "I'm no longer able to show my child(ren) how much I love them"), "contrast with previous parental self" (CO: 6 items; e.g., "I'm no longer proud of myself as a parent"), and "feelings of being fed up" (FU: 5 items; e.g., "I can't take being a parent any more"). Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to a few times a year or less (2), once a month or less (3), a few times a month (4), once a week (5), a few times a week (6), every day (7). The total score is the sum of the individual items; a higher score indicates more burnout related thoughts and feelings. The total score is commonly used as an outcome measure within research on parental burnout (see e.g., Bian et al., 2025; Dzielińska et al., 2024; Hassanein et al., 2025).

Sociodemographic Characteristics.

A range of sociodemographic characteristics was collected, including parental (age, sex, education level, single-parent status and ethnicity), child (age, living situation, type of CCN), and family characteristics (number of children). Sociodemographic variables were harmonized across the two datasets. For harmonization of the data and to describe the samples, the following response categories were created: parental sex was recoded into female = 1, male = 0; parent education level was classified as0 = none,1 = low (primary education or prevocational secondary school),2 = middle (higher secondary education/preparatory university education or intermediate vocational education), and3 = high (college or university); ethnicity, which was based on what ethnical group the respondent felt they belonged to and was coded as0 = Dutch and1 = non-Dutch or bi-ethnic (i.e., Dutch in combination with other ethnicity); and single parent was coded as0 = not single and1 = single parent. To test the associations of sociodemographic characteristics with burnout, education was recoded to ensure sufficient respondents per response category. Categories were merged into secondary/ vocational education = 0 (encompassing primary education, pre-vocational secondary school, higher secondary education/preparatory university education or intermediate vocational education) and higher education = 1 (encompassing college or university degree).

For the child with CCN the following information was obtained: age; living situation of the child categorized as "at home", "not at home", "partially at home"; the presence of behavioral problems; and type of CCN. For the type of CCN, parents could choose one or more of the following predefined response options: chronic/ serious illness; behavioral, emotional, or learning disorder; motor or sensory disability/ impairment; intellectual disability; or other. See Table 1 for the sociodemographic characteristics.

Analytic Plan

All analysis took place in JASP statistical software version 0.95.4 (JASP Team, 2025), which uses the Lavaan package version 0.6-19 (Rosseel, 2012) for invariance analysis.

Responses were screened for straight-lining patterns, that is, those providing identical response options across the PBA items, as an indicator of potentially careless or insufficient-effort responding (Curran, 2016; Desimone et al., 2015). A total of n = 51 parents engaged in straight-lining, with the majority of the respondents being parents of children without CCN (n = 47). As parents of children without CCN received financial compensation (five euros per wave) and as approximately half of these parents belonged to the fastest 15% of the group who filled in the questionnaire, these respondents were excluded from the subsequent analyses. This resulted in a total sample of n = 666 parents. This approach is consistent with Curran (2016), who recommends combining straight-lining patterns with response times as an initial strategy for identifying and excluding potentially careless respondents.

Invariance Testing. First, we investigated the number of missing values for the PBA. We found a small number of missing values (n = 3; participants who discontinued the PBA midway).

Therefore, a listwise deletion method was applied to handle missing data (Shi et al., 2020). Second, the PBA was analyzed to determine whether it should be considered as ordinal or interval. Third, following the approach by Aunola et al. (2020) and Hassanein et al. (2025), three models were specified to assess the factor structure of the PBA. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed for each model separately in both samples. First, a theoretical four-factor model (M1) was tested, comprising four correlated latent factors representing the dimensions of the PBA: emotional exhaustion (EE; 9 items), contrast with previous parental self (CO; 6 items), feelings of being fed up (FU; 5 items), and emotional distancing (ED; 3 items). Second, a one-factor measurement model (M2) was examined, in which a single latent factor was specified to underlie all 23 PBA items. Third, a second-order measurement model (M3) was assessed, to examine whether the associations among the first-order factors in M1 could be explained by a higher-order factor representing overall parental burnout (see Figure 1).

Model fit was evaluated to determine whether the model should be retained for subsequent invariance testing, using multiple indices: Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) values greater than .90 were considered indicative of acceptable fit and values > .95 indicative of good fit; Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) values of ≤ .08 indicated acceptable fit, with values ≤ .05 reflecting a better fit (van de Schoot et al., 2012); and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) values ≤ .08 were considered acceptable and < .05 good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999).

Next, we ran a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis to assess measurement invariance across parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN. Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis was conducted in a stepwise manner as proposed by Leitgöb et al. (2023), with each subsequent step undertaken only if the model demonstrated an acceptable fit at the preceding level and changes in the model fit indices. We began with (1) configural invariance, examining whether the four-factor structure -specifically, the number of factors and the pattern of factor loadings -was consistent across groups. At this stage, no equality constraints were imposed on the parameters (factor loadings, item threshold/ intercepts, residual variance). A good model fit under configural invariance implies that both groups conceptualize the construct in a similar manner. Then, we assessed (2) metric invariance, testing whether the factor loadings were equal across the groups. This was done by constraining the factor loadings -that is, the strength of the relation with the item and the factor loading-to be equal across the subgroups. A good model fit under metric invariance implies that the strength of the relation between the items and the underlying factor is the same across groups. Next, we assessed (3) scalar invariance, testing whether the item intercepts/ thresholds were equal across groups, in addition to equal factor loadings. A good fit under scalar invariance indicates that parents of children with CCN and those without CCN who have the same level of burnout were equally likely to choose the same response options on each item. Attaining scalar invariance is a prerequisite for comparing mean scores across groups. Finally, we tested (4) strict (residual) invariance across the groups, by additionally constraining the residual variances of the items to be equal, testing whether the amount of measurement error was consistent across the two groups, implying that any remaining differences in observed scores reflected true differences in parental burnout rather than differences in response reliability or measurement precision. At each step of the invariance testing, we assessed whether the fit of the more constrained model showed any meaningful deterioration by examining changes in the RMSEA, CFI, and SRMR. Models were compared by computing the differences (Δ) in RMSEA, CFI, and SRMR values between each successive level of measurement invariance.

Measurement invariance was supported if the change in the fit indices was small, specifically when ΔRMSEA <.015, ΔCFI <.01, and ΔSRMR <.03 for metric invariance or <.015 for scalar and strict invariance (Chen, 2007; Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). Invariance testing was also conducted across parents of children with CCN and parents in general (i.e., including those parents who had indicated that their child has some form of CCN within the LISS panel) (see Supplement 1).

Internal consistency of the PBA was analyzed in both samples separately for PBA total score and per dimension, using Cronbach's alpha (α) and McDonald's omegas (ω). Scales with more items tend to yield higher reliability estimates; therefore, stricter benchmarks were used for the EE dimension (9 items) and for the total PBA score (23 items), compared with the shorter ED, CO, and FU subscales (Pontekotto & Ruckdeschel, 2007). Thus, for the PBA dimensions ED, CO, and FU, values of ≥ .85 were considered excellent, .80-.84 good, .75-.79 moderate, .70-.74 fair, and < .70 unsatisfactory. For the EE dimension, values of ≥ .90 were considered excellent, .85-.89 good, .80-.84 moderate, and < .75 unsatisfactory. For the total PBA score values of ≥ .90 were considered excellent, .85-.89 moderate, and < .80 were considered unsatisfactory.

Differences in PBA Scores and Per Dimension.

The distribution of the burnout scores was assessed using Shapiro-Wilks test and Q-Q plots to determine whether normality assumptions were met. Differences in the overall PBA score between groups were examined using an independent sample t-test. In case of potential deviations from normality, the Mann-Whitney U test, a nonparametric alternative to the independent-samples t-test was used. Differences across the four burnout dimensions were examined using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), which accounts for multiple testing and for potential correlations among dimensions. If the MANOVA indicated significant overall group differences, univariate analyses were planned for each dimension to identify specific effects. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. The effect sizes were converted into Cohen's d to facilitate comparisons across findings. Effect sizes were interpreted using the following thresholds: very small = 0.10, small = 0.20, medium = 0.40, and large = 0.63 (Funder & Ozer, 2019; Lenhard & Lenhard, 2022).

Associations Between Socio-demographics and Burnout.

Eight sociodemographic variables (parental age, sex, ethnicity, educational attainment, single parent, minimum child age, maximum child age, and the number of children) were examined as predictors in separate moderation analyses. Both youngest and oldest child age were included to capture potential differences related to caregiving demands and duration across having a child with CCN or not. To compare the effect of child age for both parents with and without a child with CCN, the minimum and maximum age was used. This variable was not related to the child with CCN. Accounting for minimum as well as maximum child age offered a conservative proxy for early caregiving demands and for the cumulative duration of caregiving responsibilities.

A stepwise regression was conducted for each sociodemographic variable with the total PBA score as dependent variable. In the first step, the sociodemographic variable of interest was entered. In the second step, the dichotomous variable indicating whether the parent has a child with CCN or not (moderator) was added together with the interaction term. In case the hierarchical model showed the presence of an interaction effect, this interaction effect was scrutinized using the JASP PROCESS module (A. F. Hayes, 2022; Lüken et al., 2024). All regression models were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap samples to generate bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals. All continuous variables were mean-centered to facilitate meaningful interpretation of the regression coefficients (Aiken & West, 1991) as part of the PROCESS module (Hayes, 2025).

Statistical Power.

Using semPOWER by Moshagen and Bader (2024), a post hoc analysis was conducted for CFA. Assuming a RMSEA of .08, with α = .05, df = 224, and n = 328 (the smallest group -parents of children without CCN), the analyses indicated very high statistical power (1 -β = .999) to detect model misfit.

We also ran a sensitivity power analysis to estimate the smallest detectable effect sizes for comparisons of PBA total scores, subdimensions, and the effects of sociodemographic characteristics on PBA. For differences in PBA total scores between parents of children without CCN (n = 328) and parents of children with CCN (n = 335), a sensitivity analysis for an independent-samples t test in G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007 (Faul et al., , 2009) ) with α = .05 and power = .80 indicated a minimum detectable effect size of d = .22. For group differences across the four PBA subdimensions, a sensitivity analysis for a MANOVA with two groups and four dependent variables (α = .05, power = .80, N = 663) indicated a minimum detectable multivariate effect size of f²(V) = .02. Finally, for the effect of sociodemographic predictors on PBA total scores, a sensitivity analysis using an F test for linear multiple regression (R² increase) with three predictors (α = .05, power = .80, N = 663) indicated a minimum detectable effect size of f² = .02. These analyses demonstrate that the study was sufficiently powered to detect small effects for the research question regarding the differences in mean scores and the research question regarding the effect of socio-demographics on parental burnout.

RESULTS

First, we evaluated if parents of children with CCN and parents without children with CCN differed on sociodemographic variables (Table 1). Significant differences were observed across all sociodemographic variables. Examination of item distribution of the PBA revealed deviations from normality, particularly among parents of children without a child with CCN. These deviations were conceptually plausible, as mental health indicators, such as burnout, often show asymmetric distributions (Ferr, 2025). The higher response categories ("daily", "a few times a week", and "once a week") were seldom endorsed by parents of children without CCN, resulting in a positively skewed distribution (Figure 2).

Invariance Testing

Treating the PBA items as ordinal rather than continuous resulted in improved model fit across all factor structures M1-M3 (Supplement 2), suggesting that modelling the PBA items as ordinal better captures the nature of the response data (see Table 4). Consequently, the ordinal specification of the PBA was retained and therefore model parameters were estimated using the Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator. However, due to the sparse responses in the extreme categories of the PBA, response categories were collapsed to ensure a minimum of five responses in the outermost response categories to facilitate CFA analysis. Very sparse categories can complicate threshold estimation for ordinal data, in particular when some categories have no counts or very low counts, making threshold constraints difficult to estimate (Rutkowski et al., 2019). These modifications were done by collapsing the highest response categories of the 7-point Likert scale into a 6-point Likert scale (PBA items: 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, and 21), a 5-point Likert scale (PBA items: 4, 5, 6, 13, 18, 19, 22, and 23), or a 4-point Likert scale (PBA items: 16, 17, and 20).

CFA M1: Four-factor Structure.

The four-factor structure of the PBA demonstrated acceptable fit in both groups. Among parents of children without CCN (n = 328), model fit indices were generally within acceptable ranges, χ²(224) = 734, p < .001, CFI = .946, TLI = .940, SRMR = .071, although RMSEA was slightly above the recommended threshold (RMSEA = .083). Similarly, for parents of children with CCN (n = 335), the model showed acceptable fit, χ²(224) = 1021, p < .001, CFI = .948, TLI = .942, SRMR = .067, with RMSEA again exceeding preferred cut-offs (RMSEA = .103). Modification indices were examined in both samples; however, the suggested model adjustments differed between the two groups, indicating that no single structural modification could be applied consistently across the two groups. Overall, despite elevated RMSEA valuesparticularly in the CCN group-the four-factor model demonstrated robust and comparable fit across samples, supporting its suitability for further invariance testing.

CFA M2: One-factor Structure.

The one-factor model showed poorer fit relative to the fourfactor solution. Among parents of children without CCN, the model yielded χ²(228) = 858, p < .001, CFI = .934, TLI = .927, RMSEA = .092, SRMR = .084. For parents of children with CCN, fit was weaker; the CFA yielded χ²(230) = 1349, p < .001, CFI = .928, TLI = .921, RMSEA = .121, and SRMR = .083. Although CFI and TLI values suggested marginally acceptable incremental fit, both RMSEA and SRMR exceeded commonly accepted thresholds (≤ .08; van de Schoot et al., 2012), particularly in the CCN group. Modification indices consistently identified residual covariances between item pairs PBA17-PBA18 and PBA1-PBA3 as potential sources of misfit. Allowing covariance between PBA1 and PBA3 led to a modest improvement in SRMR among parents without CCN but did not improve fit in the CCN group and none of the suggested modifications substantially reduced RMSEA. Given the limited improvement and lack of strong theoretical justification, no post hoc modifications were retained. Overall, the one-factor model demonstrated inferior fit compared to the multidimensional alternatives.

CFA M3: Second Order-factor Structure.

The second-order factor model, in which the four first-order factors from M1 loaded onto a higher-order factor -parental burnout -demonstrated comparable fit to the four-factor model (M1). Among parents of children without CCN the model yielded χ²(226) = 727, p < .001, CFI = .948, TLI = .941, RMSEA = .082, and SRMR = .072. For parents of children with CCN, the fit indices were χ²(226) = 1017, p < .001, CFI = .949, TLI = .943, RMSEA = .102, and SRMR = .067. As with M1, RMSEA values were elevated, particularly in the CCN group. Modification indices again suggested different adjustments across groups. Specifically, among parents of children without CCN, residual covariance was suggested between item pair PBA1-PBA3, which are related to the exhaustion dimension. Among parents of children with CCN, however, residual covariance was suggested between item pairs PBA17-PBA18 and PBA5-PBA13, which are related to the contrast dimension. Since these suggestions were not consistent across groups, it was decided to retain the model without modifications. The second-order structure (M3) showed overall fit that was comparable to, and in some respects slightly better than, the first-order four-factor model (M1), while providing a more straightforward representation of parental burnout as a higher-order construct. Given the superior performance of M1 and M3, subsequent invariance testing focused exclusively on these two models. These models were retained without modifications despite the elevated RMSEAs because the three other fit indices indicated acceptable fit, parameter estimates were reasonable, and the models are consistent with existing theory regarding the structure of the PBA (Kline, 2023; Marsh et al., 2004; Roskam et al., 2021), Invariance M1: Four-factor Structure. Table 4 presents the model fit indices for the four-factor structure. The covariance between the four factors were > .70. Configural invariance was mostly supported, with acceptable fit despite RMSEA slightly above the conventional threshold (.093), indicating that parents of children with and without CCN conceptualize burnout similarly. Metric invariance was supported, suggesting factor loadings were equivalent and items were interpreted similarly across groups. Scalar invariance was also supported, indicating that item intercepts were comparable and allowing for valid comparisons of latent mean scores. The fit indices did not change between scalar and strict invariance. Finally, a substantial decline in fit was observed for structural invariance, indicating that the pattern of the relations among the factors differed across parents of children with CCN and parents without children with CCN.

Invariance M3: Second-order Factor Structure.

The second order model showed the same patterns as observed in M1. Configural through scalar invariance was supported, with slightly elevated RMSEA at each step (ranging from .092 to .086), indicating overall acceptable fit and largely consistent model structure across groups. Supporting that the first-order factors (i.e. dimensions) collectively reflect an overarching second-order factor -parental burnout -and because scalar invariance was established, the overall PBA score means can be meaningfully compared across groups. However, structural invariance was not supported, indicating that although the PBA measures burnout consistently across both groups, the way that the factors relate to each other differed between parents of children with CCN and parents of children without children with CCN.

Excellent internal consistency was found for all dimensions for both groups, except for t FU, which showed good rather than excellent reliability among parents without a child with CCN and ED, which showed only fair reliability in both groups (see Table 5).

Sensitivity Analysis: Mothers Only.

Due to the unequal gender composition of the sample of parents of children with CCN, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to examine whether the measurement invariance findings remained the same when the analyses were restricted to mothers only (CCN group, n = 307; non-CCN group, n = 166). The CFA results for the M1 four-factor and M3 second-order factor models in the two groups were broadly consistent with those obtained in the full samples. For both the M1 four-factor model and the M3 second-order factor model, the results were consistent with the primary analyses, with scalar invariance supported (ΔCFI < .01, ΔRMSEA < .015, and ΔSRMR < .015). Full CFA and measurement invariance results for the mothers-only analyses are presented in Supplement 1.

Difference Between Parents of Children with CNN and Parents of Children without CCN in PBA Scores

The PBA dimension-specific scores deviated significantly from normality for the collapsed PBA scores and the original PBA scores (Shapiro-Wilk p < .001, with Q-Q plots showing deviations at the tails). Table 6 presents total PBA mean score and mean scores per subscale for the collapsed and original PBA scores. The MANOVA including the four PBA dimensions showed that parental burnout was on average higher among parents of children with CCN than parents of children without CCN (Pillai's trace = .33, F(4, 658) = 82.23, p < .001). Similar results were observed for the collapsed version of the PBA (Pillai's trace = .34, F(4, 658) = 83.18, p < .001). Follow up univariate tests showed that parents of children with CCN had significantly higher mean scores on all PBA subdimensions. Furthermore, large effect sizes were observed for group differences on the dimensions of emotional exhaustion, contrast with previous parental self, and feelings of being fed-up. Medium effect size was observed for differences on the dimension of emotional distancing.

Associations Between Sociodemographic Characteristics and PBA

As shown in Table 7, for the effect of sociodemographic characteristics on the total PBA score we found significant associations for parental sex and the number of children, but the other sociodemographic variables were non-significantly related to the PBA. In addition, the association between parental sex and total PBA score was significantly moderated by having a child with CCN.

The difference in outcomes between fathers and mothers was larger among parents of children with CCN than parents of children without CCN. Specifically, mothers of children with CCN scored significantly higher on the PBA than fathers (B = 14.93, SE = 4.08, 95% CI [6.46, 23.14], p = .002).

No other interaction effects were significant, indicating that the associations between sociodemographic factors and parental burnout did not statistically differ between parents with and without children with CCN. In addition, in all analyses the dichotomous group variable was significantly related to the PBA, also after controlling for sociodemographic variables.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the measurement invariance of the Parental Burnout Assessment (PBA) across parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN, compared burnout scores between these groups, and explored how sociodemographic characteristics related to parental burnout within each parenting context. The PBA demonstrated scalar invariance across parents with and without children with CCN for both the theoretically proposed four-factor structure (M1) and the second-order structure (M3), in which the first-order latent variables (i.e. dimensions) were accounted for by the overarching factor, parental burnout. Parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN therefore tended to interpret the items of the PBA similarly and to attach similar value to differences in strength of parental burnout. Speaking to differences in parental burnout, parents of children with CCN reported higher PBA total scores and per dimension compared to parents of children without CCN, with moderate to large effect sizes. Regarding sociodemographic characteristics, the most consistent predictor across all models was group membership: being a parent of a child with CCN explained substantially more variance in the total PBA score than any sociodemographic characteristic. Furthermore, parental sex and the number of children were also significantly associated with parental burnout. However, only the association between parental sex and total PBA score was significantly moderated by being a parent of a child with CCN or not.

Specifically, being a mother of child with CCN was associated with substantially higher PBA scores compared to being a father of a child with CCN, this difference was smaller among parents of children without CCN.

While qualitative research has suggested that the lived experience of parental burnout may manifest itself differently in parents of children with CCN -often involving long-term stress from intense caregiving tasks such as, administrative burden, and coordination of social and health care (Abdoli et al., 2020; Patty et al., 2025) -our findings indicate that the core construct of parental burnout as operationalized by the subscales of the PBA has comparable meaning across these two care contexts. Still, it is plausible that the drivers of burnout differ in intensity or impact. This might not be surprising as the parenting context is always present, also for parents of children with CCN even if these parents engage in tasks beyond normative parenting. As such, our results support the use of the PBA in research when interested in burnout within the parenting domain.

Clear and substantial differences between mean scores of the PBA among parents of children with CCN and parents of children without CCN were found. Parents of children with CCN reported significantly higher total PBA scores and higher scores across all dimensions. This aligns with prior research demonstrating higher burnout scores among parents of children with CCN compared to parents without children with CCN (e.g., Lindahl Norberg et al., 2014; Lindström et al., 2010). The present study extends these findings by showing that such differences are also observed when parental burnout is assessed with the PBA, an instrument specifically developed to capture burnout within the parenting role and for which initial evidence of validity has been established. Together, our findings further support the validity of using the PBA for comparing burnout between parents of children with CCN and without. However, although the results of the invariance analyses allow the interpretation of mean scores across groups, some caution is warranted. First, the strict invariance model was not considered invariant and its model parameters were similar to the scalar results, indicating that changes from scalar into strict invariance might not have been applied by the software (Tse et al., 2024). Second, some studies argue that strict invariance is necessary in order to compare mean scores when using ordinal data (Tse et al., 2024). Our results indicated a good fit under scalar invariance meaning the four-factor structure of the PBA was consistent across groups, as were the factor loadings and item thresholds, which is consistent with several studies arguing that scalar invariance is sufficient when comparing mean scores among ordinal data (see e.g., Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Svetina et al., 2020).

As for the associations with socio-demographics, the most consistent predictor across all models was group membership: being a parent of a child with CCN explained substantially more variance in the total PBA score than any sociodemographic characteristic. This pattern mirrors previous findings suggesting that being a parent of a child with CCN is associated with parental burnout (e.g., Roskam & Mikolajczak, 2023). However, parental sex also showed an association with parental burnout. Mothers exhibited higher PBA scores than fathers, and this difference was significantly amplified among parents of children with CCN. The association of being a mother and thereby having a higher parental burnout score is in line with previous research across both populations (e.g., Hassanein et al., 2025; Lin & Szczygieł, 2022; Lindström et al., 2010). Mothers often assume disproportionate shares of both routine parenting and (specialized) caregiving compared to fathers. Furthermore, although the number of children was initially associated with higher burnout, this effect disappeared once being a parent of a child with CCN or not was considered, again highlighting the dominance of caregiving context above sociodemographic factors in explaining parental burnout.

Despite evidence from the general parent population indicating that having young children (<5 years) is associated with higher burnout related thoughts and feelings (Mikolajczak et al., 2018), our findings did not support this association, as minimum child age was not associated with parental burnout. An explanation, consistent with the broader parental burnout literature, is that parental burnout reflects the accumulation of ongoing demands over time rather than age-specific caregiving demands. From this perspective, burnout-related thoughts and feelings emerge when pressures persist without sufficient opportunities for recovery (Mikolajczak & Roskam, 2018), rendering minimum child age a less salient predictor of parental burnout. In line with this reasoning, we also hypothesized that maximum child age -particularly among parents of children with CCN -would be associated with higher burnout levels, a proxy reflecting the length of engagement in caregiving. This hypothesis was also not supported. One possible explanation is that maximum child age was derived from all children in the household rather than specifically the child with CCN, and that children older than 15 years were excluded due to privacy regulations in the Netherlands. Because caregiving and parenting responsibilities for children with CCN often extend into late adolescence and adulthood, future studies should include older children to more accurately capture the potential compound effect of caregiving duration. In addition, longitudinal designs are needed to examine how burnout develops and changes within parents over time, as such designs are better suited to capture cumulative and nonlinear processes than cross-sectional comparisons.

Limitations and Future Directions

First, although scalar invariance across groups was established, the sparse distribution of responses for most items necessitated collapsing response categories for 22 of the 23 items; this was required to ensure a sufficient number of responses per category for model estimation. Research in single group models has shown that in case of low cell frequencies, collapsing response categories introduces less bias in model estimates compared to treating the data as continuous (DiStefano et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2026). While this approach enabled model convergence and stable parameter estimates, it reduced the granularity of the original response scale. Notably, item thresholds of the two groups could not be compared across groups for the upper response categories of the scale. Therefore, category collapsing might have resulted in an overestimation of the level of invariance and may have blurred meaningful differences in the interpretation of the highest response categories for parents of children with CCN versus parents of children without CCN (Rutkowski et al., 2019). . Consequently, the invariance results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should examine whether larger samples allow for full-scale invariance testing without collapsing response categories. Second, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference regarding the effect of sociodemographic characteristics on parental burnout. Longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to understand social-ecological factors that might contribute to or reduce parental burnout. Third, both samples had overrepresentation by highly educated parents and the sample of parents of children with CCN predominantly consisted of mothers of Dutch descent. This limits the broader generalizability of our findings and highlights the need for recruitment approaches that facilitate greater participation of fathers and parents from more diverse educational and cultural backgrounds. Importantly sensitivity analysis restricted to mothers only yielded measurement invariance consistent with the findings of the primary sample including also fathers, suggesting that unequal gender composition did not account for the observed measurement invariance results. Nevertheless, further research into more genderbalanced and diverse samples is warranted. Fourth, recruitment procedures differed across samples: parents of children with CCN were recruited using materials that explicitly mentioned burnout, which may have created a self-selection bias by attracting parents who resonated with the term, potentially inflating burnout levels in this group. On the other hand, the study recruitment also mentioned resilience, which may have attracted parents with different experiences. Parents of children without CCN received financial compensation, which may have increased the risk of insufficient-effort responding. Although straight-liners were excluded to enhance data quality, we acknowledge that identical response patterns do not necessarily indicate careless responding and that some valid responses may therefore have been excluded. Future studies should employ more harmonized recruitment strategies that avoid burnout-related terminology and minimize financial incentives to reduce potential biases and enhance comparability across groups. Fifth, online participation in itself can be selective, with some groups more likely to participate than others (Hargittai & Karaoglu, 2018). Parents connected to organizations involved in recruitment or more inclined to participate in online research may therefore have been more likely to participate in this study Although the sample of parents without CCN was based on probability sampling, participation was also subject to nonresponse and study-specific sampling. Consequently, neither sample should be considered fully representative of its respective parent population. Considering the differences in recruitment and participation between the two samples, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that the recruitment and data-collection context contributed to differences between the samples (Schnell & Klingwort, 2024).

IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE, APPLICATION, THEORY, AND POLICY

Although the PBA was found to be invariant across parents of children with CCN and parents without children with CCN, a few considerations should be taken into account when applying the PBA in practice among parents of children with CCN. First, as the PBA assesses burnout within the parenting domain, it does not specifically describe burnout-related feelings with respect to caregiving. For parents of children with CCN, these two domains are considered intertwined (Patty, van Meeteren, Willemen, et al., 2024). Hence, the questions in the PBA might not fully capture the lived experiences of parental burnout among this group. Unique and nuanced experiences may remain which are only partially captured by the PBA (Abdoli et al., 2020; Patty, van Meeteren, Verdonk, et al., 2024).

Adjusted domains could potentially improve the assessment of parental burnout for parents of children with CCN, a question yet to be explored. Second, some of the granularity was lost as a result of collapsing the response categories. Taking these points into consideration, the PBA can be useful for policy and program evaluation. As for clinical practice, this study does not provide clinical evidence for applying the PBA in the clinical practice, such application would require further research, for example, how PBA scores translate to clinical symptoms.

Considering the significantly higher PBA scores among parents of children with CCN, this highlights the disproportionate burden experienced by parents of children with CCN and underscores the need for targeted support interventions and awareness for parental wellbeing among professionals (Rosenbaum, 2026). This is further supported by the finding that group membership was the strongest predictor for parental burnout as opposed to the other sociodemographic correlates. Together these findings underscore the heightened vulnerability to parental burnout among parents of children with CCN. Future research should further explore amendable contextual factors in relation to parental burnout in parents of children with CCN and without CCN.
