Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing Need a Better Definition of "Test" 2 ßAbstract Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing determine their scope by defining a "test". This definition is of practical consequence due to the significance of Standards for test evaluation, individual-level and policy decisions, and legal proceedings. I argue the current version lacks sufficient clarity. In light of the ongoing revision of the Standards, I consider the language of the current codex and its purpose, along with its predecessors, and propose a new definition of "test" that addresses the issues of the current definition -big and small -while being rooted in the contemporary approach to test validity. The new definition helps clarify how Standards regulate different kinds of psychological and educational testing, including the anticipated rise in AI-driven assessment.

[div-01]

One way to ensure that psychological testing proceeds de lege artis is to agree upon, codify and enforce a set of basic requirements directed at users, developers, publishers, and policy makers.

But even when optimally drafted, such codices may prove ineffectual when their scope is set up incorrectly. Too broad, it makes the requirements unfeasible to follow, thus eroding their authority. Too narrow, it lets improper instances of incompetent testing evade scrutiny.

This issue raises a question familiar to legal theory -correct application of legal concepts is obvious for some real-world cases, but difficult for others. To use Hart's ( 2012) classic example, one may wonder if a rule "no vehicles in the park" restricts roller-skates as well. Proper framing of scope, as described above, presents an alike problem. Therefore, we may use similar solutions. For instance, we can construct a coherent reading of the guidelines' text to develop a reasonable interpretation of their intended scope (e.g., Scalia & Garner, 2012, Ch. "The "Fair Reading" Method"). Or, just like when roller-skates might be let into the park if one can prove that the rule's purpose was to avoid high-speed vehicles, we may rely on the guidelines' purpose to serve as an interpretative aid.

In this paper, I argue that special attention needs to be given in the ongoing revision of the Standards for Psychological and Educational Testing (henceforth "the Standards") to the delineation of their scope. I begin by interpreting the scope of the current version of the Standards (AERA/APA/NCME, 2014; henceforth "Standards 2014") based on their plain text, stated purpose, and changes between preceding versions. Next, I critique the scope as currently defined and propose a revised version that may prove a better alternative.

Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing Need a Better Definition of "Test"

"Test" in Standards 2014

The core definition of the Standards' 2014 scope is based on the concept of "test" defined as: […] a device or procedure in which a sample of an examinee's behavior in a specified domain is obtained and subsequently evaluated and scored using a standardized process (p. 2).

"Test" is also distinguished from assessment: […] a broader term than test, commonly referring to a process that integrates test information with information from other sources. (ibid) This definition implies the following material characteristics of "tests": (1) They are devices or procedures designed to sample from a behavioral domain. (2) Their outcome is evaluation and scoring of this sample. (3) This process is standardized. "Assessment" is not "test" because, according to the Standards 2014, it lacks connection to a specific course of behavioral sampling -assessment relies on extra-test information, which is not sampled but used in full. The conclusion that assessments (described as something not anchored to behavioral samples) are not tests thus flows from the main definition.

Standards 2014 then mention additional clarifications. And for good reason. Read plainly, the main definition may have the Standards apply to school tests: math tests, for example, sample pupils' performance on tasks from a math knowledge domain, the outcome is scored and evaluated through grades, and everyone's test gets administered and graded the same way. Yet, this is an area typically seen as outside of the Standards' normative pressure, and indeed even the text carves an exception. As Standards 2014 add: the precise demarcation between measurement devices used in the fields of educational and psychological testing that do and do not fall within the purview of the Standards is difficult to identify. (p. 2) In this context, Standards 2014 mention that "rigorous application of the Standards" is "generally not possible" in "unstandardized employment assessments", "the broad range of unstructured behavior samples used in some Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing Need a Better Definition of "Test" forms of clinical and school-based psychological assessment" and "instructor-made tests that are used to evaluate student performance in education and training". They add that [i]t is useful to distinguish between devices that lay claim to the concepts and techniques of the field of educational and psychological testing and devices that represent unstandardized or less standardized aids to day-to-day evaluative decisions. (p. 3) Here, Standards 2014 describe an out-of-scope devices and procedures by emphasizing they are "aids" which are of "day-to-day" use.

There are several ways to interpret these additions vis-à-vis the main definition. First, they may simply constitute an interpretative guideline, as evident from the phrases "generally not possible" and "it is useful". If deliberate choices, they may be read as general statements designed to help the reader understand the main definition. On the other hand, they may also be seen as normative, intended to alter it. I would argue in favor of the second reading. Setting up a scope of a normative document and then immediately commenting on the inadequacy of the definition seems like a poor approach (see the surplusage canon in Scalia & Garner, 2012).

Similarly, I find it surprising the Standards would use a criterion referring to the procedure's or device's "laying claim" on educational and psychological concepts and techniques as a simple, non-binding advice. The Standards are obviously only applicable to "tests", however defined, at least somewhat associated with the field of educational and psychological testing. This characteristic is completely missing from the main definition, despite being crucial. Hence, I would conclude that at least this part of the text is intended as a part of the "test" definition.

Broader context of the Standards

Let us now consider how these two additions align with the Standards' broader context. I discuss three major sources: the Standards' history, the Standards 2014's preface (see the prefatorymaterials canon in Scalia & Garner, 2012) and validity theory.

History of the Standards' scope

American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, and National

Council on Measurement in Education (AERA/APA/NCME) have been releasing versions of Standards since 1954, and the definition of "test" has changed between the historical revisions of the Standards.

Older versions tended to rely on a broad description of a goal (diagnosis, practical decision, prognosis) followed by a (likely non-exhaustive) list of test types that fall under the Standards' scope. Standards 1954 and 1966 (AERA/APA/NCME, 1954 , 1966) define their scope as follows: these recommendations cover not only tests as narrowly defined, but also most published devices for diagnosis and evaluation. (p. 3) Test is not defined at all, not even in the alluded-to "narrow" sense, so Standards 1954/1966 seem to refer to a broadly shared representation of the term (issue I discuss further in the closing remarks). However, we do learn that a test's purpose is "diagnosis" or "evaluation", with "prognosis" added in Standards 1966, and that this implicit definition ought to be interpreted broadly. Based on this, it can perhaps be claimed that right from their inception, have the Standards been intended to apply to Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing Need a Better Definition of "Test" (psychological or educational) devices of some real-world consequence. Moreover, the publication status of the evaluated device seemed to be relevant to the determination of whether it falls under the scope (likely associated with their particular focus on test documentation; e.g., Standards 1954, p. 2).

Standards 1974 (AERA/APA/NCME, 1974, pp. 2-5) take a different angle as they are designed to apply to all assessment procedures, devices or aids, defined as "any systematic basis for making inferences about characteristics of people" (p. 2). Test is then defined as "a special case" of assessment: a set of tasks or questions intended to elicit particular types of behaviors when presented under standardized conditions and to yield scores that will have desirable psychometric properties such as high reliability and high validity (ibid). A non-exhaustive list of examples then follows. Interestingly, Standards 1974 apply to assessments as well as tests, but to the first, they apply in "varying degrees". Validity theory and the Standards

As shown above, the Standards have not been framed without considering validity theory (for historical summary see Kane and Bridgeman, 2021). Writing about validity in the Standards, Cizek (2020, Ch. 2), speaks of the "primacy of validity". Inquiring into Kane's (1992) approach to validity explains why Standards 2014 define validity as "the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses of tests" (p. 11). Briefly summarized, testing helps the user make a particular decision. This inference is warranted if the user can make a coherent, plausible argument that the test scores were correctly constructed, they can be appropriately generalized, they can be extrapolated to a non-test domain, they can be provided a theoretical interpretation and (even if all of the above holds) they can be used for a proposed decision (Kane, 1992).

How can acknowledging the close association between Kane's approach to validity, the issue of fairness and the Standards 2014 help us interpret their scope? If we accept Cizek's (2020, Ch. 2) claim that the Standards have, both today and historically, been driven by validity, perhaps the "test" definition ought to conform to this principle as well. In the current version, this consideration is either completely missing or integrated quite well. The answer depends on whether you see the additional clarifications as having normative value. Including "test score interpretation for a given purpose" and "test consequences" into the explicit definition thus appears consistent with the Standards' intent.

Purpose of the Standards

The stated purpose of Standards 2014 is quite neutral: to provide criteria for the development and evaluation of tests and testing practices and to provide guidelines for assessing Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing Need a Better Definition of "Test" 9 the validity of interpretations of test scores for the intended test uses. (Standards 2014, p. 1) But the prior sentences are more revealing. The goal of the Standards 2014 is to "promote sound testing practices and to provide a basis for evaluating the quality of those practices" because tests, properly used, can effect "better decisions about individuals and programs than would result without their use and can also provide a route to broader and more equitable access to education and employment". While "improper use of tests […] can cause considerable harm to test takers and other parties affected by test-based decisions". I would read this statement as emphasizing the value of "making better decisions", "preventing harm", and "inclusion and equity". In effect, our definition of "test" should, perhaps, closely align with these values.

Of course, it is no surprise that the Standards would be framed with these values in mind, especially the latter two instances. Anyone worth their psychometrics salt is painfully aware of the terrible role psychological and education testing has played in the discredited policies of the West (e.g., Zwick, 2021). Fairness first appeared in Standards 1974 alongside a more explicit rebuke of specific instances of discriminatory testing practices (Sireci & Randall, 2021).

Therefore, these maxims can perhaps be useful for interpreting and setting up the Standards' scope as well. For instance, we may ask if the extant definition supports pursuing these values: does it avoid creating loopholes through which harmful practices can escape evaluation? Test is (a) any device or procedure in which a sample of observations pertaining to a content domain is generated and (b) subsequently evaluated using a standardized process (c) for the purpose of making consequential decisions for an individual or for public policy, and (d) this evaluation has the potential to make a reasonable user believe that it presents evidence important for making such decision (e) by laying claims on concepts from psychological or educational theory or claiming that psychometric, machine-learning or similar techniques were used in development.

The first part (a) is left virtually unchanged from Standards 1999/2014, containing a "sampling domain criterion". It alludes to the sampling domain theory as a framework of sufficient generality. Unlike the preceding versions of the Standards, this approach lets the framers avoid using a list of typical test types; instead, focusing on their common material characteristic as a behavior sampler.

Similarly, part (b) overlaps with Standards 1999/2014 by defining test as a standardized procedure, creating a "standardization criterion". Arguably, this criterion serves to distinguish between (standardized) testing and (unstandardized) assessment. However, it surely does not mean to imply that foregoing the standardization process makes a device or procedure fall Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing Need a Better Definition of "Test" 11 outside of the Standards. Otherwise, no procedure could ever violate the Standards as it could be claimed that lack of quality follows from imperfect standardization, hence the procedure is out of the Standards' scope. Rather, standardization refers to test use which is broadly designed as a process in which test users follow a set of instruction that turn raw observations into scores and, subsequently, their interpretation for a given purpose.

Furthermore, the new phrasing would remove one possible source of confusion. The Standards 1999/2014 definition speaks of "scoring and evaluation". Does a procedure stop being a test because the behavior samples are only evaluated, not scored? What if they are only scored and never evaluated? Brown's (1975, p. 9-10) commentary on Standards 1974 speaks of a "descriptive" use of test, such as when tests are administered within a continuous relationship with a non-specific future use in mind. Nevertheless, Brown concludes the Standards apply in these cases as well. Since scores always need to be evaluated in some way to be useful for making decisions (and if not, they are of no consequence), I suggest getting rid of the scoringevaluating doublet and keeping "evaluation" only.

Part (c) contains a "purpose criterion" designed to make the purpose of the Standards explicit in the definition of "test". In several places, Standards 1999/2014 emphasize that it is the consequences of psychological and educational testing that motivated their framing. In other places, on the other hand, they place "day-to-day decisions" (p. 2) out of their scope. Therefore, consequences appear to implicitly determine the Standards' scope. The change is not without its limits, though, as it relies on another vague concept: the "consequential" qualifier.

Part (d) contains an "evidence impression criterion", an entirely new addition based on two ideas. First, it uses the "reasonable person" standard that is typical in the law of various jurisdictions. The standard is used to evaluate whether the test documentation presents itself (in Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing Need a Better Definition of "Test" its documentation or advertising) as if it is a psychological or educational test. Arguably, this approach is useful to target non-scientific methods that nevertheless impress the users into thinking they have scientific value. In other words: if the test claims authority, it needs to back it up with evidence outlined in the Standards.

The second idea of part (d) is the concept of "evidence". Inspired by Bayesian reasoning, I propose to focus on the test user's subjective belief regarding the evidence needed to warrant a test interpretation. For instance, a test may be designed to support binary classifications. An assumption underlying the use of such a test is that it can meaningfully shift the pre-test odds of the target characteristic's presence in a client. Formally, one could describe such an effect using likelihood ratios derived from sensitivity and specificity (). Informally, a test documentation or advertising may allude to "high accuracy" and so on. Under the proposed definition, both descriptions create an impression that the test's classification is a meaningful piece of evidence for the intended interpretation. Therefore, the Standards would apply to both, and the informally advertised test too would have to prove its classification utility.

Part (e) introduces a "theoretical-technical claim criterion" which follows a formulation from Standards 1999/2014: devices that lay claim to the concepts and techniques of the field of educational and psychological testing (p. 2). Because Standards 1999/2014 mention this criterion when demarcating between "tests" and "non-tests", I assume that claiming educational and psychological concepts or techniques is crucial for the Standards' scope. Therefore, it ought to be integrated into the explicit definition. Note that the theoretical-technical claim (e) must be connected to the evidence impression (d). Thus, simply discussing psychological theory would not activate this clause, unless it is connected to eliciting the impression of the test's evidentiary value. One could point out that viewing the Standards as a quasi-legal document is misguided: they were never intended to be interpreted using legal techniques, and there are several places in their text that support such perspective (e.g., on p. 7, the Standards 1999/2014 reject being interpreted as a checklist and provide other limiting statements), although there are others that counter it, such as the APA Guidelines for Psychological Assessment and Evaluation that describe the Standards as mandatory and enforceable (APA, 2020). Yet I do not believe that subscribing to this position changes much. The issue of scope is still there. The unclear definition -still there. The need to apply the Standards in a coherent and informed way? Still there. The only material consequence would be that quasi-legalist arguments are given less weight than this paper assigns them. That is fair, and I would find it beneficial if our community explicated what kind of reasoning it finds convincing when interpreting the Standards. The legalistic way is, indeed, merely a suggestion grounded in a discipline that has been grappling with similar issues for centuries. Similar -not identical.

Another possible criticism could turn my own framing against my argument. The opening reference to Hart's (2012) work implies that language makes it difficult to come up with perfect Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing Need a Better Definition of "Test" 14 definitions of normative concepts. Hart even writes that a margin of uncertainty should be tolerated, and indeed welcomed in the case of many legal rules, so that an informed judicial decision can be made when the composition of an unforeseen case is known and the issues at stake in its decision can be identified and so rationally settled. (pp. 286-287). Perhaps a similar insight is behind the difference between Standards 1974's and Standards 1985's way of defining their scope. Maybe this is a wise move -but I would like to see it defended against alternatives, such as the more elaborate definition proposed here, or the one used in Standards 1974.

Finally, one can be comfortable with the proposed approach but not with the new definition. I welcome any improvement. One way is to use the methods I show above and arrive to different, sounder conclusions. Another way is to take an empirical approach. My lawyer colleagues and I are exploring ways for empirically mapping the contents and structures of vague legal concepts, like judicial dignity and public order, using the social representations approach (Smejkalová, 2024; for specific examples Palíšek et al., 2025 , Smejkalová et al., 2024). Social representations fit this topic. A vague legal concept can still be a useful regulative tool because the community may implicitly agree on what situations fall under its scope and which do not.

Similarly, what "test" is, or rather, to what kind of human activities the Standards ought to apply, is perhaps represented the same way. Test developers, users and publishers know these devices "when they see them", even if unable to properly define them. It may be illuminating to conduct an empirical investigation into the social representation of "test" (as it pertains to the Standards' scope), and have these findings inform the new definition.
