Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Marginal Significance works

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Full text

Abstract

Metacognition is commonly defined as the monitoring and control of one's own cognition, yet the term covers related phenomena that differ in their theoretical organisation, assessment, and application. This historically oriented narrative review examines how the convergence of cognitive, developmental, and educational research traditions produced this plurality. It reconstructs the construct's empirical and theoretical antecedents in memory monitoring, children's knowledge of cognition, and strategic regulation of learning, and compares influential accounts in terms of their explanatory aims, central components, and levels of analysis. The comparison shows that different traditions came to emphasise metacognitive knowledge, experiences, monitoring, regulatory activity, or the functional relation between object-and metalevel cognition. These differences are interpreted not as competing definitions of an identical phenomenon, but as reflecting the distinct problems and methods from which each account developed. The review argues that metacognition is best approached as a historically assembled family of related but partially non-interchangeable facets, without assuming either complete unity or fragmentation. This interpretation clarifies why indicators derived from different traditions often diverge, and why specifying the facet, function, and level of analysis intended by the term should be a condition for cumulative research across theory, measurement, and application.

Introduction

Metacognition refers to the human capacity to monitor and control one's own cognitive processes (Nelson & Narens, 1990). It is involved in a wide range of everyday activities, including evaluating whether we have understood a text (Yang et al., 2023), acting and communicating with others (Heyes et al., 2020), pursuing short-and long-term goals (Scott & Gilbert, 2024), and making decisions (Basu & Dixit, 2022). Metacognition has consequently become a central construct in cognitive, developmental, and educational psychology (Norman et al., 2019). Yet this apparently straightforward definition conceals a conceptually plural field. Researchers use the term metacognition to refer to partially overlapping phenomena, including knowledge about cognition, the monitoring of cognitive activity, feelings of knowing, confidence judgements, learning strategies, planning, and regulatory control (Dunlosky & Metcalfe, 2009). Although related, these phenomena do not necessarily involve the same processes, operate at the same level of analysis, or serve the same explanatory purposes. This plurality is not merely a product of inconsistent contemporary terminology. It also reflects the historical convergence of distinct research programmes that originally addressed different psychological questions (Brown et al., 1983). Experimental research in cognitive psychology examined how individuals assess the accessibility and accuracy of their memory processes (Hart, 1965; Nelson, 1984). Developmental researchers investigated children's growing understanding of language and memory, and their use of strategies to regulate learning (Flavell et al., 1966 (Flavell et al., , 1970;; Gleitman et al., 1972). In educational psychology, researchers focused on how metacognitive knowledge and regulatory strategies could be elicited, observed, and taught in meaningful activities such as reading comprehension and problem solving in educational contexts (Baker & Brown, 1984; Brown, 1978; Palincsar & Brown, 1984). Although these programmes eventually came to share the label metacognition, they originated in different empirical problems and developed partly distinct concepts, methods, and explanatory aims.

These different origins shaped how the construct was theoretically organised. Some accounts focused on the knowledge and conscious experiences involved in monitoring one's own cognition (Flavell, 1979); others emphasised the executive processes through which cognitive activity is planned, monitored, and regulated (Brown et al., 1983); still others proposed functional architectures that link object-level cognitive activity to meta-level representations (Nelson & Narens, 1990). Later frameworks sought to integrate these perspectives by distinguishing relatively stable metacognitive knowledge, task-related metacognitive experiences, and regulatory metacognitive skills, while specifying how these facets interact across levels of functioning (Efklides, 2008). These approaches therefore represent more than alternative models of an otherwise identical phenomenon: they retain different assumptions about what metacognition comprises, how its components relate to one another, and what a theory of metacognition is intended to explain.

Contemporary reviews have documented the breadth of metacognition and the diversity of definitions, models, and methods used to study it (e.g., Azevedo, 2020; Fleur et al., 2021; Norman et al., 2019). However, these accounts are typically organised around present-day conceptual distinctions, measurement approaches, or applications. Comparatively less attention has been paid to reconstructing how the construct's early research traditions shaped the later development of its theoretical models. A historically informed account can therefore clarify not only when particular concepts and models emerged, but also why different traditions came to emphasise particular components, levels of analysis, and explanatory questions. This article presents a historically oriented narrative review of the origins and theoretical traditions that shaped metacognition into a plural construct. It has three aims: (i) to identify the principal empirical and theoretical antecedents of the construct; (ii) to compare influential accounts developed within cognitive, developmental, and educational research traditions; and (iii) to examine how differences in those traditions' historical origins continue to shape what is understood as metacognition. The first section reconstructs the lines of research that preceded the formal introduction of the construct, from early studies of memory monitoring and strategic behaviour to the emergence of the concepts of metamemory and metacognition. The second section examines influential theoretical models and compares how each defines, organises, and explains metacognitive activity, and shows that each model's emphasis is traceable to the empirical problems its parent tradition had made observable. The final section draws out the implications of this historically grounded plurality: it argues that the divergence among contemporary indicators and the contested boundaries of the construct are consequences of the traditions from which models and instruments descend, and that specifying the facet, function, and level of analysis intended by the term is a condition for cumulative research across theory, measurement, and application.

Historical antecedents of metacognition

The study of metacognition, also known as "cognition about cognition" (Flavell, 1985, p. 104), has deep roots in both philosophy and psychology (Lyons, 1986). The idea that the mind can "turn back" upon its own operations has a long philosophical history, appearing in reflections on introspection and consciousness long before the emergence of scientific psychology (Schwitzgebel, 2024). Its psychological antecedents can be traced to authors such as William James (1890/2016), John Dewey (1910 Dewey ( /2009)), Edward Thorndike (1913 ), and Charles Spearman (1927 /2022), yet the formal investigation of this idea only began during the so-called cognitive renaissance of the late 1950s and early 1960s (Bruner et al., 1956; Miller et al., 1960). After behaviourism, characterised by the predominance of stimulus-response explanations, cognitivism emerged, placing the mind and cognitive processes once again at the centre of psychology (Miller, 2003). Despite its uneven impact in both American and European psychology (Brock, 2013), this movement provided the conditions for psychologists on both sides of the Atlantic to ask how individuals know about their own mental activity: how they monitor it, how much they know about it, and how they use that knowledge (Baker & Brown, 1984; Lyons, 1986). Within this context, metacognition came to be understood as the monitoring and control of one's own mental processes (Flavell, 1979).

One of the earliest empirical attempts to study how individuals monitor their own cognition was conducted by Joseph Hart (1965) in his study of feeling-of-knowing experiences, understood as an individual's belief that, despite being unable to retrieve certain information at a given moment, they would be able to recognise it later. Hart (1965) set out to evaluate whether these beliefs are precise indicators of the real contents of a person's memory, seeking evidence of a self-monitoring mechanism over it. To this end, he applied a single-answer general information questionnaire to a group of individuals. If they did not know a correct answer, they were instructed to judge whether they could identify it from a list of options. Finally, to evaluate the accuracy of these judgements, the participants had to answer a second questionnaire with the same questions they could not initially answer, but this time with multiple-choice questions. The results showed that performance on the multiple-choice questionnaire was better when participants had prospectively indicated that they believed they could identify the correct answer from a list of options than when they thought they could not. These findings suggested that people could monitor the accessibility of information in their own memory, even when they could not retrieve it.

These findings, on what Hart (1967) termed the memory monitoring process, constituted a turning point in memory research and were even considered by some as the future of the discipline (Tulving & Madigan, 1970). In the following years, researchers extended the experimental study of related phenomena involving memory monitoring and learning. These included tip-of-thetongue states, first examined experimentally by Brown and McNeill (1966), though originally described by James (1890/2016) and later discussed by Woodworth (1934); ease-of-learning judgements, defined as the ability to judge how easy or difficult a particular material will be to learn (Underwood, 1966); and judgements of learning, defined as the ability to prospectively evaluate the likelihood that studied information will be remembered later (Arbuckle & Cuddy, 1969). Collectively, these studies pointed in the same direction as Hart's (1965) findings: individuals can monitor their own cognition, and, although this ability is far from perfect, their judgements are systematically related to subsequent memory performance. As Hart himself observed, "when people feel that they know something, it is very likely that they do know it, and when they feel that they do not, it is likely that they do not" (1967, p. 196).

A further step was taken by Juola et al. (1971), whose findings raised questions about the processes underlying memory monitoring. In a study on recognition memory, they observed that when a word feels unfamiliar, participants can correctly reject it as "new" almost instantly. These rapid negative responses were significantly faster than what would be expected if participants had to mentally search through an entire memorised list of words. Later work by Kolers and Palef (1976) provided further evidence that memory monitoring may operate independently of an exhaustive search process. These results provided a basis for distinguishing between the retrieval of stored information and the processes through which individuals assess its accessibility.

In parallel with cognitive research on memory monitoring in adults, developmental research focused on children's use of strategies to regulate learning and memory performance (Daehler et al., 1969; Hagen et al., 1970). Earlier developmental theories had already addressed processes that would later become central to metacognition. Piaget examined how children progressively move beyond egocentric thought to coordinate perspectives, reflect on rules, and develop increasingly organised forms of reasoning (Piaget, 1926 (Piaget, /1959)), whereas Vygotsky described how externally mediated verbal regulation could become self-directed speech and, eventually, inner speech (Vygotsky, 1934 (Vygotsky, /2020)).

One of the researchers who stood out in this period was John H. Flavell, whose earlier work had helped introduce Piagetian developmental theory to anglophone psychology. Situated within developmental debates shaped by the work of Piaget and Vygotsky on self-regulation in children, Flavell et al. (1966) studied children's use of spontaneous verbal rehearsal through a memorybased experimental paradigm. They found that second and fifth grade children not only performed better than kindergarteners but also presented more spontaneous verbal production during the task. These verbalisations were far from accidental: children used them to improve their ability to remember the pictured items, thereby regulating their performance. Moreover, children who engaged in verbal rehearsal were not only aware of their strategy use but also of its specific purpose.

Subsequent studies continued to explore the use of verbalisation as a strategy for enhancing children's performance on memorisation tasks (e.g., Hagen & Kingsley, 1968; Keeney et al., 1967; Kingsley & Hagen, 1969). Flavell et al. (1970) identified a relationship between the use of self-regulation strategies and memory monitoring. In a study with 84 children from kindergarten to fourth grade, they demonstrated not only that older children were capable of using relatively complex memorisation strategies but also that, compared to younger children, they were better at predicting their own memory capacity and judging whether they had memorised a list of words well enough for later recall. These findings suggested that successful memorisation -in this case, of a word list-was predicted by the use of more effective learning strategies. This strategic competence, in turn, was closely related to the knowledge and awareness that children had regarding how their own memory worked or, as Tulving and Madigan described it a few months earlier, "knowledge of their own knowledge" (1970, p. 477). Thus, Flavell et al. (1970) provided early empirical evidence that cognitive monitoring and self-regulatory learning strategies are fundamentally related.

The consolidation of research on cognitive monitoring and self-regulation strategies found a point of convergence at the 1971 symposium of the Society for Research in Child Development, where Flavell first coined the concept of metamemory. This construct, defined as the different storage and retrieval strategies an individual uses alongside the intelligent monitoring and knowledge about those strategies (Flavell, 1971), provided a bridge towards the formal study of metacognitive processes in psychology (Dunlosky & Tauber, 2016; Ornstein & Haden, 2001; Rhodes, 2019).

Metamemory synthesised previous work and, for the first time, provided the field of study of metacognition with a distinct name and identity (Dunlosky & Tauber, 2016).

Although the literature emphasises the relationship between metacognition and metamemory, parallel lines of research also emerged in psychology, education, and linguistics and played an important role in shaping the emergence of this field (Mayor et al., 1995). These lines examined how people know about and monitor other cognitive processes and how such abilities develop in childhood. As noted by Brown (1978) and Flavell (1976), the intellectual climate of the 1960s and 1970s fostered the proliferation of many "metas", that is, neologisms using the prefix meta (from the Greek μετά, "after") to "elevate the original meaning of such a word to a higher, more abstract philosophical level" (Thomas, 1984, p. 16). This development laid the foundations for related constructs such as meta-learning, meta-attention, meta-comprehension, and metalinguistics (Brown, 1978).

In 1972, Gleitman and colleagues captured this zeitgeist and summarised it in a single word: "metacognition". While studying the emergence of grammatical reasoning in children -what we call today metalinguistic knowledge or awareness-, Gleitman et al. (1972) coined the concept of metacognition and referred for the first time to "meta-cognitive" processes to describe a series of higher-order psychological processes that have as their object other cognitive processes, such as memory, "as if the homunculus perceived the operations of a lower-order system" (p. 161). They drew explicit parallels between how we can reflect "meta-linguistically" and similar processes in memory such as recollection, intentional learning, and the "tip-of-the-tongue" phenomenon. In their view, metacognition marked the capacity not only to use cognitive functions like memory or language but also to recognise and reflect upon their operation. They further noted developmental evidence suggesting that such skills typically emerge close to the five-to-seven-year age range, a period consistent with broader advances in children's reasoning abilities (Ginsburg & Opper, 1969; Yendovitskaya, 1971). Although this early use of the term has received considerably less attention than Flavell's later formulation, it was the first to conceptualise metacognition as a phenomenon extending beyond memory monitoring and into multiple cognitive domains.

The term acquired wider recognition a few years later, when John Flavell provided its first widely recognised definition. In a brief chapter entitled Metacognitive aspects of problem solving, Flavell (1976) highlighted the relevance of metacognition for intelligent behaviour and problem-solving.

In this chapter, Flavell defined it as "one's own knowledge concerning one's own cognitive processes and products or anything related to them, e.g., the learning-relevant properties of information or data" (p. 232), and added that "metacognition refers, among other things, to the active monitoring and consequent regulation and orchestration of these processes in relation to the cognitive objects or data on which they bear" (p. 232). According to the author, metacognition functions as a source of regulatory control that allows the individual not only to determine how, where, and when to use available regulatory strategies but also to be aware of those strategies themselves.

Flavell's definition brought together two functions that had become increasingly prominent across these lines of research: knowledge and monitoring of cognition, on the one hand, and the active regulation of cognitive processes, on the other. This formulation established a central distinction for the literature, still relevant today, where metacognition involves both representations or judgements about cognition and processes through which cognitive activity is monitored and regulated (Boldt & Gilbert, 2022; Craig et al., 2020; Fleur et al., 2021; Tarricone, 2011; Veenman et al., 2006; Winne & Azevedo, 2014).

Despite the increasing use of the term throughout the 1970s and 1980s, the emerging literature did not converge on a single definition, nor did it establish clear boundaries around it (Brown et al., 1983). The rapid adoption of the construct across multiple disciplines intensified this complexity, fostering a proliferation of terms, components, and proposed subprocesses whose boundaries were often difficult to delineate (Baker & Brown, 1984). Classic commentaries captured the situation vividly, describing metacognition as a "many-headed monster" (Brown et al., 1983, p. 137), as "vague and imprecise" (Weinert, 1987, p. 8) or an inherently "fuzzy concept" (Flavell, 1981).

Commentators at the time echoed similar concerns, noting the lack of shared definitions between authors (Reynolds & Wade, 1986) and the surprising absence of precise conceptualisations even in the midst of growing empirical interest (Jacobs & Paris, 1987). By the mid-1980s, authors such as Wellman (1983), Kitchener (1983), and Schoenfeld (1987) characterised metacognition as a family of related but ill-defined processes, connected by loose similarities rather than by a common theoretical core. These longstanding tensions laid the foundation for the different theoretical models that emerged in the literature, each shaped by the goals and assumptions of its respective research tradition.

Theoretical models of metacognition

The theoretical models that followed organised the emerging construct in different ways, reflecting the empirical inquiries of their respective research traditions. One of the first systematic attempts to organise the construct of metacognition can be found in Flavell and Wellman's (1977) work on metamemory. In that paper, the authors proposed that children gradually acquired knowledge about their own memory processes along two dimensions: (i) sensitivity to situations in which deliberate memory-related efforts are needed, and (ii) knowledge about the factors that influence memory performance, namely, person, task, and strategy variables, as well as their interactions. Critically, they distinguished two functionally distinct forms of metamemory: a general, previously acquired knowledge about the self and others as mnemonic agents, and a more immediate, situational ability to "monitor and interpret concrete experiences in the here and now" (Flavell & Wellman, 1977, p. 18).

Following this work, Flavell (1979) expanded the scope of metamemory into a broader theory of metacognition. In his seminal work, he described four classes of phenomena through whose actions and interactions cognitive monitoring occurs (see Figure 1A): metacognitive knowledge, metacognitive experiences, goals (or tasks), and actions (or strategies). Metacognitive knowledge refers to what individuals know and believe about themselves and others as cognitive agents, about the different types of tasks they perform, and about the strategies they use for learning or remembering -categories that echo the person, task, and strategy variables in his earlier work with Wellman. Metacognitive experiences, in turn, correspond to the conscious cognitive and affective events that precede, accompany, or follow any cognitive enterprise. Finally, goals and actions refer respectively to the objectives of such enterprises and to the cognitions or behaviours used to achieve them. According to this model, cognitive monitoring occurs through the continuous interactions among these four phenomena: metacognitive knowledge provides the basis for interpreting one's cognitive activity, metacognitive experiences provide conscious information about its course and outcomes, and goals and strategies guide its direction and adjustment.

Although Flavell (1979) did not describe regulation as a distinct component, his model clearly implied it: both metacognitive knowledge and experiences were conceived as mechanisms through which the individual can select, activate, evaluate, revise, and abandon goals and strategies in order to accomplish a cognitive enterprise. Alongside Flavell's formulations, Ann L. Brown played a pivotal role in consolidating the study of metacognition and bringing it closer to educational contexts. Brown, however, placed greater emphasis on the executive and regulatory functions of metacognitive activity. As Reder (1996) points out, the processes she labelled as metacognitive skills (Brown, 1978; Brown et al., 1983), such as planning, monitoring and evaluating, had already been discussed in earlier theories of executive control and processing. Brown's main contribution was to recast these processes as metacognitive skills, while framing their development as a transition from socially supported other-regulation to conscious self-regulation in learning and problem-solving (Brown, 1978; Reeve & Brown, 1985). Later, Brown et al. (1983) articulated this view by distinguishing two closely related components: knowledge about cognition, i.e., the declarative information that individuals have about their own cognitive processes and those of others, and regulation of cognition, i.e., the cluster of activities used to control and oversee those processes. Brown further noted that knowledge about cognition tends to be relatively stable and verbally accessible, whereas regulation is more situation-dependent and not necessarily available to conscious report (Brown, 1987; Brown et al., 1983).

Subsequent models in education elaborated on this distinction by identifying specific subcomponents within each domain. For instance, Paris et al. (1983) differentiated between declarative, procedural, and conditional knowledge in the first domain, a distinction subsequently incorporated into strategy instruction by Paris et al. (1984). Schraw and Dennison (1994), in turn, operationalised knowledge and regulation within the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory, incorporating subcomponents such as planning, information management, monitoring, debugging, and evaluation under the second domain (see Figure 1B). Together, both models emerged from the need to operationalise metacognition, translating Brown's theoretical distinction into a set of teachable and measurable skills and providing frameworks for applied research and instruction in educational settings.

While these educational approaches to metacognition focused on identifying, taxonomising, and operationalising the components of metacognition, cognitive psychologists sought to understand the underlying architecture that makes these processes possible (Kuhn & Dean, 2004; Son, 2007).

Within this cognitive-experimental tradition, researchers examined judgements of learning, feelings of knowing, and confidence estimates as windows into the mechanisms through which individuals monitor and regulate their own cognition (e.g., Metcalfe & Wiebe, 1987; Nelson, 1984). It is within this tradition that the model of Nelson and Narens (1990) emerged. Rather than specifying subcomponents, their model (see Figure 1C) conceptualised metacognition as a dynamic system arising from the interaction between two levels of cognition: an object-level, where primary cognitive activity takes place, and a meta-level, which contains a dynamic representation of that activity. The functions of metacognition manifest through communication between these two levels: monitoring involves an upward flow of information from the objectlevel that updates the meta-level's representation, whereas control (or regulation) involves a downward flow of information through which the meta-level modifies the state or processes of the object-level. Within her framework, metacognitive knowledge is described as a relatively stable system of beliefs about oneself, tasks, and strategies, grounded in offline monitoring and past experiences (Efklides, 2008). In contrast, metacognitive experiences correspond to the online, affect-and cognitive-based signals -such as feelings of difficulty, knowing, or confidence-that arise during task engagement and provide feedback about the course of cognitive processing (Efklides, 2001 (Efklides, , 2008)). Finally, metacognitive skills refer to the deliberate control processes involved in planning, monitoring, and evaluating one's actions (Efklides, 2006). Efklides (2008) further organised these facets within a multilevel structure that distinguishes a nonconscious object level, comprising cognition and emotion, as well as their respective regulatory systems, and a meta level at which explicit awareness, monitoring, and regulatory decisions take place. At this level, the three facets of metacognition operate in coordination, with metacognitive knowledge and experiences informing the deployment of metacognitive skills to guide regulation. Efklides also acknowledged a broader social level of functioning, involving the monitoring and regulation of others' cognition in interactive contexts. Although this framework offers a coherent account of how metacognitive components interact during learning, it also illustrates how different theoretical traditions emphasise distinct facets and levels of analysis within the broader construct of metacognition.

Brown (1978; Brown et al., 1983) asked how cognitive activity is planned, monitored, and regulated, and framed metacognition as a set of executive-like skills that develop from socially supported other-regulation to self-regulation; the explanatory target is the regulatory organisation of activity. Later integrative accounts (Dunlosky & Metcalfe, 2009; Efklides, 2008; Pintrich et al., 2000) drew on all of these, but each retained a distinctive emphasis traceable to the tradition it synthesised. These models therefore overlap in their subject matter without being interchangeable: they are emphases, not exclusive territories, and the history of the field is not a developmental sequence culminating in one superior model (Norman et al., 2019; Veenman et al., 2006). This comparison also clarifies why the differences among the models are not accidental. Each model was developed to answer an empirical question that its parent tradition had already made tractable: memory-monitoring experiments made confidence and feeling-of-knowing judgements measurable; developmental strategy research made the emergence of knowledge about cognition observable; classroom pedagogy and assessment made strategic regulation observable in authentic tasks; and functional-architecture research made the monitoring-control relation formalisable. A model's emphasis is therefore not a free theoretical choice but is conditioned by the empirical problem it was built to solve. The consequence of this history is that the boundaries of what counts as metacognition are still drawn differently depending on which tradition a researcher is working within. The same behaviour (a student pausing to reconsider a strategy, a participant reporting confidence in a memory judgement, a child explaining why a strategy worked) is classified as metacognitive knowledge, as monitoring, as regulation, or as strategy use depending on the framework brought to bear (Dörrenbächer-Ulrich & Bregulla, 2024; Veenman et al., 2006). Because those classifications are theory-laden rather than self-evident, they shape what counts as evidence, what counts as a metacognitive failure, and what an intervention is meant to change. Whether this plurality should be read as a defect of the construct or as a condition of its productivity, and what follows from it for theory, measurement, and application, is the question taken up in the final section.

Discussion and conclusions

The three aims of this review point to the same conclusion. The first section showed that metacognition did not emerge from a single research programme but from several, each of which was addressing a different empirical problem (Brown et al., 1983). The second section showed that each model built the construct around the phenomena its own tradition was able to observe. Different facets became central, in other words, for empirical reasons before they became matters of definition. The third aim follows from the first two. Because these emphases survive in current models and instruments, they still shape not only how metacognition is labelled but what counts as evidence of it. Metacognition is therefore best understood as a historically assembled family of related but partially non-interchangeable facets, rather than as a single, settled construct (Dunlosky & Metcalfe, 2009; Norman et al., 2019). This does not mean that the facets are independent, or that common mechanisms cannot be found. Task-based metacognition shows both shared and domain-specific components, which warns against replacing the assumption of complete unity with an equally sweeping assumption of fragmentation (Lehmann et al., 2022). Nor does plurality make the construct useless. Its value as an umbrella term lies in connecting questions about cognition as something that can be known, experienced, monitored, and controlled, as long as the intended meaning is made explicit (Norman et al., 2019).

The historical account also helps to make sense of a familiar methodological problem. Self-report inventories, verbal protocols, and task-based indicators of metacognition often correlate weakly or inconsistently with one another (Craig et al., 2020; Terneusen et al., 2024; Zepeda & Nokes-Malach, 2023), and their associations with academic achievement depend on which measure is used (Dent & Koenka, 2016; Ohtani & Hisasaka, 2018). Seen historically, this is not surprising.

Inventories such as the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory descend from Brown's distinction between knowledge and regulation of cognition; they were designed to make teachable skills reportable. Confidence-based indices descend from the memory-monitoring experiments of Hart and Nelson; they were designed to quantify how accurately people judge their own performance. Each kind of instrument inherits its lineage's assumptions about what metacognition is and how it can be accessed. Brown et al. (1983) themselves noted that regulation depends on the situation and is not always open to conscious report. Consistent with this, Rovers et al. (2019) found that selfreports capture general self-regulation but correspond less closely to behavioural indicators of specific strategies, and Barrientos et al. (2025) found that different metacognitive indicators were differently related to associative learning and academic performance. This argument does not, however, exempt instruments from validity testing. Double (2025) found that self-reported monitoring ability was negatively associated with several online indices of monitoring, and that higher self-ratings went together with greater confidence and poorer performance. Findings of this kind call into question whether such scores measure monitoring at all; they are not simply another example of legitimate differences between facets. The appropriate conclusion is therefore conditional. Divergence between measures does not automatically invalidate one of them, nor does it establish that they measure distinct constructs. What it requires is scrutiny of what each measure was built to capture and of its methodological properties (Craig et al., 2020).

Conceptually, the same history explains why specifying the facet, function, and level of analysis one has in mind is not a terminological nicety but a condition for interpreting findings across traditions. Dinsmore et al. (2008) found that definitions of metacognition, self-regulation, and selfregulated learning overlap considerably, even though the three constructs differ in how they are measured and in the contexts in which they are studied. A more recent systematic review reached a similar conclusion for self-regulated learning and executive functions: the two are only weakly to moderately associated, the strength of the association depends on how each is measured, and metacognition may mediate the relation between them (Dörrenbächer-Ulrich & Bregulla, 2024).

Integrative accounts can make these relations explicit without settling the construct's boundaries. Efklides and Schwartz (2024), revisiting the Metacognitive and Affective model of Self-Regulated Learning (MASRL), distinguish person-level characteristics from task-specific functioning. This offers a way to study relations across levels; it does not provide grounds for equating metacognition with the wider system in which it participates. Strategy use or executive control should therefore not be taken as evidence of metacognition in general unless that inference is justified. The same requirement applies to applied research, where education has been the main testing ground. Janssen and Lazonder's (2024) meta-analysis illustrates the specificity required: its evidence concerns absolute monitoring accuracy in problem solving, and the effects depended on the type of intervention. An improvement in one facet should not be reported as an improvement in metacognition as such. Likewise, evaluations should distinguish evidence that the targeted facet changed from evidence that learning improved and should examine the link between the two rather than infer one from the other. These conclusions have limits. As a narrative comparison of selected traditions, the review cannot determine the empirical structure of metacognition or settle every proposed relation among its facets. What it offers is a historical account of why those relations are contested. The reconstruction is also selective in a second sense. It follows the Anglo-American lines of research from which the dominant models descend and pays less attention to traditions that developed alongside or after them. These include the German research on metacognition and motivation represented in the Weinert and Kluwe (1987) volume; the later development of the memorymonitoring lineage into a signal-detection and neuroscientific research programme on confidence (Fleming, 2024), whose current methods this review draws on but whose history it does not reconstruct; and accounts of the social and cultural origins of metacognition (Heyes et al., 2020).

Each would add further facets, and perhaps further lineages, to the family described here. One direction for future work is to extend the historical reconstruction to these traditions and to the decades in which the construct moved from developmental and educational psychology into cognitive neuroscience, clinical psychology, and comparative research. Another is to use the lineages identified here as an analytical tool: mapping current instruments and intervention targets onto the tradition whose assumptions they inherit would make it possible to predict, rather than merely observe, where they converge and where they diverge.

More than four decades ago, Brown et al. (1983) described metacognition as a many-headed monster of obscure parentage. Its parentage can now be reconstructed. The heads of the monster correspond to research traditions that asked different questions about cognition as an object of cognition and built the methods needed to answer them. The construct's characteristic difficulties in definition, measurement, and application follow from the same historical pluralism that made it productive in the first place. Progress does not depend on agreement about a single encompassing framework. It can proceed through clearer statements of what is being explained, measures that fit those claims, and applications whose scope matches the evidence. Understood historically, the plurality of metacognition becomes interpretable, without the expectation that it must disappear.
