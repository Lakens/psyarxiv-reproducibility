Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Purpose: Formal and peer (informal) leaders play crucial roles in fostering team effectiveness and member well-being. Mapping Leadership in Teams: An Empirically Grounded Taxonomy of Formal and Peer

Leadership Roles.

Leadership is a key driver of individual, team, and organizational outcomes (Avolio et al., 2009; Sarros, 2011). Over the past decades, leadership research has increasingly focused on leader behaviors, demonstrating their influence on a wide range of organizational outcomes (Judge et al., 2004; Piccolo et al., 2012). Despite this progress, the field remains fragmented. Leadership behaviors are often examined as isolated constructs or grouped into broad leadership styles, resulting in disconnected frameworks that hamper cumulative theory development (Banks et al., 2023; Cronin et al., 2021; Homan et al., 2020). This fragmentation is particularly evident in peer leadership research, where role taxonomies differ substantially in their conceptualizations and behavioral classifications (Driskell et al., 2017; Hollenbeck et al., 2012). Consequently, the field lacks an empirically grounded taxonomy that integrates formal and peer leadership within a common behavioral framework. This fragmentation reflects two conceptual challenges. First, leadership is commonly conceptualized through broad styles or follower perceptions rather than the specific behaviors through which it is enacted. Second, although leadership is increasingly recognized as emerging from both formal and peer leaders, these sources are typically studied separately. Research has predominantly focused on formal leadership (Leino, 2022), whereas shared and informal leadership have rarely been examined alongside it within an integrated behavioral framework (Wang et al., 2014). Consequently, it remains unclear which leadership behaviors are shared across formal and peer leaders, which are source-specific, and how they jointly shape leadership in teams.

To address these challenges, we developed an empirically grounded taxonomy of formal and peer leadership behaviors based on employees' perceptions. Rather than identifying new behaviors, our contribution lies in integrating dispersed knowledge into a coherent leadership framework for everyday team practice. We conducted qualitative interviews to identify behaviors associated with effective formal and peer leaders, organized them into distinct leadership roles using inductive and Classification: Internal deductive classification procedures, and compared the resulting taxonomy with existing leadership frameworks.

Our study contributes to leadership research in three ways. First, it provides an empirically grounded taxonomy that integrates formal and peer leadership within a single conceptual framework, supporting programmatic theory development (Cronin et al., 2021). Second, by shifting attention from broad leadership styles to concrete behaviors, it offers greater conceptual precision regarding how leadership is enacted in teams. Third, by distinguishing shared from source-specific behaviors, the taxonomy provides a stronger foundation for future research on shared and multi-source leadership processes (D' Innocenzo et al., 2016; Edelmann et al., 2023).

Theoretical Background

Leadership Behavior Research: Fragmentation and Conceptual Limitations

Building on the behavioral tradition, leadership research has expanded into approaches such as transformational, authentic, ethical, and servant leadership (Dansereau et al., 2013; Dinh et al., 2014). While these have generated important insights, they typically emphasize broad leadership styles or follower perceptions rather than observable behaviors.

Consequently, scholars have called for more behaviorally specific and integrative approaches that capture how behaviors combine into broader patterns of leadership practice (Fleishman et al., 1991; Morgeson et al., 2010; Yukl, 1999).

A complementary perspective is provided by the functional approach to leadership (e.g., Fleishman et al., 1991), which conceptualizes leadership as fulfilling the functions required to meet a team's needs (McGrath, 1962). From this perspective, leadership effectiveness depends on ensuring that essential cognitive, motivational, affective, and coordination functions are fulfilled, regardless of who performs them (Burke et al., 2006; Zaccaro et al., 2001). This functional lens shifts attention from leadership styles to behaviors Classification: Internal that support effective team functioning, providing a suitable foundation for a behavioral taxonomy of leadership roles.

Leadership in Teams as a Multi-Source Phenomenon

Traditional leadership theories have primarily focused on formal leaders occupying designated managerial or supervisory roles (Morgeson et al., 2010). However, the functional leadership perspective argues that leadership functions can be performed by whoever is best positioned to meet a team's needs, regardless of formal authority (Ford et al., 2021).

Accordingly, leadership is increasingly viewed as a multi-source phenomenon in which both formal and peer leaders contribute to team effectiveness (Morgeson et al., 2010). Formal leaders primarily influence through formal authority, resource allocation, and role responsibilities, whereas peer leaders rely more on expertise, credibility, proximity, and dayto-day interactions. Examining both sources therefore provides a more complete understanding of leadership in teams.

We define peer leaders as team members without formal leadership positions who are recognized by colleagues as exercising leadership alongside the formal leader (Bednarek et al., 1976; Loughead and Hardy, 2005). Consistent with Wellman et al. (2019), peer leadership refers to influence behaviors through which team members establish shared goals, motivate collective task accomplishment, and foster a positive team climate. Peer leadership may complement or, under certain conditions, substitute for formal leadership (Kerr and Jermier, Classification: Internal 1978), particularly in teams characterized by high expertise, task interdependence, or limited formal leadership (Hoch and Kozlowski, 2014; Lisak et al., 2022).

Although the term peer leadership is most common in sport psychology (e.g., Fransen et al., 2014; Loughead and Hardy, 2006), similar phenomena have been studied as informal, emergent, distributed, and shared leadership (Carson et al., 2007; Morgeson et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2014). Despite growing recognition that leadership is distributed across multiple actors, behavioral taxonomies continue to focus primarily on formal leaders or treat leadership as a collective process. Consequently, little is known about how formal and peer leadership behaviors compare and complement one another. This study addresses that gap by examining both leadership sources in parallel (Wang et al., 2014).

Classifying Team Roles and Informal Leadership: A Fragmented Field.

Research on team roles increasingly recognizes that leadership is not exclusively exercised by formal leaders but can emerge through interactions among team members. This perspective is reflected in theories of shared, distributed, and collectivistic leadership, which conceptualize leadership as a dynamic influence process enacted by multiple actors rather than a single formal leader (Carson et al., 2007; Denis et al., 2012; Pearce and Conger, 2003; Yammarino et al., 2012). Together, these perspectives position peer leadership as an emergent, relational phenomenon that contributes to team functioning.

Alongside this work, numerous frameworks have classified team roles. Early models distinguished task and social roles (Bales, 1950 (Bales, , 1970;; Benne and Sheats, 1948), while later frameworks identified broader behavioral and functional roles within teams (Barry, 1991; Belbin, 1981 Belbin, , 1993;; Burke et al., 2018; Driskell et al., 2017; DuBrin, 1995; Lehmann-Willenbrock et al., 2016; Mathieu et al., 2015; Mumford et al., 2006; Stempfle et al., 2001).

However, these frameworks primarily describe team rather than leadership roles, providing limited insight into the leadership behaviors enacted by team members.

A related stream of research has examined peer leadership more explicitly. Pearce and Sims (2002) proposed a behavioral typology applicable to both formal leaders and team members, while Grille and Kauffeld (2015) operationalized these dimensions in the SPLIT instrument. Similarly, Fransen et al. (2014) identified four peer leadership roles in sports, later extended to organizational teams (Edelmann et al., 2020). Although these studies demonstrate that leadership can emerge from multiple sources, they rely on predefined behavioral categories and examine only a limited set of leadership roles.

Taken together, three limitations remain. First, existing role taxonomies differ substantially in terminology, structure, and level of analysis, limiting cumulative theory development (Driskell et al., 2017; Wildman et al., 2011). Second, many frameworks emphasize team roles, individual traits, or leadership emergence rather than the observable behaviors through which influence is exercised (e.g., claiming/granting and leadership emergence; Marks et al., 2001), providing limited insight into leadership in practice (Driskell et al., 2017; Lehmann-Willenbrock et al., 2016; Mathieu et al., 2015; Mumford et al., 2006).

Third, peer leadership frameworks primarily focus on leadership emergence or predefined dimensions rather than empirically identifying leadership behaviors (e.g., DeRue and Ashford, 2010; Uhl-Bien et al., 2014). Consequently, we still lack an integrated, behaviorally grounded understanding of how formal and peer leaders jointly enact leadership within teams.

Such knowledge is essential for understanding how leadership configurations shape team dynamics and outcomes and for informing interventions that support effective leadership in practice (Wildman et al., 2011).

Integrative Approaches to Formal and Informal Leadership.

Although leadership research has traditionally examined formal and peer leadership separately, a growing body of work recognizes leadership as emerging from multiple interacting sources (Keller, 2006; Leino, 2022; Zhang et al., 2012). Formal and peer leadership coexist within leadership Classification: Internal networks (White et al., 2016), interact in everyday practice (Van De Mieroop et al., 2020), and jointly influence team outcomes (Pan et al., 2018). Their influence mechanisms nevertheless differ, with formal leaders relying primarily on authority and peer leaders on relational influence and expertise (Luria and Berson, 2013; Pielstick, 2000; Potluka, 2021).

Unresolved Tensions and Research Gap

Despite substantial advances, leadership research remains fragmented. Existing frameworks emphasize leadership styles or broad behavioral categories, overlooking the specific behaviors leaders enact in practice (Banks et al., 2016; DeRue et al., 2011; Homan et al., 2020; Yukl, 2012). Moreover, although leadership increasingly involves both formal leaders and team members (Pearce and Conger, 2003; Pielstick, 2000), most taxonomies focus on formal leadership or treat leadership collectively without distinguishing leadership sources. Even integrative models such as Morgeson et al. (2010) apply the same leadership functions across actors rather than distinguishing their behavioral repertoires. Similarly, shared leadership research often adapts traditional leadership constructs to the team level (Chan, 1998; Pearce and Sims, 2002), despite growing recognition that shared leadership extends beyond the collective expression of hierarchical leadership (Hoch and Kozlowski, 2014; Mehra et al., 2006). Finally, existing taxonomies primarily emphasize team effectiveness while giving less attention to employee well-being (Inceoglu et al., 2018; Park et al., 2017). Together, these limitations highlight the need for an empirically grounded taxonomy distinguishing formal and peer leadership.

Method Study 1: Interview-Based Study of Leadership Behaviors in Teams

Research Approach

To capture employees' perspectives on leadership behaviors, we adopted a qualitative research design within a post-positivist paradigm (Ponterotto, 2005). Consistent with calls to study leadership as an emergent behavioral phenomenon (Lehmann-Willenbrock et al., 2016), we focused on concrete leadership behaviors rather than predefined leadership dimensions.

We used a primarily inductive approach informed by existing leadership theory. Specifically, the task-and relationship-oriented distinction from the Ohio State studies served as a sensitizing framework for the interview design, while no predefined categories were imposed during coding. Semi-structured interviews generated descriptions of leadership behaviors, which were inductively clustered into broader leadership roles using coding procedures Classification: Internal inspired by Grounded Theory (Strauss and Corbin, 1998). Concept mapping consisted of two phases. First, participants individually sorted the 49 formal and 39 peer leadership functions from Study 1 into conceptually similar clusters. Discussion

This study developed an empirically grounded taxonomy of leadership behaviors enacted by formal and peer leaders. By clustering employees' descriptions of leadership behaviors into roles and comparing these with existing frameworks, we provide an integrated overview of leadership across hierarchical levels. Our analyses identified 10 formal and 10 peer leadership roles. Consistent with classic leadership research (e.g., Bales and Slater, 1955), the findings confirm the enduring importance of task-and relationship-oriented leadership while revealing a more differentiated behavioral repertoire within these broad categories. This supports recent calls to move beyond broad leadership styles toward more behaviorally specific conceptualizations of leadership (Banks et al., 2023).

Comparison with more than 20 leadership and team-role frameworks (Tables IV and V) showed substantial convergence while identifying behaviors that extend previous taxonomies. Together, these findings suggest that the taxonomy aligns with established leadership research while providing a more integrated understanding of leadership across formal and peer contexts. Frequencies of individual leadership functions (Tables II and III) should be interpreted as indicators of perceived importance rather than effectiveness.

These findings highlight the importance of interpreting leadership roles alongside their underlying behavioral functions.

Several roles were source-specific. Proximity Leader, Unity Leader, and Selfdevelopment Leader emerged only for formal leaders, whereas Critical Innovation Leader and Logistics Leader were unique to peer leaders. These differences likely reflect the distinct positions of formal and peer leaders within teams. Formal leaders are expected to remain accessible and develop their leadership capabilities, whereas peer leaders are expected to take initiative, drive improvement, and provide day-to-day operational support (Harari et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021; Martínez-Córcoles and Zhu, 2020). Notably, the emergence of the Critical Innovation Leader and Logistics Leader contrasts with Morgeson et al. (2010), who argued that challenging the team and providing resources are primarily formal leadership responsibilities. This difference may reflect the increasing autonomy and innovation demands of contemporary organizations, where employees are expected to take greater ownership of team functioning.

Overall, the taxonomy converged strongly with existing leadership frameworks while identifying less-studied roles, including the Proximity Leader, Logistics Leader, Social Activity Leader, and Unity Leader. Although modest variation in the frequency of specific Classification: Internal leadership behaviors was observed across industries (Appendix C), the overall pattern was highly consistent.

Theoretical Contributions

This study advances leadership theory by offering a more behaviorally specific understanding of leadership in teams. First, by adopting an inductive, data-driven approach, this study responds to calls to focus leadership research on observable behaviors rather than broad leadership styles or generalized perceptions (Banks et al., 2023). Grounded in employees' descriptions of everyday leadership, the taxonomy provides greater behavioral specificity than many existing frameworks and a stronger foundation for future measurement development. Although several roles overlap with established leadership theories (e.g., transformational, authentic, and servant leadership), these overlaps reflect the integration of complementary perspectives into a coherent behavioral taxonomy rather than conceptual fragmentation.

Second, the taxonomy conceptualizes leadership as concrete behaviors enacted by both formal and peer leaders rather than as a formal position or shared team property. By distinguishing overlapping from source-specific roles, it provides a framework for understanding how different leadership sources jointly contribute to team functioning. Third, the taxonomy contributes to shared leadership research by specifying which leadership roles are shared across formal and peer leaders and which remain source-specific. This provides a more precise basis for examining how leadership responsibilities are distributed and how different leadership sources complement one another (D' Innocenzo et al., 2016). In doing so, it moves beyond an either/or view of formal versus distributed leadership by demonstrating that effective team leadership comprises both overlapping and source-specific behaviors enacted by different leadership sources.

Finally, by consolidating dispersed leadership behaviors into an empirically grounded taxonomy spanning formal and peer leadership, this study provides a foundation for more programmatic theory development and measurement and offers a more unified framework for understanding team leadership (Cronin et al., 2021).

Practical Implications

The proposed taxonomy offers practical guidance for formal leaders, teams, HR professionals, and organizations. By distinguishing between formal and peer leadership roles, it clarifies which responsibilities should remain with formal leaders and which can be shared with team members, supporting more deliberate delegation and shared leadership. It also provides a common language for diagnosing leadership gaps, overlap, and overload, informing leadership development, team interventions, onboarding, role clarification, performance conversations, and assessment (e.g., Shared Leadership Mapping; Fransen et al., 2020). Our findings further show that both formal and peer leaders are expected to enact a broad repertoire of task-and relationship-oriented behaviors, highlighting the increasing complexity of formal leadership (Yukl, 2010) while creating opportunities to distribute leadership more effectively across team members, strengthening ownership and accountability for team outcomes (George, 1992; Williams et al., 1981). By explicitly defining leadership functions, the taxonomy also supports clearer role expectations and accountability, reducing role ambiguity and facilitating coordination within teams (Feldman, 1984; Taggar and Ellis, 2007).

Strengths of the Present Research

A key strength of this study is its bottom-up qualitative design, which generated rich insights into leadership behaviors that are difficult to capture using surveys (Carton, 2022).

The stratified sample enhanced diversity across organizational contexts, while combining current and ideal leadership behaviors provided a comprehensive overview of employees' Classification: Internal leadership expectations. Combining individual and group concept-mapping exercises further strengthened the taxonomy by integrating independent and collective judgments.

Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Although the qualitative concept-mapping approach generated rich insights, the taxonomy requires quantitative validation through scale development and confirmatory factor analysis. Future research should also examine how leadership behaviors and configurations influence team functioning across leadership sources and organizational contexts. A limitation of Study 2 is that participants assigned every leadership function to a role, making some conceptual overlap inevitable. Future research should therefore examine the distinctiveness of the identified roles and the combinations most beneficial across contexts. Social network analysis could examine whether multiple roles are enacted by the same or different individuals, whereas Necessary Condition Analysis (Dul, 2018) may identify leadership roles that are essential but not sufficient for effective team functioning.

Because our data were collected primarily from intact teams, leadership behaviors particularly important during early team development may be underrepresented (Kozlowski and Ilgen, 2006; Tuckman, 1965). Future research should examine how leadership functions evolve across team development and whether roles are most effective when enacted by formal leaders, peer leaders, or both. Likewise, it remains unclear whether peer leadership is most effective when multiple leadership roles are concentrated in one individual or distributed across several team members (e.g., Fransen et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2018).

Conclusion

This study presents an empirically grounded taxonomy integrating formal and informal leadership behaviors into a cohesive framework, addressing fragmentation in the existing literature. Our findings show that many leadership roles are shared across leadership sources, whereas others are unique to either formal or peer leaders, highlighting how leadership is distributed within teams. By consolidating these roles, we offer a foundation for future research on leadership as a shared phenomenon involving both formal and peer leaders.
