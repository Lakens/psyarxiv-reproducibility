Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Any difference in responding to the test compounds, therefore, is considered as reflecting differential learning in the compound training stage.

In Rescorla's (2000, Experiment 2) study animals showed lower responding to the test compound AD than BC, indicating greater changes in associative strength for cue A during AB-training. In another experiment in which the compound was reinforced on every trial, AB+, the test compound AD also elicited lower responding than BC, indicating greater changes in associative strength for cue B (Rescorla, 2000, Experiment 1a). These findings were observed in both rats and pigeons and were also obtained when B had been pretrained as neutral (i.e., B-) rather than as a conditioned inhibitor (Rescorla, 2000 (Rescorla, , 2001)). Rescorla interpreted these findings as evidence that learning about the elements of a compound also depends on the elements' individual prediction errors, with the cue producing the greater individual prediction error at the outset of the compound training showing greater changes in its associative strength.

The findings reported by Rescorla (2000 Rescorla ( , 2001) ) in rats and pigeons have subsequently been replicated in humans using predictive learning procedures. Similar results have been observed both when the compound was reinforced, AB+ (e.g., Chan et al., 2024, Experiments 1 and 2; Jahani & Üngör, 2026, Experiments 1 and 3), and when it was nonreinforced, AB-(e.g., Chan et al., 2024, Experiment 3; Haselgrove & Evans, 2010). For example, Haselgrove and Evans (2010) asked participants to imagine that they were health inspectors at a fictitious hospital and to determine which foods were safe or unsafe to consume on the basis of records describing food-related outcomes (e.g., illness vs. no illness). On each trial, participants viewed a food item and predicted the outcome before receiving feedback regarding the actual outcome. Foods A and C were consistently followed by illness (A+, C+), whereas foods B and D were followed by the absence of such outcome (B-, D-). Subsequently, participants received nonreinforced presentations of the AB compound (AB-). At test, participants rated the probability of illness as lower following AD than BC, indicating a greater decrease in associative strength for cue A-associated with the larger individual prediction error-than for cue B.

Studies of human predictive learning employing the compound test procedure (Rescorla, 2000 (Rescorla, , 2001)), however, have also provided evidence that, under certain conditions, the cue associated with the smaller individual prediction error can undergo greater associative change (e.g., Chan et al., 2024, Experiments 2 and 4; Spicer et al., 2020 Spicer et al., , 2022)). This pattern has been observed when participants are less certain about the causal status of the cue generating the smaller individual prediction error than about that of the cue generating the larger error. In one experiment (Spicer et al., 2020, Experiment 1), for instance, participants learned about the relationship between different foods and the occurrence or absence of an allergic reaction.

Cue X was established as a blocked cue using a blocking procedure, A+ AX+ (e.g., Kamin, 1969), whereas cue Y was trained as an uncorrelated cue in a simple discrimination schedule, BY+ CY-(e.g., Uengoer et al., 2013). Additional control cues received the same training (D+ DW+; EZ+ FZ-). Previous research had demonstrated that participants judged a blocked cue from a blocking procedure (e.g., X) to be a stronger predictor of the outcome than an uncorrelated cue from a simple discrimination (e.g.,Y), while being relatively less certain about the causal status of the former than the latter (Jones et al., 2019). Participants in Spicer et al.'s study then completed a second training stage in which the compound XY was followed by the outcome (i.e., allergic reaction), XY+. Subsequently, in the compound test stage, participants judged WY as less likely than XZ to be followed by an allergic reaction.

These results indicated that the cue about which participants were relatively less certain underwent more changes in its associative strength, despite generating the smaller individual prediction error during the compound training. Spicer et al. therefore proposed that, when encountering an unexpected outcome, people would protect the belief about the causal status of the more certain cue and attribute the outcome to the less certain one. They referred to this account as theory protection. Subsequent studies in humans have provided additional evidence consistent with theory protection (e.g., Chan et al., 2024, Experiments 2 and 4; Spicer et al., 2022).

Despite these findings, the available evidence does not yet allow firm conclusions about the role of certainty in associative learning within the compound test procedure (Rescorla, 2000 (Rescorla, , 2001)). Some studies did not assess certainty at all (e.g., Haselgrove & Evans, 2010; Le Pelley & McLaren, 2004). Other studies that assessed certainty have found that differences in relative certainty do not necessarily determine learning about cues trained in compound. For example, Chan et al. (2024, Experiments 3 and 5) observed nondifferential learning under associative-loss conditions (e.g., AB-), even though confidence ratings obtained before the compound training confirmed that the cues differed in relative certainty. Jahani and Üngör (2026) also examined the role of relative certainty in associative change using the compound-test procedure (Rescorla, 2000 (Rescorla, , 2001)). Across three experiments, participants learned to predict how different foods affected chronic inflammation in a hypothetical patient. During initial training, cue A was established as excitatory, whereas cue B was trained to acquire inhibitory properties, with cues C and D serving as their respective controls. Separate experiments using forced-choice confidence judgments indicated that participants were relatively more certain about the causal status of the inhibitory cue B than about the excitatory cue A. In the compound-test experiments, initial training was followed by compound training under associative-gain conditions (e.g., AB+) and a final test of outcome expectations for AD and BC. In Experiments 1 and 3, participants rated AD lower than BC, indicating greater associative change for B, the cue with the larger individual prediction error and about which participants were expected to be relatively more certain. In Experiment 2, however, participants rated BC lower than AD, indicating greater associative change for A, the cue generating the smaller individual prediction error and about which participants were expected to be relatively less certain. Thus, across the three experiments, differential learning did not show a consistent relationship with relative certainty.

To explain these diverging findings, Jahani and Üngör (2026) considered other procedural features that may have produced differences in prediction-error magnitude. However, the compound tests and the assessment of relative certainty were conducted in separate experiments, so interpretations concerning the role of relative certainty relied on crossexperiment comparisons. Moreover, Jahani and Üngör (2026) examined only associative-gain conditions (e.g., AB+), leaving open whether similar patterns would emerge under associative-loss conditions. This distinction is particularly relevant given the asymmetry between associative gains and losses reported by Chan et al. (2024).

Accordingly, the present experiments aimed to investigate whether the differential associative change observed by Rescorla (2000 Rescorla ( , 2001) ) in animals generalizes to human predictive learning and to examine the role of relative certainty in this process. Extending Jahani and Üngör (2026), the present experiments examined compound training under associative-loss conditions (e.g., AB-) and assessed relative certainty before the compound training and subsequent associative change within the same experiments.

In the initial training phase (Training 1) of the present experiments (Table 1), one of the tobe-compounded cues, A, was always trained as excitatory, whereas the other to-becompounded cue, B, was always trained to acquire inhibitory properties. In addition, Cues C and D were trained to match the learning histories of cues A and B, respectively. Prior to the compound training, relative certainty between cues A and B was assessed at Test 1.

Subsequently, during the compound training (Training 2), AB was presented under an identical condition in all experiments that should produce associative loss (AB-). At the final test phase (Test 2), participants provided outcome expectation ratings for the test compounds AD and BC. In each of the present experiments, the initial training schedule (Training 1) was expected to produce greater relative certainty about cue A than cue B, with A generating a larger individual prediction error than B during the compound training.

The present experiments used the allergy task procedure by Jahani and Üngör (2026).

Experiment 1

Table 1 summarizes the design of Experiment 1. In Training 1, participants were trained with individual and compound cues (AW+10, EW0, CZ+10, FZ0, X+10, XB0, XD0, H-10), where the numbers indicate the change in inflammation level.

Cues AW, CZ, and X consistently produced strong increases (+10), while EW, FZ, XB, and XD produced no change (0). By the end of Training 1, cue A was expected to have been learned as an excitatory cue causing an increase in the inflammation level, and cue B as an inhibitory cue associated with decrease in the inflammation level, with higher relative certainty for A than B. To our knowledge, certainty about the causal status of the cues trained in these schedules has not been directly assessed previously. The descriptive results reported by Jones et al. (2019), however, suggest that participants may be relatively more certain about an excitatory cue trained within a simple discrimination schedule (e.g., B in BY+ CY-), than about a cue expected to acquire inhibitory properties within the same discrimination schedule (e.g., C in BY+ CY-). Moreover, compared with generative causal relations, preventive causal relations may be more ambiguous because the cues could be interpreted either as having a preventive effect or as having no causal effect (see Dennis & Ahn, 2001). Cue H, which predicted a strong decrease (-10), served to balance trial frequency across outcome types and to further emphasize that cues could decrease inflammation. Following Training 1 at Test 1, participants rated the cues with respect to their outcome expectations and confidence without receiving feedback. In Training 2, they were trained with the key compound cue, AB, which was followed by a strong decrease (-10) in inflammation. A control compound, JK, predicting a strong increase (+10) was included to equate trial numbers across outcome types.

Learning about the individual elements of the AB compound was assessed in a subsequent test phase (Test 2) using the compound test procedure (Rescorla, 2000 (Rescorla, , 2001)). At Test 2, where the same outcome expectation measure was used as in Test 1, participants rated their outcome expectations for the two test compounds AD and BC.

If learning during Training 2 is mainly driven by individual prediction error, as suggested by Rescorla (2000 Rescorla ( , 2001)), cue A should undergo a greater decrease in associative strength than cue B, resulting in lower expectation ratings for AD than BC at Test 2. However, if participants enter Training 2 being relatively more certain about the causal status of A than B, the theory protection account (e.g., Spicer et al., 2020, 2022) predicts that the AB compound training should generate a greater change in associative strength for B than for A, leading to lower expectation ratings for BC than AD. Method Participant. Data were collected from 53 participants via the online crowdsourcing platform Prolific (34 males and 19 females; age range = 20-63 years, median = 35). Twelve participants were excluded according to the exclusion criteria (see below), resulting in a final sample of 41 participants (24 males and 17 females; age range = 20-63 years, median = 34). Participants received monetary compensation at a rate of €9 per hour; the experiment lasted approximately 20 minutes.

An a priori power analysis for the primary within-participant comparison (AD vs. BC) indicated a target sample size of 38 participants to detect a medium effect, Cohen's dz = 0.50, with 85% power at α = .05. This target was also consistent with the sample sizes used in previous studies employing similar procedures, which reported small-to-medium effects (Spicer et al., 2020).

Because online studies can have relatively high exclusion rates due to failed comprehension checks, incomplete data, and other quality-control criteria, we recruited beyond the target sample size, assuming an exclusion rate of up to 30%, to ensure that the required final sample size was retained after exclusions. A final sample size of up to five participants above or below the target was considered acceptable. This procedure was applied consistently across all experiments reported here.

Materials.

The experiment was programmed using PsychoPy (version 2024.1.5; Peirce et al., 2019) and hosted on Pavlovia (https://pavlovia.org/). Images of foods (apple, banana, broccoli, carrot, coconut, cherry, lemon, pear, orange, grape, kiwi, pineapple) were used as cues. For each participant, fruit images were randomly assigned to cues A, W, E, C, Z, F, X, B, D, H, J, and K.

Outcomes were displayed on a bidirectional segmented color band (21 segments) ranging from green at -10 (strong decrease) to yellow at 0 (no change) to red at +10 (strong increase), with a gradual color transition between three main levels. An arrow labeled "Decrease in

Inflammation" pointed toward -10 and an arrow labeled "Increase in Inflammation" pointed toward the +10 extreme. The starting part of the outcome (0) was labeled "No Change." On each trial, the outcome was indicated by an empty square positioned on the relevant segment.

Images of meals appeared at the center of the screen (height and width = 0.2). When two foods formed a compound, they were presented side-by-side, separated by a plus sign, with left-right positions randomized on each presentation.

Participants were told that the effect of the meals on inflammation had been recorded over several months. A progress bar at the top of the screen, accompanied by a month icon, was displayed to represent the passage of time across training and test.

Design. Participants completed a within-subjects design (Table 1). In Training 1, they learned three types of cue-outcome associations in which food cues were followed by a strong decrease in inflammation, no change, or by a strong increase in inflammation. Within each block, AW, CZ, and X were always followed by a strong increase (+10), whereas EW, FZ, XB, and XD were followed by no change (0). Food cue H was consistently followed by a strong decrease in inflammation (-10). Training 1 comprised 80 trials divided into 10 blocks, each block containing one presentation of each of the eight trial types (AW+10, EW0, CZ+10, FZ0, X+10, XB0, XD0, H-10). Trials were presented in random order, with the restriction that the last trial of one block and the first trial of the next block could not display the same trial type. In Training 2, participants received 10 trials divided into five blocks, each block comprising one presentation of the critical compound AB, followed by a strong decrease (-10), and one presentation of a filler compound JK, followed by a strong increase (+10). During each test stage, each individual or compound cue was presented once for the expectation rating, immediately followed by the confidence rating. The testing order of the cues was randomized across participants.

Procedure. After reading an information sheet and giving their consent, participants answered questions about demographics information. Participants then received instructions/ cover story as follows:

However, based on the data from several months and numerous instances of consumption for each food, you should be able to determine the relationship between each food and its impact on inflammation at the given time.

You will see the meals the patient consumed and be asked to predict their effect on the inflammation level. After submitting your prediction, you will receive feedback about the actual outcome. This process will repeat for each meal. A meal may consist of either one or two foods. At first, you may need to guess; however, over time, you will learn how the different foods influence the inflammation level, allowing you to make more accurate predictions.

To provide your prediction please use the rating scale that will be presented with the meal. After submitting your prediction, you will be shown the actual outcome of the meal on the inflammation level on a colored segmented scale (as shown in the image below). In the training trials, either an individual or a compound cue was presented in the center of the screen. A text displayed above the cue stated: "The meal is:" and a text below prompted participants to make their prediction based on the cue: "What outcome do you expect the meal to have on inflammation? Please use your mouse to rate." Participants provided their predictions using the rating scale ranging from "-10: Strong decrease in inflammation" to "0:

No change" to "+10: Strong increase in inflammation". Clicking at any point on the rating scale triggered the appearance of a "Submit Response" button. After submitting their response, participants were shown the actual outcome: a segmented color band indicating either a strong decrease in inflammation (-10), no change (0), or a strong increase (+10) in inflammation, displayed for two seconds. Additionally, a text appeared above the outcome stating: "The outcome of this meal on inflammation is:".

After completing a training stage, participants proceeded to a test stage, which was introduced by the following instructions:

For the next part of the experiment, you should continue to make predictions about the effect a meal has on inflammation. However, in this part, you will NOT receive any feedback. You will also be asked to indicate how confident you are in your predictions, from not confident at all (0) to very confident (10). Press the space bar to continue the experiment.

Each cue was presented once for the expectation test and again in the subsequent trial for the confidence test. Expectation test trials were identical to the training trials, except that no outcome was presented following the submission of the expectation. Instead, after submitting their expectation, participants were shown the rated cue and their selected rating: "You just rated that you expect the outcome of this meal on inflammation to be:" (with the expectation rating's value displayed on the next line). They were then asked to rate their confidence about this prediction: "How confident are you in your rating?" Confidence was rated on an 11point scale ranging from "0: not confident at all" to "10: very confident." In all rating scales used throughout the experiment, only the midpoint and the extreme ends displayed numerical labels.

At the end of the experiment, participants' understanding of the task and whether they had used notes during the task were assessed. Understanding was tested at the end to avoid any potential influence on learning.

Analysis. Data were processed and analyzed in R (Posit Team, 2026). For graphical presentation, within-subject error bars were calculated by normalizing the data within participants to remove between-participant variability and applying the Morey (2008) correction to the resulting standard errors and confidence intervals, as implemented in the summarySEwithin() function of the Rmisc package (Hope, 2022).

The primary comparison of interest was between expectation ratings for the AD and BC test compounds at Test 2, assessed using a two-sided paired-samples t-test. Individual cue ratings were additionally analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA implemented with the afex package (Singmann et al., 2024). Comparisons of theoretically relevant cue pairs were conducted using paired-samples t-tests.

Bayesian analyses were conducted where appropriate, following the procedure described by Dienes (2011), implemented in R using a function based on Baguley and Kaye (2010). The AD-BC comparison used a nondirectional normal prior centered on zero with SD = 2. This prior assumed no directional difference between the compounds and treated relatively small differences as most plausible, given previous research (e.g., Jahani & Üngör, 2026; Spicer et al., 2020 Spicer et al., , 2022)). The priors for comparisons of expectation ratings of individual cues were based on the rating scale and the differences considered plausible from the training contingencies. The A-B confidence comparison at Test 1 used a directional uniform prior from 0 to 10, reflecting the prediction that confidence would be higher for cue A than for cue B.

Bayes factors are reported as BF10, representing evidence for the specified alternative hypothesis relative to the null hypothesis. Values greater than 3 were interpreted as substantial evidence for the alternative hypothesis, values below 1/3 as substantial evidence for the null hypothesis, and values between 1/3 and 3 as inconclusive (Jeffreys, 1961). Where useful for interpretation, BF01 is also reported to express evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the alternative hypothesis.

For all tests in the experiments reported in this article, a significance threshold of α = .05 was adopted. Bonferroni adjustments and Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied where appropriate. Only the results most relevant to the experimental hypotheses are reported.

Experiment 2

Experiment 2 had two aims. The first was to replicate the compound test results from Experiment 1. The second was to assess confidence before the compound training using a potentially more sensitive measure. To this end, a forced-choice method was used, as in Spicer et al. (2022, Experiment 4). In this forced-choice confidence test, participants were shown the outcome expectation ratings they had given for cues A and B and were required to choose whether they felt more confident in their rating for A or in their rating for B.

If the Training 1 schedules for cues A and B produced a difference in relative certainty that was not fully captured by the confidence measure used in Experiment 1, such a difference should be more likely to be detected with a forced-choice measure. Apart from this change at Test 1, Experiment 2 was identical to Experiment 1.

The predictions were thus identical to those in Experiment 1. If learning during AB-10 training is mainly driven by individual prediction error (e.g., Rescorla, 2000 Rescorla, , 2001)), cue A should undergo greater associative loss than cue B, resulting in lower expectation ratings for AD than BC at Test 2. By contrast, if participants are more certain about the causal status of A relative to B before the compound training, the theory protection account (e.g., Spicer et al., 2020 Spicer et al., , 2022) ) predicts greater associative change for B than for A, resulting in lower expectation ratings for BC than AD.

Experiment 3

One possible reason why Experiments 1 and 2 did not produce the learning pattern predicted by the theory protection account (e.g., Spicer et al., 2020 Spicer et al., , 2022) ) is that the difference in relative certainty between the to-be-protected cue, A, and the to-be-updated cue, B, may not have been sufficiently large. Experiment 3 was therefore designed to increase certainty about the causal status of A and, consequently, to increase the relative-certainty difference between A and B before the compound training. Specifically, Experiment 3 replaced the EW0 trials used in Experiments 1 and 2 with W-10 trials, while retaining the AW+10 trials. Presenting W both alone and in compound with A was expected to make it easier for participants to attribute the strong increase in inflammation on AW+10 trials to A, thereby clarifying A's causal role. Apart from this change, which was also applied to the corresponding control cue C, the training schedule in Experiment 3 was identical to that used in Experiments 1 and 2.

If the absence of theory protection in Experiments 1 and 2 reflected an insufficient difference in relative certainty between A and B, then increasing certainty about A should increase the likelihood of observing theory protection. Therefore, if training A with AW+10 and W-10 trials in Experiment 3 make participants more certain about A's causal status and thereby increase the difference in relative certainty between A and B, then, according to the theory protection account (e.g., Spicer et al., 2020 Spicer et al., , 2022)), this should lead to lower outcome expectation ratings for BC than for AD at the final test. By contrast, if learning is driven by individual prediction errors (e.g., Rescorla, 2000 Rescorla, , 2001)), outcome expectation ratings should be lower for AD than for BC at Test 2.

General Discussion

The present experiments examined the distribution of associative change (i.e., learning) between cues trained in compound using a variant of the compound test procedure (Rescorla, 2000 (Rescorla, , 2001)), where the compound consisted of two cues, one of which pretrained as Training 2 was followed by a final test (Test 2), during which participants rated their outcome expectations for the test compounds AD and BC.

Consistent with the individual prediction error account (e.g., Rescorla, 2000 Rescorla, , 2001)), the results from Experiments 1, 2, 5, and 6 indicated more learning about the cue with greater theory protection account could, in principle, also accommodate the results of Experiment 1, where confidence ratings showed no significant difference in relative certainty between cues A and B, because this account does not rule out a role for prediction error in learning when confidence in the cues' causal status does not differ. The results of Experiments 2, 5, and 6, however, pose a challenge to theory protection. In these experiments, participants were relatively more certain about A than B, yet AD received lower ratings than BC, indicating greater associative change in the cue about which participants were relatively more certain.

Within the present set of experiments, therefore, differential learning did not appear to be strongly governed by participants' relative certainty about cue A versus cue B. To further examine whether the AD-BC pattern was related to participants' relative certainty about cues A and B at Test 1, an additional exploratory analysis was conducted on the combined data from all experiments. Test 1 relative certainty was coded as A > B or A < B, based on scalar confidence ratings in Experiments 1 and 3 and forced-choice relative certainty judgments in the remaining experiments. The participant-level AD-BC difference score was then submitted to a 7 × 2 ANOVA with experimental group and relative certainty group as between-participants factors. When collapsed across experiments, the AD-BC difference was highly similar for participants classified as A > B and those classified as A < B (Figure 15A), as reflected in the nonsignificant main effect of certainty group, F(1, 426) = 0.49, p = .484, ηp² = .001. The main effect of experimental group approached significance but did not reach the conventional significance threshold, F(6, 426) = 2.04, p = .059, ηp² = .028.

Importantly, the Experimental Group × Certainty Group interaction was also not significant, F(6, 426) = 0.40, p = .877, ηp² = .006, indicating that the relation between relative certainty and the AD-BC difference did not vary reliably across experiments (Figure 15B). Follow-up comparisons likewise showed no reliable differences between the A > B and A < B groups within any individual experiment, all ps ≥ .293. This provides further support for the conclusion that, in the present experiments, differential learning during compound training was not governed by relative certainty. One component of the EXIT model that may nevertheless be relevant to the present study is Experiment 5, however, challenges a simple version of this explanation. This experiment directly manipulated the magnitude of the prediction error generated by A during the compound training, but both the Low-PE and High-PE conditions produced an AD < BC pattern, consistent with an individual prediction error account (e.g., Rescorla, 2000 Rescorla, , 2001)).

Thus, the magnitude of the prediction error generated by A does not, by itself, appear sufficient to explain the deviating pattern observed in Experiment 3. However, it remains possible that the present experiments did not consistently produce prediction errors large enough to trigger this type of attentional shift. Future research should therefore examine more directly the role of prediction-error magnitude in the compound-test procedure.

Thus far, we have interpreted the present findings primarily within an associative-learning framework, according to which repeated cue-outcome pairings lead to the formation and modification of associations, with learning determined by prediction error (Rescorla & Wagner, 1972; Rescorla, 2000 Rescorla, , 2001)). An alternative perspective is provided by propositional and causal-model accounts of human associative learning, which emphasize that learners may reason about relations between events and use assumptions about causal structure when interpreting new evidence (e.g., Booth & Buehner, 2007; Griffiths & Tenenbaum, 2009; Mitchell et al., 2009; Waldmann & Holyoak, 1992). From this perspective, participants may have used the relatively transparent causal structure of the present tasks and the limited number of cues to infer specific relations between individual foods and outcomes and to derive their test ratings accordingly.

Considered within this framework, confidence may be better understood as part of a broader reasoning process rather than as a separate mechanism that directly determines learning.

Confidence may signal how well specified participants' hypotheses are about a cue's causal role, given the instructions, task structure, training histories, and plausible cue-outcome relations. However, how participants attribute new evidence may not depend on confidence alone. Instead, it may depend on whether the task structure provides a plausible way to assign the unexpected outcome to one cue rather than another. Future experiments could directly assess participants' causal hypotheses and test this possibility more directly.

In conclusion, the present findings were more consistent with an individual prediction error account (e.g., Rescorla, 2000 Rescorla, , 2001) ) than with the theory protection account (e.g., Spicer et al., 2020 Spicer et al., , 2022)). Across Experiments 1, 2, 5, and 6, the cue generating the larger individual prediction error generally showed greater associative change, even when participants were relatively more certain about that cue. Experiment 3 provided the clearest evidence for a pattern consistent with theory protection, but Experiment 4 failed to replicate this pattern despite showing the predicted difference in relative certainty between A and B. Thus, relative certainty did not consistently determine how learning was distributed across cues, challenging the generality of the role assigned to relative certainty in the theory protection account (e.g., Spicer et al., 2020 Spicer et al., , 2022)).

At the same time, these findings do not rule out a role for certainty in learning. Rather, they suggest that the influence of certainty may depend on boundary conditions that remain to be specified. One possibility is that theory protection requires not relative certainty alone, but a particular configuration of absolute certainty, in which one cue is held with sufficiently high confidence and another remains sufficiently uncertain. Future research should therefore identify when learning is primarily determined by individual prediction error and under what conditions certainty shapes the distribution of learning across cues.

Another account that could explain greater learning about the cue with the smaller individual prediction error is the EXIT model (Kruschke, 2001). Livesey et al. (2025) recently demonstrated that it can account for several findings previously attributed to theory protection (e.g., Spicer et al., 2020 Spicer et al., , 2022)). The EXIT model can be viewed as an extension of Mackintosh's (1975) attentional account, in which attention is redistributed among cues according to the prediction errors they generate. Importantly, in the EXIT model, attention can be redistributed within a trial before associative weights are updated. Thus, cues that initially generate larger prediction errors may lose attention before substantial learning occurs.

(2025), retaining their implementation of the model, parameter-sampling procedure, and randomized sampling of participant-level trial sequences, while adapting the task-specific components to the present designs.
