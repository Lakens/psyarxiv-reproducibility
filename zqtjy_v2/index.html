Given that brief digital psychological interventions typically yield small-to-moderate effects (Groot et al., 2023), the analysis was based on an effect size of f = 0.15, with α set at 0.05 and statistical power at 0.80. To account for an anticipated attrition rate of approximately 25-35%, which is common in digital intervention studies (Linardon & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz, 2020), the target sample size was increased to 140 participants, with 70 women allocated to each group. Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The high prevalence of body image concerns during the transition to motherhood underscores the need for theory-driven, accessible interventions that promote positive body image, reduce body dissatisfaction, and enhance women's resilience to unrealistic appearance ideals. This paper presents the protocol for a two-arm randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating the effectiveness of BODYPOSI, a four-week digital self-help program based on positive body image theory. The trial will include women between 6 weeks and 12 months postpartum, who will be randomly assigned to either the intervention or a waitlist control group. Primary outcomes will include body appreciation, body functionality appreciation, appearance and weight satisfaction, evaluation of postpartum body changes, and perceived sociocultural appearance pressure related to postpartum body changes. Secondary outcomes will include postnatal depressive and anxiety symptoms, self-compassion, and general health status.

Outcomes will be assessed at baseline, post-intervention, and three-month follow-up. Analyses will compare changes between the intervention and control groups and examine the maintenance of intervention effects at follow-up for intervention group. If shown to be effective, BODYPOSI has the potential to improve body image and psychological well-being among postpartum women while supporting the wider implementation of accessible digital interventions in postpartum care.

Introduction

Research on postpartum body image has received increasing attention in recent years (Lee et al., 2025; Siqueira et al., 2025). The postpartum period represents a unique developmental transition characterized by substantial biological, psychological, and social changes as women adapt to motherhood (Hwang et al, 2022). During this period, women experience rapid physical changes associated with pregnancy, childbirth, recovery, breastfeeding, and infant care, making body image particularly salient.

Research suggests that body image concerns are common during both pregnancy and the postpartum period. However, there are no official representative statistics, and prevalence estimates reported across studies vary depending on the populations studied, assessment methods, and definitions of body dissatisfaction. During pregnancy, body dissatisfaction has been reported in approximately one-third to nearly one-half of women (Roomruangwong et al., 2017; Sui et al., 2013). Postpartum studies similarly indicate that body image concerns remain prevalent after childbirth. Grajek et al. (2022) found that 68.8% of postpartum women were dissatisfied with their current body weight and figure, while Owusu et al. (2025) reported that only 56.2% of women were satisfied with their bodies after delivery. In addition, Krupa-Kotara et al. (2025) found that most women expressed concerns about their postpartum weight and that approximately half showed a predisposition to developing an eating disorder. Although elevated levels of body dissatisfaction appear less common, affecting approximately one in ten women at three and six months postpartum (Linde et al., 2024), longitudinal evidence suggests that body image concerns may persist or worsen over time. For example, Gjerdingen et al. (2009) found that body dissatisfaction increased significantly between one and nine months postpartum. Together, these findings indicate that body image concerns affect a substantial proportion of women during the transition to motherhood and represent an important target for intervention.

Postpartum body dissatisfaction is not only common but also clinically relevant. Studies have linked negative body image to depressive symptoms (Bakhteh et al., 2023; Kariuki et al., 2022; Schlaff et al., 2020), diagnosis of perinatal depression (He et al., 2025), and disordered eating behaviours (Rodgers et al., 2018). Furthermore, body image concerns may extend beyond maternal psychological wellbeing and affect aspects of the mother-infant relationship. Higher body dissatisfaction has been associated with lower breastfeeding self-efficacy and greater perceived breastfeeding barriers (Kapa et al., 2022; Rodgers et al., 2018). These findings suggest that body image concerns may influence not only women's emotional wellbeing but also important health behaviours and early parenting experiences.

Traditionally, body image research has focused on body dissatisfaction and appearance-related distress. However, contemporary theoretical perspectives conceptualize positive body image as a distinct construct rather than merely the absence of negative body image (Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015a; Webb et al., 2015). Positive body image encompasses body appreciation, body acceptance, respect for the body's needs, appreciation of body functionality, and the ability to maintain a favourable view of one's body despite sociocultural appearance pressures (Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015a). Individuals with a positive body image tend to appreciate their bodies for what they can do rather than focusing exclusively on appearance and are better able to resist unrealistic appearance ideals. This perspective may be particularly relevant during the postpartum period. Pregnancy, childbirth, breastfeeding, and infant care can substantially alter women's bodies and challenge appearance-based evaluations of self. At the same time, women are frequently exposed to societal expectations regarding rapid physical recovery and a return to their pre-pregnancy appearance (Liechty et al., 2018). Positive body image theory suggests that fostering body appreciation, self-compassion, and appreciation of body functionality may help women adapt more successfully to these changes than approaches focused solely on reducing body dissatisfaction (Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015a). Indeed, the postpartum body is not only an object of appearance but also a body that has undergone pregnancy and childbirth and may be nourishing and caring for an infant.

Consequently, interventions that encourage women to appreciate their bodies' capabilities, resilience, and functionality may be particularly beneficial during this life stage (Alleva et al., 2015; Cerea et al., 2024). However, postpartum body image is influenced by a range of relational, psychological, and sociocultural factors. A recent systematic review (Siqueira et al., 2025) identified sociocultural influences, social support, and breastfeeding as key determinants of postpartum body dissatisfaction. In addition, regression analyses included in the review identified maternal weight, depressive symptoms, maternal age, mode of delivery, and sexual functioning as significant correlates of postpartum body dissatisfaction. Among these factors, sociocultural influences appear to play a particularly important role. Postpartum body dissatisfaction has been associated with perceived appearance pressure from partners, friends, and the media, with these associations often mediated by the internalization of the thin ideal (Lovering et al., 2018). Similarly, greater internalization of appearance ideals and partner appearance pressure have been associated with higher body dissatisfaction among postpartum women (Rodgers et al., 2022). In contrast, greater self-compassion, defined as an attitude of kindness and understanding toward one's own difficulties, encompassing mindfulness, selfkindness, and common humanity (Neff, 2003), has consistently been associated with more positive body image (Turk & Waller, 2020). Together, these findings highlight the need for interventions that not only address body dissatisfaction but also strengthen women's ability to resist unrealistic appearance expectations, cultivate self-compassion, and develop a more positive relationship with their postpartum bodies.

Evidence suggests that psychological interventions can improve body image and related outcomes during the postpartum period. A recent systematic scoping review found beneficial effects of cognitive-behavioural, mindfulness-, and self-compassion-based interventions delivered both face-to-face and through digital platforms (Blinded for per-review, 2026). These interventions improved body image, body appreciation, self-esteem, sexual satisfaction, and body-related distress. Digital delivery formats, including websites, mobile applications, and social media-based programmes, appeared particularly promising because they offer flexibility and accessibility for mothers who may face time, transportation, and childcare constraints.

Beyond practical advantages, digital interventions may also reduce feelings of isolation by

providing women with access to information, support, and experiences shared by other mothers, thereby fostering a sense of connectedness during the postpartum period.

Despite these encouraging findings, the review also identified several important gaps (Blinded for per-review, 2026). Existing interventions vary considerably in their theoretical foundations and intervention components. Most were based on cognitive-behavioural, mindfulness, selfcompassion, or psychoeducational approaches, whereas only one intervention was explicitly developed within a positive body image framework (Becker et al., 2022) and another incorporated selected positive body image elements within a broader self-compassion intervention (Papini et al., 2022). Consequently, interventions specifically targeting core positive body image constructs, such as body appreciation, body functionality, and resistance to sociocultural appearance pressures, remain scarce in the postpartum literature. Furthermore, evidence regarding long-term effectiveness remains limited. These findings highlight the need for theory-driven interventions that explicitly promote positive body image and can be delivered in an accessible format to postpartum women.

To address these gaps, we developed the Positive Body Image Digital Self-Help Programme (BODYPOSI), an online intervention grounded in positive body image theory and informed by evidence-based approaches, including self-compassion, appreciation of body functionality, and resistance to unrealistic appearance ideals. Delivered through a digital self-help format, BODYPOSI aims to provide flexible and accessible support for postpartum women while accommodating the practical demands of early motherhood.

Aims and research questions

The present RCT intends to test the developed online self-help intervention BODYPOSI, which applies concepts of positive body image theory (Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015a) as the programme's theoretical framework. The main aim of the study is to evaluate whether the BODYPOSI intervention improves the positive body image of postpartum women, and whether these effects are maintained at the three-month follow-up. The study will also examine whether improvements in positive body image mediate the effects of the intervention on psychological wellbeing. The following research questions are addressed: 1.

Design and methods

Study design and population

The study is designed as a two-arm RCT comparing the intervention group with a waitlist control group across baseline and immediate post-intervention assessment. Participants will be eligible if they are between 6 weeks and 12 months of postpartum and do not report having an eating disorder or other medical conditions that could substantially affect their body image perception. Participants assigned to the intervention condition will have immediate access to a four-week online positive body image self-help programme designed for women in the postpartum period. Participants in the waitlist control condition will receive access to the programme after four weeks, when the post-intervention assessment for both groups is completed. The intervention will be delivered through an online platform enabling participants to access the program remotely from any location with internet access. Outcomes will be measured at three time points for the intervention group: baseline before randomisation (T0), immediately after the intervention (T1), and three months after the intervention (T2).

Participants in the waitlist control group will complete assessments at T0 and T1. After this, they will receive access to the programme (Figure 1).

Recruitment and randomisation

Participants will be recruited online through social media groups and online communities for postpartum women, media dissemination, as well as collaborations with public figures and organisations that discuss postpartum well-being, perinatal mental health, motherhood, and body image. Interested women will be directed to the study website, where they will receive detailed information about the programme and study procedures.

After creating an account on the study platform and providing electronic informed consent, participants will complete eligibility screening and baseline assessment. Once T0 is completed, participants will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the waitlist control group using an automated randomisation algorithm integrated into the online platform. Due to the nature of the intervention, participant blinding will not be possible.

Statistical power

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.6; Faul et al., 2007) to determine the required sample size for this study. Given that brief digital psychological interventions typically yield small-to-moderate effects (Groot et al., 2023), the analysis was based on an effect size of f = 0.15, with α set at 0.05 and statistical power at 0.80. To approximate the planned linear mixed-effects model, the sample size calculation was conducted using the repeated-measures ANOVA within-between interaction option. The calculation was performed for two groups, two measurement points, an assumed correlation of 0.50 between repeated measures, and a nonsphericity correction of 1, indicating a required total sample size of 90 participants. To account for an anticipated attrition rate of approximately 25-35%, which is common in digital intervention studies (Linardon & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz, 2020), the target sample size was increased to 140 participants, with 70 women allocated to each group. The sample size was determined based on the primary intervention-effectiveness analysis. Mediation analyses are secondary and will be interpreted as exploratory, as the study was not specifically powered to detect indirect effects.

Statistical analyses

Prior to the statistical analysis, the data will be screened for missing values, outliers, and distributional characteristics relevant to the planned statistical models. Descriptive statistics will be calculated for all study variables. Linear mixed-effects models will be estimated using maximum likelihood estimation, allowing participants with missing data to be included (under the assumption that the data are missing at random). All analyses will be performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 1998 -2017) version 8.6 with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals will also be reported.

The primary intervention effect will be evaluated using linear mixed-effects models comparing the intervention and waitlist control groups from T0 to T1. These models will include time, group, and the group × time interaction as fixed effects, with a random intercept for participants to account for repeated measurements. The group × time interaction will be considered the main indicator of intervention efficacy. Estimated marginal means will be used to examine within-and between-group differences, and Bonferroni correction will be applied for multiple comparisons.

The maintenance of intervention effects within the intervention group will be examined using linear mixed-effects models, which will include T0, T1, and T2 assessments. Time will be included as a fixed effect, with a random intercept for participants. Planned pairwise comparisons will be conducted to evaluate changes from T0 to T1, from T1 to T2, and from T0 to T2. Bonferroni correction will be applied to control for multiple testing.

In addition, exploratory analyses will be conducted to examine whether changes in positive body image are associated with changes in postnatal depressive symptoms, postpartum anxiety, self-compassion, and general health status over time. If adequately powered, mediation analyses will be conducted to examine whether improvements in positive body image mediate the effects of the intervention on psychological outcomes.

The intervention

Framework

The intervention is grounded in the conceptual framework of positive body image proposed by Tylka and Wood-Barcalow (2015a). According to this framework, positive body image is conceptualized not merely as the absence of body dissatisfaction, but as a distinct and multifaceted construct characterised by body appreciation, body acceptance and love, broad conceptualizations of beauty, adaptive appearance investment, inner positivity, and the ability to interpret appearance-related information in a body-protective manner.

The framework emphasises the appreciation of the body's functionality and uniqueness, the engagement in self-care behaviours, and the development of a compassionate and accepting relationship with the body despite sociocultural appearance pressures. It also highlights the importance of protective psychological processes, such as resisting unrealistic appearance ideals and critically evaluating appearance-related messages (Tylka & Wood-Barcalow, 2015a). This approach may be particularly relevant for postpartum women, who often experience substantial bodily changes alongside sociocultural and self-imposed pressures to rapidly return to their pre-pregnancy appearance following childbirth.

Intervention development

Given the multidimensional nature of positive body image, the intervention was designed to target the cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and reflective processes related to body image. To achieve this, the programme incorporates multiple approaches, including psychoeducation, reflective writing, mindfulness-based activities, and self-compassion exercises. Previous research on body image interventions suggests that combining diverse approaches may help address multiple aspects of body image simultaneously while also enhancing engagement and acceptability of digital formats (Guest et al., 2019; Conboy et al., 2024).

Initially, 22 tasks targeting different components of positive body image were developed by the study authors using a variety of task approaches. The tasks were discussed and refined to improve the clarity, diversity, logical structure, and sensitivity of the content. Subsequently, 12 tasks were selected based on their ability to cover the key components of positive body image, while maintaining diversity of activities and clear instructions. The order of the tasks was determined through discussions among the study authors, taking into account the logical progression of content, task depth, and the sensitivity for participants without prior knowledge of positive body image concepts.

Structure and content

BODYPOSI was developed to address the limited availability of positive body image interventions specifically tailored for postpartum women (Blinded for per-review, 2026). It is a four-week digital self-help intervention consisting of 12 tasks, which are delivered through an online platform, with participants completing three tasks per week. The programme includes psychoeducational, reflective, and experiential activities aimed at promoting a positive body image during the postpartum period. The tasks are organised in a gradual, logical sequence, beginning with psychoeducation and body image awareness and progressing toward deeper reflection, body acceptance, and the long-term maintenance of positive body image practices.

Pilot study

Initial testing of the programme's technical functionality, clarity of instructions, logical structure, questionnaire accuracy, and overall usability was conducted with eight Health Psychology Master's students using a structured online questionnaire containing open-ended questions. The feedback indicated that the registration process was straightforward, the instructions and order of tasks were logical and easy to follow, and the content was engaging, clear, and acceptable overall. Based on participants' feedback, several revisions were made to the programme. These mainly involved correcting typographical errors in the questionnaire items and standardizing font sizes to improve the programme's visual clarity.

Following the programme revisions, a pilot experimental study was conducted with 10 postpartum women using the same procedures planned for the main trial. Participants completed the baseline assessment (T0), the 4-week intervention, and the post-intervention assessment (T1). Following completion of the intervention, they participated in individual semi-structured interviews to explore their experiences with the programme, including its acceptability, perceived benefits, organisation, and questionnaire burden. The interviews were conducted via Microsoft Teams or Zoom, audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim.

The data were analysed using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022), guided by the key domains of intervention feasibility proposed by Bowen et al. (2009), to identify recurring patterns in participants' experiences. Overall, participants perceived the programme as feasible, acceptable, and relevant. They also reported that the questionnaires did not impose a significant burden and that the intervention promoted greater awareness and acceptance of postpartum body changes.

The qualitative interviews identified three main themes: (1) perceived improvements in body image and acceptance of postpartum body changes, (2) positive experiences with specific intervention components, particularly body functionality and mindfulness exercises, and (3) suggestions to improve the programme's flexibility and usability.

A recurring theme was that the programme encouraged participants to shift their attention from appearance-related concerns towards appreciation of their body's functionality and achievements during pregnancy and childbirth. One participant reflected: Other participants noted that restrictions on task completion, such as the automatic closure of weekly modules, reduced flexibility because they were sometimes unable to submit tasks or complete all programme activities. They suggested that allowing multiple tasks to be completed in one day and providing additional reminders would improve engagement and task completion.

Based on these findings, the programme was refined before the main trial. The revisions included redefining Week 1 to begin with the first completed task, adding a weekly countdown timer, removing the one-task-per-day limit while retaining the three-tasks-per-week cap, and improving reminder messages and access links to enhance clarity, usability, and participant engagement.

Discussion

The BODYPOSI study was developed to address several important gaps in the existing literature on postpartum body image interventions. Despite growing recognition of the importance of positive body image during the perinatal period, relatively few interventions have explicitly targeted positive body image constructs, and even fewer have adopted a comprehensive approach encompassing multiple dimensions of positive body image.

Furthermore, accessible interventions that can be integrated into the time constraints and responsibilities of early motherhood remain limited. The present study will evaluate the effectiveness of a brief digital self-help programme designed to promote positive body image while also examining its potential effects on broader psychological well-being. If effective, the BODYPOSI programme may provide an accessible, low-cost intervention that could be incorporated into routine postpartum care to support women's body image and psychological well-being.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. First, the use of a randomised controlled trial design with a waitlist control group provides a rigorous approach for evaluating the efficacy of a psychological intervention. Random allocation helps minimise selection bias and reduces the likelihood that observed differences between groups reflect pre-existing differences in motivation, symptom severity, or other confounding variables. Second, the BODYPOSI programme was developed based on contemporary theoretical and empirical evidence on positive body image and integrates multiple evidence-informed techniques targeting complementary mechanisms involved in postpartum body image adaptation, including body functionality appreciation, self-compassion, mindfulness, and reduction of sociocultural appearance pressures. This multidimensional approach addresses several components of positive body image rather than focusing on a single mechanism of change. Third, the internetbased delivery format offers substantial practical advantages. It enables women to participate from home at times convenient to them, which is particularly important for postpartum women who face significant time constraints due to infant care responsibilities. It also reduces barriers to recruitment and retention commonly encountered in clinic-based postpartum research, such as travel, childcare needs, and scheduling conflicts. Finally, the inclusion of a three-month follow-up assessment allows evaluation of the durability of treatment effects beyond immediate post-intervention improvements.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the use of a waitlist control group is ethically appropriate given the absence of an established evidence-based intervention for promoting positive body image in postpartum women, it does not control for non-specific intervention effects, such as participants' expectations or the additional attention associated with receiving an intervention. Consequently, while the study will determine whether the BODYPOSI programme is more effective than no intervention, it will not be possible to determine the extent to which the observed effects are attributable to the programme's specific therapeutic components rather than to these non-specific factors.

Moreover, the exclusion of women with diagnosed eating disorders, while methodologically prudent to ensure participant safety and avoid confounding with a distinct clinical condition, limits the generalisability of the findings. Many postpartum women experience subclinical eating disorder symptoms or disordered eating behaviours that may respond differently to the intervention. Additionally, recruitment is likely to attract women who are motivated to improve their body image and who have adequate internet access and digital literacy. Although the recruitment materials invite all eligible postpartum women to participate and emphasise contributing to the evaluation of the programme rather than only seeking help for body image concerns, some degree of self-selection bias is unavoidable and may limit the representativeness of the study sample.

Lastly, the 4-week duration of the intervention and the requirement to complete three short tasks per week may contribute to participant attrition, particularly among women experiencing high infant care demands, sleep deprivation, or postpartum mood difficulties. Although attrition rates will be monitored and reported transparently, differential dropout between groups could bias effect estimates. Primary analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, with all randomised participants included using appropriate methods to account for missing outcome data.

Despite these limitations, the BODYPOSI trial has the potential to provide important evidence regarding the efficacy of a brief, internet-based positive body image self-help programme for postpartum women. If effective, the BODYPOSI programme may offer an accessible and lowcost approach to promoting positive body image and psychological well-being among postpartum women and provide a foundation for larger-scale effectiveness studies and future implementation in postnatal care.
