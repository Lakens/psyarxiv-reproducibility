Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

This cross-sectional study examined which interpretations of shared adversity were associated with solidarity across sectarian boundaries during the 2026 hostilities in Lebanon. In a face-toface survey of 450 Lebanese adults (August 2026), we assessed common and intergroup threat, three formulations of inclusive victimhood, inter-sect contact, sectarianism, political interpretations, and hosting displaced people. In preregistered adjusted models, common threat was positively associated with inter-sect solidarity, whereas inter-sect and displacement-related threat were negatively associated. Common-humanity victimhood, which recognizes shared humanity despite differences in suffering, showed a substantial positive association with solidarity; beliefs that sects suffered equally showed no association. Global victimhood similarity was positively associated only when examined separately from common-humanity victimhood.

Contact frequency and quality were positively associated with solidarity, whereas sectarianism was negatively associated; the hypothesized common-threat × sectarianism interaction was not supported. Lebanon-Israel framing and having hosted displaced people were also positively associated with solidarity in their respective models. In exploratory omnibus models, commonhumanity victimhood, contact, and displacement-related threat retained independent associations, but common threat, sectarianism, and war framing did not. These correlational results distinguish recognizing shared vulnerability from perceiving other groups as threats and suggest that solidarity can coexist with recognition of different experiences of suffering.

Introduction

War poses a dilemma for divided societies: common danger may encourage solidarity across group boundaries while intensifying mistrust between groups. Social identity research links perceived common fate to mutual support (Drury, 2018), whereas intergroup threat research links perceived outgroup danger to unfavorable attitudes (Riek et al., 2006). The question is how people define who is threatened, interpret others' suffering, and determine whom to support.

We examine these distinctions in wartime Lebanon, where sectarian affiliation structures political representation and displacement creates demands for assistance across communities. We define inter-sect solidarity as affective concern for members of other sects and intentions to assist them. We distinguish perceived common threat from threats attributed to other communities, and recognition of shared victimhood from claims of equal suffering. Contact, sectarianism, political interpretations, and hosting provide additional perspectives on solidarity's boundaries.

Lebanon: Sectarian Divisions and Wartime Displacement

Lebanon's confessional power-sharing system embeds religious affiliation in political representation. The 1943 National Pact established sect-based conventions for senior offices, while the 1989 Taif Agreement restructured power sharing following the 1975-1990 civil war (Haddad, 2001). These arrangements have sustained sectarian categories as a basis of political competition (Delatolla, 2021). National belonging coexists with these divisions: studies in Lebanon have linked religious and national identifications to different orientations toward other sects and to divergent interpretations of civil-war victimization and responsibility (Licata et al., 2012; Zahreddine et al., 2026).

Hostilities involving Israel and Hezbollah since October 2023 escalated in 2024 and again in 2026, producing substantial displacement despite a November 2024 cessation-of-hostilities arrangement (Data Friendly Space, 2026; United Nations Security Council, 2026). By mid-August 2026, approximately 352,000 people remained internally displaced, most outside collective shelters (United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs [OCHA], 2026). Such circumstances make cooperation across sectarian boundaries consequential, while potentially heightening concerns over resources, security, and communal relations.

Common Threat and Sectarian Boundaries

Shared emergencies may make broader social categories salient. The social identity approach to emergencies links perceived common fate to shared identity and mutual support (Drury, 2018), while the common ingroup identity model suggests that inclusive categorization can reduce bias (Gaertner & Dovidio, 2000). Emphasizing Indian national identity experimentally increased Hindu participants' donations to Muslim beneficiaries (Charnysh et al., 2015), and inclusive identification strengthened solidarity with co-victimized workers (Subašić et al., 2011). These findings suggest that perceived shared vulnerability may broaden concern, although exposure to the same danger does not necessarily produce a subjective sense of common fate.

The same conflict may also heighten concerns about other communities. Perceived threats to resources, political influence, values, and identity are associated with less favorable outgroup attitudes (Riek et al., 2006); displacement can make resource competition and cultural change especially salient. Thus, perceiving a war as threatening all sects and perceiving other sects or displaced people as threatening one's own community are distinct appraisals that may relate to solidarity in opposite directions.

Sectarianism may further shape these associations. Group attachment does not necessarily imply outgroup hostility: in Lebanon, sectarian collective narcissism was associated with support for collective violence whereas ordinary sectarian identification was not independently associated with it (Abou-Ismail et al., 2023). Nevertheless, orientations that prioritize sectarian interests and advantage may restrict concern for other communities.

Sectarianism may therefore be negatively associated with inter-sect solidarity and may weaken the extent to which common threat corresponds to broader solidarity.

Inclusive Victimhood and Recognition of Difference

Beyond anticipating danger, people interpret how suffering is distributed across groups.

Inclusive victim consciousness recognizes connections between ingroup victimization and others' suffering (Vollhardt, 2015) and has been associated with support for refugees and immigrants and responsibility toward victims of violence (Vollhardt et al., 2016). A recent meta-analysis linked inclusive victimhood to less conflict-enhancing attitudes, in contrast with non-inclusive beliefs (Kljajić et al., 2026), while competitive victimhood has been associated with lower intergroup forgiveness (Noor et al., 2008).

Recognizing others' suffering does not require judging all groups' losses to be equal.

Inclusive narratives may acknowledge common experiences while preserving differences in severity, history, and recognition (Vollhardt et al., 2021). Such acknowledgment matters to victimized groups (Bilali & Vollhardt, 2019). Research on Lebanese civil-war memories likewise documented recognition of outgroup suffering alongside ingroup-centered responsibility attributions (Zahreddine et al., 2026). Recognizing another group as victimized is therefore conceptually distinct from asserting equivalence between its losses and one's own. Inclusive victimhood also differs in its referent: perceived equality among local groups, parallels with victimized groups elsewhere, or common humanity despite differences in suffering. General inclusive victimhood has been associated with willingness for some forms of contact with former adversaries in Bosnia-Herzegovina and the Basque Country (Rupar et al., 2022). We do not treat these formulations as established dimensions: each is represented by a theoretically motivated single item. Their associations offer an initial comparison of equality, similarity, and recognition of difference as correlates of solidarity.

Inter-Sect Contact, Hosting, and Political Interpretations

Intergroup contact offers a more interpersonal perspective. Meta-analytic research links contact to reduced prejudice (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006), including among individuals perceiving high intergroup threat (Van Assche et al., 2023). Frequency and quality nevertheless differ: negative encounters may be especially consequential for outgroup attitudes (Barlow et al., 2012).

In Lebanon, contact with Syrian refugees has been associated with more supportive hosting attitudes (Ghosn et al., 2019). Internal displacement may similarly combine opportunities for contact with practical assistance.

Political interpretations of war may also mark boundaries of collective concern. A Lebanon-Israel framing may foreground the country as a shared political community, whereas an Israel-Hezbollah framing may emphasize a particular military and political actor. The interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and neither measures national identification. Research on inclusive categorization (Gaertner & Dovidio, 2000) suggests examining whether such understandings of the conflict's scope correspond to inter-sect solidarity.

The Present Study and Hypotheses

We examined inter-sect solidarity in a cross-sectional, face-to-face survey of 450 Lebanese adults during the 2026 hostilities. The outcome combines affective concern and intentions to help members of other sects; both components were also analyzed separately. We distinguished common threat from inter-sect and displacement-related threats, and equal victimhood from global similarity and common-humanity victimhood. We also examined contact, sectarianism, war interpretations, and hosting. Because all measures were collected at one time point, the study tests associations rather than whether war increased solidarity.

The preregistered primary and secondary hypotheses concern associations rather than causal effects (Zahreddine, 2026).

Primary Hypotheses H1 (common threat).

Perceiving the war as threatening all Lebanese sects will be positively associated with inter-sect solidarity.

H2 (intergroup threat).

Inter-sect threat (H2a) and displacement-related threat (H2b) will be negatively associated with solidarity.

H3 (inclusive victimhood).

Equal-victimhood beliefs (H3a), global victimhood similarity (H3b), and common-humanity victimhood (H3c) will each be positively associated with solidarity.

H4 (contact).

Contact frequency (H4a) and positive contact quality (H4b) will each be positively associated with solidarity.

H5 (sectarianism).

Sectarianism will be negatively associated with solidarity.

H6 (moderation).

Sectarianism will weaken the positive association between common threat and solidarity.

Secondary Hypotheses H7 (war interpretations).

Endorsing a Lebanon-Israel interpretation will be positively associated with solidarity, whereas endorsing an Israel-Hezbollah interpretation will be negatively associated with solidarity.

H8 (hosting).

Current and former hosts will report greater solidarity than nonhosts. Among hosts, those who accommodated at least one person from another sect will report greater solidarity than those who hosted exclusively within their own sect (H8a).

Exploratory analyses examined war exposure, broader regional-conflict framing, and sectarianism as a moderator of inclusive-victimhood associations (Zahreddine, 2026).

Method Participants and Procedure

A cross-sectional survey of 450 Lebanese adults (18-73 years; M = 44.17, SD = 14.35; 50% women) was conducted by Information International from August 11 to 19, 2026.

Respondents were registered as Sunni Muslim (23.8%), Shia Muslim (24.0%), Druze (7.6%), or Christian (44.7%) and lived in Mount Lebanon (66.4%), Beirut (21.1%), or Saida city (12.4%).

They included currently displaced (7.6%), previously displaced (16.0%), and never-displaced (76.4%) participants (Table S1). Religious affiliation refers to the sect recorded in official civilregistry documents rather than an independently measured degree of religious identification.

Information International, a Lebanon-based survey research agency, administered the Arabic questionnaire face-to-face on tablets using 12 fieldworkers. Fieldwork was restricted to Beirut, the Baabda, Metn, Aley, and Chouf districts of Mount Lebanon, and Saida city.

According to the agency's methodological report, interviews were allocated geographically using 2020 resident-population figures; neighborhoods were selected to reflect confessional diversity; households were selected systematically using estimated building counts; and an adult within each household was selected using a birthday procedure. Of 638 households approached, 450 completed interviews, 178 refused, six were ineligible, and four were nonresponses after followup. The agency's reported 71.7% participation rate is calculated as 450/(450 + 178), excluding ineligible and other nonresponse cases. Because fieldwork areas were restricted and neighborhoods were selected partly to ensure confessional diversity, the sample is not nationally representative. Eligible respondents were Lebanese citizens aged at least 18 residing in Lebanon.

Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained, and no directly identifying information was collected. The University of Geneva approved the study (CUREG-2026-03-23).

Sample size was primarily budget-constrained; a sensitivity analysis indicated approximately 80% power to detect an incremental effect of f² ≈ .018 (two-sided α = .05).

Measures

Unless otherwise specified, focal attitudinal and behavioral-intention items used fivepoint response scales, with higher scores indicating higher levels of the construct. Responses of 'I do not know/have no opinion' (998) and 'Prefer not to answer' (999) were treated as missing.

Scores were calculated as item means under the completion rules in Table S2, which also reports reliability estimates. The questionnaire defines 'sect' using the Arabic term ṭāʾifa, referring to Lebanon's officially recognized religious sects, and the 'current war' as hostilities affecting Lebanon since 2024.

Background Characteristics and War-Related Experiences

Background variables included age, gender, education, family-income adequacy, officially registered sect, and residence. Displacement distinguished currently displaced, returned, and never-displaced respondents. Hosting distinguished current, former, and neverhosts; the questionnaire defined hosting as providing displaced people with accommodation free of charge. Among hosts, cross-sect hosting indicated whether at least one guest belonged to a different officially registered sect. War exposure covered injury (participant or close family), bereavement, and damage to a residence (participant or close family). These indicators were study-developed.

Threat Perceptions

Two study-developed single items assessed common threat ("The current war poses a threat to all Lebanese religious sects") and direct threat to one's own sect. Four inter-sect threat items covered cultural and value incompatibility, resource competition, and political influence (α = .67, ω = .69). Two displacement-related threat items assessed resource competition/tensions and cultural change associated with displaced people from other sects (inter-item r = .62; Spearman-Brown = .76). Inter-sect and displacement-related items were informed by realistic and symbolic intergroup threat measures (Stephan & Stephan, 2000; Stephan et al., 1998).

Sectarianism

Six items assessed sectarian pride and attachment, perceived ability to serve Lebanon better than other sects, political consideration of sectarian interests, preferred government quotas, and sect superiority. Five were adapted from Harb (2010); the superiority item followed Al-Amine et al. (2025). Items were averaged (α = .78, ω = .79). This composite therefore includes both identification-related items and positions concerning political advantage or superiority; it should not be interpreted as religious attachment alone.

War Interpretations

Three study-developed agreement items assessed interpretations of the war as primarily between Israel and Hezbollah, between Lebanon and Israel, or as part of a broader regional conflict. Participants could endorse multiple interpretations. The three items were entered simultaneously in the confirmatory war-interpretation model; their separate associations should not be read as choices among mutually exclusive accounts.

Inclusive Victimhood

Three single items assessed equal victimhood, global victimhood similarity, and common-humanity victimhood. Equal victimhood asserted that harm to one's sect was 'neither less nor more severe' than harm to other sects. Global similarity asked whether victimized groups worldwide had suffered in ways similar to one's sect. Common humanity acknowledged 'essential differences' between groups' suffering while affirming, in the administered Arabic wording, that 'a victim remains a victim, whether from my sect or another sect.' The global item was adapted from Vollhardt et al. (2016); the other items were developed with reference to inclusive victimhood research (Vollhardt, 2015; Vollhardt et al., 2021). The English reference questionnaire uses the phrase "whether here or there" for the final clause of the commonhumanity item. These formulations are conceptually distinct single indicators, not established multi-item scales; their exact wording is documented in the questionnaire.

Inter-Sect Contact

Contact frequency was assessed with one item asking how often participants interacted with members of other sects during the war (1 = Never,5 = Very often). Those reporting contact also rated its overall valence (1 = Mostly positive,5 = Mostly negative), reverse-coded so higher scores indicated more positive contact. The study-developed items reflected the frequencyquality distinction (Islam & Hewstone, 1993); contact quality was structurally missing for respondents reporting no contact.

Inter-Sect Solidarity and Ingroup Prioritization

Helping intentions comprised four items concerning practical assistance, donations, participation in community initiatives, and speaking out against unfair treatment of people from other sects (1 = Very unlikely,5 = Very likely; α = .84, ω = .85). Affective solidarity comprised concern and empathy toward war-affected members of other sects (inter-item r = .75; Spearman-Brown = .86). The items drew on work on outgroup solidarity, advocacy, and empathy (Glasford & Calcagno, 2012; Neufeld et al., 2019; Özkan et al., 2024). The primary score averaged the two subscale means with equal weight. Although the helping items concern possible actions, they measure self-reported intentions rather than observed behavior. A separate study-developed item measured ingroup help prioritization and was excluded from the solidarity composite.

Analytic Strategy

Preregistered hypotheses were tested in R 4.5.2 using multiple linear regression with HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. The threat dimensions and, separately, the three war interpretations were entered simultaneously; inclusive victimhood items were tested individually and jointly in a robustness model. Contact quality was analysed among respondents reporting contact, adjusting for frequency. For the common threat × sectarianism interaction, both predictors were centered around their respective means in the complete-case analytic sample; significant interactions were probed at -1 SD, the mean, and +1 SD of the moderator.

Confirmatory models adjusted for age, gender, education, economic situation, registered sect, residence, displacement, injury, bereavement, and property damage, except when a covariate was the focal predictor. Models were repeated for helping intentions and affective solidarity separately. Ingroup prioritization was examined using linear regression, with ordinal logistic regression as a sensitivity check.

All tests were two-tailed (α = .05). Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied separately to the 10 primary and four secondary directional hypothesis tests; support required the predicted coefficient direction and adjusted p < .05. Primary analyses used complete cases.

Where missing predictor or covariate data excluded over 5% of participants with observed outcomes, multiple imputation by chained equations (50 datasets) assessed robustness; structurally missing contact quality was not imputed. Diagnostics included residual plots, variance inflation factors, and Cook's distance.

Exploratory analyses examined war exposure, sect-group differences, sectarianism as a moderator of inclusive-victimhood associations, and models entering multiple psychological predictors together. The omnibus models included smaller complete-case samples than the preregistered models; differences between estimates are therefore descriptive and may reflect both added covariates and sample composition. Additional psychometric, subgroup, and factor analyses are reported in the Supplementary Materials. Confirmatory and exploratory findings were kept distinct.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

Overall inter-sect solidarity was relatively high (M = 3.94, SD = 0.87). Affective solidarity (M = 4.12, SD = 0.85) was somewhat higher than helping intentions (M = 3.74, SD = 1.03). Common-humanity victimhood was also strongly endorsed (M = 4.28, SD = 0.87), whereas equal-victimhood beliefs were more moderate (M = 2.91, SD = 1.34). Means, standard deviations, and Pearson zero-order correlations among the focal variables are presented in Table 1. More detailed descriptive statistics, including variable-level missingness, are reported in Table S3.

Tests of Inter-Sect Solidarity

Threat Perceptions (H1, H2a, H2b)

In the model in which the four threat indicators were entered simultaneously, perceiving the war as a common threat to all Lebanese sects was associated with greater inter-sect solidarity, b = 0.123, 95% CI [0.064, 0.182], β = .203, pBH < .001, supporting H1. In contrast, inter-sect threat was associated with lower solidarity, b = -0.124, 95% CI [-0.224, -0.023], β = -.129, pBH = .023, supporting H2a. Displacement-related threat showed a stronger negative association, b = -0.216, 95% CI [-0.290, -0.141], β = -.297, pBH < .001, supporting H2b. Direct threat to one's own sect was not independently associated with solidarity once the other threat dimensions and covariates were taken into account, b = 0.010, 95% CI [-0.052, 0.072], β = .017, p = .754.

Inclusive Victimhood (H3a-H3c)

The three forms of inclusive victimhood showed distinct associations with solidarity.

Equal-victimhood beliefs were not associated with solidarity, b = -0.013, 95% CI [-0.072, 0.047], β = -.019, pBH = .752; thus, H3a was not supported. Global victimhood similarity was positively associated with solidarity, b = 0.114, 95% CI [0.017, 0.212], β = .140, pBH = .027, supporting H3b. The strongest association was observed for common-humanity victimhood, b = 0.527, 95% CI [0.419, 0.635], β = .527, pBH < .001, supporting H3c.

A preregistered robustness model entered all three victimhood measures simultaneously (N = 420). Common-humanity victimhood remained associated with solidarity, b = 0.518, 95% CI [0.404, 0.633], β = .524, p < .001, whereas equal victimhood (β = -.005, p = .892) and global victimhood similarity (β = .019, p = .714) were not independently associated. Thus, global similarity's positive association in its separate adjusted model did not persist after accounting for common-humanity victimhood. This comparison does not by itself establish which psychological process underlies either response.

Inter-Sect Contact and Sectarianism (H4a-H6)

More frequent inter-sect contact was associated with greater solidarity, b = 0.236, 95% CI [0.169, 0.303], β = .344, pBH < .001, supporting H4a. Among participants reporting at least some inter-sect contact, more positive contact quality was also associated with greater solidarity while controlling for contact frequency, b = 0.296, 95% CI [0.210, 0.381], β = .346, pBH < .001, supporting H4b.

Higher sectarianism was associated with lower solidarity in its preregistered adjusted model, b = -0.228, 95% CI [-0.341, -0.116], β = -.194, pBH < .001, supporting H5. The common threat × sectarianism interaction, however, was not statistically significant, b = 0.007, 95% CI [-0.070, 0.084], β = .009, pBH = .857, and H6 was not supported. The data therefore did not establish the predicted moderation; they do not demonstrate equivalent common-threat associations across levels of sectarianism. Full primary hypothesis tests appear in Table 2, with complete models in Tables S6-S8.

Secondary Confirmatory Tests: War Interpretations and Hosting

H7 received partial support. Stronger endorsement of the interpretation that the conflict was primarily a war between Lebanon and Israel was associated with greater solidarity, b = 0.132, 95% CI [0.060, 0.204], β = .195, pBH < .001. The predicted negative association for interpreting the conflict primarily as a war between Israel and Hezbollah was not observed, b = -0.009, 95% CI [-0.093, 0.075], β = -.013, pBH = .835. The broader regional-conflict framing, which was exploratory, showed a positive but conventionally nonsignificant association with solidarity, β = .114, p = .052.

Participants who were currently hosting or had previously hosted displaced people reported greater solidarity than those who had never hosted displaced people, b = 0.501, 95% CI [0.318, 0.683], β = .201, pBH < .001, supporting H8. Among current and former hosts, however, hosting at least one person from another sect was not associated with greater solidarity compared with hosting only members of one's own sect, b = -0.144, 95% CI [-0.509, 0.220], β = -.126, pBH = .571, N = 62. H8a was therefore not supported; this host-only comparison was also substantially less precise because of the small analytic sample. The four secondary directional tests are summarized in Table 3. Complete coefficient estimates for the war-interpretation and hosting models are reported in Table S9.

Secondary Outcomes and Robustness Checks

The analyses were repeated separately for helping intentions and affective solidarity. The overall pattern was largely consistent across the two components. Common threat was positively associated with both helping intentions (β = .132, p = .008) and affective solidarity (β = .260, p < .001), and displacement-related threat was negatively associated with both outcomes (β = -.210 and -.361, respectively, both p < .001). Inter-sect threat was negatively associated with helping intentions (β = -.189, p < .001) but not affective solidarity (β = -.037, p = .504).

The victimhood measures again showed differentiated associations. Equal victimhood predicted neither component. Global victimhood similarity was associated with affective solidarity (β = .246, p < .001), but not helping intentions (β = .031, p = .570). Common-humanity victimhood was associated with both helping intentions (β = .382, p < .001) and affective solidarity (β = .612, p < .001). The latter association was stronger, raising the possibility of shared evaluative content between common-humanity victimhood and feelings of concern or empathy; the former indicates that the pattern is not confined to the affective subscale. Contact frequency and positive contact quality were associated with both components, and sectarianism was negatively associated with both. Lebanon-Israel framing was associated with affective solidarity (β = .297, p < .001) but not helping intentions (β = .091, p = .107). Hosting was positively associated with both helping intentions (β = .208, p < .001) and affective solidarity (β = .153, p < .001). Full models appear in Tables S10-S17.

Analyses of the separate ingroup-prioritization outcome provided convergent evidence for several focal predictors. Inter-sect threat was associated with stronger ingroup prioritization (β = .141, p = .021), positive contact quality with lower ingroup prioritization (β = -.136, p = .014), and sectarianism with stronger ingroup prioritization (β = .183, p = .002). Ordinal-logistic sensitivity analyses yielded the same substantive conclusions for these focal main effects. One difference emerged for the common threat × sectarianism interaction: the interaction was not significant in the linear model (β = .091, p = .113) but was significant in the ordinal model (OR = 1.21, 95% CI [1.01, 1.46], p = .039). This sensitivity result concerns the separate ingroupprioritization outcome and does not alter the conclusion that the H6 interaction for inter-sect solidarity was unsupported. Full linear and ordinal-logistic results for ingroup prioritization are reported in Table S18.

The specified multiple-imputation criterion was triggered for the threat model, the globalsimilarity model, and the common threat × sectarianism interaction model. Across 50 imputed datasets, the substantive conclusions of the complete-case analyses were unchanged: the effects of common threat, inter-sect threat, displacement-related threat, and global victimhood similarity remained statistically significant in the same directions, whereas the common threat × sectarianism interaction remained nonsignificant. Model diagnostics did not indicate problematic multicollinearity or influential observations that altered the main conclusions. Model-specific complete-case loss is reported in Table S4, full multiple-imputation results in Tables S19-S20, and model diagnostics in Table S21.

Additional Exploratory Analyses

Exploratory omnibus models entered focal substantive predictors together with background covariates. In the broad model (N = 372), common-humanity victimhood (β = .426, partial R² = .232, p < .001), contact frequency (β = .302, partial R² = .150, p < .001), and displacement-related threat (β = -.151, partial R² = .031, p = .003) retained independent associations with solidarity. Common threat, sectarianism, and Lebanon-Israel framing did not retain statistically significant independent associations in this specification. In the contact-quality subsample (N = 312), common-humanity victimhood (β = .402, p < .001), positive contact quality (β = .189, p < .001), contact frequency (β = .170, p < .001), inter-sect threat (β = -.136, p = .028), and displacement-related threat (β = -.124, p = .041) were independently associated.

These models are exploratory, involve different complete-case samples, and should not be interpreted as formal tests of mediation or causal priority (Table S27). None of the three exploratory inclusive victimhood × sectarianism interactions was significant (all p ≥ .584; Table S23).

Exploratory comparisons indicated higher mean solidarity in the broad Muslim-category group (Sunni, Shia, and Druze respondents combined) than among Christian respondents (M = 4.11 vs. 3.72, pBH < .001; Table S28), but the pattern varied across the constituent registered sects (Tables S29-S30). Only the common-humanity victimhood × broad-group interaction survived multiplicity correction (b = .207, SE = .053, 95% CI [.102, .312], pBH = .002; Table S31). These unplanned comparisons do not establish a uniform difference between religious populations: the broad categories combine heterogeneous communities, the geographic sample is restricted, and measurement equivalence was not established.

Exploratory comparisons also indicated variation in solidarity across region and familyincome categories. Mean solidarity was higher in the Saida analytic category than in Mount Lebanon (M = 4.23 vs. 3.89, pBonf = .001; Tables S32-S33), and it was lower among participants whose income covered needs and allowed saving than in either of the other two income groups (Tables S34-S35). These differences are unadjusted subgroup comparisons, not representative estimates of geographic or socioeconomic disparities.

Unadjusted comparisons showed higher reported solidarity among participants who had ever been displaced (M = 4.40 vs. 3.79), reported residential damage (M = 4.48 vs. 3.85), or reported injury and/or bereavement (M = 4.59 vs. 3.91; Table S36). The last comparison included only 19 exposed respondents. These exploratory associations should not be interpreted as effects of wartime exposure. Additional zero-order, psychometric, moderation, exposure, and subgroup results appear in Table S5, Tables S22-S26, and Figures S1-S3.

Discussion

We examined whether solidarity across Lebanon's politically salient sectarian boundaries differed according to interpretations of a shared wartime crisis. In preregistered models, perceived common threat and common-humanity victimhood were positively associated with solidarity, while inter-sect and displacement-related threats were negatively associated; equal victimhood showed no association. Exploratory omnibus models identified the most persistent independent associations for common-humanity victimhood, contact, and displacement-related threat. These patterns do not demonstrate that war unified communities. Instead, they distinguish appraisals of danger and suffering associated with concern and helping intentions in a divided political setting (Delatolla, 2021).

Shared Danger and the Boundaries of Collective Concern

Social identity accounts associate perceived common fate with shared identity and mutual support (Drury, 2018; Gaertner & Dovidio, 2000). Consistent with this perspective, common threat was positively associated with solidarity in the preregistered threat model. Yet inter-sect and displacement-related threats showed negative associations in the same model, whereas threat to one's own sect was not independently associated. These contrasting findings suggest that the perceived scope and source of danger matter beyond a generalized sense of insecurity, connecting shared-fate accounts to intergroup threat research (Riek et al., 2006).

Lebanon's sectarian political divisions (Delatolla, 2021) help explain why shared external danger may coexist with fears of competition over resources or assistance. Respondents could perceive national vulnerability while anticipating disadvantages for their sect. This possibility highlights the political interpretation of insecurity alongside its intensity, but the study did not measure national identification, shared-identity recategorization, or a causal sequence from threat to solidarity.

Research on co-victimization similarly suggests that shared exposure is not sufficient for solidarity without inclusive identity (Subašić et al., 2011). Moreover, common threat was no longer independently associated with solidarity in exploratory omnibus models, whereas displacement-related threat remained negatively associated. Because these models also used smaller complete-case samples, the attenuation cannot be attributed solely to the additional predictors. The preregistered association should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of a stand-alone mechanism.

Inclusive Victimhood Without Equivalence of Suffering

The victimhood findings distinguish perceived equality of suffering from recognition of shared humanity despite differences. Inclusive victim consciousness has been associated with support for other victimized communities (Vollhardt, 2015; Vollhardt et al., 2016), but its formulations were not interchangeable here. Equal victimhood was unrelated to solidarity, while common-humanity victimhood remained strongly associated when the items were modeled jointly. The common-humanity item explicitly acknowledged 'essential differences' in suffering.

This pattern is consistent with inclusiveness without presumed equivalence, but does not show that acknowledgment of inequality caused greater solidarity.

The two formulations also require different judgments: equal victimhood compares the severity of losses, while common humanity permits recognition without agreement on that comparison. Lebanese civil-war research found recognition of outgroup victimization alongside group-centered attributions of responsibility (Zahreddine et al., 2026). The present findings extend that question to contemporary solidarity, suggesting that concern for others may coexist with disagreement about relative suffering. Respondents' reasons for endorsing either item, however, were not assessed.

Prior research emphasizes the importance of recognizing groups' particular experiences (Bilali & Vollhardt, 2019; Vollhardt et al., 2025). A common-humanity formulation may preserve such recognition where an equality claim appears to flatten differences. Yet we did not measure perceived acknowledgment, denial, or participants' interpretations of the wording. The null equal-victimhood association therefore does not show that equality beliefs are harmful.

Future research should examine when recognition of shared experience is compatible with distinct historical and contemporary losses.

Global victimhood similarity was positively associated with solidarity in its separate preregistered model but not when all victimhood items were modeled jointly or in the exploratory omnibus. Links between generalized inclusive victimhood and contact (Rupar et al., 2022), or victim-category accessibility (Sharvit & Kremer-Sharon, 2023), offer possible explanations that were not tested here. Common-humanity responses may also overlap conceptually with compassion or universalistic concern, especially the empathy component of solidarity. The association with helping intentions argues against a solely empathy-item association but does not establish discriminant validity or actual helping. Experiments could vary recognition of different levels of harm independently of appeals to common humanity.

Contact and Hosting Under Conditions of Displacement

Contact frequency and positive quality were associated with solidarity and retained independent associations in the contact-subsample omnibus model. These results extend contact research (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006) to wartime concern and helping intentions. Positive contactattitude associations have also been documented under high perceived threat (Van Assche et al., 2023), but our study did not test threat as a moderator of contact. Its contribution concerns a different outcome in a conflict setting, not evidence for that moderation process. Contact during displacement may involve accommodation, work, or everyday encounters across sectarian boundaries. Such interactions could personalize others' needs and complement common-humanity beliefs. Nevertheless, independent associations do not demonstrate this sequence: respondents already inclined toward solidarity might seek more contact or perceive it more positively. Frequency also need not imply cooperation, and negative contact can have especially adverse associations with attitudes (Barlow et al., 2012).

Hosting was associated with greater solidarity, but selection into offering free accommodation may reflect prior concern, housing resources, or networks. The cross-sect versus own-sect hosting comparison was imprecise (N = 62), so its null finding cannot establish equivalence. Lebanese research on contact with Syrian refugees (Ghosn et al., 2019) and experimental contact evidence in another setting (Mousa, 2020) provide relevant, though not directly equivalent, context. Future research should distinguish who hosts, who is helped, the quality of interactions, and whether intentions translate into sustained assistance.

Sectarian Commitments and Political Interpretations of the War

Sectarianism was negatively associated with solidarity in its separate adjusted model, but not independently associated in the exploratory omnibus. The measure combined attachment with political advantage and superiority, and thus cannot isolate the role of religious identification. Prior work distinguishes ordinary identification from collective narcissism (Abou-Ismail et al., 2023; Licata et al., 2012). The unsupported common threat × sectarianism interaction likewise does not establish uniform associations across sectarianism levels. More differentiated measures are needed.

A Lebanon-Israel interpretation was positively associated with solidarity, whereas the predicted negative association for an Israel-Hezbollah interpretation was not observed. Both were entered in the same model; their differing significance does not establish a statistically tested difference. National-identity salience experiments offer relevant context (Charnysh et al., 2015), but a war-framing item is not a measure of national identification. Seeing Hezbollah as a combatant need not exclude concern for civilians. This remains an interpretation of the null rather than evidence about respondents' political allegiances or reasoning.

Exploratory comparisons also caution against treating sectarian categories as fixed dispositions. The broad Muslim comparison group included Sunni, Shia, and Druze respondents with heterogeneous patterns. The common-humanity association differed between broad groups after multiplicity adjustment, but aggregation, restricted score variation, and untested measurement equivalence limit interpretation. In Saida, relatively high mean solidarity cooccurred with higher ingroup prioritization at the group level; this does not demonstrate their coexistence within individuals. These findings cannot establish intrinsic differences in generosity among religious communities.

Theoretical and Practical Implications

Taken together, the findings suggest that solidarity in divided societies requires distinguishing shared vulnerability, perceived intergroup competition, equivalence of suffering, interpersonal connection, and political interpretation. These are related orientations, not interchangeable signs of a unified response. Inclusive identity theory suggests why broader categories may extend concern (Gaertner & Dovidio, 2000); inclusive-victimhood research highlights the importance of acknowledging particular experiences (Vollhardt, 2015); and contact research identifies interpersonal associations with more supportive relations (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). Lebanon brings these processes into focus within an institutional order that sustains sectarian divisions while displacement creates needs across them. In exploratory omnibus models, common-humanity victimhood, contact, and displacement-related threat showed the most persistent independent associations; preregistered associations for common threat, sectarianism, and war framing were more model-dependent. The contribution is to clarify these distinctions, not to infer institutional reconciliation or political change from reported concern and intentions.

Displacement-related threat remained associated with solidarity in exploratory omnibus models, whereas exploratory income comparisons did not show a corresponding threat gradient; respondents whose income permitted saving reported lower solidarity. Unadjusted comparisons also linked displacement and property damage with higher solidarity. Thus, economic hardship should not automatically be equated with exclusion, nor do these findings establish that hardship promotes generosity. Interventions could separately test material support for receiving communities, recognition of differentiated suffering, and opportunities for cooperative contact.

Their effectiveness is not demonstrated by this survey.

Limitations and Future Directions

The cross-sectional design cannot establish whether threat appraisals, victimhood beliefs, and contact precede or follow solidarity. Preregistration, covariate adjustment, robust standard errors, and multiple-imputation checks strengthen transparency, not causal identification. The exploratory omnibus models also used smaller complete-case samples, so attenuation may reflect added predictors, sample composition, or both. Fixed-sample nested-model comparisons and prospective designs are needed to clarify these differences; such nested comparisons are not reported here.

Most victimhood, framing, common-threat, and contact indicators were single items.

Inter-sect threat showed modest reliability, and sectarianism combined attachment with hierarchy-related orientations. The common-humanity item may overlap with compassion or universalistic concern, particularly the empathy component of solidarity. Associations with helping intentions do not establish discriminant validity, and neither outcome measures actual assistance. Ceiling effects and social desirability may further shape responses. Future work should use multi-item indicators, test discriminant validity and measurement invariance, include observed helping, and independently manipulate acknowledgment of unequal suffering and common humanity.

Generalization is limited by fieldwork in Beirut, selected districts of Mount Lebanon, and Saida city, with neighborhoods chosen partly for confessional diversity. Systematic household and birthday-based respondent selection within these areas does not make the sample nationally representative. Broad Christian-Muslim comparisons conceal sectarian heterogeneity, and the referent of 'other sects' differs across respondents. Region, income, and exposure analyses are exploratory; the injury/bereavement subgroup had only 19 exposed participants. Future studies should specify recipient groups, measure local resource and contact opportunities, and preregister adequately powered tests of group differences and moderation.

Conclusion

These cross-sectional findings do not establish that war increased solidarity. In the sampled areas of Lebanon, common-humanity victimhood, inter-sect contact, and lower displacement-related threat showed the most consistent independent associations with affective concern and helping intentions in exploratory models. Solidarity may therefore coexist with recognition of differences in groups' experiences of suffering rather than depend on claims of equal suffering. Whether these orientations shape actual assistance and political cooperation requires longitudinal and experimental research.
