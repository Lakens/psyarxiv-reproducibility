Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

2.2.1. Google Trends as a Measurement Tool

The primary measurement tool was Google Trends (GT), which enables the analysis of changes in search interest for specific queries across selected geographic areas and time periods. The use of GT as a source of information on dream content has a precedent in the Google Trends project "The Shape of Dreams" (Fragapane, 2020).

One advantage of GT is that it enables the observation of spontaneous, natural behaviors directly related to specific dream content across large populations and over long periods. In this sense, a search query and a dream diary entry share a similar observational status: both constitute a verbal trace of specific dream content. GT, however, allows researchers to detect whether independently occurring external events (e.g., highly publicized volcanic eruptions) are accompanied by systematic changes in the frequency of dream-related queries. For research questions formulated in this way, GT-based methodology is better suited than traditional approaches such as dream diaries. Identifying individual characteristics of searchers or employing independent judges to code content is unnecessary. What matters is simply whether, following a particular event, the frequency of verbal traces left by dreams increases.

GT does not report raw search volumes for a given phrase. For a researcherdefined time period (e.g., 2012-2024, comprising 156 months) and geographic area (e.g., the Philippines), it draws a representative sample of all searches. It then determines the proportion of this sample consisting of searches for the target phrase (e.g., "volcano dream meaning") in each of the 156 months. This produces 156 values representing the ratio of target searches to all searches conducted in a given month. The tool then scales this sequence of proportions to a 0-100 index over the specified period (here, 156 months). A value of 100 indicates the month with the highest ratio of searches for the given phrase to all searches during the analyzed period, while the remaining values are proportionally lower (e.g., 50 indicates search interest half as high as that in the peak month). A value of 0 indicates insufficient data for a given point, which may result either from an absence of searches or from search volume that is too low to be reflected by the tool (Google, n.d.).

When several phrases (e.g., "football" and "volcano") are analyzed within the same GT query, they are scaled jointly, and their values can therefore be compared directly. Thus, a value of 90 for one phrase indicates relative search interest nine times as high as a value of 10 for another phrase.

For phrases with relatively high underlying search volumes, GT results are stable, and repeated sampling yields very similar time-series patterns. Conversely, for queries with low underlying search volumes, GT samples are less stable, and repeated retrievals of the same series may return somewhat different values, with some observations taking a value of 0. For this reason, in analyses sensitive to sampling variability (Hypotheses 2 and 4), each relevant query panel was retrieved 12 times, following the recommendations of Eichenauer et al. (2022).

For the same reason, in disaster analyses conducted at the regional or national level, where the more restrictive "X dream meaning" queries could yield sparse time series, broader "X dream" queries were used (e.g., "volcano dream," "earthquake dream," "tornado dream"). In worldwide analyses and all comparisons between disasters and sports, the more restrictive and unambiguous "X dream meaning" queries were employed. This stricter approach was also necessary for sports comparisons, because phrases such as "football dream" or "boxing dream" may refer to aspirations or ambitions unrelated to sleep.

To avoid inconsistencies arising from cross-linguistic comparisons, English was used exclusively as the search language throughout the study.

The study analyzed "X" queries (e.g., "volcano," "football," "boxing") as behavioral indicators of users' attention to a given topic, whereas dream-related queries (e.g., "X dream meaning") were treated as population-level indicators of attention to the corresponding dream content.

An individual search need not arise from the user's own dream-it may concern another person's dream or stem from another source. For analyses of temporal trends, however, what matters is the aggregate change in the frequency of such queries; an increase in this frequency was interpreted as a population-level indicator of an increased presence of the corresponding dream content.

2.2.2. Statistical Analysis Procedure

We tested (1) whether the occurrence of a disaster was accompanied by an increase in search interest for that disaster, (2) whether the occurrence of a disaster was accompanied by an increase in searches for disaster-related dreams, and (3) whether an increase in search interest for a given disaster was accompanied by an increase in searches for such dreams.

Three types of disasters were selected for analysis: earthquakes, tornadoes, and volcanic eruptions. These events were chosen because they typically have a clear, discrete onset and are difficult to predict far in advance, allowing for more precise observation of changes in search behavior. Floods do not meet this criterion equally well as they may develop gradually. Tsunamis were also excluded because their occurrence is often directly linked to earthquakes, which could result in partial overlap between the two event categories.

The method involved compiling a list of actual disasters and comparing the timing of these events with search patterns reflected in GT data. Accordingly, three research hypotheses were formulated: H1. The occurrence of an actual disaster will be accompanied by an increase in search interest in the corresponding phenomenon (e.g., a volcanic eruption will be accompanied by an increase in searches for "volcano").

H2.

The occurrence of an actual disaster will be accompanied by an increase in searches for disaster-related dreams (e.g., a volcanic eruption will be accompanied by an increase in searches for "volcano dream").

H3. An increase in search interest in a particular type of disaster will be accompanied by an increase in searches for the meaning of dreams related to that disaster (e.g., "volcano" -"volcano dream meaning").

2.2.3. Geographic Screening-Selection of Areas for Analysis

Hypothesis testing began by defining the analysis period as January 2012 through December 2024, comprising 156 consecutive months. For a time series of this length, GT aggregates data at a monthly resolution. Geographic units were then screened to identify areas affected by the analyzed disasters.

In the first step, the 249 geographic units included in the ISO 3166-1 standard (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?i31Grk.) were screened and those with populations of at least 10 million were selected. This criterion was intended to exclude countries that might yield insufficiently stable GT signal due to their small populations. Each selected unit was then assessed to determine whether at least one of the analyzed types of disasters had occurred within it (see Supplement S01).

For each country meeting these criteria, the usability of the English-language GT time series for disaster-related dream queries was then assessed. Based on pilot analyses, an arbitrary usability criterion was adopted: a series was considered usable if it contained at least eight months with values exceeding those of both adjacent months. If a series did not meet this criterion, the corresponding country-disaster combination was excluded from further analyses due to potential signal instability arising from GT sampling variability.

At this stage of the screening, five countries met the criteria: the United States and India (for all three disaster types), the Philippines (for earthquakes and volcanic eruptions), and Canada and Australia (for tornadoes). To mitigate the risk of local signals being diluted in geographically large countries, additional screening at a subnational level was conducted for the United States (9.83 million km²), India (3.29 million km²), Australia (7.69 million km²), and Canada (9.98 million km²); the Philippines (approximately 300,000 km²) was retained at the national level. This subnational screening led to the exclusion of India and Australia because no usable GT signal was obtained at the subnational level. Canada was also excluded because an independent, complete tornado registry covering the analyzed period was unavailable (see Supplement S01). Ultimately, the United States and the Philippines qualified for the event-based analyses. Within the United States, further narrowing of the analysis to the state level identified California (424,000 km²) for earthquakes and Texas (696,000 km²) for tornadoes. The full geographic screening procedure is detailed in Supplement S01.

2.4. Conclusions

The results indicate that people respond to natural disasters with increased searches for disaster-related dreams. The first indication of this association emerged in the U.S. "volcano dream" search series. This finding was subsequently supported by several distinct statistical analyses involving different disaster types, conducted both locally and worldwide. Associations were found between the occurrence of actual disasters and increases in searches for these dreams, as well as correlations between search interest in disasters and searches for such dreams. Thus, despite relying on different measures and levels of analysis, all analyses converged on the same conclusion: natural disasters are clearly associated with searches for disaster-related dreams.

Measurement validation.

The analyses also serve to validate the measurement procedure used in this study. Across all analyses, associations were observed in the expected direction, indicating that both the measurement tool and the procedure are sensitive to the phenomena under investigation.

2.5. Threat Simulation Theory

The observed pattern of results is consistent with both the general continuity hypothesis discussed above and Threat Simulation Theory (TST; Revonsuo, 2000; Valli & Revonsuo, 2009). TST posits that experiencing real threats during waking activates the threat simulation system (TSS). During sleep, this system generates perceptually and behaviorally realistic simulations of threatening situations. The repeated rehearsal of these simulations is theorized to have enhanced the ability to recognize, avoid, and cope with threats in the ancestral environment (Revonsuo, 2000; Valli & Revonsuo, 2009).

According to TST, dreaming is not a random or accidental reflection of waking experience, but a selective process in which threat-related content receives particular priority. Experiencing real threats during waking is hypothesized to increase TSS activity, and the intensity of the resulting dream simulations should be directly related to the degree of personal threat experienced in the original situation (Valli & Revonsuo, 2009).

Furthermore, the content of threat simulations may also derive from events learned about through the media: "These types of threats usually come to our knowledge through the mass media, and include elements such as exotic animals, war, floods and other natural catastrophes" (Valli & Revonsuo, 2009, p. 25). Subsequent research suggests that the presence of threats in dreams is associated with both threats and stress levels experienced during the preceding day (Lafrenière et al., 2018).

The present findings concerning natural disasters are consistent with these predictions: disasters constitute threatening events, and their occurrence was accompanied by an increase in search queries referring to dreams about the same type of event.

3. Sports in Dreams

Sporting events meet nearly all of the conditions considered conducive to the incorporation of waking-life content into dreams. Moreover, in many respects, they satisfy these conditions far more strongly than natural disasters do.

Major tournaments and competitions, as well as individual matches, capture attention for many days. Interest in sports is active, personal, and intentional: fans actively seek out information, anticipate the event, follow results, and repeatedly return to related content. Engagement is strong and personal-people invest time and money, travel long distances, form fan clubs, get team names tattooed, and build part of their identity around their teams. Sports evoke strong emotions, both positive and negative. The outcome of a competition remains unresolved for an extended period, and event-related content is repeatedly thought about and revisited. Sports-related content appears on bags of chips, in television advertisements, in public debates, and in statements by politicians. Exposure to it is simultaneously intense, direct, voluntary, prolonged, and linked to personal goals.

By contrast, the average person encounters natural disasters only incidentallytypically briefly and "in passing"-unintentionally, and without personal involvement or direct experience, with their attention drawn to them involuntarily.

TST predictions. The classification of threats employed in TST research is not limited to physical threats, but also encompasses threats to psychological well-being, subjectively experienced threats, and failures (Valli & Revonsuo, 2009). Natural disasters and sporting events therefore both fall within this classification, although they differ markedly in the strength and nature of the observer's personal involvement. Given these differences, one specific TST prediction becomes particularly relevant: "only real threatening events can fully activate the threat simulation system" (Valli & Revonsuo, 2009, p. 18), and the intensity of subsequent dream simulations should be directly related to the degree of personal threat experienced in the original situation (Valli & Revonsuo, 2009, p. 19). Based on TST's assumptions, sporting events should therefore provide a much stronger stimulus for generating intense threat simulations during sleep than brief media reports of disasters that remain both physically and psychologically remote.

Consequently, both the continuity hypothesis and TST raise the same question: how much more strongly are sporting events incorporated into dreams than natural disasters?

3.1. Method

The analysis examined the extent to which search interest in sports was reflected in search interest in sports-related dreams.

Three sports were first selected to preserve symmetry with the three disaster types analyzed. Football was chosen as a widely followed sport with global reach, for which interest is not concentrated primarily in any single country or region. Basketball was included as a second team sport to assess whether any pattern observed for football was specific to that sport. Boxing, by contrast, was selected as a qualitatively different sport-an individual sport based on direct physical competition, with a different event structure, symbolism, and mode of audience engagement. This selection made it possible to assess whether the observed effect applied to sports as a broader class of content rather than to the specific characteristics of a single discipline.

In ordinary searches, sports-related content overwhelmingly dominated disasterrelated content, accounting for 91.4% of the combined representation of the six analyzed topics, whereas disaster-related content accounted for only 8.6%. 3.3. Interpretation

Two aspects are particularly striking: the reversal of proportions and the magnitude of that reversal. Disaster-related content, despite being far less prominent than sportsrelated content in ordinary searches, strongly predominated in dream-related searches. The contrast was substantial: the relative representation of disasters was approximately 24 times greater. Thus, the factors identified by the continuity hypothesis as important for dreamcontent selection led to in incorrect predictions.

A potential source of bias should also be noted. GT may underestimate conscious attention directed toward sports because a substantial proportion of sports-related engagement occurs outside search engines. If so, the observed advantage of sports over disasters in ordinary searches is itself underestimated, meaning that the true magnitude of the anomaly may be even greater.

Why is content that satisfies the standard conditions for transfer to a lesser extenta media-reported disaster-more strongly represented in dreams than content that satisfies them far better-a sporting event?

3.3.1. People Do Not Dream About Sports

Perhaps people do not have sports-related dreams, or experience them only very rarely?

Research indicates that sports-related dreams occur regularly among a broad range of people (Bulkeley & Schredl, 2024; Erlacher & Schredl, 2010). The present study likewise demonstrates that people regularly enter queries such as "football dream meaning" and "basketball dream meaning" into search engines. The simplest possibility-that the low representation of sports-related dreams in searches reflects the fact that people simply do not dream about sports-can therefore be rejected. People do dream about sports.

3.3.2. Sporting Events Do Not Affect Dreams

Perhaps sports-related dreams have some particular property that prevents them from responding to daytime events?

An additional analysis of the dynamics of all six topics was conducted to determine whether sporting events are reflected at all in dream-related searches. Searches related to football and basketball dreams fluctuated in parallel with changes in search interest in the sports themselves. The boxing series was too sparse to yield a reliable result. Sports-related dreams are not "unresponsive."

Therefore, the large disparity in the relative representation of disaster-related and sports-related content in dream-related queries cannot be explained by a lack of responsiveness of sports-related dreams to sporting events. Such a response does occur.

Nevertheless, sports-related content remains far less strongly represented in dream-related searches relative to the intensity of waking attention it commands. Details of the analysis are provided in Supplement S07.

3.3.3. Sports-Related Dreams Are Obvious

Perhaps, then, sports-related dreams occur frequently but are simply not "mysterious" enough to require interpretation? "People may experience many sports-related dreams, but they consider them self-evident and therefore do not search for their meaning.

Hence the disparity in the observed searches."

An explanation based on the alleged obviousness of sports-related dreams raises further questions. What is the empirical basis for claiming that sports-related dreams are "obvious"? There is no scientific evidence to suggest that sports-related dreams are any less or more "mysterious" than dreams about animals, bodily injuries, disasters, or anything else.

If their alleged "obviousness" were supposed to stem from linking the dream to a recently watched match ("I dreamed about sports because I watched a match. Obviously."), the same logic should apply to disaster-related dreams ("I saw a volcanic eruption on the news, so I dreamed about a volcano. Obviously.").

There is no inherent reason why a sports-related dream should not contain ambiguous, unexpected, absurd, or symbolic elements. "That boxer looked like my father!

Why couldn't I move before taking the penalty kick?" Reasoning based on the alleged "obviousness" of sports-related dreams therefore has the structure of a classic just-so story: a known result is retrofitted with an explanation that could just as easily have been constructed for the opposite result-by invoking the ambiguous symbolism of athletic competition and the "simple obviousness of a smoking mountain" (Gould & Lewontin, 1979).

A strong empirical argument against the obviousness hypothesis emerges from a comparison with sexual dreams. If sports-related dreams are "obvious," then sexual dreams should be doubly so, and searches for their meaning should be even rarer. Yet, over the analyzed period (2012) (2013) (2014) (2015) (2016) (2017) (2018) (2019) (2020) (2021) (2022) (2023) (2024), the mean GT index for "sex dream meaning" was 57.20, compared with 3.89 for "football dream meaning" and 0.85 for "sport dream meaning"approximately 15 and 67 times higher, respectively (see Supplement S02).

The alleged obviousness fails to explain the observed disparity. The data suggest the existence of an underappreciated or previously unrecognized mechanism governing dream content selection. Such a mechanism is posited by Phantom Anxiety Theory (PAT), described in greater detail elsewhere (Florkowski, 2026c). The present study was designed as a test of this mechanism.

3.3.4. Phantom Anxiety Theory (PAT)

The theory distinguishes between two operating modes of the human anxiety system: a reactive mode, triggered by a consciously or unconsciously identified threat and extensively described in the existing literature, and an endogenous mode, which generates a state of anxious readiness independent of the immediate presence of a threat. Endogenous anxious arousal resembles the vigilance observed in animals whose survival depends on anticipating danger (Creel et al., 2014; Liley & Creel, 2008).

PAT proposes that, over millions of years, persistent exposure to lethal threats shaped the nervous systems of our ancestors, fundamentally embedding anxiety-based vigilance within them. Such undirected vigilance is adaptive in an environment saturated with lethal threats. However, in an environment largely devoid of ancestral existential threats, its evolutionary mismatch becomes apparent: anxious arousal continues to be generated despite the absence of corresponding threat objects. This arousal is referred to here as phantom anxiety, by analogy to phantom pain (Ramachandran & Hirstein, 1998).

Undirected alarm arousal is an unstable state because it strongly motivates the anticipation of potential threats; therefore, it is rapidly bound to any object capable of serving as an anxiety carrier. This endogenous alarm is not a passive state of arousal: it initiates active environmental scanning, preferential detection of information signaling potential threats, and the prioritization of such information. When suitable anxiety carriers are absent from the perceptual field, they may even be generated ex nihilo, as observed in a specific class of nightmares-generative nightmares (Florkowski, 2026b https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?cg85E4.

An anxiety carrier, however, must be sufficiently credible as a threat. An implausible object will quickly be recognized as fictitious and lose its regulatory function-such as the notion of evil Reptilians ruling the world-prompting the anxiety system to seek a more stable object.

The optimal anxiety carriers in a world largely devoid of immediate dangers are "safe threats" (Florkowski, 2026a)-objects sufficiently credible to provide a plausible justification for the underlying anxious arousal.

3.3.4.1. How PAT Predicts Dream Content

Distant natural disasters possess the properties of "safe threats," whereas sporting events do not. The latter may generate concerns about goal attainment, but they do not evoke concerns regarding personal safety. PAT therefore predicts that the endogenous mode of the anxiety system will assign special weight to disaster-related events, but not to sporting events.

The theory further assumes that sleep does not deactivate the selective vigilance of the anxiety system, because otherwise the system could not fulfill its alarm function: a sleeping mother is not awakened by a car passing outside the window, yet the quiet cry of her own child awakens her. During the day, a sporting event may capture conscious attention many times more strongly, but after sleep onset, many of the processes that confer significance on it cease to organize ongoing mental activity. The endogenous anxiety system, by contrast, remains active, and so do the contents to which it has previously assigned weight as potential threat objects. To put it figuratively: if trumpeter A plays ten times louder than trumpeter B, while both are playing, we hear mainly A. But when A falls asleep, we hear B.

PAT therefore predicts that natural disasters should be disproportionately represented in dreams relative to sporting events, beyond what would be expected based on their relative share of waking attention.

3.3.4.2. Further Predictions

The proposed mechanism generates additional predictions. If a disaster enters dreams because it serves as an effective carrier of phantom anxiety, its impact need not depend on posing an actual threat to the dreamer. Rather, the distance from the disaster may even facilitate its function as a "safe threat."

Two further hypotheses were therefore formulated:

H4. Disasters occurring thousands of kilometers from the observer and posing no direct threat to them leave a trace in searches for corresponding disaster-related dreams.

H5.

Visually dramatic disasters that cause no damage, or only minimal damage, also leave a trace in searches for corresponding disaster-related dreams.

3.3.4.3. Tests of Further Predictions

H4 was tested using search data from the United Kingdom, where English is widely spoken, residents experience only weak earthquakes that pose no direct threat, and volcanic eruptions do not occur; the disasters analyzed here occurred thousands of kilometers from the United Kingdom. The analysis employed the queries "volcano dream" and "earthquake dream." For both series, mean values from 12 independent GT retrievals were used (Eichenauer et al., 2022).

For 15 globally visible eruptions, identified based on worldwide peaks in GT interest in volcanoes, the mean value of "volcano dream" searches during the eruption month or the following month was 25.73, compared to an expected value of 18.50; the effect was in the predicted direction but did not reach statistical significance (p = .064). To maintain symmetry with the sample of 15 eruptions, the earthquake set comprised the 15 highest local peaks in worldwide GT interest in earthquakes. The mean value for "earthquake dream" was 34.31, compared to an expected value of 19.86 (p = .005; see Supplement S06). The results were therefore consistent with H4 for both disaster types, with the effect reaching statistical significance for earthquakes.

Hypothesis 5.

H5 was tested using worldwide data. Of the 15 globally visible eruptive episodes previously identified based on worldwide peaks in GT interest in volcanic eruptions, all events meeting the adopted criterion of low or minimal harm were selected.

Low or minimal harm was defined as the absence of serious destructive consequences for people or infrastructure; temporary ashfall, short-term disruptions, or minor damage were permitted. Harm was classified using independent sources describing the consequences of each eruption, including reports from the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program and the USGS, government communications, and Associated Press reports; a complete list of sources for each event is provided in Supplement S05. Six eruptions met this criterion:

Holuhraun/Bárðarbunga (Iceland), Anak Krakatau (Indonesia), Fagradalsfjall (Iceland), Mauna Loa (Hawaii, USA), Merapi (Indonesia), and Popocatépetl (Mexico). These events spanned Europe, Asia, Oceania, and North America.

For this full six-event sample, worldwide search interest in "volcano dream meaning" was higher than expected: for the window comprising the event month and the following month, the mean was 13.33 compared to 7.84 in the seasonality-matched null distribution (p = .03). Details of the analytical procedure and full results are provided in Supplement S05. This result is consistent with H5: for a disaster to leave a trace in dreamrelated searches, it need not cause serious actual harm.

4. Discussion

Sporting events satisfy many of the conditions previously considered conducive to incorporation and command vastly more conscious interest than disasters. Yet in dreamrelated searches, their advantage not only disappears but reverses in favor of disasters. This suggests that current knowledge of the conditions governing the incorporation of content into dreams is incomplete.

This finding also poses a problem for TST: content associated with highly engaging sporting events is weakly represented, whereas both remote disasters and those causing little or no serious harm leave a clear trace in dream-related searches, even though the degree of personal threat is low in these cases. Both findings are difficult to reconcile with the TST assumption that the intensity of threat simulations should increase with the degree of personal threat experienced in the original situation.

This same pattern, however, is consistent with the predictions of Phantom Anxiety Theory (PAT), which proposes that special priority is given specifically to content involving "safe threats"-objects or events that appear threatening, yet are remote and require no immediate action-rather than simply to content that most strongly engages conscious attention (the continuity hypothesis) or to content associated with a greater degree of personal threat (TST).

If Phantom Anxiety Theory correctly describes the observed pattern of incorporation, this may indicate that the anxiety system has its own information-selection criteria, operating partially independently of conscious goals and interests.

It is possible that some findings supporting the continuity hypothesis in its current form result from a specific measurement artifact. The idea of continuity between waking-life content and dream content is intuitively compelling, and research participants themselves hold such beliefs (Beaulieu-Prévost et al., 2009; Lambrecht et al., 2013). The research procedure itself may therefore distort dream reports through processes of memory selection and reconstruction (Loftus, 2005; Loftus & Palmer, 1974). Participants who are consciously and deliberately motivated to report their dreams may "notice" certain contents and associations and regard some as more important or more worthy of reporting, because they believe that "this is what a dream should look like." The researcher then receives material that has already been filtered through participants' expectations and memory processes and is therefore affected by a self-confirming measurement artifact.

GT-based dream research is free from limitations arising from the research setting itself. It is therefore possible that when the entire "magic of scientific research" disappears, a pattern of incorporation emerges that differs from what has previously been assumed. It is also possible that the pattern detected by the present method is difficult to capture using traditional methods and becomes visible only through the analysis of aggregated, spontaneous behavior of millions of users.

The study raises a broader question: Does the mind contain relatively autonomous systems that assign their own priorities to information? Is the selection of content for incorporation partially governed by such hidden priorities, rather than solely by the intensity of waking attention, emotion, and engagement?

5. Limitations

Operationalizing dream content using specific Google Trends queries constitutes a limitation of this study. Different query formulations may capture slightly different search behaviors; therefore, it cannot be assumed that using other semantically similar phrases would yield identical effect sizes.

The present study does not determine where the observed disparity between sports-related and disaster-related dream content primarily arises-whether during the generation of dream content or during its subsequent recall. An explanation based solely on differences in the tendency to seek dream interpretation was previously found to be insufficient.
