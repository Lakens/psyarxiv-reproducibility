Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

We found 21 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Over the past decade, research on reading disorders (RD) has expanded beyond its traditional focus on cognitive-linguistic factors to give increasing attention to socioemotional functioning.

The present study leveraged two existing datasets oversampled with RD (112 elementary school children aged 7 to 10 years and 110 early-and-mid adolescents aged 11 to 15 years) to examine various manifestations of reading anxiety (RA), including cognitive symptoms, physiological symptoms, and behavioral avoidance, as well as the overall RA effects. Our findings revealed that (1) RA was prevalent among both children and adolescents, although correlations among RA domains were stronger in adolescents than in children, (2) higher RA was moderately associated with poorer word reading performance in both age groups, (3) no significant differences in RA were observed between girls and boys, and (4) RA was negatively associated with reading performance across levels of word reading ability, with no significant evidence that the association was stronger among lower-performing readers than among middle or higherperforming readers. These findings reinforce the observed moderate negative association between RA and reading performance in prior research and suggest that RA may affect individuals across reading levels and developmental periods.

[div-01]

Reading disorder (RD), also known as developmental dyslexia, is a specific learning disability characterized by persistent difficulties with accurate and/or fluent word recognition, decoding, and spelling abilities (Lyon et al., 2003), affecting approximately 5-20% of the population (Wagner et la., 2020). While the cognitive, neural, and genetic bases of RD have been extensively investigated (Catts et al., 2024; Dobrynin et al., 2025; Snowling et al., 2020), historically, less attention has been given to the psychoaffective correlates of RD. Over the past decade, however, there has been growing interest in the role of socioemotional functioning in understanding reading development and RD (Catts & Petscher, 2022; Haft et al., 2016 , Stein et al., 2024). A growing body of evidence indicates that anxiety is associated with reading performance, and individuals with RD exhibit higher rates of internalizing problems, including anxiety (Francis et al., 2019; Hendren et al., 2018; Mammarella et al., 2016; McArthur et al., 2024; Nelson & Gregg, 2012; Paquette et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2024). In fact, over a quarter of children and adolescents with specific learning disorders (SLD), of which RD is by far the most common, meet the clinical criteria for anxiety disorder (Margari et al., 2013). Emerging research indicates that reading-specific anxiety, referred to as reading anxiety (RA), plays an important role in reading outcomes (e.g., Chung et al., 2024; Fishstrom et al., 2024; Jalongo & Hirsh, 2010; Johnson et al., 2026; Macdonald et al., 2021), potentially compounding challenges experienced by individuals with RD. In a recent meta-analysis (64 studies, 180 effect sizes, N = 14,467), Johnson and colleagues (2026) demonstrated that RA was moderately negatively associated with reading achievement (r = -.30), reinforcing the importance of the relationship between RA and reading outcomes.

RA is broadly defined as elevated fear, worry, and/or physiological discomfort when engaging in or thinking about reading activities (Katzir et al., 2018; Piccolo et al., 2017; Zbornik & Wallbrown, 1991). Despite the central role of reading in academic achievement, RA has received far less research attention than other forms of academic anxiety, such as test anxiety and math anxiety (Barroso et al., 2021; Von der Embse et al., 2018). Because anxiety may disrupt cognitive processes such as attention and working memory and consequently impair task performance (Eysenck & Derakshan, 2011), anxiety triggered by reading may be highly relevant in learning to read, including the development of decoding and reading comprehension skills. Furthermore, individuals with RD experience internalizing symptoms at rates significantly higher than typical readers (e.g., Nelson & Gregg, 2012; Vieira et al., 2024); RA may, therefore, be amplified in individuals with RD and may mitigate the effectiveness of reading interventions.

Research on attentional biases in learning disorders indicates that avoidance of reading stimuli may represent a top-down regulatory strategy that may ultimately impair the development of reading skills through reduced exposure (Haft et al., 2019). If reading tasks evoke anxiety, children may disengage and avoid practice, thereby undermining intervention effectiveness, creating a vicious cycle of worsening performance despite access to otherwise high-quality instruction. Understanding RA in reading development is important not only for addressing emotional vulnerabilities, but also for informing instructional approaches for both typically developing readers and those with RD. Emerging evidence from a randomized controlled trial (RCT, Grills et al., 2024 , Vaughn et al., 2022) and a case series suggests that reading interventions that incorporate anxiety-related management may improve struggling readers' reading outcomes (Francis et al., 2021) and reduce symptoms of anxiety (Grills et al., 2024).

Moreover, Vaughn et al. (2029) found trends suggesting that anxiety management may improve reading scores in students with reading difficulties, though effects did not attain statistical significance. Overall, the evidence base remains limited, and the observed effect sizes are modest and somewhat variable.

The Negative Association Between RA and Reading Performance

The existing literature suggests that RA contributes unique variance to reading performance and RD, beyond other forms of anxiety such as generalized or test anxiety. For example, Macdonald and colleagues (2021) found that RA uniquely predicted reading comprehension in struggling fourth and fifth-grade students, even when controlling for generalized anxiety, working memory, and verbal knowledge, while generalized anxiety showed only weak associations with reading fluency. Similarly, Fishstrom et al. (2024) reported that RA, rather than generalized or test anxiety, explained variance in word reading fluency and comprehension among third-to fifth-grade students with reading difficulties. Chung et al. (2024) further demonstrated that although eighth-grade readers with dyslexia exhibited higher levels of both RA and generalized anxiety compared to their typically developing peers, only RA uniquely accounted for their reading comprehension performance. General anxiety did not uniquely predict any reading skill after accounting for reading anxiety, reading self-concept, and cognitive factors. Taken together, these studies suggest that RA is a distinct construct dissociable from generalized anxiety and is uniquely associated with reading abilities and RD.

Multiple Manifestations of Reading Anxiety

Existing literature has established the link between RA and reading performance.

However, most existing research treats RA as a single global construct and less is known about how different manifestations of RA are expressed when individuals think about or engage in reading activities. In a recent RA validation study, Jones and colleagues (2025) identified three factors of RA, including generalized RA (i.e., worry about reading in general), social RA (i.e., worry about reading in social or evaluative contexts), and physiological symptoms. Because the study did not include measures of reading ability, the authors were unable to determine how these RA factors relate to reading performance. The two worry-related factors (e.g., generalized and social RA) align with cognitive models of anxiety such as attention control theory (Eysenck & Derakshan, 2011). According to this framework, anxiety may disrupt attention and working memory, reducing processing efficiency and compromising task performance, especially during complex tasks that require the coordination of multiple cognitive resources. Accordingly, if reading induces worry, this may affect reading performance by interfering with the efficient allocation of cognitive resources. Additionally, the physiological factor highlights physical discomfort related to anxiety which has been well documented in existing literature (Campo, 2012; Ginsburg et al., 2006; Haug et al., 2004; Weems et al., 2005). In the context of reading, studies have shown that RD is associated with elevated physiological complaints (e.g., headaches, stomachaches), which frequently co-occur with internalizing difficulties such as anxiety (Genizi et al., 2013; Willcutt & Pennington, 2000). Additionally, Barnes and colleagues (2024) found that the physical symptoms were negatively associated with both second graders' reading comprehension, and that attention further mediated the relationship between physical symptoms and reading comprehension.

Behavioral avoidance, although less studied in the RA literature, is a well-documented behavioral manifestation of other anxiety forms, such as math anxiety (Ashcraft, 2002; Choe et al., 2019; Richardson & Suinn, 1972). Additionally, task-avoidance behavior is well-established in the reading literature, suggesting a reciprocal relationship between avoidance behavior and reading performance (Hirvonen et al., 2010; Lundberg & Sterner, 2006 , Morgan et al, 2008).

Children with specific learning disorders including RD have shown an attentional bias away from reading-related stimuli, which may reflect the effects of top-down attentional control in response to reading threat cues (Haft et al., 2020). Avoidance directly reduces exposure to text and opportunities for reading practice, potentially driving a vicious cycle between reading difficulties and anxiety (Grills-Taquechel et al., 2012; Polychroni et al., 2006; Poskiparta et al., 2003 Stanovich, 1986).

To date, little is known how different domains of RA manifestations are associated with reading performance and RD. Different domains of RA manifestations aforementioned may coalesce to create cascading effects where reading challenges induce anxiety, which may further impair cognitive function, heighten psychological and somatic distress, and promote avoidance, resulting in a vicious cycle.

The Present Study

The present study was motivated by two broad considerations and goals. First, we sought to expand our understanding of different RA manifestations that are theoretically aligned with the existing anxiety and reading literature. We leveraged secondary data that included survey items broadly covering three domains of RA manifestations: cognitive symptoms, physiological symptoms, and behavioral avoidance. These items capture which situations may evoke RA, and ways in which RA may manifest cognitively, physiologically, and behaviorally. This broad categorization of RA manifestations is not intended to establish these domains as psychometrically distinct constructs, but rather provides a theoretically informed framework for examining the nature of RA. We specifically examined RA manifestations in primary school children and early-and-mid adolescents who were enrolled in two respective studies designed to oversample participants with RD. We then examined how each of the three domains of RA manifestations, as well as overall RA (i.e., a composite score across the three domains), was associated with word reading performance in these two cohorts. We expected that each domain and overall RA would be negatively related to word reading performance in both samples.

Second, research on RA is still emerging, and much work remains to be done to understand the nature of the association between reading performance and RA. In the present study, we specifically examined age-cohort and gender related differences in RA and whether the magnitude of the RA-reading association varied across the distribution of reading performance.

Existing evidence does not indicate consistent age-related differences in RA (Johnson et al., 2026). Our understanding of age-related differences is also limited because existing research has largely focused on samples of children and adolescents either in separate studies or as a single combined group. It is possible that RA may be higher among primary school children than among adolescents, as younger children may find reading tasks more effortful and frustrating because they are still acquiring foundational reading skills. Additionally, primary school children are often asked to read aloud when learning to read, which may contribute to higher levels of social RA. On the other hand, increasing reading demands in adolescence, including the emphasis on more complex tasks such as reading comprehension, as well as the accumulation of negative reading experiences over time, may contribute to higher RA among adolescents than among primary school children. Further, we expected that the effect of age cohorts on RA would be moderated by word reading performance, with stronger age-related differences related to poorer word reading performance. Findings on gender differences in the RA literature are also limited and mixed. For example, Katzir et al. (2018) reported higher RA in girls than in boys, whereas Ramirez et al. (2019) did not observe overall gender differences in RA, though the negative impact of early RA on later reading development appeared to be stronger in boys than in girls. A recent meta-analysis by Johnson and colleagues (2026) found that gender did not significantly moderate the association between RA and reading achievement. To examine age-related and gender differences in the present study, we used a cross-sectional approach to compare overall RA between elementary school children and early-and-mid adolescents and examined gender differences in RA within each cohort. We further examined whether potential age-cohort and gender differences in RA varied as a function of word reading performance.

Lastly, we explored whether the association between RA and word reading performance varied across the distribution of word reading performance, with particular attention to lowerperforming readers. We chose to explore the RA-reading association across the spectrum of reading ability, motivated by evidence that RD is characterized as the lower tail of a continuous distribution of reading skill, varying in severity, rather than a discrete category between typical and impaired readers (Peterson & Pennington, 2012). The lower portion of the reading distribution in the present study was of particular interest because both the children and adolescent datasets oversampled readers with RD or low reading achievement, allowing us to evaluate whether RA is especially relevant among readers with RD. Meta-analytic evidence has not yet supported learning disability status as a moderator for the RA-reading association (Johnson et al., 2026). To our knowledge, no previous study has examined whether the magnitudes of the RA-reading association vary across the reading performance distribution in both children and adolescents. Individuals with RD are at higher risk for anxiety and other internalizing symptoms and often report lower self-esteem and face repeated academic failure and stigmatization related to reading difficulties (Chung et al., 2024; Haft et al., 2016; Katzir et al., 2018; Zuppardo et al., 2021). Accordingly, we expected the magnitude of RA-reading association to be greater among lower performing readers, including those with RD, than among middle and higher performing readers in both cohorts.

Below is a summary of the four major research questions: (1) How frequently are the specified domains of RA manifestations, i.e., cognitive symptoms, physiological symptoms, and behavioral avoidance, reported in the child and adolescent samples, and how are these domains and the overall RA associated with word reading performance in each sample?

(2) Does overall RA differ between the child and adolescent samples, and are age-cohort differences in RA moderated by word reading performance?

(3) Does overall RA differ by gender within the child and adolescent samples, and are gender differences in RA moderated by word reading performance within each sample? (4) How does the magnitude of the association between overall RA and word reading performance vary across conditional quantiles of word reading performance, particularly at the lower quantiles (representing lower reading performance, including readers with RD) compared to the middle and upper quantiles (representing average and higher reading performance, respectively)?

Methods

Participants and Procedures

This study drew on two datasets collected from separate reading research projects focused on different age ranges: primary school children aged 7-10 years and early-to-mid adolescents aged 11 to 15 years old. Participants were included in the current study if they completed the RA questionnaire and correctly responded to an embedded attention-check item (i.e., "Please select 'sometimes' for this question."), ensuring data quality and adherence to task instructions. Because the datasets sampled different age ranges, we refer to them for ease of reference as the children dataset (N = 112 [43 girls; 69 boys]; Mean age = 8.81 years, SD = .69) and the adolescent dataset (N = 110 [67 girls; 43 boys]; Mean age = 12.93 years, SD = 1.18). Both participants' birthdates and gender were parent-reported. The children dataset included inperson administration of the reading and RA measures, whereas all assessments in the adolescent dataset were conducted virtually. In the children dataset, the questionnaire was completed using a pen-and-paper format, and the experimenter read each questionnaire item aloud and participants circled their responses. In the adolescent dataset, reading measures were administered live via Webex virtually, whereas the RA questionnaire was completed independently in REDCap with a read-aloud (text-to-speech) function available.

Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics for both groups, including demographic information and performance on Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI-II)'s matrix reasoning task (Wechsler, 2011) and word reading measures. Both study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Connecticut. In both datasets, parents reported that participants had normal hearing and normal or corrected-to-normal vision; no parents reported autism, intellectual disability or other serious neurodevelopmental conditions. Participants with parent-reported diagnoses of ADHD were included. Prior to data collection, parents were asked to confirm that their children had no additional behavioral or neurological conditions that would interfere with study participation or compliance. Parental consent and participant assent were obtained prior to the start of the assessments. Using a common reading performance threshold for RD or low reading achievement in reading research (e.g., Lovett et al., 2022; Nelson et al., 2015), about 36% of the children (37 out of 107 participants) and 32% of the adolescent (33 out of 104 participants) met the criteria of RD as they scored 85 or below in their standard scores (corresponding to the 16 th percentile) of the word reading composite on the TOWRE-2 (Torgesen et al., 2012). Although both datasets oversampled with RD or lower reading achievement, the reading scores are largely normally distributed, with skewness of -.07 and .36 and kurtosis of -.68 and -.11 for the children and adolescents' datasets, respectively. The Shapiro-Wilk tests also indicated that reading scores did not significantly deviate from normality for the children, W= .99, p = .30 and the adolescent samples, W= .98, p = .24. Descriptive information on matrix reasoning and word reading measures, based on participants with available data for each measure, is summarized in Table 1, along with the corresponding sample sizes.

Measures

Reading Anxiety Questionnaire

RA was assessed using a questionnaire developed by our research team to measure anxiety experienced in reading-related situations. Specifically, the questionnaire was designed to capture which situations may evoke RA, and ways in which RA may manifest. Initial items were generated based on open-ended questions of RA experiences provided by 7 college mentors knowledgeable about dyslexia and were then iteratively refined through qualitative feedback and pilot testing. During pilot testing, 80 participants responded to an initial pool of RA items and were subsequently asked to report experiences that they felt were not adequately captured by the initial item pool through open-ended questions about situations or experiences that elicited RA, associated with physiological and emotional reactions, and the effects of RA on their behaviors.

Based on the pilot testing results, items were removed, revised, or added based on patterns of exploratory analyses and the qualitive feedback from the open responses. This iterative process resulted in a final set of 18 RA items. The questionnaire is listed in Appendix A. The questionnaire comprised 18 RA items and 1 attention-check item (19 total). Participants rated each statement on a 4-point scale from 1 (Never) to 4 (Almost Always). During development, items were conceptually categorized into three domains, including cognitive symptoms, physiological symptoms, and behavioral avoidance. The domain of cognitive symptoms focuses on unpleasant thoughts and emotions related to reading, such as worry and fear. The domain of physiological symptoms includes physiological discomfort associated with reading activities, such as stomachache and headache. Lastly, the behavioral avoidance domain captures thoughts and behaviors related to avoidance of reading.

To ensure that the items in the questionnaire adequately represented each domain quantitatively, we conducted item screening by combining the two datasets and using separate one-factor analyses for each domain. Within each domain, we retained items with factor loadings > .40. This resulted in 5 items in cognitive symptoms (factor loadings: .57 -.76), 4 items in physiological symptoms (factor loadings: .59 -.69), and lastly 5 items in behavioral avoidance (factor loadings: .41 -.72). These items are bolded in the questionnaire listed in Appendix A.

Each domain had acceptable internal item consistencies for both datasets (Cognitive symptoms: Cronbach's α = .72; ωₜ = .80 in children; Cronbach's α = .86; ωₜ = .88 in adolescents; Physiological symptoms: Cronbach's α = .71; ωₜ = .76 in children; Cronbach's α = .77; ωₜ = .80 in adolescents; Behavioral avoidance: Cronbach's α = .70; ωₜ = .83 in children; Cronbach's α = .78; ωₜ = .83 in adolescents). In behavioral avoidance, 1 item "I choose to read when given a choice" is a reverse-scored item as it reflects the opposite of behavioral avoidance in reading.

Scores were computed by summing retained items within each domain. We also computed an overall RA composite for each participant by summing all the scores of the 3 domains.

Upon closer examination of the excluded items, we speculated possible explanations for their relatively low factor loadings. First, the two positively worded items, "I am happy when I have to read" (cognitive domain) and "When I read, I feel relaxed" (physiological domain), may not necessarily capture the direct opposite of anxiety-related symptoms. These items may instead reflect positive reading-related experiences that are not simply the absence of anxiety. Their positive wording, in contrast to the other negatively worded items, may also have potentially contributed to weaker associations with the remaining items. Second, the item "Reading things with small font and no pictures gives me a headache" may capture reading-related visual strain or effort rather than physiological symptoms specifically associated with RA. Finally, the item "I pretend to be sick so I don't have to do reading homework" may reflect a relatively extreme form of behavioral avoidance that is less commonly experienced than other avoidance behaviors captured by the survey, such as attempting to escape reading situations or disengaging when encountering difficult words.

Because the items included in our analyses have not been psychometrically validated, we conducted an exploratory comparison with existing RA questionnaires to ensure the content of our items was broadly aligned with how RA has been operationalized in existing literature. The results revealed that the included items in our analyses show conceptual overlap and similar content coverage to those in the Reading Anxiety Scale of Children (Grills et al., 2026) and the Reading Anxiety Test for children and Adolescents (Jones et al., 2025), two RA instruments that were formally validated. Our items also show overlap with other RA measures that have not been formally validated but are nevertheless attested in the literature, such as those reported in Ramirez et al. (2019) and Pollack et al. (2021). Detailed item-level comparisons are provided in Appendix B.

Word Reading Performance

Word-reading performance was assessed with the Test of Word Reading Efficiency-2 (TOWRE-2; Torgesen et al., 2012), using the Sight Word Efficiency (SWE; Form A) and Phonemic Decoding Efficiency (PDE; Form A) subtests. For each timed task, participants read aloud as many words (SWE) or pseudowords (PDE) as possible, as quickly and accurately as they could, within 45 seconds. Raw scores for each subtest were derived from the total number of items read correctly, which were then converted to age-normed standard scores. A composite word-reading fluency score was derived from the normed TOWRE-2 Index Score based on the standard scores of the two subtests. Reported test-retest reliability from Grades 1-12 is high, with average coefficients ranging from .89 to .93 across subtests and the index score (Torgesen et al., 2012).

Statistical Approach

All analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.2.2, R Core Team, 2022) using the RStudio (Version 2024.12.1.563; Posit team, 2025).

To characterize response patterns across the three RA domains, we generated bar charts for each questionnaire item, showing the percentage of participants selecting each response option (i.e., Never, Sometimes, Often, and Always), relative to the total number of participants who selected that option. These visualizations supported item-level inspection and helped to identify symptoms and behaviors that are representative of RA in each domain. We also computed partial Pearson correlations by controlling for age among each RA score, overall RA, and word reading performance (using the ppcor package Kim, 2015). Although reading scores in the current datasets were age-normalized, they showed small associations with age in both groups, with poorer reading associated with older age in children (r = -.20, p = .04) but better reading associated with older age in adolescents (r = .20, p = .04). In addition, the RA questionnaire scores did not have age-normed metrics. Therefore, age was included as a control variable in the correlation analyses. Similar to typical response patterns observed in anxiety questionnaires, each domain's RA scores and overall RA scores in the present study were found to be positively skewed, with skewness values ranging from .63 to 1.06 in children and from .82 to .98 in adolescents. Therefore, log-transformed RA variables were used in the correlation and subsequent analyses, resulting in much reduced skewness (ranging from .01 to .28 in children and from .13 to .35 in adolescents). Holm-Bonferroni adjustments were applied to all p-values in the correlation matrix to control for multiple comparisons.

The remaining research questions focused on overall RA effects, rather than the distinct contributions of specific RA domain; therefore, subsequent analyses used overall RA rather than the individual domain's RA scores. To examine whether the overall RA differed between children and adolescents, and whether this difference is moderated by reading performance, we fit a linear model using the lm function in R. The model included age cohorts (children vs. adolescents), word reading performance (mean-centered), and their interaction as predictors, with the RA composite as the outcome variable. We analyzed the interaction effects and main effects of the group. Partial eta squared (η²partial) effect sizes were computed for the predictors of interest using Type III sums-of-squares ANOVA from the car package (Version 3.1-3, Fox & Weisberg, 2019) and the effectsize package (Version 0.8.3, Ben-Shachar et al., 2020). We used a similar approach to examine gender effects. Separate linear model analyses were used for the children and adolescent datasets, with the overall RA composite as the outcome variable, and gender (girls vs boys), reading performance, and their interaction as predictors.

Finally, to test whether the association between RA and word reading performance varied across the distribution of word reading performance, we implemented separate quantile regression (QR) analyses for the children and adolescent datasets using the "quantreg" package in R (version 5.95, Koenker, 2024). Unlike traditional linear regression using ordinary least squares (OLS) to estimate the conditional mean of the outcome, QR uses a weight matrix, with observations weighted differently depending on whether they fall above or below the fitted conditional quantile, to estimate different conditional quantiles (τ) of the outcome along the distribution (Petscher & Logan, 2014). One advantage of this approach is that it uses the full sample to estimate the RA-reading associations at specified conditional quantiles of the reading performance without requiring arbitrary cutoffs to categorize participants into discrete reading groups. We specifically examined the association at the 25th, 50th (median), and 75th conditional quantiles of reading performance, representing relatively lower, middle, and higher levels of word reading performance, respectively. Koenker-Bassett (KB) omnibus test was conducted to evaluate whether the overall regression coefficients varied across quantiles.

Additionally, planned pairwise contrasts were specified a priori, comparing the 25th-quantile slope with the 50th-and 75th-quantile slopes. We used bootstrap 5000 resampling to estimate quantile-specific effects and planned contrasts between quantiles. Word reading performance was modeled as the outcome with the overall RA composite and age as the predictors, examining whether the association between RA and reading performance varied across the distributions of reading performance after accounting for age.

Results

Prevalence of RA Across Three Domains in Children and Adolescents

Figure 1 presents the percentage of responses for each item within each RA domain, as well as the average response percentages for each domain, for the child and adolescent groups, respectively. Each item represented a cognitive, physiological, or behavioral symptom of RA.

For descriptive purposes, an item describing an RA symptom was considered frequently reported when participants selected "Often" or "Always". In both groups, a noticeable proportion of participants selected "Often" or "Always" on RA items. On average, cognitive symptoms were frequently reported by 27% of children and 26% of adolescents, physiological symptoms by 21% and 25%, and behavioral avoidance by 27% and 22%, respectively. These patterns showed similar levels of reading-related cognitive and physiological symptoms across both groups. In behavioral avoidance, response patterns were similar across items, except for the item "I act silly to try to get out of doing reading", with adolescents rarely selecting the "Always" and "Often" categories (1% and 4% respectively), whereas about 14% of children selected these options (6% and 8% respectively).

Moderate Negative Association Between RA and Reading Performance in Children and

Adolescents

The Pearson partial correlations controlling for age (shown in Table 2) indicated that overall RA composite scores were negatively and moderately associated with TOWRE's word reading efficiency in both groups (children: r = -.44; adolescents: r = -.44, ps < .001). When examining each RA domain individually, all three domains were significantly correlated with word reading performance in two groups (children: rs = -.26 to -.48, ps < .05; adolescents: rs = -.38 to -.42, ps < .05). Interestingly, correlations among the RA domains were in the moderate range in the children group (rs = .30 to .61, ps< .01), whereas correlations among the domains were in the strong range in adolescents (rs = .66 to .83, ps < .001). Follow-up analyses using Fisher's z tests, which first transformed the Pearson's partial correlations coefficients (r) to Fisher's z values, were conducted to test if the magnitudes of the partial correlations between RA domain differed significantly between the children and the adolescent samples. The results showed that adolescents had stronger correlations of the physiological domain with the cognitive (z =-4.18, p <.001) and avoidance (z =-3.61, p < .001) domains, whereas there was no significant group difference in the cognitive-behavioral correlation (z = -1.48, p = .14).

No Significant Group Differences in RA Between Children and Adolescents

The linear model results did not reveal significant interaction between age cohorts (children vs. adolescents) and their word reading performance (b = .001, 95% CI [-.002, .003], t(207) = .73, η² partial = .003, p = .47), nor a significant main effect of age cohorts (b = -.007, 95% CI [-.047, .033], t(207) = -.34, n 2 partial < .001, p =.74). There is a significant main effect of word reading performance (b = -.008, 95% CI [-.010, -.005], t(207) = -6.16, η² partial = .16, p < .001).

Post-hoc sensitivity power analyses indicated that, at 80% power and α = .05, the minimum detectable effect size for individual predictors in the age-cohort regression model was Cohen's f² = .038 (η²partial = .037), using the pwr.f2.test function in the pwr package in R (version 1.3, Champely, 2023). These results suggest that the sample had reasonable sensitivity to detect effects in the small-to-medium range but limited sensitivity to smaller effects.

No Significant Gender Differences in RA within Children and Adolescent Groups

In children, the linear model results did not show significant interaction between gender (boys vs. girls) and their word reading performance (b = -.002, 95% CI [-.005, .001], t(103) = -1.11, η² partial = .01, p = .27), nor a significant main effect of gender (b=.007, 95% CI [-.048, .062], t(103) = .25, η² partial = <.001, p = .80). There is a significant main effect of word reading performance (b = -.006, 95% CI [-.005, -.003], t(103) = -3.71, η² partial = .12, p < .001). Similar patterns were observed in the adolescent group, with no significant interaction (b = -.001, 95% CI [-.005, .003], t(100) = -.33, η² partial = .001, p = .74) and no significant main effect of gender (b = -.05, 95% CI [-.116, .015], t(100) = -1.54, η² partial = .02, p = .13) was observed.

Similarly, for the adolescent group, there is a significant main effect of word reading performance (b = -.009, 95% CI [-.013, -.005], t(100) = -4.36, η² partial = .16, p < .001).

Post hoc sensitivity power analyses (pwr package in R, Champely, 2023) indicated that, at 80% power and α = .05, the minimum detectable effect sizes for individual predictors in the gender-related regression models were Cohen's f² = .076 (η²partial = .071) for children and f² = .078 (η²partial = .073) for adolescents. These results suggest that the samples had reasonable sensitivity to detect effects of these magnitudes or larger but limited sensitivity to smaller effects.

Negative RA-Reading Associations Across Conditional Quantiles of Reading Performance in Children and Adolescents, With No Significant Differences Across Quantiles

Quantile regression analyses indicated that RA is significantly and negatively associated with word reading performance after accounting for age across the 25th, 50th, and 75th quantiles in both children and adolescents as shown in Tables 3 and 4. The KB tests indicated that slopes did not significantly differ across quantiles in both groups (children: F(4, 317) = .254, p = .907; adolescents: F(4, 308) = .57, p = .69). The planned pairwise contrasts between quantiles did not differ significantly between 25 th vs. 50 th (children: ΔB = 4.009, SE = 8.781, 95% CI [-13.691, 20.380], p = .716; adolescents: ΔB = -.69, SE = 5.92, 95% CI [-11.4, 12.4], p = .94), and 25 th vs. 75th (children: ΔB = -1.460, SE = 10.006, 95% CI [-20.157, 18.570], p = .904; adolescents: ΔB = 3.23, SE = 6.32, 95% CI [-9.67, 15.1], p = .62). Visualizations are provided in Figure 2 (children) and Figure 3 (adolescents).

To provide an indirect benchmark for the statistical sensitivity of the quantile regression analyses, we conducted OLS-based power sensitivity analyses with age included as a covariate (pwr package in R, Champely, 2023). The minimum detectable effect sizes were f² = .075 (partial R² = .070) for children and f² = .078 (partial R² = .072) for adolescents, at 80% power and α = .05. These results suggest that the samples had reasonable sensitivity to detect effects in the small-to-moderate range, although sensitivity was more limited for smaller effects. Although these estimates do not directly establish power for quantile regression or planned contrasts between quantiles, the wide confidence intervals as shown above indicate that potentially meaningful differences across quantiles cannot be ruled out.

To help to help contextualize the magnitudes of the associations in the QR analyses, we additionally ran follow-up analyses using the fitted QR models to calculate the predicted word reading performance at relatively lower and higher levels of RA, For ease of interpretation, we first defined lower and higher RA as 1 SD below and above the sample mean of RA on its original scale, respectively. Because the fitted QR models used log-transformed RA values, these raw values of lower and higher RA were subsequently log-transformed when calculating the predicted word reading scores. Table 5 presents the predicted values of word reading performance at lower and higher levels of RA and their corresponding differences at each conditional quantile (25 th , 50 th , and 75 th ). In the children sample, for example, participants differing by 2 SDs in RA were estimated to differ by approximately 15 points in TOWRE composite scores at the 25th conditional quantile (reflecting relatively a lower level of reading performance), with participants at the higher level of RA having lower predicted reading performance than those at the lower level of RA. The estimated numerical difference was generally comparable to the 50th and 75th conditional quantiles in the children dataset, where the corresponding differences were approximately 17 and 14 points, respectively.

Discussion

Over the past decade, socioemotional factors have attracted increasing attention in research on reading development and Reading Disorders. Among the negative affective factors, RA has been shown to have a unique association with individuals' reading achievement (Chung et al., 2024; Fishstrom et al., 2024; 2026; Macdonald et al., 2021 , Piccolo et al., 2017).

Extending the existing RA research, the present study examined the multiple manifestations (i.e., cognitive symptoms, physiological symptoms, and behavioral avoidance) of RA, as well as overall RA, and their associations with reading performance among elementary-school children and early-and-mid adolescents. Overall, the results support 4 key preliminary findings: (1) cognitive, physiological, and behavioral avoidance manifestations of RA were frequently reported among both children and adolescents, although correlations among RA manifestations were stronger in adolescents than in children; (2) both groups showed similar magnitudes of association, with higher RA associated with poorer reading performance; (3) no significant differences in RA were observed between girls and boys; and (4) RA was negatively associated with reading performance across levels of word reading ability, with no significant evidence that the association was stronger among lower-performing readers than among middle or higherperforming readers. These findings are discussed in the following sections, along with the study's limitations and directions for future research.

Multiple manifestations of RA and Their Relationship with RDs

Most RA research has treated RA as a global construct. However, recent validation work has demonstrated that RA consists of several distinct factors, including general RA, social RA, and physiological symptoms (Jones et al., 2025). The present study extends prior work by examining three theoretically informed manifestations of RA that have been commonly identified in the broader anxiety literature and among individuals with specific learning disabilities, including cognitive symptoms, physiological symptoms, and behavioral avoidance (e.g., Ashcraft, 2002; Barnes et al., 2024; Eysenck & Derakshan, 2011; Willcutt & Pennington, 2000). Descriptive statistics demonstrate that a noticeable proportion of children and adolescents report symptoms "often" and "always" related to these manifestations, with average rates across domains ranging from 21% to 27% in children and from 24% to 26% in adolescents. These rates suggest that anxiety related to reading activities are relatively common in both elementary school and early-and-mid adolescents. Nearly one-third of participants in both groups reported "Often" and "Always" experiencing cognitive symptoms such as worry, nervousness, and fear. These patterns are consistent with cognitive models of anxiety that emphasize worry or fear as a central component. About 21% of children and 25% of adolescents reported 'Often' or 'Always' experiencing physiological discomfort, which is consistent with evidence that anxiety frequently involves physiological arousal such as increased heart rate, muscle tension, and gastrointestinal discomfort (Campo, 2012; Ginsburg et al., 2006; Hale et al., 2018; Haug et al., 2004; Weems et al., 2005). In behavioral avoidance, about 25% of both children and adolescents responded 'Often' or 'Always', with most behavioral manifestations reported similarly across groups, except for the item involving acting silly to avoid reading. This pattern may reflect plausible developmental differences in how anxiety-related avoidance is expressed across age groups.

Alternatively, the observed difference may reflect broader age-related increases in behavioral regulation rather than specific developmental differences in anxiety expression.

Our findings indicated no significant differences in overall RA between children and adolescents. However, the observed correlations among the three domains were numerically larger in adolescents than in children, particularly for the associations of the physiological domain with cognitive and behavioral avoidance domains. The follow-up analyses also showed that adolescents had significantly stronger physiological-cognitive and physiological-avoidance correlations than children. These patterns suggest that physiological responses may become increasingly interconnected with cognitive and behavioral responses to reading-related activities during adolescence. Alternatively, it is possible that adolescents may have greater awareness of their emotional states (McRae et al., 2012), potentially leading to more consistent reporting across cognitive, physiological, and behavioral domains. In contrast, children may have more difficulty differentiating or accurately reporting these experiences, which could attenuate observed associations. However, existing understanding of how these RA manifestations interact across development remains limited. Future research using longitudinal designs is needed to clarify how these manifestations develop over time. Such work would help determine when and why cognitive, physiological, and behavioral components of RA diverge or converge across development.

It is important to highlight that our current approach to categorizing the three domains of RA manifestations, although theoretically informed and aligned with the existing literature, remains preliminary. We conducted exploratory analyses for each domain to examine whether the included items showed acceptable factor loadings and internal consistency. To further evaluate the content of our items, we conducted a detailed item-level comparison with existing RA questionnaires, including two formally validated instruments (Grills et al., 2026; Jones et al., 2025; see Appendix B). The comparison indicated that the items included in our analyses shared conceptual overlap with existing measures in terms of the symptoms assessed and the reading situations or activities that may elicit RA. The comparison also showed that cognitive symptoms have received particular attention in existing RA questionnaires. For example, Grills et al. (2026) and Ramirez et al. (2019) focused primarily on cognitive symptoms, whereas Jones et al. (2026) assessed both cognitive and physiological symptoms. Pollack et al. (2021), on the other hand, included items broadly covering all three domains examined in the present study. Although existing validated questionnaires provide important tools for assessing RA, research in this area is still emerging, and further work is needed to understand how different manifestations of RA may be related to and potentially influence one another across development. Importantly, the present study does not establish that the three domains represent psychometrically distinct constructs. Accordingly, the findings of the present study should be interpreted as preliminary evidence regarding patterns of RA manifestations and their associations with word reading performance, rather than evidence supporting three psychometrically distinct dimensions of RA.

Although the items were organized into theoretically informed domains, the extent to which these domains reflect distinct underlying constructs remains unclear. Nevertheless, examining these manifestations provides an initial understanding of how different reading-related anxiety responses are reported and associated with reading performance during childhood and adolescence. Future research using validated instruments is needed to determine whether these patterns can be replicated and whether the different manifestations of RA represent distinguishable but potentially interrelated constructs.

Higher RA Associated with Lower Reading Performance, but No Significant Differences in Associations Across Different Levels of Reading Distribution

Studies have shown that individuals with RD are at higher risk of developing internalizing problems such as anxiety (Francis et al., 2026; Hendren et al., 2018; Mammarella et al., 2016; McArthur et al., 2024; Nelson & Gregg, 2012; Paquette et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2024). The present study operationalized RD as the lower end of the continuum of reading ability, and the children and adolescent datasets oversampled reading difficulties, having more than 30% of participants qualified for RD. Our analyses suggested that higher levels of RA were associated with poorer word reading performance. In this sense, individuals with lower reading performance, including those with reading disorders (RD), may be more likely to experience elevated levels of RA. This finding reinforces existing evidence that individuals with RD may be especially vulnerable to negative affective outcomes. We further expected that RD individuals characterized at the lowest end of the reading performance distribution would have a stronger RA-reading association because they may be more likely to experience elevated RA as a result of persistent reading difficulties and repeated negative reading experiences. However, our findings in the QR analyses do not support this prediction. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, the RA regression coefficients were consistently negative and statistically significant across all three conditional quantiles of word reading performance in both the child and adolescent samples. However, the strength of the RA-reading association did not differ significantly between the lower (25th) quantile and the median (50th) or upper (75th) quantiles in either sample.

Because both children and adolescent datasets included individuals with ADHD, one possible explanation for the absence of differences across reading levels is that individuals with ADHD who were not in the lower end of the reading distribution may nevertheless experience elevated RA levels comparable to those of lower-performing readers. To address this potential confound, we conducted follow-up analyses controlling for both age and ADHD status. The pattern of results remained unchanged. Details of these analyses are reported in the supplementary material. These findings suggest that although individuals with RD exhibit higher levels of RA, RD status does not appear to strengthen the association between RA and reading performance. This interpretation aligns with findings from Johnson and colleagues' metaanalysis (2026), which reported that learning disability status did not moderate the association between RA and reading achievement.

Interestingly, Fishstrom et al. (2024) reported that RA predicted reading outcomes only amongst students in the middle and upper quantiles of reading achievement in the sample of struggling readers. The authors suggested that this pattern might reflect learned helplessness. This explanation aligns with the idea that the development of RA may depend on how individuals interpret their reading experiences, which in turn shapes their reading-related selfconcept and contributes to anxiety symptoms (McArther, 2022; Ramirez et al., 2019). For example, a task may elicit anxiety when individuals perceive themselves as lacking the ability to complete it (i.e., low self-concept). It is also plausible that some resilient readers, despite experiencing reading difficulties, may be able to buffer against reading-related stress.

Accordingly, RD may not necessarily have a stronger negative association between RA and reading achievements. Further studies are needed to clarify how resilience factors may moderate the relationship between RA and reading performance in both typical and RD readers.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although the present findings did not provide evidence of age-or gender-related differences nor differences across different quantiles, smaller effects cannot be ruled out. Post hoc sensitivity power analyses indicated that our samples had reasonable sensitivity to detect effects of small to moderate magnitudes or larger but limited sensitivity to smaller effects. Furthermore, the 95% confidence intervals for the nonsignificant results included both negative and positive values, showing relatively wide range, indicating uncertainty regarding the magnitude and direction of potential differences. Future studies with larger samples are needed to improve sensitivity to smaller effects and obtain more precise estimates of age-and genderrelated differences in RA, as well as potential variation in RA-reading associations across the distribution of reading performance.

The present study was limited to assessing reading performance using measures of word reading efficiency. Therefore, the findings may not generalize to other reading tasks. Struggling readers may be more affected by RA during tasks with higher cognitive demands, such as reading comprehension. Nonetheless, the recent meta-analysis has reported similar associations between RA and reading achievement across different reading tasks (Johnson et al., 2026).

Additionally, the word reading efficiency measures used in the present study are strongly correlated with broader reading outcomes, including reading comprehension (rs = .67-.84; Torgesen et al., 2012). Taken together, the observed patterns in the present study may not be specific to the word reading alone, although future research is needed to confirm its generalizability.

Although theoretically grounded, the measures used to assess each RA domain in the current study have not yet been psychometrically validated. Accordingly, present findings should be considered preliminary, providing initial evidence on how these domains relate to overall RA and reading performance. Examining how cognitive symptoms, physiological symptoms, and behavioral avoidance are expressed in RA may clarify how these systems interact and, in turn, identify potential anxiety-management targets during reading intervention for struggling readers.

However, to support generalizable and reproducible findings across studies, these measures require further psychometric evaluation.

It also bears noting that while we operationalized RD as a lower end of the word reading distribution, we did not confirm RD status through comprehensive neuropsychological testing or academic record review. Specifically, in the children dataset we did not obtain information regarding IEPs or 504 plans for reading-related support at school. These data may have helped us better characterize the extent of participants' reading severity and the reading support they received. Despite its limitations, our approach may offer a uniform and replicable basis for identifying the RA-reading relation. Future research should endeavor to collect more comprehensive data related to participants' reading difficulties as well as to collect reading achievement data across various reading subcomponents, which together would allow for a more thorough characterization of participants' reading profiles.

Interpretations regarding the development of RA to the broader population of children and adolescents are limited by the cross-sectional design and convenience samples. The children and adolescent datasets were collected as part of different project administration and covered different age spans, with the adolescent cohort spanning a wider age range (11-15 years) than the child cohort (7-10 years). Therefore, the current findings may partly reflect project-specific differences and/or differences in age composition rather than developmental change. Although reading performance was approximately normally distributed in both samples, the samples may not fully reflect the distribution of reading performance in the general population. Future studies using representative samples are needed to examine the generalizability of these findings.

Nonetheless, the RA manifestations and the overall negative association between RA and reading performance observed in our study are largely consistent with findings from previous research, suggesting that this general pattern is not unique to our samples. Supplementary Materials S1. Follow-up Analyses

We ran follow-up analyses by accounting for age and ADHD status in examining the relationship between RA and word reading efficiency across different word reading performance distribution. Same approach was adopted as in the main analyses, we performed separate quantile regression analyses using the "quantreg" package in R (version 5.95, Koenker, 2024) for the children and adolescent datasets to estimate conditional associations between RA and reading performance varied across different parts of the outcome distribution at the 25th, 50th (median), and 75th quantiles, but we added ADHD status as the covariates in addition to age. A KB omnibus test was conducted to evaluate whether the overall regression coefficients varied across quantiles. Additionally, planned pairwise contrasts were specified a priori, comparing the 25thquantile slope with the 50th-and 75th-quantile slopes. We used bootstrap 5,000 resampling to estimate quantile-specific effects and planned contrasts between quantiles. Reading performance was modeled as the outcome with the RA composite, age, and ADHD status as the predictors, examining whether the association between RA and reading performance varied across the distributions of reading performance after accounting for age and ADHD status.

Quantile regression analyses indicate that RA significantly and negatively explains word reading performance after accounting for age and ADHD status across the 25th, 50th, and 75th quantiles in both children and adolescents as shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The KB tests indicated that slopes do not significantly differ across quantiles in both groups, children: F(6, 315) = .90, p = .49, adolescents: F(6, 306) = 1.32, p = .25. The planned pairwise contrasts between quantiles does not differ significantly, 25th vs. 50th (children: ΔB = 12.58, SE = 8.61, 95% CI [-11.99, 21.01], p = .61; adolescents: ΔB = -4.39, SE = 5.70, 95% CI [-11.51, 10.91], p = .82), and 25th vs. 75th (children: ΔB = 1.70, SE = 9.71, 95% CI [-18.46, 18.94], p = .97; adolescents: ΔB = 3.60, SE = 6.32, 95% CI [-8.46, 16.26], p = .54).
