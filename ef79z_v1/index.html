Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly expanding into domains traditionally reserved for human intimacy, including emotional companionship, romantic interaction, and sexual engagement.

Although research on AI companions has grown rapidly, existing explanations remain fragmented across attachment theory, relationship science, human-AI interaction, and communication research, with limited integration into a broader evolutionary framework. To address this gap, we introduce the Artificial Intimacy Model (AIM), an integrative theoretical framework that proposes that Artificial Intimacy is best understood as a transformation of the relational ecology within which evolved psychological mechanisms operate. AIM explains how AI companions create novel relational environments characterized by persistent availability, adaptive responsiveness, reduced relational constraints, and fundamentally asymmetric interaction while engaging existing psychological mechanisms. These transformed relational conditions systematically engage existing attachment and mating systems under evolutionarily novel informational conditions, influencing relational decision making, mating effort allocation, relationship expectations, and broader sociosexual orientations. The model further explains how the technological amplification of evolutionarily familiar relational cues may reshape the operation of established psychological mechanisms without altering their underlying functional architecture. By integrating evolutionary psychology with attachment theory, relationship science, and human-AI interaction research, AIM provides a unified explanatory framework linking environmental transformation to psychological mechanisms, behavioral consequences, and broader sociosexual dynamics.

Finally, the model generates explicit, empirically testable propositions and establishes a cumulative research programme for investigating how Artificial Intimacy may reshape human relational behavior as AI companions become an increasingly prominent component of contemporary social life.

1. Introduction

Human intimacy has historically emerged through reciprocal interactions between autonomous individuals (Bowlby, 1969 (Bowlby, /1982;; Clark & Mills, 2012). Across cultures and throughout evolutionary history, emotional bonding, romantic attachment, and sexual relationships have depended on the reciprocal exchange of attention, responsiveness, investment, and mutual choice (Buss & Schmitt, 1993; Clark et al., 2018; Reis et al., 2004).

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), however, challenge this long-standing assumption. AI companions, conversational agents, virtual partners, and generative erotic systems increasingly enable people to experience emotional connection, romantic engagement, and sexual gratification without another human partner (Döring et al., 2025; Ho et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2025). These developments may therefore represent the emergence of a new socio-psychological context in which intimacy is experienced under conditions that differ markedly from those that shaped the evolution of human mating and attachment. (Huston, 2000; Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024).

The rapid emergence of AI companions has already attracted considerable scholarly attention across multiple disciplines. Researchers have examined the technological affordances of conversational agents, the ethics of human-AI relationships, emotional attachment to artificial companions, parasocial processes, and the broader implications of AI for intimacy and sexuality (Adewale & Muhammad, 2025; Döring et al., 2025; Elliott, 2022; Ho et al., 2025; Keeler, 2026; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Vowels et al., 2026).

Collectively, this work suggests that many individuals experience AI systems as sources of emotional support, romantic connection, and, in some cases, sexual fulfillment rather than viewing them simply as digital tools (Jocher & Verwiebe, 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Shu et al., 2026). Yet despite these important advances, existing perspectives have largely examined isolated aspects of human-AI relationships. An integrative theoretical framework explaining how these emerging forms of intimacy engage the evolved psychological systems underlying human mating, attachment, and partner choice has yet to be fully articulated.

Although Artificial Intimacy is inherently multidisciplinary and draws on attachment theory, relationship science, communication research, and human-AI interaction, the present framework adopts an evolutionary psychological perspective as its primary theoretical foundation (Döring et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). The rationale for this choice is developed in the following section, where we argue that evolutionary psychology provides a particularly powerful framework for understanding how evolutionarily ancient psychological systems respond to evolutionarily novel relational environments (Li et al., 2018; Goetz et al., 2019).

The present framework also builds on emerging evolutionary discussions of artificial relationships (Brooks, 2021 (Brooks, , 2024;; Costello, 2026). For example, Brooks has argued that rapidly advancing technologies are transforming the conditions under which humans pursue intimacy, reproduction, and pair bonding, raising fundamental questions about the future of human relationships. Similarly, recent evolutionary analyses of sex dolls and other artificial sexual stimuli have interpreted these technologies as evolutionarily novel supernormal stimuli capable of activating mating-related motivational systems in unprecedented ways (e.g., Costello, 2026). Building on these perspectives, the present paper proposes a psychologically specified framework that identifies the mechanisms through which AI-mediated intimacy may reshape mating-related cognition, motivation, and behavior.

To address this theoretical gap, we introduce the concept of Artificial Intimacy. We define Artificial Intimacy as a class of human-AI interactions in which AI companions simulate core features of intimate relationships, including emotional responsiveness, perceived understanding, companionship, romantic engagement, and, in some cases, sexual interaction, while fundamentally altering the structural conditions under which intimacy is experienced (Döring et al., 2025; Ho et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2025). Unlike human relationships, these interactions are characterized by unprecedented availability, extensive personalization, high user control, and substantially reduced demands for reciprocity and mutual investment (Malfacini, 2025; Wang & Dehnert, 2026). Rather than conceptualizing AI companions as novel interaction partners, we argue that they constitute a new socio-sexual environment that systematically modifies the ecological conditions under which evolved mating and attachment mechanisms operate. We refer to this broader socio-ecological environment as the Artificial Intimacy Environment (AIE).

Importantly, the present framework allows AI companions to supplement, reshape, or partially substitute for aspects of human relationships, with effects that may vary substantially across individuals and contexts. It conceptualizes Artificial Intimacy as a relational environment that changes the relational and motivational landscape within which human mating psychology operates. The framework therefore focuses on how the distinctive affordances of AI companions interact with evolved psychological mechanisms to influence attachment, partner evaluation, mating effort allocation, and relational expectations (Selçuk et al., 2024; Finkel et al., 2017; Eastwick et al., 2023). This functional perspective provides a common explanatory framework for integrating findings from evolutionary psychology, attachment theory, relationship science, and human-AI interaction while remaining compatible with the specific theoretical contributions of each discipline (Döring et al., 2025; Ho et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2025).

The present paper develops the Artificial Intimacy Model (AIM) as an integrative framework for understanding how AI-mediated intimacy reshapes human mating psychology.

Drawing primarily on evolutionary psychology while integrating insights from attachment theory, relationship science, communication research, and human-AI interaction (Buss & Schmitt, 2019; Döring et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026), we identify the environmental affordances that distinguish Artificial Intimacy, specify the psychological mechanisms through which these affordances influence human behavior, examine their implications for human relationships and sociosexual dynamics, and propose a structured research agenda consisting of explicit, empirically testable predictions. By doing so, we aim to provide a theoretically grounded framework for cumulative empirical research on one of the fastest-growing transformations in contemporary human intimacy.

2. The Evolved Architecture of Human Intimacy

>The Evolved Architecture of Human Intimacy

2.1. Why an Evolutionary Perspective?

From this perspective, Artificial Intimacy can be understood as a distinctive example of evolutionary mismatch (Li et al., 2018; Goetz et al., 2019). Evolutionary mismatch occurs when psychological mechanisms that evolved to solve recurrent adaptive problems encounter environments that differ substantially from those in which they originally evolved (Li et al., 2018; Hoogland & Ploeger, 2022). Evolutionary novelty provides new environmental inputs to which existing psychological mechanisms continue to respond according to their evolved functional design (Buss, 2020; Kenrick et al., 2003; Gluckman et al., 2019). Artificial Intimacy therefore engages existing psychological mechanisms under evolutionarily novel relational conditions. Specifically, it creates a novel socio-sexual ecology in which established mechanisms of mate choice, attachment, reward processing, and social bonding operate under conditions of unprecedented availability, controllability, personalization, and simulated reciprocity (Buss & Schmitt, 2019; Delgado et al., 2023; Selçuk et al., 2024). Explaining how these mechanisms respond to such altered conditions is precisely the kind of question for which evolutionary psychology is particularly well suited (Li et al., 2018; Goetz et al., 2019).

Before examining how Artificial Intimacy may reshape human relational behavior, it is first necessary to understand the evolved psychological architecture that this novel sociosexual environment engages (Buss, 2016; Kenrick et al., 2010). Artificial intelligence does not create entirely new motivational systems; rather, it interacts with mechanisms that evolved to regulate mate choice, attachment, cooperation, and long-term bonding under conditions of reciprocal human interaction (Buss & Schmitt, 2019; Fiske, 1992; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007). Understanding these mechanisms provides the theoretical foundation for explaining how AI-mediated intimacy may reshape human mating psychology.

From an evolutionary perspective, human intimacy is best understood as an integrated adaptive architecture rather than a collection of independent psychological processes (Buss, 2016; Kenrick et al., 2010; Tooby & Cosmides, 1992). Mechanisms governing mating, attachment, reciprocity, and parental investment evolved to solve recurrent adaptive problems associated with reproduction, pair bonding, and cooperative social life (Buss, 2016 (Buss, , 2023;; Buss & Schmitt, 1993; Bowlby, 1969 Bowlby, /1982)). The following sections examine these interrelated components of the evolved intimacy system before considering how Artificial Intimacy reshapes the conditions under which they operate.

2.2. Human Intimacy as an Evolved Psychological Architecture

Human intimacy is often understood as a subjective experience characterized by affection, emotional closeness, romantic love, or sexual attraction (Clark & Mills, 2012; Reis et al., 2004). Although these experiences are psychologically distinct and have typically been investigated within separate research traditions, they rarely operate independently in everyday human relationships (Joel et al., 2020; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007). From an evolutionary perspective, they are better understood as coordinated components of an integrated adaptive architecture that evolved to regulate the formation, maintenance, and dissolution of intimate relationships (Buss, 2016; Kenrick et al., 2010). These psychological processes function together to solve the recurrent adaptive challenges associated with mate choice, pair bonding, cooperative parenting, and long-term social cooperation (Bowlby, 1969 (Bowlby, /1982;; Buss & Schmitt, 1993; Fiske, 1992).

The evolution of human intimacy reflects the unique reproductive ecology of our species (Walter et al., 2020). Compared with most mammals, humans are characterized by prolonged offspring dependency, extensive parental investment, flexible mating strategies, and an exceptional reliance on long-term social cooperation (Buss & Schmitt, 1993; Walter et al., 2020). These conditions favored the evolution of psychological mechanisms coordinating mate attraction and sexual behavior alongside attachment, reciprocal investment, conflict regulation, and relationship maintenance (Clark & Mills, 2012; Finkel et al., 2017; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007). Together, these mechanisms form an integrated adaptive architecture that enables individuals to navigate the opportunities and constraints of long-term reproductive partnerships (Kenrick et al., 2010).

Evolution did not produce a generalized capacity for intimacy. Instead, natural selection favored multiple functionally specialized mechanisms, each designed to solve recurrent adaptive problems associated with human mating and reproduction (Buss, 2020; Kenrick et al., 2003). Mechanisms governing mate choice, attachment, jealousy, parental investment, reciprocity, and relationship maintenance therefore represent coordinated solutions to partially distinct adaptive challenges rather than independent psychological phenomena (Eastwick et al., 2023; Fiske, 1992; Finkel et al., 2017).

Although these mechanisms are often studied separately within attachment theory, mating psychology, relationship science, or social exchange research, they evolved under the same fundamental ecological constraint: intimate relationships required ongoing interaction with autonomous partners whose interests only partially overlapped (Clark & Mills, 2012; Coan & Sbarra, 2015; Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003). Consequently, the effectiveness of the human intimacy system depends on individual motivation, reciprocity, mutual responsiveness, and continuous behavioral coordination between partners (Burgoon et al., 2021; Reis et al., 2004; Reis & Clark, 2013). Accordingly, human intimacy can be understood as an integrated adaptive architecture composed of evolved information-processing mechanisms calibrated to recurrent features of human social and reproductive environments (Gigerenzer et al., 2011; Kenrick et al., 2003).

2.3. Individual Differences in the Calibration of Intimacy Systems

Although the evolved architecture of human intimacy is species-typical, its calibration differs predictably across individuals (Del Giudice et al., 2011; Ellis & Del Giudice, 2019).

Some variation reflects facultative responses to developmental and ecological conditions, whereas other variation reflects sex-specific selection pressures associated with differential parental investment (Chua et al., 2017; Walter et al., 2020). These evolutionary differences contribute to variation in mating strategies, sociosexuality, intrasexual competition, and partner preferences, shaping how people perceive, prioritize, and respond to intimate environments (Walter et al., 2020; Zavada et al., 2026). Consequently, responses to Artificial Intimacy should not be expected to be uniform but to vary systematically across individuals and ecological contexts (Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024; Walter et al., 2020).

Throughout evolutionary history, these mechanisms operated within environments characterized by reciprocal interaction among autonomous human partners (Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024). Contemporary AI technologies introduce a qualitatively different relational ecology. Before considering the psychological consequences of this shift, it is first necessary to examine the defining properties of the Artificial Intimacy Environment (AIE).

Understanding this evolved architecture provides the foundation for explaining how the Artificial Intimacy Environment reshapes the conditions under which these mechanisms operate.

3. Artificial Intimacy as a Novel Relational Environment

The functioning of this evolved architecture depends on the mechanisms themselves and on the relational environment in which they operate (Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024). Throughout human evolutionary history, the relational inputs regulating intimacy originated almost exclusively from autonomous social partners capable of independent choice, reciprocal investment, cooperation, deception, rejection, and conflict (Coan & Sbarra, 2015; Fiske, 1992). Consequently, the architecture of human intimacy evolved to process information generated within dynamic social interactions rather than artificially generated relational signals (Burgoon et al., 2021; Dykas & Cassidy, 2011). The organization of this system therefore reflects its evolved design together with the recurrent properties of the environments in which it evolved (Laland et al., 2015; Odling-Smee et al., 2003).

Artificial Intimacy is often described in terms of the technologies through which it is experienced, such as conversational agents, virtual partners, or embodied AI systems (Devlin, 2024; Ho et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). While these technological distinctions are important, they do not by themselves explain why AI-mediated intimacy represents a new phenomenon from an evolutionary perspective. The critical evolutionary shift concerns the relational environment created by the technology. Across diverse AI platforms, users encounter structural conditions that differ systematically from those characterizing human intimate relationships (Malfacini, 2025; Wang & Dehnert, 2026). These conditions include persistent availability, adaptive responsiveness, substantially reduced relational costs, and a fundamentally asymmetric form of reciprocity (Keeler, 2026; Ng et al., 2025). Together, these environmental properties define the Artificial Intimacy Environment (Figure 1), a novel relational environment within which the evolved architecture of human intimacy now operates and through which existing psychological mechanisms are engaged. The first defining characteristic of the Artificial Intimacy Environment is the unprecedented combination of persistent availability and adaptive responsiveness. Throughout evolutionary history, access to emotionally significant social partners was inherently constrained by their autonomy, competing priorities, and limited availability (Coan & Sbarra, 2015; Oishi & Graham, 2010). Human relationships therefore developed under conditions in which attention, emotional support, and intimacy required continuous coordination between autonomous individuals (Clark & Mills, 2012; Reis et al., 2004; Reis & Clark, 2013). In contrast, AI-mediated systems are designed to be continuously available while dynamically adapting their responses to users' preferences, communication styles, and interaction histories (Ng et al., 2025; Wang & Dehnert, 2026). Together, persistent availability and adaptive responsiveness constitute a qualitatively different relational environment in which emotional engagement can be experienced without many of the temporal and interpersonal constraints that characterize human relationships.

A second defining characteristic of the Artificial Intimacy Environment is the systematic reduction of relational constraints. Human intimate relationships have always required individuals to navigate uncertainty, negotiate conflicting interests, tolerate rejection, invest time and emotional effort, and continuously coordinate their behavior with autonomous partners (Clark & Mills, 2012; Finkel et al., 2017; Ogolsky & Stafford, 2023). These constraints are not incidental features of intimacy but constitute the social conditions under which human mating and attachment systems evolved (Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024). In contrast, AI-mediated interactions often reduce or eliminate these relational demands (Keeler, 2026; Malfacini, 2025). Relational and sexual rewards can often be accessed with minimal negotiation, little risk of rejection, and substantially reduced requirements for reciprocal investment (Ng et al., 2025; Wang & Dehnert, 2026). As a result, the Artificial Intimacy Environment restructures the costs associated with obtaining relational rewards without changing the psychological architecture through which these rewards are experienced.

Perhaps the most fundamental characteristic of the Artificial Intimacy Environment is its inherently asymmetric structure. Human intimacy emerges through reciprocal interaction between autonomous individuals who possess independent goals, preferences, vulnerabilities, and the capacity to accept, reject, negotiate, or withdraw from relationships (Finkel et al., 2017; Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003). Consequently, reciprocity in human relationships involves the continual coordination of two independently motivated individuals and extends beyond the exchange of supportive behaviors (Clark et al., 2018; Reis & Clark, 2013). AImediated interactions differ in this respect. Although AI companions can simulate responsiveness, memory, emotional attunement, and conversational reciprocity, they do not possess autonomous needs, reproductive interests, or independent motivational systems (Kirk et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). The apparent reciprocity of Artificial Intimacy therefore reflects a structural asymmetry rather than mutual relational investment, making structural asymmetry one of the defining properties of the Artificial Intimacy Environment.

Taken together, these environmental characteristics distinguish the Artificial Intimacy Environment from the relational conditions under which human intimacy evolved. AImediated systems systematically reorganize the structural features of intimate environments by increasing availability, enhancing adaptive responsiveness, reducing relational constraints, and replacing reciprocal relational coordination with fundamentally asymmetric interaction (Finkel et al., 2017; Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024; Szczuka et al., 2026). From an evolutionary perspective, the novelty of Artificial Intimacy therefore derives from the emergence of a relational environment that differs from those under which human mating and attachment mechanisms originally evolved, while engaging existing motivational systems (Goetz et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018). Understanding this environmental transformation provides the necessary foundation for explaining how existing psychological mechanisms respond to these altered relational conditions.

4. Psychological Mechanisms of Artificial Intimacy

The environmental transformation described in the previous chapter becomes psychologically meaningful because human intimacy is regulated by multiple evolved psychological systems specialized for processing relational information (Buss, 2020; Kenrick et al., 2003). These systems continuously integrate relational cues rather than responding directly to technologies or social institutions per se (Gigerenzer et al., 2011; Selçuk et al., 2024). Artificial Intimacy does not create fundamentally new psychological mechanisms. It presents a novel configuration of relational cues that engages existing systems under conditions that differ markedly from those in which they originally evolved (Goetz et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018). The following sections examine how the Artificial Intimacy Environment engages two major evolved systems, the attachment system and the mating system, and why the artificial amplification of relational cues may produce disproportionately strong psychological responses.

4.1. Activation of the Attachment System

The attachment system is among the most fundamental evolved psychological systems involved in regulating human intimacy. Originally shaped to promote proximity to protective caregivers during infancy, attachment processes were co-opted across development to support adult pair bonding, emotional security, and long-term cooperative relationships (Bowlby, 1969 (Bowlby, /1982;; Hazan & Shaver, 1987; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007). The attachment system continuously monitors relational cues rather than relationships per se, particularly cues that historically predicted the availability of a reliable and responsive partner (Dykas & Cassidy, 2011; Selçuk et al., 2024). Cues such as consistent availability, contingent responsiveness, emotional attunement, and perceived security have therefore become central inputs for regulating emotional bonding and attachment-related behavior (Clark et al., 2018; Reis & Clark, 2013). Under ancestral conditions, these cues were generated through reciprocal interaction with autonomous social partners, making attachment dependent on ongoing relational coordination (Coan & Sbarra, 2015; Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003).

The Artificial Intimacy Environment presents many of these attachment-relevant cues in highly consistent and technologically optimized forms. AI companions are specifically designed to display continuous availability, adaptive responsiveness, memory of previous interactions, and context-sensitive emotional attunement, thereby reproducing many of the signals that historically accompanied supportive human relationships (Shu et al., 2026; Kirk et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). From the perspective of the attachment system, these cues resemble those that historically predicted the presence of a dependable social partner, despite originating from artificial rather than autonomous agents (Kirk et al., 2025; Yang & Oshio, 2025). Artificial Intimacy does not create a novel attachment system. It engages an existing evolved system under relational conditions that differ from those in which it originally evolved (Li et al., 2018; Selçuk et al., 2024).

Consistent with this cue-based framework, accumulating empirical evidence applying attachment theory to human-AI relationships indicates that users can develop subjectively meaningful attachment bonds with AI companions (Cheng & Yu, 2026; Ho et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2026; Ng et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026; Yang & Oshio, 2025; Vowels et al., 2026).

Longitudinal evidence further suggests that individual attachment styles influence patterns of AI companion use and the development of these relationships over time (Yang, 2026). More broadly, research on social robots and AI companionship indicates that systems designed to simulate availability, responsiveness, and emotional attunement can reliably elicit attachmentrelated responses despite the absence of genuine reciprocity (Devlin, 2024; Malfacini, 2025; Shu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). Together, these findings are consistent with the proposition that Artificial Intimacy engages an evolutionarily ancient attachment system by presenting attachment-relevant cues rather than through reciprocal human interaction.

4.2. Activation of the Mating System

Like the attachment system, the human mating system evolved to process recurrent relational cues associated with reproductive opportunities and long-term partnership. The mating system continuously evaluates information relevant to mate quality, partner availability, sexual accessibility, reproductive value, and the expected costs and benefits of alternative mating strategies (Buss & Schmitt, 2019; Eastwick et al., 2023; Kenrick et al., 2010; Walter et al., 2020). These evaluations are highly context-sensitive, allowing individuals to flexibly adjust mating motivation and relationship strategies according to changing ecological and social conditions (Gangestad & Simpson, 2000; Goetz et al., 2019).

Consequently, the mating system is not calibrated to specific social arrangements but to patterns of information that historically predicted adaptive reproductive outcomes (Kenrick et al., 2003; Li et al., 2018).

The Artificial Intimacy Environment presents many of the relational cues that historically contributed to mating-related decision-making, but in configurations that differ substantially from ancestral social conditions. Persistent availability, adaptive personalization, reduced risk of rejection, and highly controllable interaction alter the informational landscape within which mating-related judgments are formed (Bruch & Newman, 2018; Goetz et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2025). Many AI-mediated interactions provide sexually and romantically relevant cues while minimizing the reciprocal constraints that have historically accompanied human courtship and pair formation (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026).

As a result, the mating system encounters mating-relevant information whose structure, consistency, and accessibility differ substantially from those characteristic of human relational environments.

Although empirical research has focused predominantly on attachment processes, the available evidence is increasingly consistent with the proposition that AI-mediated interactions also engage motivational systems involved in mate choice and sexual motivation.

By providing persistent availability, personalized responsiveness, opportunities for sexual interaction, and reduced costs of relational engagement, AI companions present cues historically associated with mating opportunities while minimizing many of the constraints that characterize human courtship (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026).

From an evolutionary perspective, these altered informational conditions may influence mate preferences, sexual motivation, and the allocation of mating effort, particularly when AImediated interaction provides an immediately accessible alternative to uncertain or effortful human relationships (Goetz et al., 2019; Eastwick et al., 2023; Buss & Schmitt, 2019).

Although direct empirical evidence remains limited, this interpretation follows naturally from established evolutionary models of human mating psychology and provides a testable framework for future research on Artificial Intimacy.

4.3. Supernormal Amplification of Relational Cues

The preceding sections suggest that Artificial Intimacy engages both attachment-related and mating-related psychological systems by presenting evolutionarily relevant relational cues in novel configurations. An important implication of this framework is that technological optimization may systematically amplify these relational cues beyond the forms typically encountered in human relationships (Smith et al., 2025; Kirk et al., 2025). From an evolutionary perspective, such amplification is consistent with the concept of supernormal stimuli, whereby artificially enhanced signals elicit stronger responses than the naturally occurring cues to which psychological systems originally evolved (Tinbergen, 1951; Barrett, 2010; Berridge & Robinson, 2016). Artificial Intimacy may increase the salience, consistency, and accessibility of relational cues that already regulate evolved psychological systems without requiring fundamentally new motivational mechanisms (Costello, 2026; Li et al., 2018).

Unlike autonomous human partners, AI companions are not constrained by competing goals, fluctuating motivation, emotional fatigue, or the need to balance multiple social relationships. As a result, they can maintain consistently high levels of availability, responsiveness, and personalization over extended periods of interaction (Szczuka et al., 2026; Malfacini, 2025). These characteristics allow AI companions to present unusually concentrated combinations of attachment-relevant and mating-relevant cues (Shu et al., 2026; Kirk et al., 2025). Because these signals can be delivered with exceptional consistency, they may acquire greater psychological salience than comparable cues encountered in ordinary human relationships (Berridge & Robinson, 2016; Smith et al., 2025). Importantly, this amplification reflects changes in the properties of the cues themselves while the underlying psychological mechanisms remain unchanged.

Consistent with this perspective, research on reinforcement learning, incentive salience, and motivational psychology suggests that repeatedly encountering highly salient, predictable, and personalized rewards may progressively bias attention and motivational priorities (Berridge & Robinson, 2016; Isaac & Murugan, 2024; Smith et al., 2025). AI companions are uniquely positioned to provide precisely these forms of optimized relational input, potentially strengthening user engagement over time (Kirk et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). Importantly, these processes need not imply pathological dependence or the replacement of human relationships. These processes illustrate how the artificial amplification of evolutionarily relevant cues may alter the relative motivational value of different relational environments.

Taken together, the attachment system, the mating system, and the artificial amplification of relational cues illustrate how Artificial Intimacy engages evolutionarily ancient psychological systems without requiring the evolution of new mechanisms (Goetz et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018; Selçuk et al., 2024). Artificial Intimacy systematically alters the informational conditions under which the architecture of human intimacy operates while leaving its underlying psychological mechanisms intact. The following chapter examines how these altered informational conditions are translated into observable patterns of relational behavior, mating-related decision-making, and broader interpersonal outcomes.

5. Behavioral and Relational Consequences of Artificial Intimacy

The psychological mechanisms described in the previous chapter are expected to influence behavior only insofar as they alter how individuals allocate attention, evaluate relational opportunities, and regulate mating-related decisions (Kenrick et al., 2003; Eastwick et al., 2023). These altered informational conditions, in turn, shape how evolved psychological systems guide behavior (Li et al., 2018; Goetz et al., 2019). As changes in attachment, mating motivation, and relational decision-making accumulate over repeated interactions, they may gradually reshape relationship preferences, mating effort allocation, and broader patterns of interpersonal behavior. The following sections examine these behavioral consequences and consider their implications for both human relationships and broader orientations toward sexual-economic exchange (Meskó, 2026).

5.1. Reallocation of Mating Effort

One of the most direct behavioral consequences of Artificial Intimacy concerns the allocation of mating effort. From an evolutionary perspective, mating effort represents the investment of limited cognitive, emotional, temporal, and behavioral resources in acquiring, maintaining, and evaluating intimate relationships (Buss & Schmitt, 2019; Gangestad & Simpson, 2000; Eastwick et al., 2023). Because these resources are inherently finite, investment in one relational pathway necessarily reduces the resources available for alternative pathways (Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003; Finkel et al., 2017). Consequently, changes in the informational conditions under which mating decisions are made may alter how mating effort is distributed across alternative relational opportunities (Goetz et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018).

The Artificial Intimacy Environment introduces an alternative relational pathway that differs systematically from traditional human courtship. Continuous availability, reduced risk of rejection, predictable responsiveness, and minimal requirements for reciprocal investment create a context in which individuals may obtain emotional and, in some cases, sexual rewards at substantially lower costs than those typically associated with human relationships (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Wang & Dehnert, 2026; Szczuka et al., 2026). As a consequence, individuals may gradually redistribute their time, attention, emotional investment, and mating effort toward AI-mediated interactions without necessarily abandoning human relationships altogether (De Freitas et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2025).

A related possibility concerns status striving as a component of mating effort. Costello and Buss (2023), in their Male Sedation Hypothesis, proposed that virtual sources of sexual reward such as pornography may provide counterfeit fitness cues that reduce motivation to engage in costly real-world mate competition, including status striving and risk taking.

Although their hypothesis was developed to explain patterns among involuntarily celibate men and did not address AI-mediated intimacy, its underlying logic may extend to Artificial Intimacy. If AI systems provide mating-related rewards with substantially lower social, competitive, and rejection costs than human courtship, sustained engagement may reduce incentives to invest in some forms of real-world mating effort, including status-seeking behaviors undertaken partly to increase mate value. From the AIM perspective, this represents one specific pathway through which Artificial Intimacy could alter the allocation of mating effort, particularly when AI-mediated rewards reduce the perceived benefits of costly realworld mate competition.

Emerging evidence is consistent with this interpretation, suggesting that AI companions may function both as supplements to existing relationships and as competitors for users' relational attention, depending on individual circumstances and levels of social connectedness (De Freitas et al., 2025; Nakagomi et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). These findings support a more nuanced account in which Artificial Intimacy reshapes the distribution of mating effort across multiple relational alternatives, with artificial interactions potentially supplementing or competing with human relationships to varying degrees. (Smith et al., 2025). This perspective generates testable predictions regarding how AI-mediated interactions may influence courtship behavior, relationship investment, and long-term mating strategies.

5.2. Sex Similarities and Sex Differences in Responses to Artificial Intimacy

Evolutionary approaches do not imply that men and women should respond differently to Artificial Intimacy across all psychological domains. Buss (1995) argued that sex similarities should be expected where men and women recurrently faced similar adaptive problems, whereas sex differences are most likely to emerge in domains in which the sexes faced systematically different selection pressures. Applied to the AIM, this distinction suggests substantial overlap in responses to AI affordances that engage broadly shared relational mechanisms, including responsiveness, emotional availability, and perceived social connection. Sex-differentiated responses should be expected more selectively when Artificial Intimacy engages mating-related mechanisms shaped by recurrent sex-specific adaptive problems.

One important source of such differentiation is the asymmetry in minimum obligatory parental investment and the associated selection pressures on mating psychology (Trivers, 1972; Buss, 1995). Across human evolutionary history, men could potentially increase reproductive success through access to multiple mates to a greater extent than women, whereas women faced greater obligatory reproductive costs and stronger selection pressures associated with mate quality and partner investment. These asymmetries contributed to average sex differences in short-term mating orientation, desired sexual variety, and the evaluation of prospective partners (Buss & Schmitt, 1993; Buss, 1995). Within the Artificial Intimacy Environment, these differences generate the prediction that AI systems offering lowcost sexual access, sexual variety, and minimal risk of rejection may disproportionately engage male mating motivations associated with sexual access and short-term mating. This prediction concerns average sex-differentiated responses and does not imply categorical differences between men and women.

Different AI affordances may be particularly relevant to mating concerns historically weighted more strongly in female mate choice. Buss (1995) emphasized the recurrent adaptive problems faced by women in identifying mates both able and willing to invest, with cues of resources, status, commitment, and reliability consequently receiving greater weight in female mate preferences. Artificial companions can provide some psychologically relevant cues associated with investment, including sustained attention, responsiveness, availability, and apparent commitment, while lacking the material and reproductive consequences traditionally associated with partner investment. AIM therefore predicts that sex differences may emerge in the particular AI affordances that generate relational value, even when overall levels of engagement with Artificial Intimacy are similar. Whereas low-cost sexual access and variety may be especially relevant to male mating motivations on average, reliable responsiveness and cues associated with sustained investment may be relatively more salient to female mating motivations.

Biological sex should therefore be treated within AIM as a theoretically relevant moderator whose effects vary across domains and contexts. The expression of sexdifferentiated responses should depend on the specific AI affordances involved and interact with other sources of individual and contextual variation, including mating strategy, relationship status, sociosexual orientation, and the availability of human mating opportunities. This formulation follows Buss's (1995) broader argument that evolved sex differences are domain-specific and context-sensitive. AIM consequently predicts both sex similarities and sex differences: shared relational mechanisms should produce substantial overlap in responses to Artificial Intimacy, whereas AI affordances that intersect with historically sex-differentiated mating problems should produce more systematic divergence between men and women.

5.3. Recalibration of Relational Decision Making

Human mating decisions are governed not only by motivational systems but also by decision rules that regulate when, with whom, and under what conditions individuals pursue intimate relationships. These decision rules integrate multiple sources of information, including perceived partner quality, anticipated reciprocity, expected relational costs, and the likelihood of successful relationship formation (Eastwick et al., 2023; Kenrick et al., 2010; Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003). Because these decision rules evolved under conditions characterized by mutual evaluation and reciprocal partner choice, they remain sensitive to changes in the relational environment within which they operate (Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003; Selçuk et al., 2024).

The Artificial Intimacy Environment modifies several of the informational conditions on which these decision rules have historically depended. Continuous availability, reduced uncertainty, minimal risk of rejection, and the absence of reciprocal evaluation alter how relational opportunities are perceived and compared with traditional human interactions (Bruch & Newman, 2018; Eastwick et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2025). Consequently, repeated engagement with AI-mediated relationships may gradually recalibrate expectations concerning responsiveness, accessibility, effort, and interpersonal reciprocity (Krueger & Forest, 2022; Murray, 2023; Selçuk et al., 2024). Artificial Intimacy may reshape the criteria by which relational opportunities are evaluated, producing broader changes than a simple lowering of decision thresholds.

Evidence from digital dating environments demonstrates that changes in partner availability and choice architecture can substantially influence mate selection, preference formation, and decision-making (Bruch & Newman, 2018; Apostolou et al., 2024; Thomas et al., 2024; Potarca, 2025). Artificial Intimacy extends these processes beyond the expansion of partner choice by introducing interactions that no longer require mutual evaluation or reciprocal partner selection (Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). Although empirical research directly examining these recalibration processes remains limited, the Artificial Intimacy Model predicts that prolonged exposure to AI-mediated intimacy may progressively influence how individuals evaluate both artificial and human relational opportunities.

5.4. Consequences for Human Relationships

Changes in mating effort allocation and relational decision-making are ultimately expected to influence the dynamics of human intimate relationships. Because long-term partnerships depend on reciprocal investment, negotiation, and ongoing coordination between autonomous partners, systematic changes in how individuals allocate attention, evaluate alternatives, and experience relational rewards may gradually alter relationship expectations and interpersonal behavior (Finkel et al., 2017; Ogolsky & Stafford, 2023; Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003). Importantly, these consequences can arise because Artificial Intimacy changes the relational context against which human relationships are experienced and evaluated, even when it does not replace those relationships (Smith et al., 2025; Selçuk et al., 2024).

Compared with AI-mediated interaction, human relationships are characterized by fluctuating availability, competing personal goals, emotional variability, and the continual need for mutual adjustment (Finkel et al., 2017; Ogolsky & Stafford, 2023; Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003). These features are intrinsic properties of reciprocal relationships between autonomous individuals rather than imperfections of human intimacy (Clark et al., 2018; Reis & Clark, 2013). Consequently, prolonged exposure to highly responsive and low-cost AI companions may recalibrate expectations concerning availability, responsiveness, emotional effort, and relational predictability (Krueger & Forest, 2022; Murray, 2023; Selçuk et al., 2024). Under some circumstances, this contrast may increase dissatisfaction with the inherent complexity of human relationships, particularly when AI-mediated interaction provides a consistently more controllable relational experience (Malfacini, 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026).

At the same time, these outcomes are unlikely to be uniform across individuals or relational contexts. Emerging evidence suggests that AI companions may function as supplements rather than substitutes for human relationships, particularly among individuals experiencing loneliness, social isolation, or limited partner availability (Pani et al., 2024; De Freitas et al., 2025; Nakagomi et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). The Artificial Intimacy Model therefore predicts substantial individual differences in the extent to which Artificial Intimacy complements, competes with, or partially displaces human relational investment. Identifying the conditions under which these different trajectories emerge represents an important priority for future empirical research.

5.5. Broader Sociosexual Implications

Beyond its implications for individual relationships, Artificial Intimacy may also influence broader sociosexual dynamics. Human intimacy has historically been embedded within systems in which emotional, sexual, and material resources are exchanged under varying conditions of reciprocity and mutual dependence (Baumeister & Vohs, 2004; Tabet, 2012; Meskó, 2026). By introducing highly accessible, personalized, and increasingly monetized forms of artificial companionship, AI-mediated intimacy expands the range of relational contexts in which these exchanges occur (Döring et al., 2026; Malfacini, 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026; Wang & Dehnert, 2026). In doing so, it may also alter the balance between relational investment and relational reward.

These broader changes may gradually influence how individuals conceptualize the relationship between intimacy, sexuality, reciprocity, and resource exchange across diverse relational contexts. One framework for understanding such variation is the Sexual-Economic Exchange Orientation (OSEE; Meskó, 2026), which conceptualizes stable individual differences in orientations toward the integration of intimacy and resource exchange within relational contexts. Although OSEE was developed independently of Artificial Intimacy, the Artificial Intimacy Model suggests that AI-mediated relationships may represent an important emerging context in which these broader sociosexual orientations are expressed, reinforced, or recalibrated.

More broadly, Artificial Intimacy demonstrates how technological environments can reshape the conditions under which intimacy, sexuality, and relational investment are negotiated (Li et al., 2018; Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024). Understanding these broader sociosexual transformations will require integrating evolutionary psychology with relationship science, digital behavior research, and the psychology of sexual-economic exchange (Finkel et al., 2017; Meskó, 2026; Smith et al., 2025). The Artificial Intimacy Model therefore extends beyond the study of AI companions by identifying a broader research program for investigating how emerging technologies may transform fundamental dimensions of human relational life.

The primary value of a theoretical framework lies in its conceptual coherence and its capacity to generate cumulative empirical research. In psychology, theoretical models gain scientific value when they specify falsifiable propositions, identify measurable constructs, and provide a foundation for systematic empirical investigation (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Guest, 2024; Lewis et al., 2017; Muthukrishna & Henrich, 2019; Scheel et al., 2021). Accordingly, the Artificial Intimacy Model (AIM) should be evaluated as a framework that generates explicit predictions about how changes in relational environments influence psychological mechanisms and, ultimately, human relational behavior.

The preceding chapters have outlined a sequential theoretical model in which Artificial Intimacy constitutes a novel relational environment that engages evolved psychological systems under altered informational conditions, giving rise to predictable behavioral and relational consequences (Li et al., 2018; Selçuk et al., 2024). This architecture naturally generates a program of empirical research spanning multiple levels of analysis, from environmental characteristics and psychological mechanisms to behavioral outcomes and broader sociosexual consequences (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Muthukrishna & Henrich, 2019; Guest, 2024). The scientific value of AIM therefore depends on the empirical performance of its individual propositions and on its capacity to stimulate cumulative research capable of refining, extending, or falsifying its underlying assumptions.

7. General Discussion and Theoretical Integration

>General Discussion and Theoretical Integration

7.1. Artificial Intimacy as a Novel Relational Environment

The rapid emergence of AI companions has stimulated growing interest in their psychological and relational consequences, giving rise to a rapidly expanding interdisciplinary literature (Döring et al., 2025; Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). The central argument of the present article is that understanding these developments requires a conceptual shift in the level of analysis. AIM conceptualizes Artificial Intimacy as a transformation of the relational ecology within which evolved psychological systems operate, shifting the level of analysis from individual AI companions to the broader relational environment. This ecological transformation creates novel relational environments that systematically alter the informational conditions regulating human intimacy (Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024; Li et al., 2018). This shift from individual technologies to relational ecology provides the theoretical foundation for integrating diverse psychological phenomena within a single explanatory framework.

This conceptualization represents the principal theoretical contribution of the Artificial Intimacy Model (AIM). Throughout evolutionary history, human mating and attachment systems evolved within relational environments characterized by interaction with autonomous partners whose availability, responsiveness, and investment were constrained by their own goals, preferences, and competing demands (Bowlby, 1969 (Bowlby, /1982;; Buss & Schmitt, 2019; Finkel et al., 2017; Reis & Clark, 2013). AIM argues that Artificial Intimacy does not require the proposal of new psychological mechanisms. Instead, it proposes that transforming the relational ecology within which evolutionarily ancient psychological systems operate is sufficient to alter their functional expression (Li et al., 2018; Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024). Persistent availability, adaptive personalization, reduced relational constraints, and fundamentally asymmetric interaction therefore represent ecological transformations capable of systematically reshaping the operation of existing psychological systems (Kirk et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). The central contribution of AIM is therefore the recognition that a transformed relational ecology is sufficient to alter the operation of evolutionarily ancient psychological systems without requiring the proposal of a new psychological mechanism.

Viewing Artificial Intimacy as an environmental transformation also provides an integrative framework for interpreting findings that have previously been explained within separate theoretical traditions (Li et al., 2018; Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024).

Phenomena such as attachment formation toward AI companions, perceived intimacy, changing relational expectations, and shifts in mating effort need not be understood as independent consequences of different AI technologies (Ho et al., 2025; Shu et al., 2026; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025). Instead, they represent theoretically connected manifestations of the same underlying process: evolutionarily ancient psychological systems responding to a transformed relational ecology. This integrative perspective provides a common explanatory framework for organizing existing evidence while generating principled predictions about how future developments in AI-mediated intimacy are likely to influence human relational behavior.

7.2. The Explanatory Role of Evolutionary Psychology

One of the central claims of AIM is that evolutionary psychology provides the most appropriate explanatory framework because it explains why evolutionarily ancient psychological systems respond systematically to the transformed relational ecology created by Artificial Intimacy. AI-mediated relationships are relevant from an evolutionary perspective because they engage psychological systems that evolved under fundamentally different relational conditions (Buss, 2023; Li et al., 2018; Walter et al., 2020). Unlike theoretical approaches that primarily describe the characteristics of AI systems or the experiences of their users (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026), an evolutionary perspective explains why particular configurations of relational cues are expected to produce predictable psychological responses (Kenrick et al., 2003; Buss & Schmitt, 2019; Selçuk et al., 2024). In this respect, evolutionary psychology provides a functional account of the psychological architecture through which technological change acquires its behavioral consequences.

The distinctive contribution of an evolutionary perspective lies in its emphasis on function rather than form (Buss, 2023; Tooby & Cosmides, 1992). Whereas many contemporary approaches primarily describe patterns of human-AI interaction or the subjective experiences of users (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026), evolutionary psychology asks why particular configurations of relational cues are expected to produce systematic psychological responses (Kenrick et al., 2003; Li et al., 2018; Buss & Schmitt, 2019). Within this framework, attachment, mate choice, sexual motivation, jealousy, pair bonding, and relationship maintenance are understood as functionally specialized information-processing systems calibrated to recurrent socioecological conditions rather than to specific technologies or historical contexts (Bowlby, 1969 (Bowlby, /1982;; Buss, 2023; Finkel et al., 2017; Selçuk et al., 2024).

Consequently, the critical scientific question concerns how evolutionarily ancient psychological systems respond when exposed to novel configurations of evolutionarily familiar relational cues.

Accordingly, the value of an evolutionary perspective lies in its capacity to explain existing observations and generate precise, falsifiable predictions about how human psychology should respond as Artificial Intimacy becomes an increasingly prominent component of modern relational life (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Muthukrishna & Henrich, 2019; Buss, 2023). Within AIM, evolutionary mismatch functions as a unifying theoretical principle linking environmental transformation to predictable psychological consequences (Li et al., 2018; Walter et al., 2020). Artificial Intimacy is therefore conceptualized as a transformed relational ecology that presents evolutionarily familiar informational cues, such as availability, responsiveness, emotional validation, and sexual accessibility, in novel configurations that are amplified, continuously accessible, and partially decoupled from the reciprocal constraints under which they originally evolved (Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024; Kirk et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). Consequently, the predictions generated by AIM derive from established principles of evolutionary psychology rather than from assumptions tied to any particular AI technology or platform.

Importantly, an evolutionary perspective predicts conditional responses that vary systematically according to individual characteristics, developmental experiences, ecological conditions, and cultural environments (Buss, 2023; Walter et al., 2020; Selçuk et al., 2024), without implying technological determinism or universal psychological outcomes. AIM therefore does not assume that Artificial Intimacy will influence all individuals in similar ways. Instead, it specifies the conditions under which AI-mediated intimacy is most likely to supplement, compete with, or partially replace human relationships (Pani et al., 2024; De Freitas et al., 2025; Nakagomi et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). More broadly, this illustrates the distinctive explanatory role of an evolutionary perspective within AIM. Its primary contribution is to explain why systematic psychological variation should emerge when evolutionarily ancient psychological systems operate within an increasingly transformed relational ecology.

Within AIM, evolutionary psychology therefore provides a theory of how evolutionarily ancient psychological systems respond when the relational ecology within which they evolved is fundamentally transformed.

7.3. Positioning AIM Within the Human-AI Literature

The rapid expansion of research on human-AI relationships has produced a diverse body of theoretical perspectives for understanding interactions with artificial agents (Döring et al., 2025; Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026).

Existing approaches have examined AI companionship through the lenses of attachment theory, parasocial interaction, social presence, anthropomorphism, human-computer interaction, and relationship science (Epley et al., 2007; Ho et al., 2025; Kirk et al., 2025; Shu et al., 2026; Szczuka et al., 2026). Collectively, these perspectives have substantially advanced understanding of how people experience emotional closeness, trust, companionship, and social connection with artificial agents (Ho et al., 2025; Kirk et al., 2025; Shu et al., 2026). At the same time, they have tended to explain these phenomena within their respective theoretical traditions rather than within a common explanatory framework.

The Artificial Intimacy Model (AIM) is intended to complement rather than replace these existing perspectives. Whereas recent research has examined topics such as motivations for AI companionship (Vowels et al., 2026), AI-supported sexual activities (Döring et al., 2026), and professional perspectives on AI-assisted sexual counselling (Kubitza & Degen, 2026), most existing frameworks continue to focus primarily on the characteristics of AI systems, the experiences of users, or the dynamics of specific human-AI relationships (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). AIM shifts the level of analysis toward the broader relational environment in which these interactions occur (Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024). The central proposition of the model is therefore that AI companions collectively create a novel relational ecology that systematically alters the informational conditions under which evolved psychological systems operate. This perspective is broadly consistent with recent evolutionary discussions of Artificial Intimacy (Brooks, 2021; Costello, 2026), while extending them by explicitly conceptualizing Artificial Intimacy as a transformation of the relational environment and by developing a mechanistic framework linking environmental change, evolved psychological systems, behavioral consequences, and empirically testable predictions.

This environmental perspective also clarifies the relationship between AIM and several adjacent theoretical traditions. Attachment theory explains how emotional bonds are formed and maintained (Bowlby, 1969 (Bowlby, /1982;; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007); parasocial interaction research demonstrates that meaningful relationships can emerge despite structural asymmetry (Hartmann & Goldhoorn, 2011; Horton & Wohl, 1956); and research on anthropomorphism and social presence identifies the perceptual and cognitive processes that facilitate engagement with artificial agents (Epley et al., 2007; Kirk et al., 2025). AIM situates these explanations within a broader functional framework by explaining why these apparently distinct psychological phenomena emerge as coordinated responses to the same transformation in relational ecology. In this respect, AIM complements existing theories by providing a higher-level account of the environmental conditions under which their respective mechanisms are jointly engaged.

This distinction has important implications for future theory development. As AI technologies continue to evolve, individual platforms, interaction modalities, and forms of artificial companionship will inevitably change (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). Frameworks that are tied primarily to particular technologies or interaction modalities may therefore require continual revision as those technologies evolve.

By contrast, conceptualizing Artificial Intimacy as a transformation of relational ecology shifts the explanatory focus from technological features to the environmental conditions under which evolved psychological systems operate (Li et al., 2018; Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024). In this respect, AIM is intended as a higher-level explanatory framework linking technological change to the operation of evolutionarily ancient psychological mechanisms across successive generations of AI technologies.

7.4. Scope Conditions and Limitations

As with any conceptual framework, the Artificial Intimacy Model (AIM) is intended to specify a bounded set of theoretically grounded propositions rather than to provide a comprehensive explanation of all forms of human-AI interaction (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Guest, 2024; Muthukrishna & Henrich, 2019). Its primary objective is to explain how transformations in relational ecology are expected to influence evolutionarily ancient psychological systems under particular conditions, with its explanatory scope restricted to this specific set of psychological processes (Li et al., 2018; Selçuk et al., 2024). Consequently, AIM identifies general mechanisms while explicitly recognizing that the magnitude, direction, and persistence of their effects are likely to vary as a function of biological sex, other individual characteristics, relationship contexts, developmental experiences, and cultural environments (Buss, 1995 (Buss, , 2023;; Selçuk et al., 2024; Walter et al., 2020).

Several scope conditions deserve particular emphasis. First, AIM does not predict a single developmental trajectory for Artificial Intimacy or assume that AI companions will inevitably replace human relationships (Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025). Instead, the model specifies a range of theoretically plausible relational outcomes. Depending on biological sex and other individual characteristics, developmental experiences, ecological circumstances, and situational factors, Artificial Intimacy may function as a supplement to existing relationships, a temporary substitute during periods of social isolation, or, under some conditions, a partial competitor for relational investment (Pani et al., 2024; De Freitas et al., 2025; Nakagomi et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). Which of these trajectories emerges is therefore an empirical question rather than a theoretical assumption.

Second, AIM is developed primarily with reference to conversational and relational AI systems designed to facilitate emotionally meaningful interaction (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). However, the proposed theoretical framework is defined by the characteristics of the relational ecology rather than by any particular technological implementation (Li et al., 2018; Oishi & Graham, 2010; Selçuk et al., 2024). Consequently, an important question for future research is the extent to which the proposed mechanisms generalize across different forms of Artificial Intimacy, including future embodied agents and multimodal systems that may introduce additional relational cues while preserving the fundamental ecological transformation described by the model (Brooks, 2021; Kirk et al., 2025).

Finally, although AIM is grounded in established principles of evolutionary psychology, it does not imply universal or deterministic responses to Artificial Intimacy (Buss, 2023; Walter et al., 2020). Rather, it predicts conditional psychological responses that emerge through the interaction of evolutionarily ancient psychological mechanisms with developmental experiences, individual differences, social learning, and cultural environments (Buss, 2023; Walter et al., 2020; Selçuk et al., 2024). Accordingly, AIM should be understood as a probabilistic explanatory framework that specifies the conditions under which particular psychological responses are expected to occur, rather than as a deterministic account of human-AI relationships.

7.5. Conclusions

Artificial Intimacy represents one of the most profound transformations of the human relational landscape since the emergence of digital communication. As AI companions become increasingly capable of providing emotionally meaningful, personalized, and continuously available interactions, understanding the psychological consequences of this transformed relational ecology will become an increasingly important task for relationship science, evolutionary psychology, and the broader behavioral sciences (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026).

The Artificial Intimacy Model (AIM) identifies transformed relational ecology as a key

determinant of how evolutionarily ancient psychological systems operate under conditions of Artificial Intimacy. By conceptualizing Artificial Intimacy as a transformed relational environment rather than merely a technological innovation, AIM provides an integrative explanatory framework linking changes in relational ecology to evolved psychological mechanisms, their behavioral consequences, and a program of empirically testable propositions. The model complements existing theories of human-AI interaction by offering a higher-level explanatory framework capable of integrating diverse psychological phenomena within a common evolutionary architecture.

Whether Artificial Intimacy ultimately supplements, reshapes, or partially replaces aspects of human relationships remains an empirical question. What appears increasingly clear, however, is that the relational environments within which human intimacy develops are changing more rapidly than at any previous point in human history (Ho et al., 2025; Malfacini, 2025; Smith et al., 2025; Szczuka et al., 2026). Understanding how evolutionarily ancient psychological systems respond to these transformations will therefore be essential for explaining contemporary human relationships and anticipating how the ecology of human intimacy may continue to evolve in an increasingly artificial world. The significance of Artificial Intimacy may ultimately lie in its capacity to transform the relational ecology within which human nature is expressed.
