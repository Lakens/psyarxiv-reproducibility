Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .15 In contrast, no significant differences in network structure were observed between adolescents younger than 14 years and those aged 14 years or older (M = .23, p = .15).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Avoiding Feelings and Resisting Closeness: A Network Analysis of Attachment and Affect

Regulation in Adolescents with Mental Health Concerns

Adolescence is a critical period for the emergence of emotional and behavioural difficulties, with approximately half of all lifetime mental disorders developing before age 18 (Solmi et al., 2022). At the same time, adolescents face increasing emotional, social, and interpersonal demands, making affect regulation particularly important for healthy adjustment (Sisk & Gee, 2022). Among the factors that promote resilience during this developmental period, secure attachment relationships have consistently been associated with better emotional, social, and behavioural functioning (Godor et al., 2024; Malik et al., 2021; Sancho et al., 2025). Attachment theory proposes that repeated interactions with caregivers shape internal working models, or expectations regarding the availability and responsiveness of others that guide how individuals interpret social experiences, seek support, and respond to distress (Allen & Tan, 2016; Godor et al., 2024; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007). Increasingly, researchers have identified affect regulation as a key mechanism through which these attachment-related expectations influence adjustment and risk for psychopathology (Eilert & Buchheim, 2023; Lewczuk et al., 2021; Pascuzzo et al., 2015; Salavou et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2022). Consistent with this perspective, attachment insecurity has been linked to difficulties understanding, managing, and responding to emotional experiences, as well as elevated symptoms of anxiety, depression, and behavioural concerns (Chan et al., 2023; Vernon & Moretti, 2024; Wang et al., 2025).

Despite strong theoretical and empirical support for the connection between attachment and affect regulation, research has predominantly examined these constructs at the level of broad attachment dimensions and affect regulation processes rather than individual experiences and beliefs. Consequently, relatively little is known about how specific attachment experiences and affect regulation processes are organized and interconnected. For example, fears of abandonment, reluctance to seek support, emotional suppression, and beliefs about emotions may each play distinct roles in adolescent adjustment. Examining how these experiences relate to one another may provide a more nuanced understanding of the processes linking attachment insecurity and affect regulation difficulties during adolescence.

Network Approaches to Attachment and Affect Regulation

Network theory provides a useful framework for investigating these questions by conceptualizing psychological phenomena as systems of mutually interacting experiences, beliefs, emotions, and behaviours (Schmittmann et al., 2013). Within this framework, attachment and affect regulation can be understood as interconnected systems in which specific attachment-related expectations and regulatory processes co-occur and reinforce one another. Rather than focusing exclusively on broad dimensions of attachment insecurity or affect regulation, network approaches allow researchers to identify which individual experiences are most strongly interconnected and which may serve as bridges linking different psychological domains.

Recent network studies have begun to apply this approach to attachment, affect, and emotion regulation. In adult samples, network analyses have demonstrated that attachment insecurity is closely intertwined with emotion regulation difficulties and psychopathology, with maladaptive regulatory strategies such as expressive suppression and rumination emerging as important bridge processes linking attachment-related experiences to symptoms of psychological distress (Fuchshuber et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2024). Similarly, adolescent network studies have identified attachment-related experiences as central features within broader networks of anxiety, self-injury, childhood trauma, peer relationships, and family functioning (Dong et al., 2025; Joss et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings support the view that attachment is embedded within broader socioemotional systems and is closely linked to emotion regulation and mental health outcomes. However, despite growing interest in network approaches to attachment and emotion regulation, relatively little research has examined how parentadolescent attachment and affect regulation processes are jointly organized at the level of specific experiences within clinical adolescent populations. This represents an important area for investigation given theoretical models positioning attachment relationships as central contexts for the development and organization of affect regulation and psychopathology during adolescence (Allen & Tan, 2016; Gross & Cassidy, 2019).

Present Study

The present study examined the organization of attachment-related experiences and affect regulation processes within a clinical sample of adolescents. Using network analysis, we investigated how specific attachment and affect regulation experiences clustered together and interconnected at the item level. Particular attention was given to identifying bridge indicators linking attachment insecurity and affect regulation, as these experiences may provide insight into how attachment-related vulnerabilities are associated with adolescent emotional and behavioural difficulties. Based on previous research linking attachment anxiety with heightened emotional reactivity and attachment avoidance with emotional suppression (Brenning et al., 2012; Gross & Cassidy, 2019), we hypothesized that attachment anxiety would be more closely associated with affect dysregulation, whereas attachment avoidance would be more closely associated with affect suppression. We also explored whether the structure of the attachment-affect regulation network differed across age and sex.

Methods

Participants & Procedures

Baseline data were collected from youth (N = 608; 57% female; age 8-19, Mage = 13.98, SD = 2.36) whose caregivers enrolled in a large-scale evaluation of an evidence-based parenting program offered to caregivers seeking support for adolescent behavioral and social-emotional concerns ("Connect"; Moretti, 2020; Moretti et al., 2025). Caregivers were referred through urban and rural community mental health agencies, schools, and hospitals due to concerns regarding their adolescent's mental health and/or behavioral functioning. Exclusion criteria were low intellectual functioning (IQ < 70) or a diagnosis of psychosis. Youth reported their ethnicity as: 63.9% White, 14.4% Indigenous Canadian (e.g., First Nations, Métis, Inuit), 5.4% Asian, 8.0% reported infrequent responses, and 8.3% did not report ethnicity. Parents and adolescents were each given a $25 honorarium upon completing the questionnaire. Prior to participation, parental consent and youth assent were obtained. All procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Board at -BLINDED-university (Study Number: -BLINDED-).

Measures

Attachment

The Adolescent Attachment Anxiety and Avoidance Inventory (AAAAI; Moretti et al., 2015, Payne et al., under review) is a 16-item measure of adolescent-parent attachment adapted from Brennan, Clark, and Shaver's (1996, 1998) gold-standard adult self-report attachment measure, the Experiences in Close Relationships scale (ECR). Seven items of this measure assess attachment anxiety (e.g., "I worry about being abandoned by my parent") while nine assess attachment avoidance (e.g., "I usually discuss my problems and concerns with my parent", reverse scored). Youth reported on their relationship over the past 6 months with the parent or caregiver who has been most influential in raising them on a 7-point scale. Numerous studies have supported the robust psychometric properties of this measure, including strong internal consistency (Bao & Moretti, 2023; Barone et al., 2021; Benzi et al., 2023; Craig et al., 2021; Hayre et al., 2023; Pasalich et al., 2022; Moretti et al., 2025; Payne et al., under review), construct validity (Payne et al., under review), and concurrent validity (Persram & Konishi, 2022).

Affect Regulation

The Affect Regulation Checklist (ARC, Moretti, 2003) is a 12-item measure designed to assess affect regulation in youth with a 5-point Likert scale. This measure is structured around three core dimensions: (1) affect dysregulation (4 items; e.g., "I have a hard time controlling my feelings"), (2) affect suppression (4 items; e.g., "I try hard not to think about my feelings"), and (3) affect reflection (4 items; e.g., "Thinking about why I have different feelings helps me to learn about myself"). Importantly, the ARC was intentionally designed to assess general responses to affective arousal, avoiding references to specific emotions. Within this framework, affect dysregulation refers to the inability to engage in adaptive regulation strategies when confronted with intense emotional experiences. Affect suppression denotes efforts to avoid, control, or distract oneself from these strong affective states, whereas affect reflection involves a cognitive process of stepping back to consider one's emotional experiences and their underlying causes. The ARC has been empirically validated, demonstrating robust psychometric properties, including high internal consistency (Ames et al., 2022; Benzi et al., 2023; Brown et al., 2021; Craig et al., 2022; Moretti et al., 2015), as well as strong construct and structural validity (Goulter et al., 2022; Vernon & Moretti, 2024).

Data Analytic Approach

The network structure of adolescent attachment and affect regulation was examined in R version 4.3.1 using R Studio version 2023.06.1+524 (R Core Team, 2023). Items from the attachment and affect regulation measures were represented as nodes, with associations between items depicted as edges.

Pairwise Markov Random Field (PMRF) graphical models were estimated to examine direct associations among items while controlling for all other variables in the network (Isvoranu et al., 2022). To improve the stability and interpretability of the network, regularization was applied using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO; Tibshirani, 1996) in conjunction with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion Graphical Lasso estimator (EBICglasso). Following recommendations by Foygel and Drton (2010), the EBIC tuning parameter was set to 0.50, yielding a parsimonious network while minimizing the likelihood of spurious edges (Epskamp et al., 2018). Communities of densely connected nodes were identified using the walktrap community detection algorithm (Pons & Latapy, 2005). This approach has been shown to perform comparably to factor analytic methods when identifying clusters of psychological variables and may be particularly advantageous when constructs are highly correlated (Christensen & Golino, 2021; Golino & Epskamp, 2017). Node importance was evaluated using strength, expected influence, and predictability indices, estimated using the qgraph (Epskamp et al., 2012) and mgm (Haslbeck & Waldorp, 2020) packages. The stability and accuracy of network parameters were assessed using bootstrap procedures implemented in the bootnet package (Epskamp et al., 2017), including bootstrapped difference tests for centrality indices. Network differences across sex and age groups were examined using the NetworkComparisonTest package (Van Borkulo et al., 2017).

Permutation tests were conducted to compare network structure and global strength between groups.

Networks were considered significantly different when at least one edge or node differed after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Sex was self-reported. Consistent with Statistics Canada (2011) guidelines and the mean age of the sample, adolescents younger than 14 years were classified as the younger group, whereas those aged 14 years and older were classified as the older group.

Discussion

The present study examined how attachment-related experiences and affect regulation processes are organized among adolescents with mental health concerns. While attachment anxiety, attachment avoidance, affect dysregulation/suppression, and affect reflection emerged as distinct communities, they did not operate in isolation. Instead, the network was anchored by two key experiences: believing that thinking about one's feelings makes things worse and wanting closeness with attachment figures while simultaneously pulling away from it. These findings highlight beliefs about emotions and uncertainty regarding interpersonal closeness as important points of connection between attachment insecurity and affect regulation difficulties during adolescence. The broader network structure provides additional insight into how these experiences are organized and interconnected.

The finding that attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance emerged as separate communities aligns with attachment theory's view of anxiety and avoidance as distinct patterns of expectations regarding caregiver availability and responsiveness (Allen & Tan, 2016). In contrast, affect dysregulation and suppression formed a single community when examined alongside attachment dimensions. This pattern suggests that, among adolescents experiencing mental health concerns, becoming overwhelmed by emotional experiences and attempting to suppress those experiences may function as closely interconnected aspects of emotional functioning. These findings are consistent with contemporary perspectives that conceptualize emotion regulation difficulties as involving interacting patterns of emotional reactivity, avoidance, and suppression rather than discrete regulatory styles (Cole et al., 2019; Gross & Cassidy, 2019; Lougheed & Keskin, 2021).

Notably, the affect reflection items were not closely connected to the dysregulation and suppression community, suggesting that adolescents' self-reported tendency to reflect on their feelings, behaviour, and life experiences to gain self-understanding represents a distinct aspect of emotional functioning. Because these findings were based on adolescents' own reports, they reflect youths' awareness of engaging in self-reflective processes rather than the quality or effectiveness of those processes. This distinction may be important, as developmental models increasingly recognize that awareness of one's emotional experiences and the capacity to regulate those experiences are related but distinct components of emotional functioning (Lougheed et al., 2020; Nook et al., 2021). Accordingly, adolescents who perceive themselves as engaging in self-reflection may nonetheless experience significant difficulties managing or avoiding their emotions. One interpretation is that self-reflection facilitates emotional awareness and self-understanding, but does not, on its own, ensure the flexible and effective regulation of emotional experiences (Gross et al., 2019; Lougheed & Keskin, 2021). The separation of the reflection community from the dysregulation/suppression community therefore suggests that emotional awareness and emotional regulation should not be assumed to reflect a single underlying process. Rather, adolescents may report engaging in self-reflection while simultaneously experiencing substantial difficulties regulating their emotional experiences.

Developmental Differences

Age-related analyses revealed similar network structures across age groups, but stronger overall connectivity among attachment and affect regulation nodes in older adolescents. Greater network connectivity may therefore reflect increasing integration between attachment-related experiences and emotion regulation processes across adolescence. Although the present study cannot directly examine this developmental process, the findings suggest that network approaches may provide a useful methodology for investigating how attachment representations become increasingly interconnected with affect regulation over time. In contrast, evidence for sex differences was limited. Although an overall difference in network structure was initially observed, no individual edges remained significant following correction for multiple comparisons, and no differences emerged in global strength, suggesting that the overall organization of attachment and affect regulation is largely similar across adolescent males and females in the present sample.

Avoiding Feelings and Resisting Closeness

The identification of two bridge nodes highlights the interconnected nature of attachment and affect regulation during adolescence. Rather than representing isolated attachment or emotion regulation processes, these experiences occupied positions linking the two systems, suggesting that adolescents' emotional experiences are closely embedded within their expectations regarding interpersonal support.

The strongest bridge, R4 ("Thinking about my feelings just makes everything worse"), should not be interpreted simply as reflecting negative beliefs about emotions. Instead, this item may capture adolescents' broader difficulty making sense of their emotional experiences. From an attachment perspective, the ability to understand and organize emotional experiences develops within relationships characterized by caregiver availability, emotional responsiveness, and co-regulation (Allen & Tan, 2016; Godor et al., 2024; Mikulincer & Shaver, 2007). For adolescents experiencing attachment insecurity, emotional experiences may remain difficult to organize or integrate, leading reflection itself to feel confusing, overwhelming, or unhelpful. In this context, negative beliefs about emotions may represent one manifestation of a broader difficulty developing coherent meaning from emotional experiences rather than an isolated cognitive belief. The peripheral position of the broader reflection community alongside the central role of these beliefs may therefore suggest that unsuccessful emotional sense-making, rather than reflection itself, is particularly relevant to the intersection of attachment insecurity and emotion regulation.

The second bridge, Av6 ("I want to get close to my parent, but I keep pulling back"), reflects a complementary relational process. Adolescence is characterized by increasing autonomy alongside continued reliance on caregivers for emotional security (Allen & Tan, 2016; Kobak et al., 2015). For adolescents experiencing mental health difficulties, this developmental transition may become especially complex. Some adolescents may simultaneously desire parental support while expecting that support to be unavailable, ineffective, or emotionally unsafe based on previous relational experiences (Decarli et al., 2022; Gander et al., 2020). Others may resist seeking support because developmental pressures toward autonomy encourage them to manage emotional experiences independently (Babo et al., 2024).

Consequently, the desire for connection may coexist with withdrawal from available support, reducing opportunities for co-regulation precisely when emotional experiences are most difficult to navigate (Silvers, 2022). Taken together, these bridge nodes suggest that attachment insecurity and affect regulation difficulties intersect through adolescents' attempts to understand and manage emotional experiences within the context of attachment relationships. Rather than identifying isolated intervention targets, the findings highlight interconnected developmental processes whereby difficulties making sense of emotions and uncertainty regarding caregiver availability may mutually reinforce one another.

Clinical Implications

Attachment insecurity and difficulties with affect regulation are well-established transdiagnostic risk factors for a broad range of emotional and behavioural difficulties (Brenning et al., 2022; Ein-Dor et al., 2016; Klein et al., 2022). The present findings suggest that interventions targeting emotion regulation may be most effective when situated within adolescents' relational contexts. Although interventions that teach emotion regulation skills can improve psychological functioning (Espenes et al., 2024; Moltrecht et al., 2021), the current network suggests that difficulties regulating emotion may not arise solely from deficits in regulatory skills. Rather, emotion regulation difficulties appear closely intertwined with adolescents' experiences of attachment relationships, including uncertainty regarding caregiver availability and difficulty making coherent sense of emotional experiences. This interpretation complements research demonstrating that beliefs about emotions and their perceived controllability are associated with emotion regulation strategies and psychopathology (Becerra et al., 2024; Deplancke et al., 2023; Dickerson et al., 2018; Ford et al., 2018; Ford & Gross, 2019; Ge et al., 2024; Somerville et al., 2024), while suggesting that these beliefs may themselves be shaped within relational contexts.

Accordingly, interventions that simultaneously strengthen caregiver-adolescent relationships while promoting emotional understanding, reflective functioning, and co-regulation may be particularly well positioned to address the interconnected processes identified in the present network.

At the relational level, attachment-based parenting interventions have shown promise for reducing adolescent attachment insecurity, behavioural problems, and parent-child affect dysregulation (Moretti et al., 2015; Vernon & Moretti, under review). The present findings may help refine this intervention focus by highlighting the importance of caregiver emotional availability, particularly when adolescents experience uncertainty about seeking support. As adolescents seek greater independence, caregivers continue to serve as important sources of security and co-regulation, even when support needs are not communicated directly (Allen & Tan, 2016; Kobak et al., 2015). Interventions that support caregivers in remaining emotionally accessible and responsive, while helping adolescents build confidence in the availability of support, may be especially relevant for addressing the attachment-affect regulation processes identified in the current network.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the crosssectional design precludes conclusions regarding the directionality of associations among attachmentrelated experiences and affect regulation processes. Longitudinal research is needed to determine whether the bridge processes identified in the current study represent developmental pathways through which attachment insecurity contributes to subsequent emotional and behavioural difficulties. Second, all variables were assessed using adolescent self-report measures. Although self-report provides valuable insight into youths' subjective experiences, future research would benefit from simultaneously examining caregiver and adolescent perceptions of attachment and emotion regulation using multiple methodologies, including observational paradigms, interview-based assessments, and caregiver-and youth-report measures. Examining these processes across multiple levels of analysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how adolescents' emotional experiences are shaped within attachment relationships and how caregiver and adolescent regulatory processes interact over time. Third, the sample consisted exclusively of adolescents referred for mental health or behavioural concerns. While this clinical sample provided a valuable opportunity to examine attachment-related affect regulation among youth at elevated risk for emotional difficulties, the findings may not generalize to community populations. Future studies should compare attachment-affect regulation networks across clinical and non-clinical samples to determine whether the observed network structure and bridge processes are unique to adolescents experiencing mental health concerns. Finally, self-reported biological sex, rather than gender identity, was examined using a binary categorization of male and female participants. As sex and gender are distinct constructs, the present findings cannot be generalized to gender-diverse adolescents, whose attachment experiences, emotion regulation processes, and mental health outcomes differ in important ways (Caldarera et al., 2022; Mezzalira et al., 2022). Future research should adopt more inclusive approaches to assessing gender identity and examine whether the organization of attachment and affect regulation differs across diverse gender groups. Despite these limitations, the present study demonstrates the value of network approaches for investigating attachment-related affect regulation during adolescence.

Conclusion

This study examined the organization of attachment-related experiences and affect regulation processes within a clinical sample of adolescents. Attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance emerged as distinct dimensions of insecurity, whereas affect dysregulation and suppression formed a unified regulatory community. Most notably, negative beliefs about emotional reflection and conflicting feelings regarding closeness to caregivers emerged as key bridge processes linking attachment insecurity with affect regulation difficulties. These findings support perspectives that conceptualize attachment relationships as central contexts for affect regulation and highlight beliefs about emotions and interpersonal support as key features of the attachment-affect regulation network among adolescents with mental health concerns.
