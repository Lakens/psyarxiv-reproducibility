Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Cochlear implants work, but not equally. After decades of cohort studies, the familiar predictors of outcome (age, duration of deafness, residual hearing, etiology) still leave most of the spread between patients unexplained. Research on how the brain handles degraded speech, audiovisual cues and everyday sounds shows that implant outcome measures test the brain at least as much as the ear. Yet candidacy assessment still examines the cochlea, the auditory nerve and inner-ear anatomy, and rarely the cognitive and structural state of the brain that must learn to use the device. This article argues that undetected neural variability, including cognitive decline, hearing-related atrophy and incidental pathology in brains assumed to be normal, is a plausible and largely unmeasured contributor to the missing variance. It proposes routine cognitive and volumetric phenotyping of implant candidates, and of the control groups against which they are judged. Because the brain restores much of the degraded implant signal through its predictive mechanisms, the article also calls for a new line of work: the development of dedicated tests of predictive brain capacity, a measure currently absent from both candidacy assessment and outcome research.

1. The patient who should have done well

Every cochlear implant clinic knows the patient who should have done well and did not. The surgery was uneventful, the array was fully inserted, the fitting was unremarkable, and the audiological history predicted a good result. A year later, open-set word recognition is still poor, and none of the usual explanations quite fits. This is rather frequent. In the largest multicentre analyses of adult implant outcome, the classic predictors (duration of severe-to-profound loss, age at implantation, age at onset, etiology, implant experience) were statistically significant, yet together they explained only a small fraction of the differences between patients (Blamey et al., 2013; Lazard et al., 2012).

Adding newer factors, such as residual hearing and device characteristics, helped without closing the gap, and the decline in performance with longer deafness was attributed to central reorganization rather than to the ear (Lazard et al., 2012).

That central processing shapes implant outcome is not in dispute; it is the premise of most work in the field, including the studies reviewed below. The thesis here is narrower. Research has treated the brain almost entirely as something deafness acts upon, through reorganization, plasticity and crossmodal takeover. Variability that has nothing to do with deafness, such as baseline structural integrity, cognitive reserve and incidental pathology, is measured neither in candidates nor in the normal-hearing controls they are compared with.

Implant teams image the temporal bone and the auditory nerve, measure residual hearing, and screen general fitness for surgery. Cognitive testing is not a standard part of candidacy in most programmes, and where brain MRI is acquired, it is read for surgical contraindications rather than quantified. Some share of the "unexplained" variance plausibly belongs to neural factors nobody measured: cognitive decline, hearing-related atrophy, and incidental structural anomalies in brains assumed to be normal.

Work on vocoded speech, audiovisual prediction, crossmodal interaction and sound categorization shows that implant outcome measures depend heavily on central processing. The brains of implant candidates, and of the normal-hearing controls they are compared with, are less homogeneous than study designs assume. Hearing loss is itself associated with structural change and cognitive decline, and cognition predicts outcome. An important argument, to our knowledge not made before, concerns prediction. The brain continually predicts incoming sensory information (Strelnikov, 2026), and we propose that much of what the implant fails to transmit is restored by predictive mechanisms of the brain. Yet predictive capacity is measured neither in candidates nor in controls, and no dedicated test of it is in clinical use. These are the main arguments we expose below.

2. Implant outcome is a test of the brain

A speech score after implantation looks like a measure of hearing. It is better understood as the output of a chain of central operations: extracting structure from a spectrally impoverished signal, predicting what comes next, recruiting visual cues, and assigning meaning.

Intelligibility is computed.

Our vocoding experiments in normal-hearing listeners have shown that acoustic degradation affects much of the temporal lobe uniformly, whereas the loss of intelligibility is reflected in specific posterior temporal regions; voice discrimination also survives degradation far better than word comprehension (Strelnikov et al., 2011). The same degraded input therefore yields different functional outcomes depending on which cortical network has to use it.

The brain fills gaps by prediction.

It was demonstrated that a speaker's facial expressions trigger a cortical response anticipating vocal emphasis before the corresponding sound arrives (Strelnikov et al., 2015). In continuous speech, vision sets up a prediction of the auditory signal in the context of the phrase. Real-world listening with an implant leans heavily on exactly this kind of top-down anticipation. More generally, the brain continuously maintains and updates predictions about its environment; these predictions are shaped by past experience and embedded in the flow of neural activity, at rest as well as during tasks (Strelnikov, 2026). For an implant user, we argue, this predictive machinery is what restores the degraded signal: what the electrode array fails to transmit, the brain must supply from expectation. Individual differences in predictive capacity are therefore likely to translate into differences in outcome.

Recovery runs through vision.

Postlingually deaf implant users integrate auditory and visual speech more efficiently than normal-hearing listeners presented with comparably degraded sound, and they keep supra-normal speechreading for years after implantation (Rouger et al., 2007). Visual and frontal activity shortly after activation predicts later speech recovery, whereas right superior temporal activity predicts it negatively (Strelnikov et al., 2013).

The influence of vision does not switch off when speech recovers.

When implant users categorised the gender of voices paired with faces, they remained strongly influenced by the visual information even after maximal speech recovery, and even when compared with normal-hearing listeners at matched auditory performance (Barone et al., 2016). Speech scores, in other words, can normalize while the underlying audiovisual architecture remains different from controls.

Children solve the problem differently. Implanted children were shown to categorize everyday sounds much as normal-hearing peers do, despite poorer identification (Berland et al., 2019). But they relied more on acoustic features than on meaning for some sounds, and individual variation was considerable in both groups. Categorization is a cognitive strategy layered on top of the auditory signal, and children differ in how they deploy it.

The common lesson is methodological. Two patients with the same word score may have reached it through different central routes, and two patients with the same cochlea and device may reach very different scores. Any variation in the integrity of the networks for prediction, multisensory integration, working memory and categorisation feeds directly into outcome variance. If that variation is not measured, it is filed under noise.

3. The missing variance, and where the field has looked for it

If clinical history explains only a small part of outcome variance, where is the rest? For twenty-five years, the dominant answer has been crossmodal reorganization: deafness rewires the auditory and visual cortices, and the nature of that rewiring sets the ceiling for recovery. The imaging evidence is real, but it is inconsistent in direction and almost entirely interpreted through that single lens.

Study design adds a further twist. In the paediatric MRI study, following standard practice, children with developmental delay, additional disabilities or gross brain malformation were excluded, and no implanted child showed acquired brain anomalies (Feng et al., 2018). Such screening protects a study's internal validity. It also means that the brains most likely to explain poor outcomes are often removed from the samples that try to explain them, while remaining in clinical caseloads.

4. "Normal" brains often are not

Research designs treat "normal" as a property a participant either has or lacks. Population imaging says otherwise. A meaningful minority of people with no neurological complaint carry structural findings on MRI, and the proportion is higher in exactly the groups that fill implant clinics: young children with congenital hearing loss and adults over 60.

Large imaging studies of asymptomatic people consistently find clinically relevant incidental findings on a small but non-trivial share of scans, more often with high-resolution sequences, and silent vascular lesions become common with age (Morris et al., 2009; Vernooij et al., 2007). Young research volunteers are not exempt (Gur et al., 2013). In paediatric implant candidates, brain abnormalities, mostly whitematter changes but also ventriculomegaly and other structural lesions, are common on pre-implant scans (Hong et al., 2010; Moon et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2015).

In implanted children, these findings are not inert. Children with brain lesions were shown to progress over two years, though their performance remained below that of matched implanted controls, which led to the suggestion that pre-operative MRI could help anticipate poor outcomes (Moon et al., 2012). Central nervous system anomalies were likewise a negative prognostic factor in a large Italian series, even though good outcomes remained possible (Busi et al., 2015). Beyond imaging, many children with hearing loss have additional disabilities, and the type of disability shapes their language outcomes (Cupples et al., 2014).

Two consequences follow. The first concerns patients. When MRI is acquired, a white-matter change or an enlarged ventricle that does not contraindicate implantation rarely enters the outcome model. In older adults, whose scans would carry the vascular and atrophic changes documented in population imaging, imaging is often limited to the temporal bone and internal auditory canals, so the question is never asked.

The second concerns controls. Normal-hearing participants in vocoder studies and imaging comparisons are typically screened by questionnaire and audiogram, not by cognitive testing or a radiological read. Given how common incidental findings are even among young volunteers, few control groups will be entirely free of them. Most of those findings will be irrelevant to auditory processing. But no one checks which, and in group designs the control average is the baseline against which patients' reorganization is defined.

5. The hearing loss that qualifies a patient also changes the brain

For older candidates, now a growing share of implant caseloads, hidden brain variability is not merely background noise. It is partly produced by the very condition that brings them to the clinic.

In longitudinal MRI studies of older adults, hearing impairment was associated with accelerated wholebrain atrophy and faster volume loss in the right temporal lobe (Lin et al., 2014), and hearing measured in midlife predicted steeper late-life decline in temporal grey matter, the hippocampus and the entorhinal cortex (Armstrong et al., 2019).

The temporal cortex carries speech and voice processing; the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex support the associative learning that implant users rely on to map new electrical patterns onto familiar words. The networks that must adapt to the implant are among those most exposed to hearing-related decline.

Epidemiology also frames the stakes. The latest Lancet Commission ranks hearing loss among the leading potentially modifiable risk factors for dementia (Livingston et al., 2024). Such estimates may assume causality, but the mechanism remains debated.

A randomized trial adds a lesson that matters directly for implant research. In older adults with untreated hearing loss, a hearing intervention did not slow cognitive decline overall, but it did so substantially in a pre-specified subgroup with more risk factors and lower baseline cognition (Lin et al., 2023). The response to restoring hearing depended on the state of the brain receiving it.

Implant data show the same heterogeneity. Before implantation, a large proportion of older candidates scored abnormally on cognitive testing; a year later, most of them had improved, yet some of those with the best initial profiles showed a slight decline (Mosnier et al., 2015). The improvement suggests that much of the deficit reflected deprivation rather than fixed disease. Thus, some brains recover with sound; others continue on a separate trajectory that a speech score alone cannot reveal.

6. Cognition predicts outcome

If the state of the brain shapes outcome, cognitive measures taken before surgery should carry prognostic information. They do, though less cleanly than one can suggest.

Pre-operative verbal learning and speechreading skill were shown to predict much of the individual improvement in word recognition after activation (Heydebrand et al., 2007), and pre-operative long-term memory was associated with later speech perception in noise in older recipients (Mosnier et al., 2015). Contributions of working memory, inhibition and nonverbal reasoning have been documented in postlingually deaf adults, and it has been argued that poor performers deserve a diagnostic workup of their own (Moberly et al., 2016; Pisoni et al., 2017).

The evidence also carries an honest caveat. In postlingually deaf adults, Holden et al. (2013) found cognition positively related to word recognition, but the effect disappeared once age at implantation was controlled. That result is often read as showing that cognition does not matter. It can equally be read as showing that age is a proxy for something cognitive and structural that the study did not measure directly.

The instruments are part of the problem. A systematic review found that no cognitive screening tool had been validated for adults with postlingual hearing loss, and that the auditory components of these tools may depress scores in exactly this population, although the evidence on this point was mixed (Raymond et al., 2021). Screening a deaf candidate with a test administered by voice confounds the predictor with the condition being treated.

Even a valid cognitive score has a deeper limitation. It cannot distinguish a reversible deprivation effect, the kind that improved in most older recipients after implantation, from fixed structural pathology that will not respond to sound. Only the combination of cognitive testing with structural imaging can separate the two, and that combination is almost never part of candidacy assessment.

A further gap concerns what is tested. The domains examined so far, such as verbal learning, working memory, inhibition and reasoning, all contribute to prediction, but none of them measures it. Yet prediction is, we argue, the operation through which the brain restores a degraded signal (Section 2), and predictive models are thought to be embedded in brain activity even at rest (Strelnikov, 2026). There should therefore be a dedicated effort to develop tests of predictive brain capacity. To be usable before implantation, such tests must not depend on hearing. They could measure how well a person anticipates the continuation of visual or written sequences, how much a predictable context speeds and supports recognition, or how strongly the brain responds when an expected event fails to occur. Audiovisual paradigms in which a speaker's facial expression predicts forthcoming vocal emphasis (Strelnikov et al., 2015) could then follow how prediction is rebuilt with the implant after activation. If predictions are embedded in resting activity, a resting-state sequence added to an existing pre-implant scan might also provide a task-free index. None of these is yet a validated clinical tool for implant candidates, and developing them is a research perspective.

7. Variability in animal models

Human cohorts cannot separate hidden brain pathology from everything that travels with hearing loss: age, vascular risk, duration of deprivation, education. A controlled animal case can. The marmoset has been established as a primate model for implant neurophysiology, with single-neuron recordings from the auditory cortex of awake, unilaterally implanted animals (Johnson et al., 2016). However, the same concern of brain variability exists in animal models. Sadoun et al. (2015) trained young laboratory-bred marmosets on cognitive tasks, and one animal judged healthy by standard veterinary examination and showing no external signs, learned far more slowly than her peers. Because structural MRI had been scheduled for the study, an explanation was found: a marked enlargement of the temporal horn of the right lateral ventricle, with mild atrophy of the adjacent hippocampus, unchanged on a scan a year later. Temporal-horn enlargement with hippocampal volume loss lies in the medial temporal territory where hearing-related atrophy has been found in humans (Armstrong et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2014), and ventriculomegaly is among the lesions reported in implanted children (Moon et al., 2012). Had this animal been enrolled in an implant study, it would have been recorded as a poor performer, and the explanation would have defaulted to the device, the surgery or the deprivation. Thus, an outlier with no other explanation should prompt imaging, or the study risks experimental bias. Other animal models are also concerned, clinically silent ventriculomegaly is common in some laboratory rat populations (Tu et al., 2014).

8. The problem in children and in single-sided deafness

In children, hidden brain variability is both more common and harder to see. Brain findings are relatively frequent on pre-implant scans, and additional disabilities are reported in an important part of deaf children (Section 4). Yet cognition is still developing, adult screening tools do not apply, and a slow trajectory can be attributed to age at implantation for years before anyone looks further. Electrophysiological work has shown a sensitive period for central auditory development, with cortical responses more likely to mature normally when implantation occurs early in childhood (Sharma et al., 2002). A sensitive period sharpens the cost of an unrecognized confound: a child whose poor progress reflects a structural problem may be pushed toward more intensive auditory therapy when a different plan is needed.

Even in carefully selected groups, individual variation is large. In the everyday-sound sorting study, both implanted and normal-hearing children showed considerable variation in how they grouped sounds (Berland et al., 2019). Without a structural or cognitive baseline, there is no way to tell how much of the spread within the implanted group reflects deafness, and how much reflects the ordinary heterogeneity of developing brains.

Single-sided deafness raises the stakes further. In these children, one ear is normal; what fails is central integration of the two ears for localization and listening in noise. In adults with acquired asymmetric hearing loss, PET imaging has linked cortical plasticity after implantation to spatial hearing (Karoui et al., 2023), and EEG work in adults with unilateral loss has related brain plasticity to spatial adaptation (Alzaher et al., 2023); whether the same holds in children is not yet known. Outcome in single-sided deafness is close to a pure test of the central auditory system.

fNIRS, being non-invasive and little affected by movement, has been proposed for studying children with single-sided deafness (Calmels et al., 2022). However, fNIRS samples the cortical surface; it cannot see a ventricle or a hippocampus. MRI is already used to look for inner-ear and cochlear-nerve anomalies in children with sensorineural loss, and such anomalies are more frequent in unilateral loss (McClay et al., 2008). Quantifying the brain on those existing scans, and adding a brief non-auditory cognitive measure, would turn studies like this one into tests of the hidden-variability hypothesis at almost no extra cost.

9. Practical limitations

Most incidental findings call for no immediate action: very few required intervention in a large population imaging study, although their natural course remains uncertain (Vernooij et al., 2007), and a metaanalysis concluded that incidental findings do not justify screening healthy people (Morris et al., 2009). But the claim here is not that each finding matters clinically. It is that unmeasured brain variability sits inside outcome models as noise, and that quantifying it is the only way to learn which findings matter for implant use and which do not.

Reorganization predicts that the informative signal lies in deprived auditory and visual regions and scales with duration of deafness. General integrity predicts that it lies in regions deafness did not alter, in hippocampal and frontal systems, and in measures that would predict learning in any domain. The result of Feng et al. (2018) fits the second pattern. Both mechanisms can operate at once, and a design that measures both can apportion the variance.

Age is a population-level proxy. Holden et al. (2013) found that cognition no longer predicted word recognition once age at implantation was taken into account; in a model where age stands in for brain state, that is expected. A direct structural and cognitive measure should outperform it at the individual level, and that is a testable claim.

Screening should not be used to refuse implants, and the evidence argues against using brain measures as gatekeepers. Most cognitively impaired older candidates improved after implantation (Mosnier et al., 2015), and children with brain lesions still progressed (Moon et al., 2012). The purpose of phenotyping is counselling, rehabilitation planning and honest expectations, not exclusion. A candidate with hippocampal atrophy deserves an implant and a rehabilitation programme designed for slower associative learning.

MRI is expensive but many candidates a brain MRI is already being acquired, and volumetric quantification of an existing scan is a software step. The marginal cost is small compared with the device and lifetime care.

10. A proposal: phenotype the listener

The remedy is not a new technology. It is a change in what implant programmes and implant studies routinely record.

1. Measure cognition with tools built for hearing loss. Adult candidates should complete a short cognitive assessment that does not depend on audition, such as the MoCA-H, a visual version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (Dawes et al., 2023; Völter et al., 2023). Scores belong in the outcome database, not only in the clinical notes. 2. Develop tests of predictive capacity. We propose that the brain restores the degraded implant signal in large part by prediction, yet no test targets this capacity. Since predictive models are thought to be maintained continuously in brain activity (Strelnikov, 2026), a coordinated effort should develop and validate non-auditory tests of predictive brain capacity, behavioral and electrophysiological, for adults and for children, and add them to candidacy assessment and outcome databases alongside cognitive scores.

3. Quantify the scan that already exists.

Where pre-implant MRI is acquired, add an automated volumetric read of the hippocampus, lateral ventricles, temporal lobes and white-matter lesion load, referenced to age norms. Where imaging is limited to the temporal bone, a brief whole-brain structural sequence is worth considering, particularly in candidates over 60.4. Code incidental findings as variables. A white-matter change or ventricular enlargement that does not contraindicate surgery should still be recorded in a structured way, so that its relation to outcome can be estimated across centres. 5. Give poor performers a structured workup. Patients below the expected trajectory at 6 to 12 months should be reassessed for device, fitting and surgical causes and, as has already been urged for poor performers, for cognitive ones (Moberly et al., 2016). 6. Hold controls to the same standard. Normal-hearing controls in vocoder and imaging studies should receive the same cognitive screen and, where MRI is acquired, the same radiological and volumetric read. Exclusions and their reasons should be reported. 7. Pool the data. Multicentre pooling has already shown what it can do for clinical predictors (Blamey et al., 2013). A registry adding cognitive and volumetric variables would show, within a few years, how much of the missing variance belongs to the brain. 8. Imaging in animal models. In marmoset and other primate implant studies, any behavioural outlier should undergo structural MRI, since an unexplained learning deficit can reflect an unsuspected brain abnormality (Sadoun et al., 2015); ideally, a baseline scan should be standard before animals are allocated to conditions.

The central prediction of this article is falsifiable. If hidden brain variability matters, adding cognitive and volumetric measures to the established clinical model should raise explained variance in a large cohort, and should do so most among the poorest performers. The same logic applies to predictive capacity: once validated, a dedicated test should explain outcome variance beyond what general cognitive scores capture.

11. Conclusion

Decades after cochlear implants became standard care, the field still cannot say in advance which patients will struggle. It has examined the cochlea, the electrode and the history of deafness in detail, and crossmodal reorganization with real ingenuity. It has examined least the general condition of the brain that must do the learning.

The human evidence now makes that omission hard to defend. Outcome measures depend on prediction, multisensory integration and categorization, all central operations. Prediction has a special place among them, because it is how the brain supplies what the implant fails to deliver, yet no dedicated test of it exists. "Normal" brains frequently carry structural findings, and implant candidates carry more of them.

Hearing loss itself tracks with temporal and hippocampal atrophy, and cognition predicts outcome when it is measured with appropriate tools.

The proposal of the article is to measure the brain the implant depends on, including its capacity to predict, in patients and in controls, before deciding what the remaining variance means.
