How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Bright light therapy is an effective and inexpensive non-pharmacological treatment for seasonal affective disorder (SAD) and, increasingly, for nonseasonal depression. Its mechanism is a problem in vision science: light acts through the eye, chiefly through melanopsin-expressing intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) working with rods and cones. This narrative review integrates evidence on ipRGC pathways to circadian, geniculate and mood-related brain regions; blind people with and without functioning ipRGCs; melatonin-suppression action spectra; spectral trials in SAD; ocular aging and safety; and clinical efficacy. Narrow-band short-wavelength (blue-appearing) light outperforms dim long-wavelength (red-appearing) light in SAD but has not outperformed broadband white light, even though melatonin suppression peaks at 446-477 nm. Calculations from standard tabulations show that white light rated at 10,000 photopic lux delivers about 4,100-10,000 lux of melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance, depending on spectrum; that lights identical in color and luminance can differ more than sixfold in melanopic stimulus; and that ocular aging roughly halves the retinal melanopic dose between ages 25 and 75, although older adults may partly compensate. Compensating with added short-wavelength light increases blue-lighthazard-weighted exposure in the population with the most retinal disease. A candidate antidepressant action spectrum, combining clock and habenular routes, peaks near 506 nm. The therapeutic dose of light is therefore a retinal quantity that depends on spectrum, geometry, pupil size and age. Recommendations for prescribing and reporting light therapy, and open questions, close the review.

1. Introduction

Few psychiatric treatments are delivered through a sense organ. Bright light therapy is one, and neither of the disciplines most concerned with it has fully absorbed the consequences. Psychiatrists and clinical psychologists know that light therapy works, especially for seasonal affective disorder (SAD), but often treat the light box as a black box: a device rated at "10,000 lux" used for 30 minutes each morning. Vision scientists know a great deal about how the retina transduces light, but their work rarely reaches clinical trials, and their measurement standards are seldom applied to therapeutic devices. Eye-care clinicians have a stake as well, because cataract, intraocular lenses, glaucoma and macular disease alter both the dose of light that reaches the retina and its risk, and patients with these conditions are seen by optometrists and ophthalmologists rather than by the clinicians who prescribe the light. The treatment's history illustrates the gap. Light therapy entered modern psychiatry when Lewy and colleagues 1 showed that bright light suppresses human melatonin and Rosenthal and colleagues 2 described SAD and its response to bright light. Lewy, Sack, Miller and Hoban 3 then proposed that winter depression reflects a delay of circadian phase that morning light corrects. For nearly two decades the mechanism was framed almost entirely in circadian terms, and the photoreceptor responsible was unknown. In 1999, Foster and colleagues showed that mice lacking rods and cones still shift their circadian activity rhythms and suppress pineal melatonin in response to light. 4, 5 Between 1998 and 2002, vision scientists identified melanopsin and the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) that contain it, and the question of how light treats depression became, in large part, a question about the retina.

The case for light therapy is strengthened by doubts about the standard pharmacological approach. Elsewhere I have argued that the average benefit of antidepressants over placebo is small, that their adverse effects are underappreciated, and that the diagnosis of major depression groups together a heterogeneous set of conditions with different causes, several of which a drug acting on monoamine reuptake would not be expected to correct (Peterzell DH, unpublished manuscript, 2026). Disrupted circadian timing, reduced daytime light exposure, seasonal change and disturbed sleep are among those causes, and light acts on them directly. Light therapy is also inexpensive, involving a one-time device cost, and it carries no systemic pharmacology, pharmacokinetic drug interactions or withdrawal syndrome. It can be used alone or combined with medication. It is not free of risk: hypomania, headache, eyestrain and the ocular risks discussed below are real. But those risks differ in kind from the adverse effects of antidepressants and can largely be prevented through screening and correct dosing.

Ocular phototherapy here means light delivered through the eye to treat psychiatric and circadian conditions, as distinct from dermatological and neonatal phototherapy, which act on the skin. This review is organized around a single idea: the effective dose of ocular phototherapy is the dose at the retina. That dose depends on the source's spectrum, intensity and geometry, the pupil's size, the transmission of the ocular media, and the integrity of the responding retinal cells. Each of these is familiar territory for vision scientists, and each varies across patients in ways that matter clinically. The review covers, in turn, the photoreceptors and pathways, evidence from blind people, the action-spectrum work that revealed a novel photoreceptor, the spectral sensitivity of SAD and of light therapy, the aging eye and ocular safety, the clinical evidence and proposed mechanisms, and closes with practical recommendations and open questions.

Several authoritative reviews have established melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance as the preferred measure of the non-visual effects of light, 6, 7 and the same photoreceptor-weighted approach has been used to set exposure thresholds for healthy adults 8 and to characterize filters that reduce circadian input. 9 A recent guide to light measurement in field studies likewise organizes the determinants of effective photic dose into pre-ocular, ocular and post-ocular factors, including pupil size and lens transmittance. 10 To my knowledge, none of this work has applied retinal dosimetry systematically to light therapy for mood disorders. This review does so for clinicians and vision scientists together: it quantifies how age-related lens transmission reduces the melanopic dose of therapeutic sources, sets therapeutic stimulus against blue-lighthazard weighting, proposes a candidate action spectrum for the antidepressant response, and shows that visually matched sources can differ several-fold in effective retinal dose. This is a narrative, not a systematic, review. It draws on the author's research and clinical expertise, targeted searches of PubMed and Google Scholar, and the reference lists of recent reviews and meta-analyses through October 2026. The review emphasizes primary studies of melanopsin and ipRGC function, action spectra, ocular aging and safety, and randomized trials and meta-analyses of bright light therapy.

2.1. Melanopsin and Intrinsically Photosensitive Retinal Ganglion Cells

Melanopsin was first identified in the dermal melanophores of Xenopus 11 and then in the inner retina of humans. 12 In 2002, two papers in the same issue of Science established a third class of retinal photoreceptor. Berson, Dunn and Takao 13 showed that retinal ganglion cells labeled retrogradely from the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) respond to light even when synaptic input from rods and cones is blocked. Hattar and colleagues 14 showed that these cells contain melanopsin and project to the SCN through the retinohypothalamic tract. In primates, Dacey and colleagues 15 found roughly 3,000 melanopsin-expressing cells per retina, about 0.2% of all ganglion cells, with a peak spectral sensitivity near 483 nm.

Melanopsin's peak lies between those of the short-wavelength (S) cone, near 420 nm, and the rods, near 500 nm; the middle-(M) and long-wavelength (L) cones peak near 530 and 560 nm. These are photopigment peaks; at the cornea, after filtering by the ocular media, CIE S 026 places the peaks at 448 nm (S cones), 490 nm (melanopsin), 506 nm (rods), 541 nm (M cones) and 569 nm (L cones) (Figure 1a).

Wavelength and color are often confused outside vision science. Wavelength is a physical property of light; color is a percept constructed mainly from the relative responses of the three cone classes. The two are related but not interchangeable.

Lights with very different spectra can look identical (metamers), the perceived hue of a narrow-band light shifts with its intensity and surroundings, and "white" light is a broadband mixture of wavelengths rather than a wavelength of its own. Two lamps that both look white can therefore differ several-fold in their effect on melanopsin (see Section 6.3), and a "blue-enriched" lamp still looks white, if a cooler white; its spectrum differs from that of a standard lamp far more than its appearance does. Melanopsin and the ipRGCs respond to the spectrum of the light that reaches them, weighted by their own sensitivity, not to the color a person sees. In this review, wavelengths are given wherever the source spectrum is known. Color names such as "blue light" are used only as shorthand for light whose energy is concentrated at wavelengths that usually look that color, roughly 450-490 nm for blue, 500-560 nm for green and 620-700 nm for red, or to report how a trial described its source.

ipRGCs are also diverse. In the mouse, at least six subtypes have been described. 18, 19 M1 cells, which express the most melanopsin, project mainly to the SCN, the olivary pretectal nucleus (OPN) that drives the pupillary light reflex, and the intergeniculate leaflet (IGL). Other subtypes, especially the M4 (ON-alpha) cells, project to the dorsal and ventral lateral geniculate nuclei (dLGN and vLGN) and the superior colliculus. Together these cells support circadian entrainment, melatonin suppression, the pupillary light reflex, alertness, sleep regulation, and, as discussed below, mood. 20

2.2. Beyond the Clock: Direct Pathways to Mood

Traditional accounts of light therapy emphasize the circadian system, but work in mice over the past decade shows that light can also affect mood through pathways that bypass the clock. LeGates and colleagues 21 housed mice on an aberrant cycle of 3.5 hours of light and 3.5 hours of darkness. The animals' SCN rhythms and total sleep were preserved, yet they developed depression-like behavior, elevated corticosterone, and impaired hippocampal learning, and antidepressants reversed the learning deficits.

Mice lacking ipRGCs were unaffected. Fernandez and colleagues 22 then separated two pathways: SCN-projecting ipRGCs mediated effects on learning, whereas effects on mood required an SCN-independent projection to a newly identified thalamic perihabenular nucleus connected with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens. Huang and colleagues 23 described a second route, from M4 ipRGCs to GABAergic neurons of the ventral lateral geniculate nucleus and IGL and from there to the lateral habenula. Blocking this circuit abolished the antidepressant effect of bright light in mouse models of depression.

These findings come from nocturnal rodents exposed to unusual light cycles or stress paradigms. Human homologues of the perihabenular nucleus and of the geniculate-habenular pathway have not been demonstrated. The findings nevertheless shift the question from whether light acts on mood only through the clock to which retinal pathways carry its effects, and that question can be tested in humans with methods from vision science. 2.3. The Geniculate Complex

Melanopsin contributes to image-forming vision as well. Dacey and colleagues 15 showed that primate ipRGCs project to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) and encode irradiance across the full range of human vision. In mice, Brown and colleagues 24 found that melanopsin drives irradiance coding in the thalamocortical visual system, dominating at high light levels, and Allen and colleagues 25 showed that melanopsin adjusts feature selectivity in the dorsal LGN according to ambient brightness. In humans, melanopsin contributes to brightness discrimination, 26 and melanopsin-directed stimulation drives visual cortex and produces a distinct percept when penumbral cone intrusion is carefully controlled. 27 Allen, Martial and Lucas 28 reported that melanopsin alone can support crude form vision; Nugent and Zele 29 found that melanopsin vision registers spatially structured images but cannot resolve their content. The debate turns on whether cone or rod signals were fully silenced, and it shows how much depends on stimulus control.

The ventral LGN and IGL occupy an intermediate position. They receive ipRGC input, relay photic and nonphotic information to the SCN through the geniculohypothalamic tract, and, through the pathway described by Huang and colleagues, 23 may carry a non-circadian antidepressant signal. The geniculate findings make two points for this review. First, the distinction between "visual" and "non-visual" photoreception is less sharp than it once seemed. Second, the silent-substitution methods developed to isolate melanopsin in these studies are precisely the tools needed to determine which photoreceptors mediate therapeutic effects in patients, a question that clinical trials have not yet addressed.

3. Natural Experiments in Blindness

Blindness separates conscious vision from non-image-forming photoreception, and it did so before ipRGCs were discovered. Czeisler and colleagues 30 exposed 11 blind patients without conscious light perception to bright light. In three, light suppressed melatonin about as much as in sighted controls, and covering the eyes abolished the effect; these three patients appeared to be normally entrained. Zaidi and colleagues 31 studied two people without a functioning outer retina. In one, short-wavelength (blue) light suppressed melatonin, shifted the circadian clock, and increased alertness more than 555-nm (green) light did, and one participant reported a conscious awareness of light near 480 nm. Vandewalle and colleagues 32 found that brief short-wavelength (blue) light altered activity in brain regions associated with alertness and executive function in totally blind people with intact non-image-forming responses.

Hull, Czeisler and Lockley 33 examined the clinical correlates of these responses.

Melatonin suppression was preserved in a minority of totally blind people who still had eyes and was absent after bilateral enucleation or phthisis. The type of blindness did not reliably predict which patients retained the response. Most totally blind people without photoreception develop non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder, which can be treated with the melatonin agonist tasimelteon. 34 Blindness has also been used to probe the health consequences of ocular light input: lower breast-cancer incidence in blind women was among the epidemiological observations that motivated the hypothesis that light at night, by suppressing melatonin, increases cancer risk. 35 Three clinical conclusions follow. Ocular light therapy cannot work after bilateral enucleation. It may work in profound outer-retinal blindness, but candidacy should be assessed by melatonin suppression or chromatic pupillometry, which uses the sustained pupil constriction after light offset as an index of ipRGC function. 36 And diseases of the inner retina and optic nerve affect ipRGCs in ways not apparent from visual acuity. In the hereditary mitochondrial optic neuropathies, Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and dominant optic atrophy, ipRGCs survive the massive loss of other ganglion cells and light still suppresses melatonin. 37 In glaucoma, by contrast, the postillumination pupil response to short-wavelength light is reduced, 38 and reduced ipRGC activity is associated with poorer sleep quality, 39 so glaucoma may reduce both the efficacy of light therapy and the integrity of circadian photoreception.

4. Action Spectra: From a Novel Photoreceptor to Melanopic Metrics

During the 1980s and 1990s, G. C. Brainard and colleagues showed that ocular light suppresses melatonin in an intensity-and wavelength-dependent manner. Their 2001 study measured nighttime melatonin suppression in 627 tests at wavelengths from 420 to 600 nm. 40 An opsin template fit the data well (R² = 0.91), and potency peaked between 446 and 477 nm. This peak (Figure 1a, shaded band) could not be explained by the rods or by the three-cone photopic system, and the authors concluded that a novel photopigment regulates human circadian and neuroendocrine function. Thapan, Arendt and Skene 41 independently reached the same conclusion, and a comparison of five action spectra for circadian and other non-visual responses in humans and other mammals placed peak sensitivity between 446 and 484 nm. 35 Lockley, Brainard and Czeisler 42 then showed that the short-wavelength advantage extends to the response invoked by the phase-shift hypothesis: 6.5 hours of 460-nm light produced twice the circadian phase delay, and twice the melatonin suppression, of 555-nm light of equal photon density. Gena Glickman, a co-author of the 2001 study, then showed that light falling on the inferior retina suppresses melatonin more effectively than light on the superior retina. 43 Because light arriving from above the line of sight is imaged on the inferior retina, this result supports the conventional placement of light boxes above eye level on grounds of efficacy, not only comfort.

Later work showed that the action spectrum is not fixed. Gooley and colleagues 44 found that 555-nm (green) light was as effective as 460-nm (blue) light at the start of an exposure and at low irradiance, indicating cone input, whereas melanopsin dominated as exposure lengthened. St Hilaire and colleagues 45 reached a similar conclusion: early in a 6.5-hour exposure, melatonin suppression was driven largely by cones, and by the end, peak sensitivity had shifted to about 485 nm. The contribution of S cones remains unsettled. Spitschan, Lazar, Yetik and Cajochen 46 found no acute neuroendocrine or alerting response to S-cone-isolating stimulation, and Blume and colleagues 47 found no conclusive effect of calibrated changes in S-cone stimulation, which observers see as shifts along a blue-yellow color axis, on the circadian clock or sleep.

These findings led to the international standard CIE S 026, 48 which defines α-opic irradiances for the five human photoreceptor classes and the melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (melanopic EDI). A consensus panel has recommended a melanopic EDI of at least 250 lux at the eye during the day, no more than 10 lux in the three hours before bed, and no more than 1 lux during sleep. 8 For light therapy, the consequence is that photopic lux, the unit in which light boxes are rated, is weighted by the luminosity function V(λ), which peaks near 555 nm (Figure 1a, dotted curve), and says little about melanopic stimulation; two devices rated at the same photopic illuminance can differ several-fold in melanopic dose. Visual brightness is a poor guide even at the lowest intensities: of 25 children's nightlights measured at source level, only 3 stayed below 5 lux melanopic EDI on every setting. 49 A reanalysis of the actionspectrum data concluded that melatonin suppression by monochromatic light is driven predominantly by melanopsin and can begin at about 1.5 melanopic lux. 50 Melanopic EDI is not the only proposed metric. The circadian stimulus model of Rea, Figueiro and colleagues treats circadian phototransduction as subadditive, with a blue-versus-yellow spectral-opponent term and a threshold, rather than as a weighted sum of photoreceptor inputs. [51] [52] [53] The two approaches make different predictions for polychromatic light, and neither has been validated against antidepressant response. CIE S 026 itself states two limits: it describes the stimulus to each photoreceptor class but does not provide complete information for the quantitative prediction of ipRGCinfluenced responses, and it does not address health or safety issues, including those arising from light treatment. 48 Melanopic EDI is therefore the best available measure of the melanopic stimulus, not an established measure of antidepressant dose.

5.1. Early Wavelength Trials

The first wavelength trials predated the discovery of melanopsin. G. C. Brainard and colleagues 54 compared white (broadband), blue (short-wavelength) and red (long-wavelength) light in SAD and found white the most effective, although the blue source was dim by later standards. Oren and colleagues 55 found green (middle-wavelength) light more effective than red (long-wavelength) light, and Stewart and colleagues 56 found green and white light comparably effective. Lee, Chan, Paterson, Janzen and Blashko 57 pooled the available studies in a meta-analysis and concluded that light of short to medium wavelengths (blue, green and yellow, roughly 450-590 nm) was essential to the therapeutic effect, that red (long-wavelength, ≈620-700 nm) light was relatively ineffective, and that ultraviolet radiation was unnecessary and should be blocked. They proposed that SAD might arise from inefficient S-and M-cone function. In a companion meta-analysis of 39 studies, Lee and Chan 67 found a dose-response relationship with light intensity for typical, but not atypical, depressive symptoms. These cone-based accounts, together with Remé, Terman and Wirz-Justice's 68 proposal that deficient photoreceptor renewal contributes to winter depression, were the first explicitly retinal hypotheses of SAD.

5.3. Why the Action Spectrum Did Not Translate

Several factors may explain why short-wavelength light suppresses melatonin more effectively without showing superior antidepressant efficacy. Action spectra were measured with brief, moderate nighttime exposures, whereas therapy uses 30 to 60 minutes of bright daytime light, at which responses may approach saturation and spectral differences shrink. Nocturnal phase resetting and melatonin suppression follow logistic dose-response curves: about half the phase delay produced by 9,000 lux is obtained with about 100 lux, 69 and a model fitted to the action-spectrum data predicts that melatonin suppression saturates near 305 melanopic lux. 50 Whether daytime antidepressant responses saturate at similar levels is unknown, but a nominal 10,000lux box delivers thousands of melanopic lux (Figure 3a). Cone contributions vary with irradiance and duration, 44, 45 so narrow-band sources tuned to melanopsin may forgo cone-driven input, and color opponency 16, 17 means that removing long wavelengths is not neutral. The aging lens absorbs short wavelengths most strongly, 70 so sources weighted toward short wavelengths lose more of their advantage in older patients than broadband sources do: by calculation, a 75-year-old receives about 45% of the retinal melanopic dose that a 25-year-old receives from a 468-nm source, compared with about 50% from broadband white light and 60% from 520-nm green light (Figure 3b).

The clinical endpoint also differs from the physiological one: melatonin suppression is acute, whereas antidepressant response accrues over weeks and may depend in part on non-circadian pathways whose spectral tuning is unknown. Finally, patients may expect bright white light to work and dim or colored light not to, which affects comparisons between spectra as well as comparisons with sham.

5.4. Retinal Differences in Seasonal and Nonseasonal Depression

A separate question is whether people with SAD differ in retinal sensitivity.

Electroretinographic studies have reported reduced retinal sensitivity in winter depression and in its subsyndromal form, 71, 72 changes in retinal function after light therapy, 73 and an atypical modulation of rod responses by recent light history. 74 With chromatic pupillometry, Roecklein and colleagues found a reduced post-illumination pupil response, a marker of melanopsin function, in SAD during winter. 75 A missense variant of the melanopsin gene, P10L, was more common in SAD in one sample, 76 although the association has not been firmly established. Roecklein, Wong, Miller, Donofry, Kamarck and G. C. Brainard 77 proposed that individual differences in ipRGC sensitivity, measured with chromatic pupillometry, may explain why only some people develop seasonal depression.

The retina may also be altered in nonseasonal depression. Bubl and colleagues, working with Mark Greenlee and with Michael Bach, found elevated contrastdiscrimination thresholds in patients with major depression 78 and markedly lower retinal contrast gain in the pattern electroretinogram (PERG) of 40 medicated and unmedicated patients, correlated with the severity of depression. 79 Contrast gain returned to normal in patients who remitted but not in those who did not, 80 and a smaller reduction appeared in cortical visual evoked potentials. 81 Replication has been partial. In an independent sample of 20 patients and 20 controls, Fam and colleagues 82 found lower psychophysical contrast sensitivity in depression, correlated with symptom severity (r = 0.49), but no group difference in the PERG or full-field electroretinogram.

Later work from the original laboratory in Freiburg found reduced PERG amplitudes in a small sample 83 and, in a larger one, reduced contrast sensitivity and PERG ratio but no difference in raw PERG amplitude. 84 Bubl and colleagues 78 proposed a dopaminergic explanation. Because ipRGCs provide excitatory input to retinal dopaminergic amacrine cells, 85 these findings suggest an untested link between the retinal physiology of depression and the route through which light therapy acts.

Photic sensitivity also changes with season and light history. In hamsters, the circadian response to light varies with photoperiod, 86 but not uniformly: in Siberian hamsters, short photoperiods increased sensitivity for phase resetting while leaving sensitivity for melatonin suppression unchanged. 87 If the same dissociation holds in humans, melatonin suppression, the most common human index of photic sensitivity, may underestimate seasonal changes in the pathways relevant to SAD. In humans, lower ambient light exposure in winter is associated with greater melatonin sensitivity to light, 88 and the spectral composition of personal light exposure varies with the seasons. 89 Sensitivity also differs greatly between individuals: in 55 healthy young adults, the evening illuminance that halved melatonin levels ranged from 6 to 350 lux, a more than 50-fold range 90 (for a broader framework, see 91 ). Any retinal account of SAD must therefore distinguish trait differences from state-dependent adaptation. Three retinal hypotheses remain in contention: cone inefficiency, 57 deficient photoreceptor renewal, 68 and reduced ipRGC sensitivity. 77 None is established, and the obvious next study, stratifying patients by pupillometric or electroretinographic sensitivity and testing whether these measures predict response or the optimal spectrum, has not been done.

6.1. Optical and Neural Aging

Werner, Peterzell and Scheetz 70 attributed many age-related changes in vision to reduced retinal illuminance, from increasing lens density and yellowing and from smaller pupils, together with losses of neural efficiency. On this account, the senescent eye behaves in many respects like a younger eye operating at a lower light level. They also argued that cumulative exposure to short-wavelength radiation may accelerate retinal aging, so that lifetime light history is itself a risk factor and the light environment should be managed across the life span. Owsley 95 provides an updated review of vision and aging.

Both arguments bear directly on light therapy. Because the aging lens preferentially absorbs short wavelengths (Figure 1b), older patients receive less retinal melanopic stimulation than younger patients at the same corneal illuminance. Under the standard ocular-media model, transmittance at 480 nm falls from about 0.70 at age 25 to about 0.34 at age 75, roughly halving the retinal melanopic dose from a white light box for a fixed pupil (Figure 3b); measurements of donor lenses show a comparably steep decline, with transmission at 480 nm falling by 72% between ages 10 and 80. 96 Turner and Mainster 97 developed the implications for circadian photoreception. Neural losses compound the optical ones: melanopsin-expressing ganglion cells and their dendrites degenerate in the aging human retina. 98 The physiological consequences are less clear. Najjar and colleagues 94 found no reduction in melatonin suppression in older adults whose lenses transmitted about 42% less light at 480 nm, which suggests some compensation, whereas other studies found age-related losses: postmenopausal women showed less melatonin suppression than young women after 456-nm light, 99 and older men showed diminished alerting and mood responses to 456-nm but not 548-nm light, although their phase advances were not significantly impaired. 100 Bright light is nonetheless effective in older adults with depression 101 (Section 7.2), and whether older patients need longer sessions or higher doses is unknown. The optical calculation therefore marks the size of the potential loss, not the size of the required correction. Any pre-retinal filter changes the retinal dose in the same way: Glickman and colleagues 9 formalized this for eyewear with the melanopic daylight filtering density, which varied widely across 26 commercial "blue-blocking" glasses, and the aging crystalline lens is, in effect, such a filter whose density increases with age.

Compensating with added short-wavelength light has a potential cost. At sufficiently high exposures, retinal photochemical damage is greatest at short wavelengths, roughly 400-500 nm, which most people see as violet and blue. It is assessed with the blue-light hazard function B(λ), which peaks near 435-440 nm (Figure 1b); the name refers to wavelength, not perceived color. Circadian potency, by contrast, falls off below the melanopic peak: at equal photon density, 460-nm light suppressed melatonin significantly more than 420-nm light. 102 Energy below about 450 nm therefore adds B(λ)-weighted exposure faster than it adds melanopic stimulus, which is the quantitative basis for tuning therapeutic sources toward longer wavelengths. The first narrow-band trial in SAD took this approach: its 468-nm source peaked well above the B(λ) maximum, and an independent hazard analysis found its emissions far below exposure limits. 58 Greater B(λ)-weighted exposure does not itself establish risk, which depends on the radiance, duration and angular size of the source relative to exposure limits. The CIE has concluded that the blue-light hazard is not an issue for white light sources used in general lighting, including blue-enriched ones, and that claims linking ordinary blue-light exposure to age-related macular degeneration are speculative, while noting greater caution for children and for sources that emit mainly blue light. 103 The concern for light therapy is therefore narrower: deliberately shortwavelength-weighted, high-irradiance sources used daily for years by older patients, who have the highest prevalence of retinal disease, have not been studied.

Patients with intraocular lenses present the opposite problem. After cataract surgery, especially with lenses that block ultraviolet (below 400 nm) but not shortwavelength visible (≈400-500 nm) light, short-wavelength retinal irradiance can increase substantially relative to that of an age-matched phakic eye. Blue-blocking intraocular lenses trade photoprotection against photoreception. 104 In a randomized trial of blue-blocking and neutral intraocular lenses in 76 patients, cataract surgery increased the post-illumination pupil response to blue light by 17% at 2 days and 24% at 3 weeks, advanced actigraphic rhythms by 22 minutes and raised peak salivary melatonin, and the authors concluded that surgery does not adversely affect circadian rhythm or sleep. 105 How intraocular lenses alter the efficacy and safety of light therapy has not been studied directly.

6.2. Ocular Safety

Ocular safety at standard doses is reassuring. Gallin and colleagues 106 found no treatment-related damage in 50 patients with SAD examined before treatment with 10,000 lux for 30 minutes daily, after 2-8 weeks, and after 3-6 years of seasonal use.

They noted, however, that knowledge was insufficient to specify definite ocular contraindications, and they recommended that patients with pre-existing ocular abnormalities, or those taking photosensitizing medications, be treated only with periodic ophthalmologic examination. Remé and colleagues 107 measured the emission spectra of commercial light-therapy lamps, analyzed their ultraviolet and blue-light hazards, and proposed safety measures. Photic maculopathy has been reported in a patient treated with clomipramine and bright light. 108 A systematic review that screened 6,708 articles and included 43 reports found no evidence of ocular damage from light therapy apart from this case, while noting that evidence is lacking for people with preexisting eye disease or increased photosensitivity. 109 The evidence therefore supports targeted caution for these groups rather than general contraindications.

Medications that may increase photosensitivity include lithium, tricyclic antidepressants, phenothiazines, St John's wort, and some antimalarials and psoralens.

For most of these the evidence is theoretical or based on case reports, but the combination of photosensitizers and intense light is common in psychiatric practice. 110 Retinal disease, including macular degeneration, retinitis pigmentosa and diabetic retinopathy, and recent ocular surgery warrant ophthalmologic assessment before treatment. Glaucoma is chiefly an efficacy concern, because it damages ganglion cells, including ipRGCs.

6.3. Specifying the Dose

The standard prescription, 10,000 lux for 30 minutes, is a photopic quantity measured at an assumed distance. It does not specify the spectrum, the melanopic dose, the retinal area stimulated, or the patient's age. CIE S 026 48 supplies the needed metrics, and Spitschan, Stefani and colleagues 111 set out how light exposure should be measured and reported in human research. The open-access platform luox computes αopic quantities from a measured spectrum. 112 These tools make it possible to prescribe and report light therapy in units that correspond to its mechanism, and Figure 3 applies them to standard source spectra. At a nominal 10,000 photopic lux, common white sources deliver between about 4,100 lux melanopic EDI (warm-white LED, 2700 K) and 10,000 lux (daylight-like 6500 K spectra), and a cool-white fluorescent lamp of the kind used in many trials delivers about 5,700 lux (Figure 3a). Narrow-band 468-nm (blueappearing) light raises melanopic stimulus and B(λ)-weighted irradiance in about the same proportion, whereas middle-wavelength light near 520 nm (green-appearing) gives about ten times more melanopic stimulus per unit of B(λ)-weighted irradiance than daylight (Figure 3c).

This ratio compares weighting functions, not clinical benefit and risk: melanopic stimulus does not establish antidepressant benefit, and weighted exposure below exposure limits does not establish injury. These are idealized calculations rather than measurements of commercial devices, but they show why the spectrum, and not only the photopic illuminance, should be specified.

Figure 5 shows the extreme case. Three lights calculated to have the same chromaticity and luminance for the CIE standard observer, and so to look the same, deliver 1,000, 406 and 2,513 lux melanopic EDI at 1,000 photopic lux, a more than sixfold range. The high-melanopic mixture also carries about 18% less blue-lighthazard-weighted irradiance than daylight of the same luminance, which shows that melanopic stimulation and B(λ)-weighted exposure can be partly separated by spectral design (component wavelengths and weights are given in Appendix A). Field evidence supports the logic of spectral engineering: in a crossover study of 47 night-shift military personnel, light boxes enriched or depleted in short-wavelength light had different effects, with mood and sleep improving under the short-wavelength-enriched source. 113 That study combined the light boxes with blue-blocking glasses and sleep masks and was conducted in healthy workers, so it is not a test of antidepressant efficacy.

7. Clinical Efficacy

>Clinical Efficacy

7.1. Seasonal Affective Disorder

Randomized trials support bright light for winter SAD, although clinical guidelines differ in their certainty. Golden and colleagues 114 found significant effect sizes for bright light and for dawn simulation in a meta-analysis restricted to rigorous trials. Placebocontrolled trials using deactivated negative-ion generators as sham conditions, which patients find credible, confirmed that bright light is superior to placebo. 115, 116 A more recent meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials found a standardized mean difference of 0.37 in depression ratings and a risk ratio of 1.42 for response, both favoring bright light, with moderate heterogeneity and moderate-to-high risk of bias. 117 The UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence advises that people with winter depression who wish to try light therapy instead of medication or psychological treatment be told that the evidence for its efficacy is uncertain. 118 Canadian guidelines, by contrast, recommend light therapy as first-line monotherapy for seasonal (winter) depression, with Level 1 evidence, and as a second-line or adjunctive treatment for nonseasonal depression. 119

7.2. Nonseasonal Depression

Evidence for nonseasonal major depression has strengthened. Lam and colleagues 120 randomized 122 patients to light, fluoxetine, both, or sham treatment (an inactive negative-ion generator plus a placebo pill). After eight weeks, response rates were 75.9% with the combination, 50.0% with light alone, 29.0% with fluoxetine alone, and 33.3% with sham. Relative to sham, effect sizes were 1.11 for the combination and 0.80 for light alone; fluoxetine alone did not differ significantly from sham, which limits the trial's assay sensitivity. A meta-analysis of 11 trials with 858 patients found that adjunctive bright light improved response (60.4% vs. 38.6%) and remission (40.7% vs. 23.5%), with benefit apparent early in treatment. 121 Earlier meta-analyses also found benefit, 114, 122 although Golden and colleagues found an effect of light as monotherapy but not as an adjunct to medication. A meta-analysis of randomized trials comparing light therapy directly with antidepressant drugs (seven independent samples, 397 participants) found no significant difference between them (standardized mean difference 0.19, 95% confidence interval [CI] -0.08 to 0.45), although the trials were too few to establish equivalence. Combined treatment outperformed antidepressants alone (0.56, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.88), including in nonseasonal depression (0.55). 123 In older adults, Lieverse and colleagues 101 randomized 89 outpatients aged 60 or older with nonseasonal major depression to early-morning bright light (pale blue, about 7,500 lux) or dim red light (50 lux) for three weeks. Depression improved more with bright light, the advantage grew to 21% three weeks after treatment ended, evening melatonin rose and cortisol excretion fell. A 2026 meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials published between 2020 and 2025 found a standardized mean difference of 0.35 for nonseasonal major depression and 0.9 for subthreshold depression. 124 It also found that higher illuminance was associated with less improvement. This association across heterogeneous trials does not establish an inverse dose-response relationship within patients, but it shows how poorly photopic illuminance captures dose.

7.3. Bipolar Depression

Concern about inducing mania long limited the use of light in bipolar depression.

Sit and colleagues 125 randomized patients taking antimanic medication to midday bright broadband white light at 7,000 lux or dim red (long-wavelength) light at 50 lux, starting at 15 minutes and increasing gradually. Bright light produced higher remission rates and lower depression scores, and no patient switched polarity. Benedetti and colleagues 126 argued that morning light is also safe and effective in bipolar depression. Pooled evidence is mixed: a meta-analysis of six randomized trials found no significant overall advantage for remission or depressive symptoms, although trials with low indirectness favored bright light. 127 Adjunctive light in bipolar depression is therefore best treated as a specialist-supervised option, given with mood-stabilizing treatment, cautious titration and monitoring for mood switching; the optimal timing remains unsettled.

7.4. Other Populations

In antepartum depression, where avoiding fetal drug exposure is attractive, Wirz-Justice and colleagues 128 found a response rate of 81.3% with 7,000-lux white light compared with 45.5% with dim red (long-wavelength) light, following an earlier pilot by Epperson and colleagues; 129 both trials were small. Evidence in adolescents and in people with dementia is promising but heterogeneous. In a 14-week randomized crossover trial in 46 people with Alzheimer disease and related dementias, all-day tailored lighting reduced depression scores on the Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia (from 10.3 at baseline to 7.1) and improved sleep quality and agitation more than control lighting did. 130 Timed light is also used for circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline endorses it, at its second tier of confidence, for advanced sleep-wake phase disorder in adults, delayed sleepwake phase disorder in children and adolescents, and irregular sleep-wake rhythm disorder in older people with dementia. 131

7.5. Dosing, Timing, and Adverse Effects

The conventional regimen is 10,000 lux at the eye for 30 minutes soon after waking, with roughly equivalent alternatives of 2,500 lux for two hours or 5,000 lux for about one hour. 110 Timing can be individualized. In one trial, morning light was most effective about 8.5 hours after dim-light melatonin onset, or roughly 2.5-3.5 hours after the midpoint of sleep. 132 Because melatonin onset is rarely measured clinically, sleep timing or the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire serves as a proxy, with later chronotypes starting later. 110 Wirz-Justice and colleagues, 133 however, found SAD responses independent of time of day (Section 8). Adverse effects are usually mild and transient and include headache, eyestrain, nausea and agitation. 134 The serious risk is hypomania or mania, and evening light can cause insomnia. 134 Even very dim light at night affects circadian responses, 135 so the evening and night light environment deserves specification as well as the morning dose. Light can also be combined with wake therapy and sleep-phase advance to produce rapid antidepressant responses. 136

8. Mechanisms Linking Light and Mood

Several mechanisms probably contribute. Trials favoring morning over evening light 115, 116 support the phase-shift hypothesis, 3 whereas time-of-day-independent effects 133 and rapid responses in some patients challenge it. Brain serotonin turnover, estimated from internal jugular venous sampling, is lowest in winter and rises with the hours of bright sunlight on the day of measurement. 139 Dopaminergic mechanisms may involve ipRGC input to retinal dopaminergic amacrine cells 85 and habenular control of midbrain monoamine nuclei, although direct human evidence is limited (for retinal findings in depression, see Section 5.4). The direct thalamic and habenular pathways described in mice 22, 23 offer a non-circadian route. Light also affects sleep and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, 21 and improved sleep may be both a mediator and an outcome of treatment. Human neuroimaging shows that light, and short-wavelength light in particular, modulates cortical and subcortical responses during cognitive and emotional tasks, including in blind people with intact ipRGC function. 32 Blume, Garbazza and Spitschan 140 review these links between light, circadian rhythms, sleep and mood. narrow-band short-wavelength light (≈450-490 nm) has been shown to be more effective than standard white light for depression, and both increase B(λ)-weighted exposure per photopic lux, a consideration for patients with retinal disease. 5. Screen for bipolar disorder (risk of hypomania), retinal disease, recent ocular surgery, intraocular lenses and photosensitizing medications. Ocular damage at standard doses has not been found, so ophthalmologic baseline and follow-up are recommended for these at-risk groups rather than for all patients. 6. Older eyes transmit less short-wavelength light, but whether longer sessions are needed is unknown. If response is poor, extending sessions may be safer than adding short-wavelength light, although neither approach has been tested in older patients. 7. Time light by chronotype or melatonin onset. In bipolar depression, where pooled evidence is mixed, use light only as a supervised adjunct with mood-stabilizer coverage, beginning with about 15 minutes of midday light. 8. In blind patients, test non-image-forming photoreception before prescribing light. 9. Eye-care clinicians should ask older patients, and those with cataract, intraocular lenses, glaucoma or macular disease, whether they use light therapy, because these conditions change both the dose that reaches the retina and its risk.

10. Open Questions and Conclusions

Several questions are now within reach. The action spectrum for the antidepressant response, as distinct from acute melatonin suppression, is unknown.

The contribution of S cones to non-visual responses in humans is unresolved. Whether retinal or ipRGC sensitivity predicts who develops SAD or who responds to treatment is unknown, as is whether dose should be adjusted for lens density, pupil size or intraocular lenses, given evidence of physiological compensation in older adults. 94 The rodent thalamic and habenular pathways have not been shown to exist or to matter in humans. The optimal timing of light in bipolar depression is still debated. Finally, sex and gender were seldom analyzed in the studies reviewed here, so whether light sensitivity or treatment response differs by sex remains largely unknown; reporting in line with the SAGER guidelines 143 would help close this gap.

Light therapy also bears on the broader question of how depression should be treated. If depression is a syndrome with several causes, treatment should be matched to cause rather than to the diagnostic label (Peterzell DH, unpublished manuscript, 2026). Ocular phototherapy is a treatment whose route of action, through a known retinal pathway, is unusually well characterized. Its efficacy in nonseasonal depression, alone and combined with medication, 120, 121 shows that its usefulness extends well beyond the seasonal subtype, although treatment response does not by itself show that 0.0672 and 0.0102; the high-melanopic mixture has peaks at 484, 500 and 650 nm with weights 0.0623, 0.1027 and 0.1962 (each weight is the peak spectral irradiance of its component, in W•m ²•nm ¹).
