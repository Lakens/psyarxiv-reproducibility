To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 3 imprecise p values out of 10 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p < 0.05 The majority of participants (7 of 9) showed significantly different curves between luminance and chromatic conditions (p < 0.05).
p < 0.05 Results were mixed: 4 of 7 participants showed significantly different curves (p < 0.05), while the remaining 3 did not (p-values ranging from 0.419 to 0.815).
p < 0.1 At a per-cell significance threshold of p < 0.05, two filter locations showed a statistically significant difference between the luminance and chromatic banks, and three more were significant at a level of p < 0.1.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = 0.870 One participant was an outlier with p = 0.870, inflating the mean p-value to 0.096 across all participants (median p-value of 0.00).
p = 0.197 The addition of a moderate-sized flanker (305 pixels) flipped the perception from out-of-phase to in-phase in the luminance condition (p = 0.020), whereas the chromatic condition was no different from random (p = 0.197).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. Abstract

We test variants of the remote-controls illusion in configurations that modulate in luminance and in chroma. We use three different variants of the RCI to test different spatial properties of the illusion, in order to probe the differences in the perception of this illusion in settings with and without luminance contrast.

We find that the key gap-dependent phase properties of the remote-controls illusion identified by Hedjar et al. (2018) extend to versions that modulate in chroma, but with differences in arrangement consistent with larger areas of integration of chromatic contrast signals.

We extend the model developed by Hedjar et al. to incorporate a time delay between the center and surround, and to extract contrast from the edge of the modulating bar in the stimulus. We show that these modifications improve the predictive power of the model, which is able to explain many of the properties of the remote-controls illusion. We use this model to predict the scale and bandwidth of retinal ganglion cells responsible for integrating luminance contrast and chromatic contrast, and find that the predicted filters for chroma are larger in scale and lower in bandwidth than the equivalent ones for luminance. Page vii Chapter 1 Introduction Action at a Distance

A fundamental phenomenon in science is that of action at a distance-the idea that an object can be influenced by another object without direct physical contact. For instance, Newton's theory of gravitation described how bodies exert forces on one another across empty space, a concept that was so counterintuitive that Newton himself expressed discomfort with it. In vision science, action at a distance occurs when a patch of light changes its appearance due to alterations in the visual field not directly touching that patch.

Vision science has a long history of studying phenomena where a stimulus at one location alters the appearance of an object at another. For instance: Hartline won a Nobel Prize for his work with Ratliff that showed a signal in one Limulus ommatidium was influenced by signals originating in distant ommatidia (Hartline, 1967; Ratliff, 1965). Many well-known visual illusions demonstrate action at a distance. In simultaneous brightness contrast, the perceived lightness of a gray patch depends on the luminance of its surround, even when the surround and the patch do not share a border such as in the dungeon illusion (Bressan and Kramer, 2017) and the snake illusion (Adelson, 2000).

White's illusion (White, 1979) and the Wertheimer-Benary cross (Benary, 1924) show that brightness shifts can depend on the global configuration of the scene rather 1 than solely on local luminance relationships. There are other phenomena that show action at a distance which produce changes in shape and spatial position: effects unrelated to color appearance (Weiss and Adelson, 2000; Kersten et al., 1996 Kersten et al., , 1997) ) Explanations for these action-at-a-distance phenomena in vision generally fall into two broad categories. The first category appeals to higher-level or contextual processes:

Gestalt grouping rules (Wertheimer, 1923; Koffka, 1935), unconscious inferences about the structure of the scene (von Helmholtz, 1962; Gregory, 1970; Rock, 1983), Bayesian interpretations in which the visual system uses prior knowledge to estimate surface properties (Kersten et al., 2004; Knill and Richards, 1996), or object segmentation and scission processes (Anderson, 1997; Anderson and Winawer, 2005). The second category appeals to lower-level mechanisms that operate on the image itself: lateral inhibition (Hartline, 1967; Ratliff, 1965), spatial filtering by difference-of-Gaussian (DoG) or oriented DoG (ODoG) mechanisms (Blakeslee and McCourt, 1999, 2004; Blakeslee et al., 2005; Robinson et al., 2007), or the weighting of responses across multiple spatial frequency channels (Wilson and Bergen, 1979; Campbell and Robson, 1968).

Many phenomena can be partially explained by mechanisms in either category, and a complete account may well require some integration of the two approaches (Shapiro and Hedjar, 2019). For instance, some have argued that Adelson's checker shadow illusion (Adelson, 1995 (Adelson, , 2000) ) demonstrates that the perceived lightness of a surface can be influenced by cast shadows and three-dimensional scene interpretation, whereas others have argued that such phenomena are consistent with local adaptation or the removal of low spatial frequency information. It is therefore possible that the information required by inferential processes should result from some form of filtered version of the image, not just visual description at the pixel level.

Contrast Asynchrony and the Remote-Controls Illusion

Contrast asynchrony phenomena demonstrate that the visual system has separable encodings for luminance and luminance contrast. In the standard paradigm, two identical disks modulate from dark to bright and back to dark while surrounded by different annuli-one bright, one dark. The luminance information from the disks is in phase (both disks reach the peaks and troughs of their luminance cycle at the same time), but the contrast information between the disks and their annuli is out of phase. At low temporal frequencies (< 1 Hz), observers can attend to both signals simultaneously, reporting the paradoxical perception that the disks modulate in antiphase while also appearing to become light and dark at the same time. At intermediate temporal frequencies (2 -6 Hz), the out-of-phase contrast signal predominates (Shapiro et al., 2004 (Shapiro et al., , 2005;; Shapiro, 2008).

The remote-controls illusion (shown in a typical configuration in Figure 1) extends the contrast asynchrony paradigm to investigate action at a distance (Hedjar et al., 2018; Shapiro, 2016). The change in appearance can be understood by assuming that the visual system always has multiple contrast signals, and that these signals sometimes give conflicting information. This explanation is illustrated in Figure 2. As described in Hedjar et al.

(2018), we label the phases of contrast modulation either phase "A" or phase "B".

Phase "A" corresponds to the physical contrast created by modulating on a dark background: it is high-contrast when the modulating bar is bright, and low-contrast when the modulating bar is dark. Phase "B" is the opposite, corresponding to modulating on a light background (that is, creating low contrast when the modulating bar is bright, and high contrast when the modulating bar is dark).

When no flankers are present (as in Figure 2a), the bar on the dark background has high contrast (phase A), and the bar on the light background has low contrast (phase B).

Because the bars have opposite phases, they appear to modulate out-of-phase.

If the flankers are far from the modulating bars (Figure 2b), the contrast information remains the same as in Figure 2a, and the bars appear to modulate out-of-phase.

If the flankers adjoin the modulating bars (Figure 2c), the contrast phases switch: the bar on the dark background is primarily next to a light flanker (was in phase A with no flanker, is now in phase B with the flanker).

Likewise, the bar on the light background has switched phase (was in phase B with no flanker, is now in phase A with the flanker).

Because both bars have opposite phases, they will still appear to modulate out-of-phase.

In Figure 2d, the gap between the modulating bars and the flankers is small but not zero. In this configuration, there are two different sources of contrast information: one from contrast relative to the background, and one from contrast relative to the flankers.

Because the background and flankers are of opposite polarity, there is information indicating that the modulating bar is simultaneously in phase A and phase B.

With a small gap therefore, the two types of contrast are both present: one at the edge and one that acts over a larger distance. If these two forms of contrast compete or cancel each other out, the bars should appear to modulate in phase, but if one form of contrast is always dominant, the bars should appear to modulate out-of-phase. Surprisingly, the former interpretation is borne out by the appearance of the illusions and empirically in Hedjar et al. (2018): when there is a small gap, participants perceive the bars as modulating in-phase.

The question I address in this thesis is whether a gaussian model can account for the remote-controls illusion under equiluminant chromatic conditions, like it was shown to do in luminance conditions by Hedjar et al. A comparison between luminance and equiluminant remote-controls is of particular interest because of the well-documented differences between chromatic and achromatic spatial processing. Chromatic contrast sensitivity functions are low-pass-sensitivity is highest at the lowest spatial frequencies and declines monotonically with increasing spatial frequency-whereas achromatic contrast sensitivity functions are band-pass, with peak sensitivity at intermediate spatial frequencies and reduced sensitivity at both low and high frequencies (Mullen, 1985; Kelly, 1983). This difference is thought to reflect weaker lateral inhibition in chromatic processing channels (Metha and Mullen, 1996). In the cortex, color is represented through at least two types of channels, each with different spatial tuning (Conway, 2009; Conway et al., 2002; Johnson et al., 2004 Johnson et al., , 2008)), and the amount of adaptation produced by modulating lights along one color axis at one spatial channel would not necessarily affect the color mechanisms at another spatial channel (Krauskopf and Gegenfurtner, 1992; Krauskopf et al., 1986; Zaidi et al., 1998; Shapiro et al., 2003).

If the remote-controls illusion depends on the spatial frequency characteristics of the underlying contrast mechanisms, then the equiluminant version should behave differently from the luminance version-specifically, the broader spatial integration implied by low-pass chromatic mechanisms may alter the pattern of gap-dependent phase switching that characterizes the luminance-based illusion. Calibration

Prior to the main experiment, each participant completed a minimum-motion calibration task (Anstis and Cavanagh, 1983) to determine their individual point of equiluminance along the L -M axis. The task exploits the fact that perceived motion in a drifting red-green grating is minimized when the two colors are equiluminant.

Participants viewed an alternating set of gratings composed of +(L-M ) and -(L-M ) bars, followed by a grating of +(L + M ) and -(L + M ) bars. Each bar flipped its polarity between the times it appeared (see Figure 5). On each of 80 trials, a luminance offset was added to the reddish phase, and subtracted from the greenish phase (or vice versa if the offset was negative), sampled uniformly at random from the range -4 to +4 A sigmoid psychometric function was fit to the proportion of "leftward" responses as a function of luminance offset. The equiluminance point was estimated as the luminance offset yielding 50% leftward responses -the point at which the grating appears to flicker in place rather than drift in either direction. This individually determined luminance offset (∆L) was applied to all subsequent chromatic stimuli for that participant.

Across participants, the mean equiluminance offset was -0.426 units (-0.32 cd/m 2 ), with a standard deviation of 1.154 units (0.88 cd/m 2 ). Following calibration, participants were given a brief untimed break before proceeding to the main experiment.

Experiment 1 Discussion The results of Experiment 1 confirm that the remote-controls illusion exists under equiluminant chromatic conditions, but with notable differences from the luminance version. In the luminance condition, we replicate the characteristic V-shaped curve first documented by Hedjar et al.: strong out-of-phase reports when flankers abut the bar, a sharp drop to in-phase at small gaps (1-5 pixels), and a return to out-of-phase at larger gaps. The equiluminant chromatic condition, however, shows a flattened and attenuated version of this pattern. At zero gap, the chromatic condition hovers near chance (ap- The most straightforward interpretation of this difference lies in the well-documented dissimilarities between chromatic and achromatic spatial processing. Chromatic contrast sensitivity functions along the L -M axis are low-pass rather than band-pass, reflecting weaker surround inhibition in the underlying neural mechanisms (Mullen, 1985; Kelly, 1983).

The low-pass characteristic of chromatic processing is thought to arise from the receptive field organization of parvocellular neurons: when center and surround draw on different cone types, the center-surround subtraction does not attenuate low spatial fre-quencies the way it does for luminance, where center and surround share the same cone inputs (Derrington et al., 1984; Rovamo et al., 1994). In practical terms, this means that chromatic mechanisms integrate information over a broader spatial region than luminance mechanisms. For the remote-controls stimulus, broader spatial integration implies that even at zero gap, the chromatic system pools signals from a region large enough to encompass both the flanker edge and some portion of the background, partially mixing the competing contrast signals (Phase A and Phase B). This would weaken the dominance of flanker-driven contrast and push responses toward chance, which is precisely what we observe.

One limitation of Experiment 1 is that with equal-sized flankers, the spatial extent of flanker influence is relatively small. If the chromatic system integrates over a broader area, then flankers that are the same size as the modulating bar may simply be too narrow to produce a strong chromatic contrast signal. This motivates Experiment 2, which tests whether enlarged flankers can restore the V-shaped pattern in the chromatic condition.

Experiment 2: Enlarged Flankers

In Experiment 1, there was a fundamental difference between equiluminant and luminance conditions: That is, for equiluminance, the no-gap condition was reported outof-phase in approximately 50% of the trials (chance), but for luminance the large majority of the trials were out of phase. This could represent a general difference between the appearance of the two types of stimuli, however, it is well-known that the visual system integrates equiluminant lights over a larger region than achromatic lights (Mullen, 1985).

To test whether the differences in Experiment 1 are due to spatial integration, Experiment 2 examines the luminance and chromatic condition with extended-width flankers. 7 participants performed 2 blocks, at 6 different gap sizes (one of 0, 1, 5, 10, 20, or 40 pixels; the same set as in experiment 1, with one additional larger gap size), and 8 repetitions of each condition for a total of 192 trials per participant.

To accommodate the wider flankers (800 pixels, compared to 100 pixels in Experiment 1) within the display, the two stimulus configurations were arranged vertically (one above the other) rather than horizontally as in Experiment 1. While this represents a change in spatial arrangement, pilot observations suggested comparable perceptual effects across arrangements, and the critical manipulation (gap size) remained identical.

Experiment 2 Discussion

First, for the luminance condition, the V-shaped pattern is preserved, confirming that the basic phenomenon is robust to changes in flanker width.

Second, and more critically, the chromatic condition now also shows a V-shaped curve, converging substantially toward the luminance pattern-particularly at the intermediate gap sizes.

This result directly supports the spatial integration hypothesis advanced in the discussion of Experiment 1. The chromatic visual system integrates equiluminant signals over a larger spatial extent than the luminance system (Mullen, 1985; Metha and Mullen, 1996). With the narrow flankers of Experiment 1, the chromatic integration area likely extended beyond the flanker, diluting the flanker-driven contrast signal with background information and producing an ambiguous percept. Widening the flankers ensures that the flanker fills more of the chromatic system's integration area, tipping the balance so that the flanker-driven contrast dominates, just as it does for the luminance system. In essence, the wider flankers compensate for the broader spatial pooling of the chromatic mechanism.

The fact that the chromatic V-shape becomes more similar to the luminance Vshape with wider flankers, but does not become identical, is itself informative. The permutation test showed that 4 of 7 participants still exhibited significantly different luminance and chromatic curves, suggesting that while increased flanker size reduces the difference, it does not eliminate it entirely. This residual difference may reflect additional factors beyond spatial integration, such as the lower temporal sensitivity of chromatic mechanisms (Kelly, 1983), differences in the contrast gain of the rectification step for chromatic versus luminance signals, or the reduced chromatic contrast available within the display gamut compared to luminance contrast.

Together, Experiments 1 and 2 establish a coherent picture: the remote-controls illusion occurs under equiluminant chromatic conditions, but the spatial parameters that determine its appearance are shifted relative to luminance, consistent with the broader spatial integration characteristic of chromatic processing channels. This raises the question of whether other spatial manipulations -specifically, changes in flanker height rather than gap size -show analogous differences between luminance and chromatic processing.

Experiment 3: Vertically Reduced Flankers Experiments 1 and 2 examined how the horizontal distance between flanker and modulating bar affects the perceived phase of modulation, establishing that the chromatic condition departs from the luminance condition in a manner consistent with broader spatial integration in chromatic processing channels. Experiment 2 further showed that widening the flankers could partially restore the chromatic V-shaped curve, implicating the spatial extent of the flanker as a critical variable. Experiment 3 takes a complementary approach: instead of varying the gap, we fix the flankers directly adjacent to the modulating bar (a gap of zero pixels) and parametrically vary the height of the flankers relative to the bar.

This manipulation probes two distinct aspects of the stimulus simultaneously. When the flanker is nearly the same height as the bar (a small height difference), the flanker and bar share most of their vertical extent. In this regime, the stimulus is similar to the zero-gap condition of Experiment 1, but with a small region at the top and bottom of the bar where the bar extends beyond the flanker, creating a junction -a point where the bar is adjacent to the flanker on one side but adjacent to the background on the other.

The contrast signal at these junctions is mixed: the flanker-driven signal dominates along the shared edge, while the background-driven signal is exposed at the junction. Even a small exposed region may be sufficient to alter the integrated contrast signal, and thus the perceived phase. How the visual system weights these junction signals relative to the flanker-driven signal along the main shared edge is the first question addressed by this experiment.

When the height difference is large, however, the flanker is much shorter than the bar. In this regime, the stimulus approaches the no-flanker or large-gap condition (in which the bars appear out of phase) with only a small remnant of flanker present. This end of the manipulation therefore measures a second quantity: the minimum flanker height required to induce a perceptual shift from out-of-phase to in-phase appearance. Hedjar et al. (2018) demonstrated that flanker extension beyond the bar can switch the perceived phase from out-of-phase to in-phase at zero gap, and that this effect was among the findings their simple DoG model could not explain, suggesting that vertical extent engages processing mechanisms beyond the one-dimensional horizontal profile captured by standard center-surround filters. The present experiment inverts this logic by asking how much flanker must be present-rather than how far it must extend-to produce the phase reversal. If chromatic mechanisms integrate contrast over a broader spatial area than luminance mechanisms (Mullen, 1985; Metha and Mullen, 1996), then we would predict that a larger flanker is required to produce a reliable phase shift in the chromatic condition, because the broader integration area dilutes the flanker's contribution relative to the background.

The stimuli (described in Figure 12) were arranged horizontally as in experiment 1, with no gap between the modulating bar and the flanking bars. The flanking bars had their height chosen randomly on each trial from one of 0, 35, 125, 215, 305, 400, or 500 pixels (compared to a flanker with a fixed height of 500 pixels). 7 participants completed this experiment.

When no flankers were present (a height of 0 pixels), participants consistently indicated the bars modulating out-of-phase in both the luminance and chromatic condi- tions (p = 0.006, p < 0.001 respectively). The addition of a moderate-sized flanker (305 pixels) flipped the perception from out-of-phase to in-phase in the luminance condition (p = 0.020), whereas the chromatic condition was no different from random (p = 0.197).

Note that the far right point on the graph in Figure 13 is equivalent to the condition in experiment 1 where the gap size is equal to 0 pixels.

Experiment 3 Discussion

The results of experiment 3 show a clear dissociation between the luminance and chromatic conditions in how the visual system integrates contrast information along the vertical dimension of the stimulus. In fact, the dissociation between the two is strikingly similar to that shown in Experiment 1, in which the luminance condition shows a Vshaped response curve, while the chromatic condition shows matches at one extreme, and flattens into an ambiguous percept at the other extreme, and at the intermediate points.

In the luminance condition, the absence of flankers (a 0 pixel height) produces robust out-of-phase reports, confirming that without flankers the background-driven contrast dominates. As flankers are introduced and grow taller, the percept transitions from out- of-phase to in-phase, with a moderate-sized flanker being sufficient to flip the percept, as the two sources of contrast information become approximately equally weighted along the vertical dimension of the stimulus.

As the flanker grows to become the same height as the modulating bar, the percept returns to out-of-phase, as the flanker contrast dominates.

The chromatic condition tells a different story. While it matches the luminance condition with no flankers present, the moderate-sized flanker that flips the luminance percept produces responses indistinguishable from chance. In addition, the transition away from out-of-phase is considerably more gradual in this condition.

This pattern is consistent with the spatial integration account developed across the preceding experiments, now extended to the vertical dimension. The chromatic system's broader integration area means that a small flanker, which occupies only a fraction of the bar's height, contributes a proportionally smaller share of the total integrated signal. The background-driven contrast, which is present along the full height of the bar, dominates the chromatic integration area even when a flanker is present, because the flanker simply does not fill enough of the pooling region to compete. The luminance system, with its narrower and more edge-sensitive spatial filters, can detect and weight the flanker-driven contrast signal even when it occupies a small spatial extent, producing the phase reversal at much smaller flanker sizes.

Modeling Framework

Difference-of-Gaussians Model

We modeled the results of Experiments 1 and 2 with a model based on rectified difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) filters similar to that of Hedjar et al. (2018). The centersurround structure of the DoG filter computes a local contrast signal, and the rectification step discards the sign of the contrast, retaining only its magnitude. The spatial extent of the filter determines which contrast boundaries fall within its integration area, and therefore which phase dominates the output. By examining the temporal dynamics of the rectified filter output at each bar location, we can predict whether an observer should perceive the bars as modulating in phase or out of phase.

The question, then, is whether the integration of these competing contrast signals can be captured by a simple spatial filtering mechanism. Model Modifications

The present model builds upon the framework introduced by Hedjar et al., who modeled the remote-controls illusion using rectified one-dimensional DoG filters convolved with a spatiotemporal representation of the stimulus. We retained this general architecture but introduced several modifications, some minor and some substantive, which we describe here.

Three modifications were relatively minor and do not substantially alter the model's qualitative behavior but serve to more faithfully represent the psychophysical stimuli.

First, whereas Hedjar et al. (2018) modulated the center bar using a triangle wave in time, we replaced this with a sinusoidal waveform to match the temporal modulation used in our experiments. Second, where the original model applied hard rectification (absolute value) to the filter outputs, we employed a soft rectification function that is continuously differentiable and reduces sensitivity to noise near zero. Third, in place of the original proportion-of-phase (POP) statistic -which counted the proportion of time points at which the sign of the first derivative differed between the two bars -we employed a continuous, gradient-based opposition score that captures the same intuition but provides graded rather than binary comparisons at each time point.

Two modifications were more substantive and measurably improved model performance. The first was the introduction of a time delay of 4 frames (approximately 12 ms) between the center and surround components of each DoG filter. This modification is motivated by physiological evidence that the surround response of retinal ganglion cells is temporally delayed relative to the center response (Dawis et al., 1984; Enroth-Cugell et al., 1983). The second was a shift from extracting the filter response at the spatial center of each modulating bar to extracting it near the bar edge. Contrast information is most strongly represented at luminance and chromatic boundaries, and edge-based extraction proved particularly important for capturing the behavior of the chromatic conditions.

To empirically validate these choices, we compared individual-level model fit across four model variants: a baseline model matching the Hedjar et al. architecture (center extraction, no time delay), and models adding each modification individually and in combination. Chapter 4 Discussion

Contrast

Action at a distance typically requires a discussion of "contrast," which refers to some form of comparison between different points of light. This term can be a source of conceptual confusion because it may denote a physical property of a stimulus, a specific category of visual phenomena, or a perceptual description of how lights appear relative to one another; notably, each of these interpretations carries its own unique ambiguity. It is useful to keep these definitions and their limitations in mind for this discussion.

As a physical stimulus, contrast can be expressed in terms of Michelson ((L 1 -L 2 )/(L 1 +L 2 )) or Weber ((L 1 -L 2 )/L 2 ) contrast. These formulations presuppose a specific comparison between two known luminances. However, the visual system distinguishes between the local contrast formed by two lights at a shared edge and the remote contrast arising from lights separated by distance and time. Both forms of contrast are integral to perception; consequently, a single image generates a vast array of contrast values, each important to different aspects of vision.

As a visual phenomenon, contrast is often used to describe the influence of a surrounding light on the appearance of a patch of light (and is often compared to assimilation). That is, color contrast refers to a gray patch appearing greenish if surrounded by a red field, whereas color assimilation refers to the red surround making the gray patch look 36 redder. The term contrast in this context refers to the appearance of color of the object (a shift in hue, brightness, and saturation)-not to the physical or perceptual difference between the object and the surround.

As a perceptual description, contrast refers to how one light looks relative to another light. For example, black and white bars have high contrast relative to each other, while two bars of muted grays appear to have low contrast. While this perceptual description is both fundamental to how we see the world, and essential to most artists, it is often neglected in the vision science literature. Whittle (2003) noted that most comprehensive treatments of colour appearance in vision science treat perceptual contrast (which Whittle referred to as contrast colors) as a "minor side effect." The situation has barely changed in the past quarter of a century.

Contrast asynchrony and the remote-controls illusion

The remote-controls illusion is a variant of a contrast asynchrony, which serves as a method for measuring a perceptual quality that corresponds to the perceptual description of contrast. Contrast asynchrony is frequently confused with standard contrast phenomena. In contrast asynchrony illusions, an observer perceives two lights as modulating either out of phase or in phase with each other; the observer, therefore, responds to the contrast between two lights and their background rather than the hue, brightness, or saturation of the lights themselves. At the core of the effect is a conflict between the appearance of perceived contrasts between the patches and their backgrounds and the appearance of the perceived colors of the patches. That is, the asynchrony is due to contrast appearance, not contrast as a visual phenomenon.

The remote-control illusions are an attempt to understand the spatial aspects of the physical stimulus that allow for the perception of the asynchrony. Specifically, we ask what aspects of contrast allow us to see whether two lights appear to modulate in phase or in antiphase with each other. With luminance, the answer is fairly straightforward.

Our results replicate Hedjar et al. (2018) in producing a V-shaped curve for perceived contrast versus gap size: no gap is perceived as out-of-phase, a small gap as inphase, and a large gap as out-of-phase. In our framing of the effect, the V-shaped curve implies that there are at least two sources of physical contrast: a narrow-range contrast near the edge and a contrast that makes comparisons over a larger spatial range. By changing the size of the gap, either the edge contrast predominates or the two contrasts, in effect, cancel each other out.

Equiluminant remote-control illusions

For equiluminant remote-control illusions, the story is not as simple as it is for remote-control illusions in luminance. For average observers, equiluminant responses are influenced by information at a very large spatial scale. This is curious because it shows that changes at the far edge of the flanker make a difference in whether an observer perceives the lights as modulating in-phase or out-of-phase. Thus, "action at a distance" takes place over a larger spatial range for equiluminant light than it does for luminance, at least for the average observer. However, there is substantially greater inter-observer variability in the chromatic condition both in the appearance of a "V" shape and in the effects of large flankers. The variability should not be surprising since there are well-documented individual differences in the relative weighting of L and M cones in the luminance channel. He et al. (2020) found that between-observer variability in equiluminance settings was almost five times larger than within-observer precision, and subsequent modeling work (He et al., 2021) showed that this variability can be attributed to individual differences in macular pigment density, lens pigment density, photopigment optical density, and L-cone opsin polymorphisms. There are known individual differences in the L:M cone ratio, which in normal trichromats can range from approximately 1:1 to greater than 4:1 (Carroll et al., 2002; Gunther and Dobkins, 2002). The L : M ratio directly affects the magnitude of the L -M opponent signal available for the chromatic channel: observers with more extreme ratios have stronger chromatic signals, while those closer to 1:1 have weaker ones. Variation in the strength of the underlying chromatic contrast signal would translate directly into variation in how strongly the flanker or background dominates at each gap size, producing the spread of individual curves visible in the data. In the luminance condition, by contrast, L + M summation is relatively insensitive to the L : M ratio because both cone types contribute in the same direction.

One possibility is that there are "luminance artifacts" where an observer is presented with equiluminant lights but detects contrast with luminance-based processes. We attempted to correct for this possibility by calibrating equiluminance individually using a minimum-motion procedure; any residual luminance artifact-even a small one-would differentially affect the balance between the competing contrast signals in the chromatic stimulus. Since the chromatic condition already operates near the boundary between Phase A and Phase B dominance (particularly at small and zero gaps), even slight luminance contamination could tip a given observer's percept in one direction or the other, amplifying variability across participants in a way that would not occur for the luminance condition, where the contrast signals are much stronger and further from the decision boundary. However, interobserver variation is not likely to solely be due to luminance artifacts because luminance artifacts should increase the perception of an asynchrony.

The general pattern for equiluminant lights is that as the gap size shrinks to zero and the flankers adjoin the modulating bar, the perception is (at both the aggregate and individual level) ambiguous, with proportions indicated out-of-phase near 50%.

Integration of multiple spatial contrast scales

How then should we account for the variability of the equiluminant remote-controls illusion? Any differences between luminance and equiluminant conditions not due to "luminance artifacts" must be speculative because the physical underpinnings remain unknown. For instance, variation in color appearance among observers with normal color vision has been well-documented, as seen in phenomena like "The Dress" or the identification of unique hues. However, unlike luminance artifacts, the processes proposed to account for these differences often rely on interpretive strategies-such as individual differences in color constancy or Bayesian priors-developmental cultural exposure, or even linguistic structure. While these sources may be important to perception, explaining variation in constructed concepts may simply shift the problem to another level of abstraction. Explaining the variation in "The Dress" in terms of color constancy, for example, assumes both that color constancy is the correct framework and that we can measure its individual variation precisely enough to account for color labeling.

In this study, we have investigated a particular model of the remote-controls illusion. The model does reasonably well at accounting for both the average data and the responses of individual observers, but the current data cannot differentiate whether the individual variation is due to multiple spatial channels, a decision rule, or some other cause.

For instance, there are many reasons to think that spatial frequency response adapts to the visual objects in the image (Shapiro and Hedjar, 2019; Peterzell, 2025). If that is the case, then such changes should be accounted for by changes in response weightings on each of the channels so that response is maximal at the size of the object. To our knowledge, there are no models for how a shift would take place, but it would seem reasonable that any account would be affected by the contrast edges. Thus the sensitivity change would arise either from changes in gap size or from contrast modulation and may elicit varied responses in the luminance and chromatic conditions. We extended the model described in this paper with weighted combinations of multiple-scale filters, but did not find it produced significantly different results from the single-scale model.

On the other hand, the idea that inter-observer variation has to do with judgment biases seems consistent with subjective reports. Following each experimental session, par- ticipants completed an open-ended debrief addressing their subjective experience.

Participants were asked about any discomfort or motion sickness, perceived task difficulty, noticed differences between luminance and chromatic conditions, and general feedback.

Responses were not formally recorded but were noted by the experimenter and seem consistent with public demonstrations of the RCI. That is, participants in the luminance conditions generally reported clear, stable percepts that shifted predictably with gap size.

In contrast, many participants spontaneously reported that the chromatic conditions felt more difficult, with several describing the percept as "ambiguous," "unstable," or "flipflopping" over time. It is as if most observers were mostly consciously aware of the potential conflicting perceptions, but were being forced to choose one.

Conclusion

In this thesis, I have characterized the differences between versions of the remotecontrols illusion that modulate in luminance and those that modulate in chroma, over three different spatial configurations.

In the luminance condition, all three experiments reproduced the characteristic pattern described by Hedjar et al.: the bars appeared out-of-phase when the flankers abutted them or were far away, and in-phase when the gap was small or the flankers covered only part of the bar's height. In the chromatic condition, the illusion was weaker and more variable across observers. With flankers the same size as the bar, responses at zero and small gaps were near chance rather than reliably in-or out-of-phase. Widening the flankers largely restored the V-shaped curve, although 4 of 7 participants still showed significantly different luminance and chromatic curves. Finally, the intermediate

In remote-controls, two identical rectangular bars modulate in luminance sinusoidally from dark to bright; one bar is surrounded by a bright background and the other by a dark background. Bright static flanker bars are added to the sides of the modulating bar with the dark background, and dark static flanker bars are added to the sides of the modulating bar with the bright background. The sign of the contrast between a modulating bar and its flankers is therefore opposite to the sign of the contrast between the bar and its background.

The stimulus is represented as a two-dimensional array in which the horizontal axis is a spatial cross-section through the display and the vertical axis is time. A DoG filter convolved with this representation computes a local contrast signal at each point; the spatial extent of the filter determines whether the contrast with the flanker, the contrast with the background, or some mixture of both, falls within its integration area. 25 The filter output is rectified to discard contrast sign and retain only magnitude, and temporal signals are extracted at the edge of each modulating bar. An opposition score computed from these signals then quantifies whether the two bars' contrast responses move in the same or opposite directions over time, yielding the model's prediction of in-phase or out-of-phase appearance.
