Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. ABSTRACT

Patient-clinician interactions are central to diagnosis and treatment in mental health care. They involve inferential processes grounded in patients' reports of symptoms and clinicians' interpretations of signs. These processes are liable to microdynamic looping effects that drive diagnostic belief formation. In facing the epistemic challenge of reaching a shared ascription of the patient's condition, such looping effects can lead to misalignment and give rise to epistemic injustice if a credibility deficit exists between the patient and the clinician. In this paper, we use the active inference framework to develop a computational model of looping microdynamics that captures how patients and clinicians reciprocally interpret symptoms, attribute causes, and negotiate diagnostic categories over time. We present a simulation study showing how specific interactional patterns can amplify or mitigate credibility deficits, depending in part on clinicians' attributional attitudes toward patients' self-interpretations. Our results suggest that, in contexts of diagnostic disagreement, positive disconfirmationoffering an alternative explanatory framing rather than simply negating the patient's perspective-may reduce the risk of epistemic injustice. Overall, the model highlights how looping microdynamics structure mutual belief formation in clinical encounters and how attention to these dynamics can support more epistemically responsible clinical communication.

1 INTRODUCTION

Looping effects can occur at many levels in mental health, psychiatry and psychology. They can occur in the construction of categories and concepts that become ways that people identify and understand themselves; in institutional practices that deploy diagnostic systems that shape illness experience; and in the dynamics of the clinical encounter (Brinkmann, 2005; Gillespie & Wagoner, 2025; Kirmayer, 2026; Kirmayer & Sartorius, 2007). This paper presents a simulation study of the microdynamics of looping effects (Gauld et al., 2025) on the process of belief formation as it occurs in the interaction between patients and clinicians, with a focus on diagnostic practice. It seeks to illustrate how looping microdynamics can engender situations that may lead to epistemic injustices (Fricker, 2007) in clinical practice.

Our simulation employs the resources of active inference (Parr & Pezzulohttps://paperpile.com/c/1lHXLs/jic5f 2025) , which offers a methodology to model the naturalistic process of belief formation and action selection. The results shed light on the ways in which particular patterns of interaction may contribute to epistemic injustice in clinical interaction (https://paperpile.com/c/1lHXLs/JGOi3/?prefix=cf. Colombo & Fabryhttps://paperpile.com/c/1lHXLs/JGOi3/?prefix=cf 2021) , and on the potential impact of clinicians' attitudes and modes of response to patients' interpretation of symptoms, causal attributions and self-ascription of diagnostic labels.

Studying the microdynamics of looping effects and their impact on belief formation processes is challenging, since they depend on interpersonal interactions that are socially and culturally situated and hence difficult or impossible to manipulate experimentally (Hacking, 2002 (Hacking, , 2004)). Simulation studies can provide insights that simplify the problem space to allow more targeted empirical studies. A growing number of scholars have advocated for the use of computational modeling in philosophy (Scheller et al., 2022; Thagard, 2018).

Computational models can be used to reveal system dynamics that would otherwise be difficult to capture -e.g., the idea that mild in-group preference could generate major segregation patterns in society such as illustrated by Schelling's model of segregation (Weisberg, 2013).

In addition to clarifying how interactional dynamics can unfold, modeling provides a way to clarify some philosophical questions about social ontology (Epstein, 2025). As Hacking (2000) insisted, there may be many kinds of social construction. Following Epstein (2025) a theory of social construction must characterize the nature of the context, inputs, processes, and outcomes. "If one does not have explicit account of the things that are doing the construction work, one does not really have a theory of social construction" (p. 44).

Constructing an explicit computational model compels us to specify these and allows us to explore the implications of alternate inputs, processes and outcomes. Moreover, the iterative nature of an interactional model can reveal emergent dynamics not apparent in considering a single conversation exchange or outcome.

Our model borrows from Bayesian methods in computational psychiatry -specifically, computational phenotyping (Friston et al., 2014) -used to study mental disorders based on the production and manipulation of computational (neuro)phenotypes (Montague et al., 2012). By manipulating computational phenotypes, one can explore hypotheses, in silico, about lesions, pharmacology, and neurophysiology associated with mental disorders (Huys et al., 2015). Here, we adapt these methods to examine a dyadic social interaction. We formalise a computational relationship, whose dimensions and scope are defined in terms of parameters that describe the patient-clinician dyad, as well as the clinical setting itself. The approach we propose thus broadly coheres with formal work in social epistemology aiming at modelling processes of belief formation in social systems (e.g., Grim, 2019; Kirmayer, 2024). Our simulation is relevant for readers interested in the social epistemology of clinical encounters and supports the intuition that adopting a positive approach to challenging patients' beliefs -about their situation -can be done in a way that improves medical listening and consultation.

Section 5 discusses these results, their limitations, and their implications for clinical practice, and Section 6 concludes.

2. LOOPING EFFECTS AND EPISTEMIC INJUSTICES

>LOOPING EFFECTS AND EPISTEMIC INJUSTICES

2.1 The microdynamics of looping effects

Mental health assessments involve communicative interactions between patients and clinicians. Like any interpersonal interaction, this process can change the cognition and experience of both interlocutors. In clinical settings, this interaction shapes the ways in which patients recollect and present their symptoms and distress -and the ways that clinicians respond. This interactional process may introduce biases that change patients' symptom presentations over time, sometimes in unpredictable ways. This can happen when clinicians emphasize certain aspects of the patient's experience through questions (e.g., "Can you tell me more about your fatigue?") and responses, which may be shaped by clinicians' theoretical orientation, models of psychopathology, or other aspects of their background (Kirmayer, 2025). The clinician's questions may direct the patient's attention to specific symptoms or illness schemas related to causal attributions or implicit models (e.g., "Yes, now that I think about it, my fatigue is related to my backache and it's especially bad on weekdays, when I am really stressed at work"). This in turn may provide the patient with motivations to use local cultural idioms of distress to make sense of their experience, thus emphasizing a given configuration of the presentation often accompanied of an explanation or schema (e.g., "I think I'm suffering from burnout") (Kirmayer & Young, 1998; Macias-Velasquez et al., 2020).

In his earlier work, Ian Hacking (1985) framed these self-constituting and vindicating effects of diagnostic practices in terms of a larger process that he termed the "looping effect of human kinds" (i.e., the fact that people change their self-ascription and experience in response to the categories and descriptions they live under, and the fact that these categories change, in turn, as a function of people's subsequent (shared) experience). Such phenomena are typically studied by the social sciences and humanities (Hacking, 1996). In a later version of this thesis, Hacking (2000) defended a view of natural kinds that can interact with phenomena typically acting on human kinds (e.g., labelling). Though Hacking provided detailed historical studies of the looping effect in psychiatric phenomena (Hackinghttps://paperpile.com/c/1lHXLs/VF1H3+RBbca 1998a (Hacking, , 1998b)) , he was not primarily concerned with the mechanisms of looping. In a useful essay, he noted that looping processes involve large-scale discursive formations of power/knowledge; like those described by Foucault as well as the microdynamics of situated interpersonal interaction studied by sociologists like Erving Goffman (Hacking, 2004). These macro and micro dynamics interact by creating and (de)stabilizing categories in social and cultural institutions and practices.

Building on this dual (macro/micro) view of looping effects, Gauld and colleagues (2025) proposed a mechanistic account -in the context of clinical interactions -that distinguishes two temporal scales of looping effects, according to which changes in patients' presentations at short time scales would be explained by "microdynamic" looping effects based on the capacity of patients to experience change in symptoms through processes of self-construal.

Ensuing institutional nosological revision would be explained by "macrodynamic" looping effects on a longer time scale, based on the capacity of the medical institution to revise its nosology in response to clinicians' cumulative experience of new presentations emerging from communicative interactions. In their paper, Gauld and colleagues address the epistemic nature of these looping processes and the need to better understand the dynamics of looping in a clinical setting, since they may lead to epistemic injustices that disrupt the clinical relation and harm the patient.

2.2 Epistemic injustices

Fricker (2007) identified two types of epistemic injustice, testimonial and hermeneutical. We adopt Amandine Catala's (Catala, 2025; Catala et al., 2021) terminological clarification and analysis of the theory of epistemic injustice according to which an epistemic injustice occurs when a person suffers the consequences of an undue credibility deficit (testimonial injustice) or of an undue intelligibility deficit (hermeneutical injustice). Testimonial injustices happen, for instance, when an interlocutor (here, the clinician) disregards the testimony of a contributor (e.g., failing to consult a patient who could competently contribute to the exchange). In certain circumstances, the situation is such that the contributor cannot even attempt to contribute. These are cases of "silencing", or "pre-emptive" testimonial injustice.

In turn, hermeneutical injustices happen when contributors either remain misunderstood by an interlocutor (i.e., self-to-others intelligibility) or fails to understand their own situation (i.e., self-to-self intelligibility) due to an intelligibility deficit. According to Catala (2025), sources of intelligibility deficits include the lack of concepts in the common pool of meaning making resources capturing the contributor's experience, the rejection by the interlocutors of the meaning making resources of the contributor, or the misunderstanding by the locutor of existing meaning making resources.

Below, we present simulations of patient-clinician interactions under conditions that feature a "credibility deficit." The patient can contribute to the interaction, but their contribution has no effect on the clinician's understanding of the patient's situation, thereby reflecting a case of testimonial injustices, where the patient is not, overtly silenced or precluded from contributing, but where the effect of contribution is nullified, given the interactional setting.

For the sort of Bayesian agents implemented here, this credibility deficit can lead to interactions posing risks of intelligibility deficits and associated hermeneutical injustices. For instance, the clinician may hear the patient say "I am feeling sad", and respond "No, what you experience is anxiety", thereby leading to a loss of confidence in the patient model, the model reflecting a belief of the sort "Ah, OK, but then I'm not sure of what I feel anymore".

Accordingly, in this simulation, the intelligibility deficit arises from the inability of the patient to contribute to the meaning-making process implicit in the patient-clinician interaction (e.g., the clinician failing to take seriously the patient's resistance to their assessment). Practically, the patient model will suffer a credibility deficit in the sense that their testimonial contribution to the interaction will be attenuated under the conditions instantiating the credibility deficit. The consequence of this is that the shared reality between the clinician and the patient will become increasingly divorced from the patient's interpretation of their own situation, thereby leading to an intelligibility deficit. Note, also, that credibility imbalances often are created by credibility gaps in which the credibility of the contributor is reduced, while that of the interlocutor is increased (Medina, 2011). In our simulation, there will be no increased credibility of the interlocutor under the credibility deficit conditions. Only a decrease of credibility on the side of the contributor.

3 METHOD

Our simulation aims to understand the mechanisms of looping microdynamics that underwrite joint inference based upon shared and communicated observations, as well as the consequence these microdynamics have on epistemic injustices in clinical context. To simulate the kind of belief updating implicated in looping microdynamics we will use the Bayesian mechanics afforded by active inference. In particular, we address the interpretative problem faced by doctors and patients resolved under a shared generative models or narratives and, crucially, a reciprocal influence of both participants in a dyadic interaction (Friston & Frith, 2015; Frith & Wentzer, 2013). This requisite reciprocity will be studied in terms of a credibility deficit in which the doctor fails to assign credibility or precision to the patient's contribution exposing the patient to a risk of hermeneutical injustice.

3.1 The setup

The scenario we implement is an interaction between two mutually influencing cognitive agents engaged in communicative exchange. One agent represents the clinician, and the other represents the patient. The task of the two agents is to infer the category that fits the patient's situation (e.g., diagnostic category A or B), and to predict the symptom associated with the category (e.g., reports of sadness or anxiety). For the patient, this corresponds to the "subjective" framing of their situation (e.g., "I have A") and to reflecting upon their experience (e.g., "I am sad"). For the clinician, the inference corresponds to the "subjective" formulation of a preliminary diagnosis (e.g., "The patient might have B"), and to the "subjective" perception of symptoms (e.g., "I see sadness").

In our model, the diagnostic category must be inferred because it is a latent variable.

Conversely, the symptom is something that is observed and shared through the patient's selfreport, which forms the basis of diagnostic inference -and implicitly, a shared commitment between the patient and doctor to a common understanding of the symptoms at hand. We will also commit to the conventional clinical distinction between a "symptom" and a "sign". A symptom is something the patient reports, whereas a sign is something that a clinician observes. The symptom is expected to track the underlying experience of the patient, while functioning as an indicator of the patient's diagnostic category. However, in practice, there may be a mismatch between the reported symptom and the patient's experience (e.g., as in the case of autistic masking or due to the clinician's pre-theoretic assumptions). The model on offer in this paper makes the simplifying assumption that, from the point of view of the clinician, there is a lawful (i.e., deterministic) mapping between the experience and the symptom. By contrast, the mapping between symptoms and diagnostic categories is probabilistic as the model allows for uncertainty in the likelihood of each symptom given the diagnostic category.

The mutual influence between the patient and the clinician occurs through the communicative interaction, or exchange of "observable" information mediated by an "objective" process that can be influenced by the actions of the patient and the clinician (Bruineberg et al., 2018). The objective process corresponds to the process generating observations in the context of an interaction between the patient and doctor. Thus, we will call this the "interaction" process.

The interaction process includes the objective symptom-category likelihood mappings, about which both the patient and clinician have prior beliefs (called their subjective modelhttps://paperpile.com/c/1lHXLs/Y7f3b (Schwartenbeck & Friston, 2016 ).

An interactional process can be understood in terms of a shared generative structure that encodes probabilistic dependencies between inferred states and future observations, thus mediating the temporal coupling between (joint) belief updating, and subsequent symptom expression. Our model assumes that all patient-clinician interactions take place within this shared interaction process. In the case of the patient, the communicated information that shapes the interaction is about their symptom experience and their beliefs about the associated category (e.g., "I think I have A because I am sad"). For the clinician, the interaction process shaping communicative interaction involves confirming or contradicting the patient's claim (e.g., "A does not cause sadness").

3.2 The dynamics of the interaction

As a preliminary step, to characterize the convergence properties of the model, we simulated 1000 interactions (trials) per condition, for 9 different conditions (see below). We found empirically that a total of 1000 interactions were sufficient to differentiate results under all conditions and to observe convergence between the subjective models and the interaction process (< 5% change in cross entropy). Each trial should be understood as an interaction exchange loop starting with the patient expressing their understanding of the situation, thereby modifying the interaction (i.e., observable) process, and to which the clinician responds, thereby modifying the interaction process in turn (see Figure 1 for a flowchart depicting these interactions).

With each interaction, the patient provides the clinician with information through the selection of an action based on their subjective model, which updates the interaction process influencing subjective observations (e.g., through utterances, "I think 'A' causes my anxiety"). In turn, depending on the conditions, the clinician's reply will lead to a clinical emphasis or deemphasis of certain symptoms-category mappings (e.g.," 'A' generally does/does not cause anxiety"), which also changes the probabilistic structure of the presentation during the interaction (i.e., the interaction process) (see Figure 2).

Importantly, the symptoms presented by the patient and clinician are biased towards the symptom that they each previously observed (e.g., I was sad/I saw sadness one minute ago, I expect to be/expect to see sadness now) and will shape the observation process accordingly.

As such, the model assumes that symptoms are necessarily linked to an underlying generative process, without committing to the existence of an essentialist diagnostic category underlying their co-occurrence (e.g., depression as a latent entity behind sadness and anxiety) (Kendler et al., 2011).

Our goal is to explore the effects that looping microdynamics have on the patterns of ascription between the patient and the clinician. Looping microdynamics may cause the patient and the clinician to reach (or failing to reach) a common ascription of the cause "A" or "B" of the patient's symptom or may cause them to achieve (or fail to achieve) a common ascription of the patient's experienced symptom "sadness" or "anxiety". We further explore the contribution of looping microdynamics to epistemic injustices. A "disagreement" over the ascription of categories poses a risk of testimonial injustice (e.g., "I think I have A", "No, you have B", "Yet I really think there is something like A going on"). In turn, a "mismatch" between the patient's reported experience and the clinician's symptom ascription poses a risk of hermeneutical injustice (e.g., "I am feeling sad", "No, this is anxiety", "Ah, OK, but then I'm not sure of what I feel anymore"). We simulate how these risks may be exacerbated in situations of credibility (and intelligibility) deficit on the part of the patient.

In summary, we present a minimal set up to model dyadic exchanges in a patient-clinician context in which the (objective) probability of the patient presenting with a particular symptom or sign, given their clinical diagnosis, depends upon the exchange between the patient and clinician, under their (subjective) generative models. This setup induces a circular causality characteristic of looping microdynamics which allows the patient-clinician dyad to co-construct an ontology -e.g., through the tendencies of diagnostic categories to elicit certain symptoms and signs (Kotov et al., 2017). 3.3 The models

Within clinical reasoning, the processes responsible for hypothesis generation, or the making of a diagnosis can be cast as inferential processes, such as deductive and inductive reasoning (e.g., simple induction and abduction), which is supported by rule-based and causal reasoning (Corrao & Argano, 2022; Ledley & Lusted, 1959; Stanley, 2019; Stanley & Campos, 2013).

As such, they may rest on different heuristics (i.e., prior beliefs) that entail cognitive biases, such as when employing "fast-thinking" strategy (Croskerry, 2009; Hughes et al., 2020). For the patient, inference within the context of our simulation is also characterized by inductive processes that subserve attributional processes that involve causal reasoning (Kirmayer et al., 1994). In the situation we model, this may be viewed as inferential reasoning from experienced somatic and psychological evidence of one's health state, to a narrative framing of one's situation (e.g., by leveraging cultural explanatory models and folk interpretation of medical concepts) (Kirmayer & Bhugra, 2009).

The model on offer is based in active inference and therefore implements both deductive (i.e., inference based on evidence, or Bayesian update) and abductive aspects (i.e., inference to the best explanation, or model selection) of diagnostic reasoning using a Bayesian scheme that performs inference under prior beliefs (e.g., about applicable diagnostic categories) based on observed evidence (e.g., observed symptoms; for a detailed discussion, see Friston et al., 2025). Our simulation employs an active inference model of action-perception loops and learning under active inference (Da Costa et al., 2020). Using coupled active inference under Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs), we simulated a series of 1000 interactions such as depicted in Figure 1. A POMDP is a mathematical model used to describe decision-making scenarios where the agent has incomplete information about hidden or latent states generating observations. Unlike a standard MDP model (i.e., a model where the agent has full knowledge of the current state), a POMDP includes observations that provide partial insights into the state, requiring the agent to maintain a belief distribution over possible states.

This model is useful for simulating real-world clinical situations where uncertainty and partial knowledge play a role in decision-making. The full parametrization of our POMDPs can be found in the code in the appendix.

3.4 The conditions

We implemented 9 conditions -including a baseline -to manipulate the way the patient, and clinician can influence the interaction process directly, and thereby influence each other's subjective model indirectly. Manipulations under each condition are meant to test the influence of the microdynamics of looping effects on symptom attribution and diagnostic alignment between patients and clinicians.

Conditions 2-5 are "looping" conditions as they involve influences of both the patient and the clinician on the interaction process. Microdynamic looping effects are driven by patterns of emphasizing and de-emphasizing symptom-category mappings in the interaction process, where deemphasizing means acting -either through behavioral manifestation or speechso as to decrease the probability of a particular symptom in the interaction process (i.e., decreasing the Dirichlet counts in the likelihood mappings in Box 1 by multiplying by .25), and where emphasizing means increasing the Dirichlet counts (i.e., by 2.5).

Conditions 6-9 are "credibility deficit" conditions wherein the patient's ability to modify the interaction process is attenuated (from 2.5 to 1). The baseline (C1) involves no influence from either side.

4.2 Epistemic injustices and looping microdynamics

Testimonial injustices primarily arise from persistent disagreements over causal attributions, whereas hermeneutical injustices are more likely to emerge from sustained mismatches between patients' lived symptom experience and clinicians' perceptual and interpretative frameworks. Accordingly, in a situation of credibility deficit, the disconfirmation of the patient's beliefs about the cause of their symptoms can lead to disagreements posing risks of testimonial injustices. Disconfirmation under credibility deficit can also lead to a mismatch in symptom experience and perception posing additional risks of hermeneutical injustices.

Overall, we assume that epistemic injustice causing harm will tend to happen in disconfirmation conditions. We thus looked at which of the disconfirmation conditions yielded the most increase (or least decrease) in mismatch, the most increase (or least decrease) in disagreements, and the most increase (or least decrease) in mismatch and disagreements, when compared to baseline. We also looked at which of the disconfirmation conditions yielded the best results in terms of match agreements. Table 2 compares the disconfirmation conditions 2 and 3 with the disconfirmation conditions under credibility deficit 6 and 7 relative to the baseline. We found that in the absence of a credibility deficit, the disconfirmation pattern that leads to the greatest increase in matched agreement is the "negative disconfirmation" (C2). But when in a situation of credibility deficit, the disconfirmation patterns that yielded the greatest increase in matched agreement was the positive one (C7). We found that in the absence of a credibility deficit, negative disconfirmation yielded the least increase in mismatches. In turn, in the presence of a credibility deficit, positive disconfirmation yielded the most decrease in mismatches. In the absence of a credibility deficit, both disconfirmation patterns reduced matched and mismatched disagreements to the same degree (1.8% and 1.5% for both C2 and C3). In the presence of a credibility deficit, positive disconfirmation yielded a much greater decrease in both forms of disagreement than negative disconfirmation (0.1% and 0.1% for C7, versus 26.2% and 23.6% for C6).

5 DISCUSSION

5.1 Implications of the Simulation

Recognizing that failure to reach agreement on the patient's condition may reflect divergent perspectives or the lack of adequate communication between patient and clinician (Heritage & Maynardhttps://paperpile.com/c/1lHXLs/c8BYm 2006) , we presented a model to explore the effect of the microdynamics of looping effects on the alignment of patient-clinician ascription, and their contribution to epistemic injustices using active inference. Tracking the way looping microdynamics drives the mutual influence of patients' and clinicians' perspectives is of central importance to identifying successful communication and clinical intervention. We simulated patient and clinician communicative interaction during interactions under 9 different conditions, including a baseline. We compared the frequencies of the "patterns of ascription" generated by those microdynamics.

We found that positive confirmation (C8) and positive disconfirmation (C7) under credibility deficit -as well as positive confirmation (C4) in the absence of credibility deficit -yielded the best results with respect to matched agreements. While disagreements and mismatches are part of ordinary clinical interactions, reaching a common causal attribution may be beneficial in certain contexts, particularly when developing a shared diagnostic formulation or negotiating treatment decisions. However, such convergence is not necessarily a therapeutic goal in itself. For instance, in acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), patients and clinicians may pursue shared therapeutic goals while maintaining different explanations of psychological distress (Hayes et al., 2006). Similarly, in the management of medically unexplained symptoms and functional disorders, improving functioning and reducing distress may take precedence over reaching explicit agreement on the underlying causes of symptoms (Kirmayer et al., 2004; Henningsen et al., 2018). Moreover, consensus itself may sometimes conceal epistemic injustice (e.g., a patient may adopt the clinician's interpretation of their symptoms because of the clinician's perceived authority, even though their own experience remains insufficiently acknowledged). This is effectively what happens in condition C7, where 99.6% matched agreement is reached due to the fact that the patient's credibility is vitiated. Thus, matched agreement should be understood as an interactional outcome whose clinical and epistemic value depends on the context.

To examine whether situations in which patients are invalidated in their beliefs and experience may pose enhanced risks of epistemic injustice, we explored how disconfirmation would fare under conditions of credibility deficit. Interestingly, there was an inverse relationship between negative and positive disconfirmation depending on whether or not a credibility deficit was at play. The best outcomes in terms of matched agreements, in the absence of credibility deficit, were generated by negative disconfirmation (C2), whereas the best outcomes in matched agreements in the presence of a credibility deficit were produced by positive disconfirmation.

With respect to the risks of testimonial injustice (i.e., risk of category disagreement when invalidating a patient under a credibility deficit), positive disconfirmation was the least risky approach. With respect to risks of hermeneutical injustices (i.e., risks of symptom perception mismatches when invalidating a patient under a credibility deficit), positive disconfirmation was also the least risky approach. With respect to combined risks (i.e., risks of symptom perception mismatches and category disagreements when invalidating a patient under a credibility deficit), positive disconfirmation was also the least risky approach.

The main lesson of our study is that in situations of disagreement in which the patient faces a credibility deficit, the clinical approach that minimizes the risks of causing epistemic harm while promoting matched agreement with the patient is that of positive disconfirmation (e.g., "No, it is A that usually causes anxiety") rather than that of negative disconfirmation (e.g., "No, B does not usually cause anxiety"). This interactional effect was revealed by a simple approximate Bayesian update rules in the context of a computer simulation. Of course, its credibility depends on the adequacy of our model of looping microdynamics and belief formation under active inference.

5.2 Limitations

This work has several technical, conceptual, and clinical limitations. It relies on a (simplified) active inference architecture with a restricted state-space. These choices favor transparency and analytical control, to isolate and examine the microdynamics of looping effects without mixing them with higher order sources of variability. Conceptually, the model presupposes a set of candidate causes (A, B) and treats symptoms (and signs) as observable cues probabilistically related to those causes. But this does not imply an essentialist view of psychiatric disorders. Symptoms are understood as any expressed or observed indicators that may support a diagnostic interpretation, which allows the model to remain compatible with non-nosological but pluralistic accounts of psychiatric classification (even though the space of causes is fixed). Clinically, the model should be understood as a proof of concept rather than a realistic simulation of clinical encounters. By abstracting away from unexpressed symptoms and individual cognitive biases, we can clarify how looping effects can arise from minimal interactional structures, before reintroducing additional layers of clinical complexity in potential subsequent works. Additionally, people may hold multiple attributions with different levels of commitment according to past illness experience and symptoms models (Kirmayer, 2026; Kirmayer et al., 1994; Kirmayer & Robbins, 1991). It is not clear that our current description of attribution (e.g., P(sad | A) vs. P(sad | B)) is sufficient to capture such multiple attributions. Models representing attribution with tensors may be suitable for achieving this (e.g., P(sad | A,B). 6 CONCLUSION As Hacking emphasized, classifications in the human sciences can interact with the people classified, thereby reshaping self-understandings, behaviors, and subsequent classifications through looping effects. We illustrated how, at the microdynamics level, such looping effects shape clinical interactions. Our simulations made these looping dynamics explicit by modeling how clinicians' responses, interpretive stances, and diagnostic framings feed back into patients' perceived symptoms, causal attributions, and self-ascription.

Although a computational model could never capture all the complexity of looping dynamics, it can capture some of their foundational principles, allowing us to explore how small, systematic differences in interaction parameters can generate qualitatively different epistemic outcomes over time. These outcomes can then inform our intuitions about the driving forces of the modelled system. Indeed, our approach to looping microdynamics supports the intuition that adopting a positive approach -when disconfirming during medical listening -is likely beneficial in clinical exchanges, particularly when patients face credibility deficits. Listening is central to the relational logic of medical consultation and involves active engagement to elicit symptom and illness narratives (Keller & Carroll, 1994).

This can be done by restating, paraphrasing and summarizing the patient's discourse (Culley & Bond, 2011). Active listening involves producing cues that indicate when the clinician is paying greater attention to the details of the patient's story (e.g., body language, condition related worries that may be raised, refusal to provide further details, etc.).

Listening to the implicit assumptions of one's own diagnostic line of inquiry while understanding how it may influence the patient's response provides a way to connect with the patient and recognize their concerns. This points to the larger issue that assessment is not just about diagnosis but establishing an alliance and understanding the patient's predicament.

This can be done by deploying specific patterns of listening, which may result in listening trajectories that depart from the expected consultation trajectory (Cocksedge & May, 2005).

Narrative approaches in clinical care emphasize listening to the broader context of patients' experience in terms of their autobiography, lifeworld, and current concerns (Clark & Mishler, 1992; Kirmayer et al., 2025). This may provide rich resources for reframing illness explanations in ways that are epistemically compelling and resonant for patients.

Active listening in clinical interaction must also lead to finding a diagnosis that is consistent with clinical knowledge and that reflects the patient's experience. This involves confirming and disconfirming aspects of patients' interpretation of events, synthesizing information by linking symptoms to diagnostic categories, discussing the diagnosis transparently, in a collaborative process in which the clinician is flexible and able to re-evaluate and adjust the formulation based on new information. This interaction can ensure that the diagnosis reflects both clinical observations and the patient's lived experience. In situations of credibility deficit, additional attention should be paid to the patterns of listening and responding being deployed, as some strategies may pose enhanced risks of epistemic harm, and may lead to a failure to remain responsive to the patient's experience.
