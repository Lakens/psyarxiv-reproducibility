Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Chemists use verbal and visual representations of molecules to communicate and solve problems, yet little is known about how chemists efficiently interpret them simultaneously with ease. Participants (N = 200) of varied chemistry expertise completed an experiment in which they solved short representation-matching tasks with bond-line structures and IUPAC names of organic molecules. Drawing upon information-processing theory and prior chemistry education research on representational processing efficiency, we examined how expertise and varied task characteristics affected visual scanning efficiency. We recorded participants' eye movements during the tasks to quantify the extent to which their gaze was focused or exploratory using a metric called stationary gaze entropy. Chemistry experts exhibited more focused gaze within the bondline structures presented, which suggests that they more efficiently apply more chunk stimuli and activate prior knowledge connecting the representations. By looking at the effects of varied structural features and complexity, we outline inadvertent challenges that chemistry novices may face while processing these representations during chemistry instruction. We offer chemistry instructors suggestions for mitigating demands on their students' working memory. We also propose avenues for follow-up research that will allow chemistry instructors to better understand how to scaffold their students' development of the implicit skills associated with chemistry expertise.

Graphical Abstract Introduction

Chemists regularly use multiple representations, like bond-line structures and disciplinary vocabulary, to communicate and reason about phenomena that are unobservable to the naked eye. 1, 2 These representations are often likened to a foreign language. [3] [4] [5] In organic chemistry, bond-line diagrams of molecules are used ubiquitously to communicate the structure and reactivity of molecules. The challenges students face and the strategies they take to overcome them have been documented widely for translating among chemistry's various visual representations. [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] It is difficult for students to navigate learning environments with multiple representations because they generally struggle to translate among them, 8, 10 even in the most basic cases of connecting a molecule's structure to its name. Although learning modules have been developed to help students understand and translate between names (designated by the IUPAC system) and structures (shown as bond-line diagrams), 11- 13 less is known about the cognitive processes involved in encountering and using these visual and verbal representations during chemistry instruction.

During science teaching, other modes of communication including gestures, speech, and writing, provide important context for the interpretation of visual representations. [14] [15] [16] Because the fluency at which students connect meaning between these modes is integral to the development of scientific expertise, 14 we argue that multimodal processing may play an important yet little understood role in chemistry expertise. Diagrammatic representations of molecules can be traced back to the nineteenth century, 17 and so these visuals were not designed with an explicit understanding of how the brain processes them. 18 The analysis of how the properties of these visuals affect encoding may hence reveal inadvertent challenges that students face in the classroom or chemists face during their practice. Multiple representations could overload learners' cognitive resources if they are interpreted as isolated ideas rather than used in tandem during problem-solving. [19] [20] [21] Ganescu & Bongers -Draft Manuscript V.2026-10-28 pg. 2 Prior work on multiple representations has distinguished between sense-making skills and perceptual fluency as aspects of a learner's representational competence. [21] [22] [23] [24] In the present study, we adopt the term representational processing efficiency to describe the ease with which learners encode, coordinate, and retrieve information from disciplinary representations during task performance. Sense-making skills describe one's knowledge of a representation's meaning, purpose, and relationship to other representations, and have been explored in depth in chemistry education research. Herein, we investigate multimodal processing of simple chemical representations across a range of chemistry expertise. We used a combined word-picture verification and difference recognition task paradigm in which participants were required to choose which of two presented chemical structures matched the presented chemical name. Representational Processing Efficiency and Information-Processing Theory

Information-processing theory is used to model how the brain stores, reasons with, and retrieves information. 30 This framework has allowed for seminal discoveries regarding how humans solve problems and experience cognitive load, 31, 32 which have informed many innovations in chemistry education. [33] [34] [35] In terms of information-processing theory, processing efficiency is characterized by encoding larger perceptual chunks and filtering attention to focus on relevant features of stimuli. [36] [37] [38] [39] [40] Chunking is the implicit process by which stimulus features are grouped together in working memory, which frees up working memory for higher-order tasks. [40] [41] [42] Chunking efficiency is affected by both top-down and bottom-up processes. 43 The chunking ability gained through repeated exposure to stimuli is considered a top-down effect. 43 Top-down processes describe how prior experience and expectations modify one's approach to perceiving stimuli. By contrast, bottom-up processes describe how characteristics of the stimulus influence perception directly. 44 Investigated in terms of Gestalt psychology, 45, 46 spatial grouping of components and symmetry of domain-general stimuli have marked effects on chunking ability. [47] [48] [49] [50] [51] [52] [53] [54] Chemists seamlessly view and generate visual representations while reading, speaking, or hearing these terms during disciplinary discourse. 3, 14 This skill, while underexplored, may be vital for chemistry students to develop so they may communicate and reason about chemical phenomena. Recently, Braun and colleagues examined chemistry students' ability to efficiently detect resonance in chemical structures. Using structures with systematically varied features and students' accuracy and response times on the tasks, they found that many students quickly detect resonance in simpler structural motifs like allylic carbocations, suggesting that these students are implicitly detecting resonance from a structural pattern rather than reasoning about resonance from first principles. The authors argue that students may face drastically different challenges pertaining to detecting resonance.

Using Eye Movements to Study Chemistry Learning

Eye movement data are used to examine the neurocognitive processes surrounding perception, memory, and problemsolving. 44, [55] [56] [57] The eye-mind hypothesis posits that eye movements are a window into understanding cognitive processes. 58 Eye movements are behavioral indices of processes that govern attention towards relevant information while scanning complex visual stimuli. Sustained gaze towards specific areas in stimuli, called fixations, reveal what and to what extent information in these stimuli is attended to. 56 Like perception, visual attention is affected by both top-down and bottom-up processes. 44, 59, 60 For complex or goal-driven tasks, top-down processes bias visual attention toward task-relevant locations on a stimulus. By contrast, in free-viewing or memory tasks, bottom-up processes bias visual attention toward regions of visual salience. 44, [61] [62] [63] Prior research has used eye movements to examine students' problem-solving strategies based on attention allocation and scan paths. For example, researchers have investigated how students solve chemistry word problems, [64] [65] [66] problems involving spectral data or assignment of absolute configuration, [67] [68] [69] [70] and case comparison tasks. 71 Locations of sequential fixations or the ratio between fixations and transitions can be indicative of expert-like approaches to solving chemistry problems. 67, 71 Other eye-tracking metrics, like fixation disparity, have been used to quantify visuospatial cognition while viewing visual representations of molecules. 72 Notably, Braun and colleagues revealed that attention allocation relates to students' drawing of productive vs. unproductive resonance structures. In their study, participants who made unproductive resonance structures spent more time attending to unrelated structural features in previous resonance structures. The authors argue that students' visual selection of relevant features of molecules when reasoning about resonance are vital to their development of representational competence. 73 Additionally, related research by Williamson and colleagues found that visual attention to disciplinary stimuli may be context dependent. In their study, students spent more attention on provided ball-and-stick representations compared to electrostatic potential when students were provided different prompts like determining the site of nucleophilic attack in the molecule. 74 Few chemistry education research studies have explicitly examined the efficiency of information-processing of chemical representations, though. 22 Disciplinary experts have several perceptual advantages in their processing of discipline-specific stimuli. 29, 36, 75, 76 Foundational eye-tracking research was done by psycholinguists to outline the perceptual advantages and eye movement patterns of skilled readers. 58, [77] [78] [79] Countless research articles in this discipline have used eye movements to study the information-processing underlying word recognition, sentence comprehension, and reading passages, 78, [80] [81] [82] which have built the basis for the eye-mind hypothesis. 58 Two examples of these advantages, as discovered by research on reading, are: (i) With more experience viewing a type of stimulus, readers or other disciplinary experts develop a wider perceptual span, which describes the area at which they can peripherally encode information on a stimulus from a single fixation. 75, 78, 83 (ii) Fluency in a language is associated with efficient retrieval of semantic representations between words and their picture referents, which manifests as faster fixations toward matching pictures in specific experimental paradigms. 84, 85 In terms of informationprocessing theory, advantage (i) relates to the encoding of stimuli in working memory, and advantage (ii) relates to the retrieval of multimodal semantic representations from long-term memory and their effects on visual attention. We argue that the visual processing of bond-line structures of organic molecules may come with similar expertise advantages.

Present Study

In this study, we examined stationary gaze entropy (Hs) for capturing the spatial allocation of attention to visual diagrams of molecules, which are two-dimensional and modular. Stationary gaze entropy quantifies the extent to which gaze is dispersed across areas of a stimulus, 44, 64 and was chosen because it aligned with established theory, precedent, and our context. 86 Stationary gaze entropy has been used extensively to study the processing efficiency of complex visual environments and how visual attention is affected by top-down and bottom-up processes. 44 For goal-driven tasks, stationary gaze entropy is negatively correlated with top-down gaze control. 44 That is, the more focused one's gaze is on a stimulus, the more they are affected by top-down processes due to repeated exposure to the same type of stimulus. Research has investigated how stimulus complexity, 87, 88 individual variations like emotional context or sleep deprivation, [89] [90] [91] and task difficulty 92 affect the extent to which top-down processes bias visual attention. This metric has also been used to investigate the relationship between expertise and gaze behavior in surgical and air traffic control simulations, [92] [93] [94] video games, 93 and chemistry problem solving. 64 Little is known about how visual representations in chemistry are processed, let alone in multimodal environments with multiple different types of representations. 21 We designed an experiment to study the top-down processes involved in visual scanning of stimuli that included visual representations (bond-line structures) and verbal representations (IUPAC names) of organic molecules. Our study addresses the following research questions:

RQ1. How does chemistry expertise influence the extent to which visual attention is focused on specific components of visual and verbal modes of chemistry representations during a representation-matching task? RQ2.

To what extent do features of the stimuli and/or multimodal cue affect the dispersion of visual attention during this task, and how do these effects differ between experts and novices?

Via chunking, experts could encode the entire representation in working memory with fewer distinct fixations. This is expected to be influenced by various features of the stimuli including complexity, aspects of the chemical structure, or differences between the presented structures. Similarly, the retrieval of long-term semantic knowledge may be efficient enough to directly affect visual attention. This retrieval has been observed to occur implicitly during name-picture matching tasks. 84, 85 Hence, we expect chemistry experts to focus their attention on relevant portions of each representation in a task in which they must match a bond-line structure and IUPAC name. We refer to this bidirectional effect as multimodal cueing.

Methods

Context and Participants

Prior to data collection, we received ethics approval from our university's institutional review board to run this study. Recruitment and data collection occurred at a Canadian national chemistry conference and at our university. We set up a data collection booth at which potential participants could review the study details and decide whether to take part. Our booth was located at high foot traffic spots at both sites. Conference attendees and university students, faculty, and staff (N = 200) volunteered to participate in the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation in the study took 5-10 minutes, and we did not provide compensation to participants. Participants filled out a brief questionnaire that asked them to report their current role (faculty, post-doctoral fellow, graduate student, undergraduate student, or staff member), area of study (chemistry, biology, physics, etc.), and undergraduate chemistry courses taken, if the participant was an undergraduate student. We used participants' answers to this questionnaire to assign them one of four groups of expertise (Table 1). We use these groups to roughly model participants' experience processing chemistry representations. Group 1 contains participants with the most experience (chemistry faculty, post-doctoral fellows, and graduate students), and Group 3 contains participants with the least experience (undergraduate students currently in first-or second-year chemistry courses). Based on the information collected in the questionnaire, we could not classify participants who were either staff members or undergraduate students who have not taken any chemistry courses at our university into these groups. These participants are represented in Group 4. We did not collect any other demographic information like ethnicity or gender because they are not the focus of our research questions, and we wanted to minimize risks of data reidentification.

Experiment Design

Participants completed a series of 36 randomized trials in which they viewed a pair of organic molecules and an IUPAC name (Figure 2). For each trial, participants had a maximum of 15 seconds to select which organic molecule matched the IUPAC name. A fixation cross was displayed for 500 ms to center participants' gaze prior to each trial.

The task used in our experiment is a blend of two experimental paradigms from cognitive psychology: word-picture verification tasks and difference recognition tasks. In word-picture verification tasks, participants are presented with an image and word (e.g., an image of a fish and the word "fish") and must decide if they match. This paradigm is used to study how semantic representations are activated from long-term memory during multimodal processing. [95] [96] [97] [98] In difference recognition tasks, participants must locate the difference between a pair of images. This paradigm is used to study visual scanning efficiency and its interaction with disciplinary expertise for domain-specific stimuli. 28, 29 The target (correct organic molecule) and foil (incorrect organic molecule) differed by one structural motif or feature. Recognizing the visually salient difference between the two organic molecules and determining how the IUPAC name may resolve that difference hence play an integral role in the task, incorporating ideas from both experimental paradigms.

Apparatus and Data Preprocessing

Eye movements during the experiment were recorded with a Tobii Pro Spectrum 300 eye-tracker. This device is attached to a 23.8" monitor with a resolution of 1920 x 1080 pixels. Participants were seated 60-65 cm away from the monitor in a dim room. The eye-tracker was calibrated for each participant using a nine-point calibration procedure. The position of the participant's gaze on the monitor is recorded by the eye-tracker at a sampling rate of 300 Hz. The Tobii Pro Velocity- pg. 6 Threshold Identification Gaze Filter was used to preprocess the eye-tracking data. This algorithm classifies eye movements as fixations or saccades if movements in gaze position are slower or faster than 30 °/s, respectively. Visual angle degrees (°) are calculated based on the distance of the participant from the monitor and the monitor's size. Gaze position measurements from the left and right eye were averaged during data preprocessing. Data was excluded from our analysis for participants with poor calibration accuracy (above 0.5°) and participants who had at least one trial with high data loss (above 20%). 99 The final sample included in our analysis contained 132 participants.

Discussion

Using an experiment that prompted participants to choose the bond-line structure that matched a provided IUPAC name, we investigated how top-down processes like visual chunking and multimodal cueing affected gaze behavior on each type of representation. We used stationary gaze entropy, a metric calculated from the eye movements we captured during the experiment, to examine how participants' experience in chemistry and varied characteristics of the tasks modified the role of these top-down processes. To answer our first research question, we compared the stationary gaze entropy of participants based on their self-reported chemistry expertise. To answer our second research question, we varied the characteristics of the trials based on whether molecules were cyclic or acyclic (target type), the size of the molecules (complexity), and the type of visually salient difference between the two molecules (foil type).

1. Experts sample less information within the structures but not within the names.

We found that participants with more chemistry expertise generally exhibited more focused attention within the bond-line structures. While our analysis does not reveal whether these participants focused on more relevant portions of the stimuli, they generally sampled less information across the bond-line structures, which is associated with top-down gaze control. 44 Based on prior research, it is likely that experts focus their attention towards task-relevant features of disciplinary stimuli. 36, 66, 69 Most of the information in the bond-line structures is redundant, and experts have been found to ignore provided information that is not useful for solving chemistry tasks. 64 We found that accuracy per trial was only weakly correlated with focused gaze behavior, which suggests that sense-making skills and representational processing efficiency play distinct roles in the tasks. 22, 23 That is, being able to accurately match bond-line structures with IUPAC names does not necessarily mean one completes the task efficiently.

By contrast, we found no evidence that the extent to which attention was dispersed across components of the IUPAC names differed for experts and novices. The intrinsic segmentation of morphemes in the IUPAC names (either phonetically or visually with dashes) may allow for domain-general chunking via bottom-up means. 82, 104, 105 Even though the name is read with our eyes, the encoding of the IUPAC names occurs verbally in the phonological loop, 106, 107 which may not affect gaze behavior in the same way as visual chunking. Further, compared to the atoms of the bond-line structures, attention was generally more evenly distributed across components of the IUPAC names. This may be because the name is generally read left-to-right, whereas there is no constrained sequence for scanning the bond-line structures. Participants may need to sample the entire name in this manner before comparing it to the bond-line structures. It is also possible that this experiment cannot elicit topdown effects on the visual scanning of IUPAC names.

2. Chunking is a strategy to mitigate working memory demands under specific circumstances.

Our target type conditions isolated the observed effects to top-down effects of visual chunking. This is because the content and task demand of the IUPAC names between these trial types only differed in the presence of cyclo for the cyclic targets. As such, we suggest that any effects due to cueing from the IUPAC name should be the same across cyclic and acyclic target types. We found that gaze across atoms in the structures was more focused during cyclic trials, but upon further investigation, we only observed this effect for participants with higher expertise. Contrary to our hypotheses that bottom-up facilitation of chunking would occur similarly for all participants due to Gestalt principles like grouping, [46] [47] [48] this suggests that these features of cyclic molecules may not govern experts' more focused attention within the cyclic molecules. This also contrasts work by Stieff and colleagues that investigated chemistry novices' encoding of ball-and-stick models in a change detection task. They found that novice chemists leveraged spatial groupings in the stimuli during encoding that corresponded to expert chunks. 40 We considered that novice participants may always engage in the exploratory scanning associated with dispersed gaze behavior, 44 which could be due to uncertainty during the task and lack of sense-making skills. These participants' visual scanning was sensitive to the other manipulations of task characteristics, though, so we argue that students may face inadvertent challenges working with visual representations containing cyclic molecules. Chemistry instructors may overlook this challenge while highlighting structural information in a cyclic molecule or asking their students to copy down a mechanism containing cyclic molecules, both of which require the student to encode the visual in working memory.

We found that regardless of expertise, participants exhibited highly dispersed viewing behavior across the bond-line structures for trials in which the visually salient cue was the misplacement of a parent functional group or substituent. We had anticipated that experts may have experience counting carbon atoms on organic molecules and would still exhibit more focused viewing relative to novices. This skill may hence not be a component of their representational processing efficiency, perhaps due to limited repeated exposure to this type of task. It is also possible that counting does not demand much of visual working memory, as chunking strategies are typically employed implicitly to lessen load working memory during difference recognition tasks. 28

3. The extent to which multimodal cueing affects visual attention may depend on how the cue is delivered.

As the bond-line structures became larger and IUPAC names became longer, participants exhibited more exploratory gaze behavior, which is consistent with prior reports of stationary gaze entropy across tasks of varied complexity and difficulty. 87, 88, 92, 108 However, we note that our entropy values were normalized across conditions based on the total number of areas of interest, 44, 64 which we anticipated to have a different relationship with stimulus complexity. The visually salient difference between structures was generated in the same way for each complexity level. For each trial, increasing stimulus complexity made structures longer or introduced additional functionalities that were common to both structures presented.

As such, the added information is redundant and not directly relevant for distinguishing the structures based on the IUPAC name. The observed effect may hence be due to exploratory viewing resulting from poor cueing from the IUPAC name. As the name becomes longer, the cue distinguishing the correct and incorrect structure becomes less apparent, which doesn't allow for multimodal cueing to filter attention towards specific structural features.

Our variation of the foil type modified the type of visually salient difference between the structures and the corresponding cue in the IUPAC name that distinguished them. Because the type of difference could have marked effects on its recognition, 29 pg. 12 we compared trials in which the incorrect structure either had an extra group or was missing a group. We found that participants exhibited more exploratory viewing substantially more for the former condition, which we argue is due to the lack of a direct cue in the IUPAC name (i.e., there is nothing in the IUPAC name for this condition indicating that the group is not meant to be there). We also found that viewing was generally exploratory across trials of all complexity for this condition, which suggests that without the presence of the cue buried in the name, the main effect of complexity is less dramatic.

4. Attention filtering of verbal information may be affected differently by top-down processes.

By contrast to our observations for attention on the bond-line structures, participants attended to components of the IUPAC names more evenly for low complexity stimuli compared to medium or high complexity stimuli. Because this also contrasts the related research on visual scene complexity, 87, 88 we suggest that attention allocation on the names may be affected by different processes than that of the structures. Participants may have had enough space in working memory to encode the entire IUPAC name (by reading it left-to-right) before comparing it to the bond-line structures. As the names became longer, though, participants may have implicitly began to skim or chunk multiple components together to avoid cognitive overload. 28 The effects of varied foil types on attention allocation within the IUPAC names were like the effects described for the bondline structures. The way that the cue in the name is delivered (as varied by foil type) may hence also affect visual attention within the name. If a participant is searching for clues to help with their solving of the task, and does not find one (e.g., in the extra group condition), they may continue viewing the name exploratively.

Implications

Implications for Teaching

We frame these implications in terms of some of the inadvertent challenges that students may face while processing multiple modes of representations during chemistry instruction. Foremost, our findings suggest that chemistry novices are inefficient in their encoding visual representations and translating between verbal and visual representations of organic molecules. Perceptual learning activities embedded in course designs could address this issue, 23, [110] [111] [112] [113] and analogous activities for organic nomenclature may be adapted from previous work. [11] [12] [13] Since students may face these processing demands later in their organic chemistry courses, maintenance of fluency beyond that is encountered in the typical introductory nomenclature unit would be particularly useful. We also revealed that chemistry novices may encounter difficulties viewing cyclic molecules, even though they feature domain-general spatial grouping. Because the chemistry experts in our study exhibited visual-cognitive advantages over novices while viewing cyclic molecules, this challenge to learners may be especially easy for chemistry instructors to overlook. Instructors should aim to be conscious of how much time is provided for students to view and/or write down visual representations during instruction or assessment. That the relevant information in increasingly complex stimuli is difficult for both novices and experts to discern supports the practice of introducing representation types gradually while explicitly explaining their purposes and connections. 35 Further, effective signaling strategies (i.e., signaling students' attention to specific features of representations during instruction) used with complex representations may similarly help students build representational processing efficiency. 99, 114, 115 Implications for Research Our work builds upon research that has investigated how attention is allocated to visual representations during chemistry problem solving and the importance of representational processing efficiency in chemistry learning. [21] [22] [23] 69, 73 The analysis of gaze entropy may be applied to examine the efficacy of fluency-building activities in chemistry in a pre/post manner. 23 The experiment design in our study provides one means for doing so, but it could be adapted to include other types of visual representations and prompts. For example, varying the task can drastically affect students' visual attention to representations, 74 which may reflect the cognitive resources that are imposing top-down control over gaze.

Prior work has found that chemistry students may detect implicit information in bond-line structures more easily for certain substrates, and that this skill may vary substantially across students. 22 In our experiment, participants viewed bond-line structures while matching them to an IUPAC name, which meant activating long-term knowledge about the relationships between these visual and verbal representations. Chemistry experts more efficiently processed information during the tasks, and our analysis showed the various ways in which visual chunking and multimodal cueing allowed for these informationprocessing advantages. Building off of this work and a report of chemistry novices' ability to use expert chunks without having chemistry expertise, 40 we suggest that future research explore how the morphology of bond-line structures may make them easier or harder to chunk by chemistry novices (i.e., via bottom-up processes). Our findings suggest that information presented in cyclic molecules may not be easier for chemistry novices to process, despite the inherent spatial grouping. Future research should reproduce this finding and explore how other features common to bond-line structure (e.g., conjugation, heteroatoms, stereochemistry, and various functional groups) may affect encoding efficiency.

More generally, our work substantiates the need for approaches to teaching that consider students' cognitive load while learning in multimedia environments. 20, 21 It is for chemistry students to translate between different representations, 10 and the Ganescu & Bongers -Draft Manuscript V.2026-10-28 pg. 13 chemistry novices faced similar challenges in our experiment. Hence, we suggest that future chemistry education research explore how teaching practices recommended by the multimedia learning theory, like signaling, may help build chemistry students' representational processing efficiency. 20, 99

Limitations

We note several limitations of the present study. Our interpretations of eye movements are limited by the design of our experiment, which explored how chunking and multimodal cueing affected gaze behavior. Because our experiment combined a difference recognition task and a word-picture matching task, we cannot isolate the effects of either of these processes. We use our task variations to better interpret each effect independently, but we note that further experimentation is needed to more confirm our findings independently from one another. We did not collect a standardized measurement of sense-making skills in IUPAC naming, so we could not characterize the complex interplay between representational sense-making skills and processing efficiency. We use participants' self-reported expertise to represent their exposure to chemical structures and names (not their sense-making skills). Another limitation of our study is that our interpretations of eye movement data are not cross validated with other sources of data. Retrospective think-alouds can reveal participants' problem-solving strategies during experiments in which eye movements are recorded, 109 which may have allowed us to confirm that participants' viewing strategies were affected by working memory capacity and/or multimodal cueing.

Our analysis did not capture a related eye movement metric called gaze transition entropy, which captures the randomness of transitions between regions of a stimulus. Similarly to stationary gaze entropy, this metric is also associated with top-down gaze control in a variety of tasks. 44 Sequential eye movements may vary substantially based on the task, particularly in chemistry learning environments. Connor and colleagues found that chemistry experts exhibited more unidirectional viewing while comparing spectral data and chemical structures. 67 In terms of gaze entropy, such a pattern of visual scanning would be considered to have low gaze transition entropy and hence be associated with top-down effects (like experience solving similar problems) on gaze. By contrast, comparative viewing behavior may be a marker of expertise for other tasks. 71 Because our tasks were answered quickly, we did not investigate the time-course changes to attention allocation during the trials. Such an analysis may allow researchers to examine how top-down processes affect different stages of problem-solving during chemistry tasks, including familiarization, reasoning, and decision-making. For instance, Rodemer et al. characterized students' viewing of organic chemistry case comparison tasks as focused or comparative based on the ratio of fixations to transitions on the stimuli; they found that viewing generally starts off comparative, becomes focused, and then comparative again leading up to the participant's response. 71

Conclusion

This study explored how chemistry expertise and varied task characteristics influence the processing of visual and verbal chemistry representations. We captured chemistry experts and novices' eye movements while they solved a series of representation-matching tasks involving bond-line structures and IUPAC names of organic molecules. Within each type of representation, we used these eye movements to calculate participants' stationary gaze entropy, which indicates to what extent their attention was dispersed. We found that chemistry experts exhibited focused viewing among atoms of the bond-line structures, which we attributed to the top-down effects on gaze from their repeated exposure to chemistry representations. Repeated exposure is likely to allow chemistry experts to chunk components of the stimuli more efficiently in working memory and more efficiently retrieve semantic representations relating the verbal and visual representations. 21, 39, 85 We discerned between these top-down effects by varying the characteristics of the task, such as whether the molecules were cyclic and how complex the molecules were. Our findings suggest that disciplinary chunking may be especially efficient for cyclic molecules. For some types of trials, expertise had much weaker effects on visual attention, which allowed us to generate potential boundaries for how chemistry experts' representational processing efficiency interacted with the tasks. We also found that conditions in which the relevant cue in the IUPAC name were less explicit were associated with more dispersed visual attention. For instance, participants generally exhibited higher stationary gaze entropy for stimuli containing longer IUPAC names (higher complexity). For these stimuli, the relevant cue is surrounded by more information that does not directly help one solve the task. Thus, the degree to which multimodal cueing affects gaze behavior may be modulated by the accessibility of the cue. Chemistry experts and novices both exhibited relatively exploratory viewing among portions of the IUPAC names. This indicates that top-down processing may affect attention towards visual and verbal representations in distinct ways. Whereas attention within bond-line structures became more exploratory as their size increased, attention within the IUPAC names became more focused for longer names, which may reflect changing scanning strategies or chunking employed to mitigate demands on working memory. Taken together, these findings extend research on representational competence in chemistry education by highlighting the role of representational processing efficiency in processing multiple modes of representations simultaneously. Our analysis reveals the advantages that chemistry experts have during processing and inadvertent challenges that varied task demands may pose to chemistry novices (and occasionally experts). We hope that our work provides a foundation for future research and instructional interventions aimed at building chemistry students' representational competence.
