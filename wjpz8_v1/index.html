Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Full text

Abstract

Practice may improve certain cognitive skills more than others because those skills are called upon more frequently during an activity. Professional esports provides a means of measuring these demands during gameplay because match records capture the information available to players and their responses as events unfold. This study introduces the Telemetry and Replay Analysis of Cognitive Exposure (TRACE) framework, which measures seven dimensions of cognitive demand in professional esports. The analysis used records from 314 professional DOTA 2 matches, covering 558 distinct player accounts and approximately 2,184 player-hours of competition. TRACE defines four measures at the player level (cognitive control capacity, task switching, inhibitory control, and dynamic attention) and three measures at the team level (visuospatial working memory and updating, decision-making under uncertainty, and feedback learning). Hierarchical Bayesian models estimated each dimension in its original units and on a common percentile scale, accounting for differences among teams, matches, and hero-role combinations.

By identifying comparable situations across matches, TRACE can quantify how often players encounter a given demand and how they respond when it arises. This transforms routinely recorded match replays into a source of large-scale behavioral data for cognitive psychology.

Measuring Cognitive Demands in Professional Esports

The cognitive benefits of video game training vary depending on the game played, even when training time is held constant. After 20 hours of practice, for example, the action-game group became more accurate at detecting changes in object orientation. Meanwhile, the hidden-object group searched faster and recalled locations more accurately (Oei & Patterson, 2013). Action game training has been shown to improve visual attention (Bediou et al., 2023; Green & Bavelier, 2003 , 2006), and older adults have demonstrated improvements in working memory after playing strategy or multitasking games (Anguera et al., 2013; Basak et al., 2008). Participants who managed two bases in a strategy game demonstrated greater improvements in cognitive flexibility than participants who played a life simulation game (Glass et al., 2013). These differences may reflect how frequently each game engages particular cognitive skills (Dale et al., 2020; Oei & Patterson, 2013 , 2015). Within a single game, the demands on attention, memory, and decision-making can vary from one situation to the next. In the same visual environment, a change in the immediate goal redirects where people look (Rothkopf et al., 2007; Sullivan et al., 2012). When sorting objects, people retain features relevant to the next step and rely more on memory if they can anticipate which feature they will need (Droll & Hayhoe, 2007; Droll et al., 2005). Similarly, what people learn from an outcome depends on whether they expect earlier experience to be useful (Behrens et al., 2007; Nassar et al., 2010). Linking players' actions to the available information at the time would demonstrate how frequently particular demands arise during practice and how players respond to them.

Professional DOTA 2 play requires split-second decisions and long-term planning under incomplete information (OpenAI et al., 2019), making it ideal for studying how cognitive demands change during play. For example, consider a team deciding whether to start a fight. An opponent visible on the far side of the map poses a different decision problem than an opponent last seen there a minute ago. The opponent may be in the same place in both cases, but the team must act with greater uncertainty in the second case. Match replays make this distinction observable, as researchers can use recorded events to reconstruct player movements and identify encounters between opponents (Drachen et al., 2014; Schubert et al., 2016), as well as determine which opponents were visible to each team at the time (Carvalho et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026).

This study introduces the Telemetry and Replay Analysis of Cognitive Exposure (TRACE)

framework, which measures seven dimensions of cognitive demand at the player and team levels. For each dimension, TRACE defines a recurring situation in terms of observable match events and specifies which aspects can be measured when that situation occurs. Some measures describe the information available to players, while others describe how often players act or the outcomes teams achieve under specific conditions. Hierarchical Bayesian models estimate each measure in its original units and place it on a common percentile scale. Together, these measures transform routine match records into large-scale behavioral data on the cognitive demands that players encounter and how they respond during competition.

Method

Match Corpus and Replay Records

Records from 314 professional DOTA 2 matches covered 558 distinct player accounts and approximately 2,184 player-hours of competition. One hundred and fourteen of these matches were played by 80 players from 16 teams at PGL Wallachia Season 9 under game version 7.41f. This tournament served as the basis for comparing cognitive demands. The remaining 200 matches were sampled by tournament and date from the two preceding game versions (100 from each of versions 7.41d and 7.41e). These matches contributed to the estimation of six of the seven measures; dynamic attention was estimated using only the 114 matches from version 7.41f (Supplementary Table S1).

OpenDota match records identified each player's hero, or the character controlled by the player, and provided an estimate of their role on the team. The five roles are numbered from 1 to 5, in order of typical priority for in-game resources: carry, mid laner, offlaner, soft support, and hard support, respectively. PGL Wallachia Season 9 included 141 unique hero-role combinations. Match records also provided team lineups, fights, kills, and gold gains. Replays supplied player locations, team visibility, health, ability use, and combat events. The two sources were linked by match ID and elapsed game time.

Measurement of Seven Cognitive Demands

Table 1 pairs each cognitive dimension with the corresponding measurement event. The distinction between updating, switching, and inhibition is based on the work of Miyake et al. (2000). The measure of visuospatial working memory and updating is based on the role of working memory in retaining and using information (Baddeley, 1992). Four measures describe an individual player during a match. The other three measures use information or outcomes shared by a team and provide one observation per team per match. Each measure has its own observation time or set of eligible events.

Events with missing required game states were excluded. A measure was missing for a player or team with no eligible events. Information measures use base-two logarithms and are expressed in bits (Shannon, 1948). Supplementary Table S2 provides the event rules and reference distributions.

Location information gain, peripheral cue information, and feedback information gain share a calculation. Each one compares the two probabilities assigned to the actual outcome. The mean gain in bits per scored event is G= 1 where N is the number of scored events for the player or team in that match and i indexes those events.

For event i, p i is the probability of the observed outcome given the additional information, and q i is its probability under the baseline for that measure. A positive value of G means that, on average on the log scale, the additional information improved prediction of the observed outcomes.

Cognitive Control Capacity

As a player moves through the map, nearby ability casts, item uses, abrupt health losses, control onsets, and changes in hero visibility alter the stream of information available to them. The visible event information rate measures how much information this stream carries per second. Events visible within 1,600 game units were counted in 0.5-second bins. A Poisson reference model was used to estimate the probability of each channel count, taking into account the game version, hero, and game phase. Rolespecific rates were adjusted toward that baseline. The sum of the negative log probabilities across channels and bins (including empty bins) was divided by the player's observed time in seconds.

Visuospatial Working Memory and Updating

Even when an opponent disappears from a team's view, its last known position can still help predict where it will reappear. Location information gain measures the contribution of that prior spatial information. For each reappearance, the location distribution based on the last visible coordinates, time out of sight, and base movement speed was compared to the distribution of map locations within the same game version. The use of teleport mixed the map distribution into the conditional prediction. The two probabilities were evaluated at the cell where the opponent reappeared and scored for the observing team.

Task Switching

After a player leaves one lane for another, their opponents may approach the location that they just left. Risk-conditioned switching measures the proportion of confirmed lane switches that are followed by a specified risk at the original location. A switch requires the use of a teleport scroll or Twin Gate after minute 10, as well as displacement of at least 3,000 game units between lanes. The origin was checked at 3, 7, 12, and 20 seconds after the switch. The risk condition required at least two living enemies within 1,800 units or one living enemy with cumulative gold at least 1.25 times the player's. All confirmed switches for that player in the match formed the denominator.

Inhibitory Control

The appearance of a new opponent during an attack gives the player an opportunity to stop attacking and withdraw. An opportunity becomes eligible when a player with at least 60% health attacks a visible enemy within 1,600 game units and a second enemy appears 1-5 seconds later, having been unseen for the preceding 5 seconds. To disengage, the player must move at least 500 units away from the original target within eight seconds of the new arrival. They must also refrain from attacking enemy heroes and survive for 15 seconds. Disengagement probability uses all eligible opportunities for that player in the match as its denominator.

Dynamic Attention

While a player is securing a last hit, an enemy appearing on the periphery can alert them to potential danger. A peripheral cue is an enemy that has been unseen for five seconds and appears one to five seconds after the last hit, at a distance of 1,200 to 2,200 game units. In both cue and no-cue contexts, the outcome was whether the player died within 25 s after the last hit. This comparison accounted for health, game phase, and nearby visible enemies. The peripheral cue information compared the probability of the observed outcome with and without the cue, scoring only eligible cue contexts for that player.

Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

A team may initiate a fight when the locations of the key opponents are unknown. One second before initiation, the two living opponents with the most cumulative gold were identified. Initiations were classified as "informed" when both opponents were visible to the initiating team and "uncertain" when at least one opponent was unseen. An initiation was considered profitable if the initiating team gained more gold than the opposing team during the fight. The initiation profit ratio was calculated by dividing the estimated probability of a profitable outcome under uncertainty by the corresponding probability when informed. The two estimates used separate counts of profitable and eligible initiations for each team.

Feedback Learning

After a team loses a hero in a fight, the role of the first hero lost can inform predictions about later fights. Feedback information gain compares a prediction based on earlier first losses in that match with a baseline estimated from other matches in the same game version. Before each eligible fight, the updated probability is p t ( r )= 30 p 0 ( r )+ n r , t -1 30+ N t -1 , where t indexes eligible fights for the defending team in that match and r denotes a role. p 0 ( r ) is the baseline probability that the first hero lost plays that role.

The constant 30 gives the baseline the weight of 30 prior observations. n r , t -1 counts earlier eligible fights in which the first hero lost played role r, and N t -1 counts all earlier eligible fights for that team. The first fight initialized the counts. For each subsequent fight, p t ( r ) and p 0 ( r ) were evaluated at the role actually lost before that fight was added to the counts.

Statistical Estimation and Model Checks

Descriptive Statistics

Corpus summaries used all eligible observations for each dimension (see Table 2). First, continuous measures were calculated for each player or team in each match. The reported mean and sample standard deviation give these observations equal weight. For task switching and inhibitory control, the overall proportions are calculated by dividing the total number of qualifying responses or outcomes by the total number of eligible opportunities. For decision-making under uncertainty, the proportion of profitable initiations was calculated separately for each information condition and then divided (uncertain versus informed).

Hierarchical Models

A separate hierarchical Bayesian model was used to estimate each of the seven dimensions defined in Table 1. The models for cognitive control capacity, task switching, inhibitory control, and dynamic attention used one observation per eligible player per match. For the dimensions of visuospatial working memory and updating, decision-making under uncertainty, and feedback learning, each eligible team contributed one observation per match. All models included effects for team, match, and hero-role combination. The four player-level models also included player and role effects. For team-level outcomes, the model averaged the hero-role effects of the five players in the lineup. Gaussian random walks allowed the overall level and the hero-role effects to vary across successive game versions.

For cognitive control capacity, the observed information rate was divided by its median, then fitted with a gamma likelihood and log link. The shape parameter was proportional to the match duration, which gave longer observations greater precision at the same expected rate.

For visuospatial working memory and updating, dynamic attention, and feedback learning, the observed quantity was the average information gain across eligible events in a match. Each measure was centered at its median and divided by its standard deviation before fitting. The model for visuospatial working memory and updating employed a Student's t-distribution with four fixed degrees of freedom.

This specification gives extreme observations less influence on the estimated mean than a normal likelihood does, retains finite variance, and requires no separate estimate of tail thickness. For dynamic attention and feedback learning, a mixture of two normal distributions accommodated occasional high values while preserving the predicted mean. In all three models, the base residual scale decreased as the number of eligible events increased (see Supplementary Table S3).

For task switching, the numerator was the number of lane switches followed by risk at the original location. The denominator was the total number of confirmed switches. For inhibitory control, the numerator was the number of withdrawals, while the denominator was all eligible new threat opportunities. Beta-binomial likelihoods retained these denominators and permitted response probabilities to fluctuate beyond the scope of ordinary binomial variation. For decision-making under uncertainty, two beta-binomial components estimated the probability of profitable initiation under uncertain and informed conditions; each component used its own eligible initiations. The reported probability ratio was obtained by dividing the former probability by the latter. Supplementary Table S4 reports sampling diagnostics and posterior predictive checks.

To decide whether to include earlier game versions, two models were fitted to the first 25 matches of version 7.41f for each dimension: one with the 200 matches from the two earlier versions and one without. Then, predictive log densities were compared on the remaining 89 matches. The earlier versions were retained if the upper bound of the 95% match-bootstrap interval for the mean difference (with and without the earlier versions) was at least zero. This rule retained the earlier versions for six dimensions. The final dynamic attention model used the 114 matches from version 7.41f. For the four dimensions measured in bits per event or bits per second, reference probabilities were calculated from other matches across the full window for each game version. The comparison for these dimensions was conditional on those reference probabilities (see Supplementary Table S5).

Posterior Profiles and Common Reference

In version 7.41f, estimates for each hero-role combination set team, match, and player effects to zero, where applicable. For team-level dimensions, the estimates are averaged over observed lineups containing that combination. Player and team profiles retained the contexts in which they actually competed. Within each posterior draw, the estimates were weighted by match duration for cognitive control capacity and by eligible event counts for the other dimensions. For decision-making under uncertainty, the two probabilities of profitable initiation were aggregated separately before taking their ratio (see Supplementary Table S3).

The reference consisted of the 141 hero-role combinations observed in version 7.41f, each of which was given equal weight. Within each joint posterior draw, the percentile was defined as the percentage of reference estimates that fell below the estimate of interest. Ties were assigned half weight.

The reported percentile is the mean of these draw-level percentiles. Estimates in the original units are posterior medians. For each scale, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the posterior draws define the 95% posterior interval.

To summarize the variation among the reference values, the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the 141 modeled values were calculated in their original units within each posterior draw. Each quartile is reported as the median across draws, with a 95% posterior interval. This summary includes all 141 combinations, even those for which an estimate depended on the hierarchical model because the combination had no eligible events in the target version.

Computation and Model Checks

The models were fitted in PyMC 5.28.5 (Abril-Pla et al., 2023) using the No-U-Turn sampler (Hoffman & Gelman, 2014) in nutpie 0.16.11. Each of the four chains underwent 2,500 warm-up iterations, followed by 4,000 retained draws, targeting a .995 acceptance probability. ArviZ 0.23.4 (Kumar et al., 2019) provided the rank-normalized R-hat statistic and the bulk and tail effective sample sizes (Vehtari et al., 2021). The study required a maximum R-hat of 1.01 or lower and minimum bulk and tail effective sample sizes of 400 or higher with zero divergent transitions.

Posterior predictive checks compared the fitted observations with replicated data drawn from the posterior predictive distribution (Gelman et al., 1996). Two sets of counts were contributed to decisionmaking under uncertainty: one for informed initiations and one for uncertain initiations. This yielded eight observed outcomes across the seven models. For each outcome, the observed mean was compared with the central 95% interval of simulated means. The second check required that at least 90% of the observations fall within their respective 95% posterior predictive intervals. Both checks used the scale on which the outcome was fitted, including event counts for the beta-binomial models (see Supplementary Table S4).

The task-selection rules in Supplementary Table S6 use estimated profiles to match players to targets along one dimension, while accounting for other dimensions, estimation uncertainty, prior experience, and five-player roster constraints.

Results

Cognitive Demands Across Professional Matches

For cognitive control capacity, visible events carried an average of 4.01 bits/s (SD = 0.65) across 3,140 player-match observations (Figure 1). For task switching, the original location met the risk criterion after 719 out of 6,450 confirmed lane switches (11.1%). For inhibitory control, players withdrew on 3,182 out of 16,673 eligible occasions when a new opponent appeared during an attack (19.1%). For dynamic attention, the information that a peripheral cue provided about subsequent death or survival averaged 0.063 bits per cue context (SD = 0.321) across 1,001 player-match observations in version 7.41f. Table 2 provides the sample size and denominator for each dimension.

For visuospatial working memory and updating, the average location information gain was 2.61 bits per reappearance (SD = 0.25) across 628 team-match observations. This gain describes the improvement in predicting where an opponent would reappear based on the last known position and time out of view. Regarding decision-making under uncertainty, 570 out of 1,071 initiations were profitable when at least one of the two highest-gold living opponents was out of view (53.2%), compared to 159 out of 315 when both were visible (50.5%). The observed probability ratio was 1.05. For feedback learning, using earlier fights to predict the role of the first hero lost by the defending team in the next fight yielded a mean gain of 0.0064 bits per feedback event (SD = 0.0739) across 581 team-match observations.

Variation in Cognitive Demands Within the Target Game Version

Version 7.41f included 114 matches, 80 players, 16 teams, and 141 unique hero-role combinations. The middle half of the modeled values for cognitive control capacity across these combinations ranged from 3.67 to 4.03 bits/s. For inhibitory control, the corresponding range was .160 to .226 for the probability of withdrawal when a new opponent appeared. Figure 2 shows the three quartiles and their 95% posterior intervals in the original units for each dimension.

For decision-making under uncertainty, the median ratio of profitable initiation probabilities across these combinations was 1.05 (95% posterior interval [0.92, 1.23]). The estimated 25th and 75th percentiles were 1.00 and 1.11, respectively. A ratio of 1 indicates the same probability of profitable initiation under uncertain and informed conditions. Supplementary Figure S1 illustrates how to use a profile to select a practice task.

Posterior Predictive Checks for the Seven Cognitive Dimensions

Each model used one observation per eligible player or team in a match (see Figure 3). Decisionmaking under uncertainty provided separate counts of profitable initiations under informed and uncertain conditions, resulting in eight observed mean outcomes across the seven models. Checks on the fitted data revealed that all eight observed means fell within the corresponding 95% intervals for means from posterior predictive replicates. Across the eight outcomes, 96.9%-100% of observations fell within their respective 95% posterior predictive intervals. All seven models met the sampling criteria (see Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

The match records revealed the available information during play, how frequently players performed a specific action when the opportunity arose, and the outcomes teams achieved under various information conditions. Estimated profiles varied across hero-role combinations within a single game version, demonstrating that players competing under the same rules experienced different patterns of demand.

Match replays help explain the cognitive demands of an action by showing what the player was

doing and what information was available at the time. Inhibitory control is one example. When a previously unseen opponent appeared during an attack, the player had the opportunity to stop an action that was already in progress. Players stopped attacking and withdrew on 3,182 of the 16,673 eligible occasions (19.1%). Distinguishing between occasions when players encountered a new threat and occasions when they withdrew when one appeared is important. Task context plays a similar role in studies of visual attention, in which people look at different objects in the same visual environment depending on their immediate goal (Rothkopf et al., 2007).

Differences in cognitive demands within the same game enable researchers to compare the opportunities players have to practice under the same rules and with the same objective. In DOTA 2, for example, estimated demand profiles varied across hero-role combinations within the same game version (see Figure 2). Therefore, two players could spend the same amount of time in DOTA 2 yet face different decisions repeatedly. This distinction is important when a study uses playing time to determine how much practice participants have received. An hour in which a particular decision arises repeatedly and an hour in which it never arises count equally toward total playing time. By counting the number of times each participant faced a particular decision, researchers can compare how much opportunity each participant had to practice making that decision.

The extent to which practice in a game transfers to an untrained task may depend on how closely the cognitive demands of the two activities align. Oei and Patterson (2015) classified four action games according to their perceptual and attentional demands. They then examined how performance on related tasks changed after 20 hours of training. Participants who trained on the game requiring the fastest shifts of attention improved at detecting a second target presented shortly after the first. A DOTA 2 study could use TRACE profiles to select practice tasks that differ in visible event information rate. Players would be randomly assigned to these tasks for the same number of hours, and their replays would provide an estimate of the actual information rate encountered by each player. Then, an independent test of cognitive control capacity, administered before and after training, could establish whether higher information rates during practice predict greater improvement on the test.

Limitations

The reference distribution is based on 114 professional matches played in version 7.41f. Players with different skill levels or playing other versions of the game may encounter different situations or respond to them differently. Therefore, the same measured value could correspond to a different percentile in those settings. Additionally, the study lacked independent cognitive test scores with which to evaluate differences between players. Three of the seven dimensions describe information or outcomes shared by a team. To interpret differences between players as differences in ability, it would be necessary to demonstrate that the differences recur across matches and correspond to performance on independent tests of the relevant ability (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Hedge et al., 2018).

Conclusion

Match replays preserve the information available to players when they act, enabling researchers to identify repeated decisions and examine players' responses. These routinely recorded matches allow cognitive psychologists to study the decisions players made during many hours of competition while retaining the context of each decision.
