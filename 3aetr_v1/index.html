Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Number personification (NP), the attribution of gender, age, or character to digits, is common among 9-to 12-year-olds, but its relationship to other forms of childhood personification remains unknown. Because NP and imaginary companions (ICs) share the attribution of personhood to non-living entities and high idiosyncrasy, we hypothesized that NP is a developmental extension of ICs. In a web survey, 2,193 Japanese mothers of children in grades 1-6 reported their child's current and past IC and NP status and fantasy orientation. Current ICs were reported for 15.1% of children, and current NP for 1.2%. Contrary to our hypothesis, past IC did not predict current NP; instead, children with a current IC had four times the odds of NP (odds ratio = 4.16). Grade, gender, and fantasy orientation were related to ICs but not, or only weakly, to NP. NP appears to co-occur with ICs rather than to follow them developmentally.

1 Introduction

It is generally known that children tend to attribute personification to various objects (Piaget, 1929). Young children personify everything in their expressions (e.g., "The wall feels it's being knocked on. ") As they grow older, personification is observed in objects that move autonomously (e.g., "The moon knows it's moving."). Even when they understand that an object is inanimate, they still attribute personality traits to it and interact with it with care and affection, as if it is truly alive (Piaget, 1929; Singer and Singer, 1990; Taylor and Mottweiler, 2008; Moriguchi and Shinohara, 2012; Lin et al., 2016; Moriguchi and Todo, 2017).

"An imaginary companion" (IC) is defined as an invisible character with a specific name that exists for a period of at least several months. This character is referenced in the child's daily conversations, plays with the child, and is perceived by the child as a real presence (Svendsen, 1934). Recent studies have expanded the concept of IC to include not only invisible friends (IF) but also personified objects (PO), such as dolls or stuffed animals treated as real friends (Singer and Singer, 1990). A case study describes a child with an IC named "Ume-chan," an invisible girl who taught her various things (Moriguchi and Shinohara, 2012). A famous Japanese film, "My Neighbor Totoro," depicts a girl discovering "Makkuro Kurosuke" in the darkness and finding "Totoro" in the forest, which has been interpreted as an example of finding POs within nature (Moriguchi and Shinohara, 2012).

The prevalence of ICs varies across cultures but is particularly high in preschool years. In Western countries, about 50% of preschool children are reported to have IFs (Pearson et al., 2001; Boerger et al., 2009; Lin et al., 2016), whereas in Japan, approximately 40% have POs and 8% have IFs (Moriguchi and Shinohara, 2012; Motoshima et al., 2014; Moriguchi and Todo, 2017). ICs are most frequently observed between ages 2 and 6, peaking around age 4, and then gradually decline as children enter school age (Pearson et al., 2001; Boerger et al., 2009; Lin et al., 2016; Moriguchi and Todo, 2017). Matsuda et al. (2018) found that even abstract concepts such as numbers can be personified-a phenomenon known as Number Personification (NP). They asked 151 children aged 9 to 12 and 55 adults to assign personality traits (gender, age, morality, and sociability, each with a "none" option) to each of the ten digits, twice with a one-month interval, and used the temporal consistency of the responses as an indicator of NP. The younger group (ages 9-10) showed high temporal consistency, which diminished with age in the older group (ages 11-12) and in adults; with age, participants increasingly chose "none" instead of specific traits, indicating the disappearance of NP.

NP is of interest beyond its curiosity value. In the learning of abstract concepts, linking them to familiar and concrete knowledge is argued to help children grasp what abstract symbols stand for (Fyfe and Nathan, 2019); NP may represent one such link, in which abstract numbers are connected to children's familiar knowledge about people, and it has been suggested to aid the discrimination of numbers and the understanding of relations among them (Matsuda et al., 2018). However, little is known about which children show NP and how it arises. To address this question, we focus on the most extensively studied form of personification in childhood, the imaginary companion.

Taken together, these lines of research indicate that NP and IC share two features. The first is that both involve attributing personhood to entities that are not alive. The second is idiosyncrasy. As the cases of "Ume-chan" and "Totoro" illustrate, each child creates an IC of his or her own, and the same holds for NP: for the digit "2," one child may report a masculine impression while another reports a feminine one (Matsuda et al., 2018). There is also some evidence that children with ICs more readily attribute mental states to animals and inanimate objects (Tahiroglu and Taylor, 2019).

These similarities motivate the hypothesis that IC and NP are two expressions of the same imaginative disposition, placed at different points of development. Both can be regarded as forms of children's fantasy play, that is, play in which children act on an imagined rather than a real state of affairs, of which fantasy orientation is a general measure (Singer and Singer, 1990; Taylor and Carlson, 1997). The preschool years are regarded as the "high season" of fantasy orientation, during which ICs are most frequently observed (Moriguchi and Todo, 2017). NP, which emerges after school entry and is directed at an abstract rather than a concrete target, may therefore reflect a more developed form of fantasy orientation in the school-age years (Matsuda et al., 2018). On this view, NP would not be an isolated curiosity but the school-age continuation of the same imaginative disposition that gives rise to ICs in preschoolers.

The present study tested the hypothesis that NP is a developmental extension of IC, predicting that children who currently show NP would be more likely to have a history of ICs. In addition, the presence of IC is known to vary with age, gender, and fantasy orientation, the latter being an individual's tendency to think and play in imaginative contexts (Singer and Singer, 1990; Taylor et al., 1993; Moriguchi and Todo, 2017). We therefore built a model predicting current NP status from these factors in order to clarify how NP arises. A web-based survey was administered to 2,193 parents of elementary school children. Because directly questioning children imposes considerable practical constraints, children's ICs have traditionally been studied through parental report (Bouldin and Pratt, 1999; Manosevitz et al., 1973; Moriguchi and Todo, 2017). Parents are not always aware of the details of their children's ICs (Taylor, 1999); nevertheless, parental reports are known to agree with children's own reports (Gleason, 2004), and we adopted this paradigm here.

2 Methods

2.1 Participants

A total of 6,000 Japanese women aged 30-50 years who had at least one child were recruited from a commercial database (iBRIDGE, Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Each participant was asked to provide information about one of her children. Although the study was advertised as a survey on "elementary school children's personification in their surroundings," many respondents reported on children who were not in elementary school (preschool: 1,629 cases; junior high school or older: 1,850 cases). These cases were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, 328 responses were excluded due to incomplete or invalid data. The most common reason for exclusion was selecting "don't know" or "none" in the questions regarding fantasy orientation (described later). After these exclusions, responses from 2,193 participants were analyzed (mean age of mothers = 39.6 years, SD = 4.8; all female). We examined the demographic profiles of the excluded 328 cases and did not find systematic differences in child age or gender compared with the included group (Table 1). Household income, reported in 15 bands, had a median in the band of 5-6 million yen (interquartile range, 3-4 to 7-8 million yen); 761 mothers (34.7%) worked part-time, 615 (28.0%) were full-time homemakers, and 472 (21.5%) were full-time employees. Parental education was not collected. We recruited mothers rather than fathers because, in Japan, mothers are more often the primary caregivers of elementary school children and were therefore expected to be more familiar with their children's everyday play; this choice is also consistent with previous parental-report studies of ICs in Japan (Moriguchi and Todo, 2017). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Interfaculty Initiative in Information Studies, The University of Tokyo (No. 21-26).

2.2 Questionnaire

An internet-based questionnaire was conducted. All procedures were carried out in Japanese. The questionnaire was composed of four parts that corresponded to four themes: 1) background information (child's age, gender, and birth order), 2) IC, 3) NP, and 4) fantasy orientation. In the questionnaire, participants are required to answer all questions. The following are the details. First part: Background information on the age and gender of their children was asked.

Second part: We asked parents to describe their children's ICs. We explained to parents about IC by providing the following example based on the previous work on IC (Moriguchi and Todo, 2017): Sat-chan, who is a 6-year-old girl, has a dear friend named 'ai-chan.' Ai-chan is not a real child but a teddy bear. One day, she told the teddy bear, 'I will go out with my mother. So, please be a good girl, Ai-chan. Here are cookies for a tea break. Feel free to take some.' Then, she made the bear sit on the chair, put some cookies on the table, and went out with her mother."

We asked parents whether their children currently or previously played like the example above, with puppets and dolls as if they were human friends. If the parents answered the question affirmatively, we asked them to provide further information: (a) the age of the children when the IC appeared and disappeared, and (b) a specific description of ICs (puppets, dolls, or imaginary beings), including age, gender, and name. Third part: We asked parents to describe the children's NP. We explained to parents about NP by providing the following examples based on the previous research (Matsuda et al., 2018): "Number 1 is an energetic girl, and numbers 2 and 3 are siblings. Number 4 is a female who always cares about other numbers." We asked parents whether their children currently or previously attributed such personality traits to numbers. If parents answered affirmatively, we asked them to provide further information (a) the age of the children when the NP appeared and disappeared, and (b) a specific description of NPs (gender, personality, age, goodness, body shape, relationship, social status). Fourth part: We asked parents about fantasy orientation. The questionnaire consisted of 5 open-ended questions on the child's favorite solitary game, favorite game with other children, favorite story, favorite toy, and favorite TV show, and two forced-choice questions on children's tendency to pretend to be animals or other people, and children's private speech before going to sleep (Singer, 1973; Taylor and Carlson, 1997; Moriguchi and Todo, 2017).

2.3 Analysis

ICs were classified into PO and IF based on the responses describing the type of IC. If a physical object, such as a "stuffed animal" or "doll" was mentioned, it was classified as PO. If the response included terms like "imaginary" or "invisible," it was classified as IF. In addition, responses that did not mention a physical object but instead described a character, such as "Maria, a girl" or "a fairy" were also classified as IF. Vague responses like "various" were not classified into either category. If both PO and IF were mentioned, such as "a stuffed animal and an imaginary character," the response was counted in both categories. Therefore, the sum of IF and PO does not necessarily equal the total IC count as depicted in Table 2. In subsequent analyses, when referring to IC, it indicates IC before classification into IF and PO.

For the analysis of fantasy orientation, the answers to the five open-ended questions were classified as either fantasy-oriented or reality-oriented, based on the schema given by Taylor and Carlson (1997). For example, the answers to the favorite game with other children were coded as fantasy-oriented (e.g., pretend play) or reality-oriented (e.g., chase game). Video games and game consoles (e.g., "Nintendo Switch") were coded as realityoriented, because the questionnaire did not allow us to determine whether the child engaged in imaginative play through them. Answers that could not be classified (e.g., "nothing in particular") did not add to the score. All answers were coded independently by two coders: the first author and a master's student who was blind to the hypotheses and to the children's IC and NP status. Of the 10,965 answers (2,193 children × 5 questions), 10,667 (97.3%) were coded by both coders; inter-rater agreement on these answers was high (agreement = 94.4%, Cohen's κ = .88). The first author's codes were used in the main analyses, and the analyses were repeated with the second coder's codes as a sensitivity check (Section 3.4). We calculated the score of fantasy orientation as the summation of (1) the number of answers classified as fantasy-oriented among the five open-ended questions and (2) the number of affirmative answers among the two forced-choice questions (ranging from 0-7).

3.2 Correlation

Next, we examined the relationships among IC, NP, and related variables. Spearman partial correlations were calculated among school grade, gender, fantasy orientation scores, current IC status, past IC status, current NP status, and past NP status, controlling for the effects of all other variables in the set. The results are summarized in Table 3, which shows the partial correlation coefficients for each pair of variables. The significance levels are denoted as follows: *** p < .001, ** p < .01, * p < .05.

Current NP. The strongest significant association was observed between current NP and current IC (r = .091, p < .001), indicating that children who currently have ICs are more likely to show NP. Current NP showed no significant correlations with school grade (r = .003, p = .892), gender (r = -.008, p = .696), fantasy orientation (r = .033, p = .119), or past IC (r = .011, p = .600).

Past NP. Past NP was positively correlated with fantasy orientation (r = .080, p < .001), past IC (r = .084, p < .001), and current IC (r = .053, p = .013), and showed weaker associations with school grade (r = .064, p = .003) and gender (r = .042, p = .048). Current IC. Current IC was negatively correlated with school grade (r = -.124, p < .001) and gender (r = -.136, p < .001; i.e., more frequent in girls), and positively correlated with fantasy orientation (r = .218, p < .001). Past IC. Past IC was positively correlated with fantasy orientation (r = .058, p = .006) and negatively with gender (r = -.127, p < .001); its association with school grade was not significant (r = .016, p = .446).

Fantasy orientation.

Fantasy orientation decreased with school grade (r = -.320, p < .001) and was higher in girls than in boys (r = -.158, p < .001). In summary, current NP was not related to school grade, gender, or fantasy orientation in the partial correlations, but was significantly associated with the presence of current ICs. This suggests that children who currently have ICs are also more likely to exhibit NP. Past NP, although rare, showed weak but significant associations with fantasy orientation and past IC status, indicating that past NP, like current NP, co-occurred with IC of the same period. In comparison, ICs were more frequently observed in younger children, particularly in girls rather than boys, and in children with a higher fantasy orientation, consistent with previous studies (e.g., Moriguchi and Todo, 2017).

3.3 Logistic regression predicting NP

To further examine the predictors of NP, we conducted a logistic regression analysis with current NP status (0 = no,1 = yes) as the dependent variable. School grade, gender, fantasy orientation scores, current IC status, and past IC status were entered as predictors. Past NP status was not included because current and past NP were mutually exclusive response options in the questionnaire. The results are summarized in Table 4.

The overall model was statistically significant (χ 2 (5) = 24.6, p < .001), although the explanatory power was limited (Nagelkerke R² = .092). The presence of a current IC was the strongest predictor of NP (β = 1.42, SE = 0.468, z = 3.04, p = .002). This corresponds to an odds ratio of e1.42 ≈ 4.16 (95% CI [1.66, 10.40]), indicating that children who currently have ICs were about four times more likely to also show NP compared to those without ICs.

Fantasy orientation was also a significant positive predictor (β = 0.320, SE = 0.134, z = 2.38, p = .017, OR = 1.38 per point, 95% CI [1.06, 1.79]), whereas school grade (p = .825), gender (p = .824), and past IC status (p = .567) were not significant predictors. Adding two-way interactions did not improve the model fit (AIC increased from 269.7 to 278.8).

In summary, logistic regression confirmed that NP was most strongly associated with the presence of current ICs, with an additional, smaller contribution of fantasy orientation; demographic variables did not predict NP. This result highlights the importance of IC experience as a proximal factor for the emergence of NP, consistent with the correlational analyses.

3.4 Sensitivity analysis with the second coder's classification

We repeated the partial correlation and logistic regression analyses using the second coder's classification of fantasy orientation. The pattern of results was unchanged: in the partial correlations, current NP was associated with current IC (r = .092, p < .001) but not with fantasy orientation (r = .029, p = .168); in the logistic regression, current IC (β = 1.47, p = .002, OR = 4.35) and fantasy orientation (β = 0.287, p = .034, OR = 1.33) remained significant predictors of current NP, and no other predictor was significant.

We also re-estimated the logistic regression with household income (band midpoint, in millions of yen) added as a covariate, because socioeconomic status was not part of our hypotheses but was available. The effects of current IC (β = 1.38, p = .004, OR = 3.99) and fantasy orientation (β = 0.312, p = .022, OR = 1.37) were essentially unchanged. Household income itself showed a small positive association with current NP (β = 0.091 per million yen, SE = 0.040, p = .025, OR = 1.10), whereas it was unrelated to current IC (p = .35) and fantasy orientation (Spearman's ρ = .008). Given the small number of children with NP and the fact that the prevalence of NP did not increase monotonically across income terciles (1.1%, 0.9%, and 1.8%), we do not interpret this association further.

4 Discussion

This study aimed to test the hypothesis that number personification (NP) in schoolage children may represent a developmental extension of imaginary companions (IC). Specifically, we predicted that children who had experienced ICs in the past would be more likely to show NP in the present. To examine this, we conducted a large-scale web survey of 2,193 parents, assessing their children's current and past IC status, current NP status, and fantasy orientation.

Contrary to our hypothesis, the analyses did not reveal a significant association between past IC and current NP. Instead, NP was associated with the presence of current ICs. The adjusted odds of current NP were 4.16 times as high for children with a current IC as for those without one, whereas past IC did not predict current NP. The latter finding warrants caution: current NP was reported for only 26 children, and mothers had to recall ICs that may have ended several years earlier. Nevertheless, the pattern across the partial correlations is informative. Past NP (n = 10) was associated with past IC and, more weakly, with current IC, whereas current NP was associated only with current IC (Table 3). That is, NP was linked to IC of the same period rather than to IC of an earlier period. This pattern is more consistent with NP and IC co-occurring than with NP succeeding IC, although our cross-sectional design cannot establish whether IC typically precedes NP or whether the two experiences begin and end together within an individual child. The age-related decline in NP reported by Matsuda et al. (2018) and the lower prevalence of current IC in the higher grades of our sample likewise describe differences between age groups rather than within-child change.

In addition, NP showed no significant partial correlations with school grade, gender, or fantasy orientation, although fantasy orientation had a small positive effect on NP when the other variables were controlled in the logistic regression. IC, on the other hand, was clearly related to these factors: it was more frequent in younger children, in girls than in boys, and in children with higher fantasy orientation. Likewise, fantasy orientation itself decreased with school grade and was higher in girls. Both of these patterns, for IC and for fantasy orientation, align with previous research (e.g., Moriguchi and Todo, 2017) and indicate that the present dataset is consistent with established findings on IC and fantasy play.

Building on this pattern, we propose an asymmetric organization: broader developmental and dispositional factors -school grade (lower grades), gender (girls), and fantasy orientation (higher scores) -are linked to the presence of IC, whereas NP shows at most a weak direct link with fantasy orientation and no link with school grade or gender, and is instead mainly contingent on the presence of IC. In practical terms, NP was uncommon overall in our sample, yet the odds of NP were substantially higher when IC was present (OR ≈ 4.2; see Table 4). Thus, NP is better viewed as a rare, idiosyncratic marker that co-occurs with IC, rather than as a direct expression of children's general fantasy play (quantified by the fantasy-orientation measure); the small effect of fantasy orientation on NP is consistent with NP sharing an imaginative basis with IC, but this effect was much smaller than that of IC itself.

We emphasize that this account is descriptive and does not imply causality. The cross-sectional design and the low base rate of NP limit directional inferences; future longitudinal work and rare-event-appropriate analyses will be necessary to test whether NP merely co-occurs with IC or also contributes to the maintenance of IC in a subset of children. We also note that the fantasy orientation score depends on coding decisions for frequent answers such as video games, which we treated as reality-oriented; when video games were instead treated as fantasy-oriented, the effect of fantasy orientation on NP was no longer significant, whereas the association between IC and NP was unchanged.

4.1 Prevalence

Previous studies on children's NP have indicated that this behavior is commonly observed in younger children (Matsuda et al., 2018). However, in our study, only 1.2% of parents reported that their children displayed NP (Table 2). Furthermore, while the earlier research suggested that NP decreases as children grow older, we did not observe any significant age-related changes in our findings. These discrepancies may be due to methodological differences. Previous studies were based on children's own responses in a forced-choice consistency test (Matsuda et al., 2018), whereas the present study relied on parental reports. Regarding IC, even if parents are not fully aware of the details, it is known that parental reports generally align with children's self-reports, making them a common approach in IC research (Taylor, 1999; Gleason, 2004; Bouldin and Pratt, 1999; Manosevitz et al., 1973; Moriguchi and Todo, 2017). Since IC is correlated with fantasy orientation, it is considered to be closely related to children's daily play, making its manifestations in daily life easier to observe (Table 3; Moriguchi and Todo, 2017). On the other hand, NP may be more introspective and less easily communicated to others. To compute its prevalence more accurately, it is essential to rely on children's own responses, like the previous research, rather than solely relying on parental reports.

The second discrepancy, the absence of age-related change in NP, may partly reflect a lack of statistical power. As illustrated in Figure 1c, the prevalence of NP appears to decrease consistently from 1st to 4th graders (ages 6-10), but a discontinuous change is noted among 5th and 6th graders. Analyzing a larger sample might reveal an age-related decline in NP similar to the trend observed in grades 1-4.

Another possibility, which could account for both discrepancies, is that the two studies captured NP of different strength. Matsuda et al. (2018) found that the temporal consistency of NP was distributed continuously across children rather than dividing them into those with and without NP, and their forced-choice task could detect NP that a child would never mention spontaneously. A parental report, by contrast, requires that the child's NP be expressed in everyday life clearly enough for the parent to notice it. The 1.2% reported here may therefore correspond to the pronounced end of this spectrum, and NP that is pronounced enough to be visible to others may also be more stable across ages. Although this interpretation is inferential, it is consistent with the fact that NP of this kind resembles a form of synesthesia, ordinal linguistic personification (OLP), as discussed below.

4.2 NP, Ordinal Linguistic Personification, and Synesthesia

OLP is a form of synesthesia in which personality traits such as gender, age, or social roles are attributed to ordinal sequences such as letters, numbers, days, and months (Simner and Holenstein, 2007; Smilek et al., 2007). Synesthetes with OLP give highly idiosyncratic but temporally consistent reports, e.g., "The number '3' is such a jerk! He only thinks of himself" (Smilek et al., 2007), and OLP is found in around 1% of the adult population (Simner and Holenstein, 2007; Smilek et al., 2007; Amin et al., 2011; Sobczak-Edmans and Sagiv, 2013; Simner et al., 2016; Ward and Simner, 2022). Children's NP shares with OLP both the personification of numbers and the idiosyncrasy of the attributed traits, and this similarity has already been noted by Matsuda et al. (2018).

The NP reported by parents in this study resembles OLP in two further respects. Its prevalence (1.2%) is close to that of OLP, and it did not change with school grade, whereas NP assessed with a forced-choice consistency test declines with age (Matsuda et al., 2018). Although age-related changes in OLP have not been studied directly, the prevalence of grapheme-color synesthesia is similar in 6-7-year-old children and adults (Simner et al., 2009), suggesting that the prevalence of OLP may also be stable across ages. One reading of these observations is that NP in childhood is common but mostly weak, and that only in a small proportion of children it is pronounced enough to be noticed by parents and to persist; this pronounced NP may correspond to OLP in adults. We stress that this is a hypothesis. Whether parent-reported NP and adult OLP are the same phenomenon, and whether childhood NP is developmentally continuous with adult OLP, cannot be determined from the present data, and addressing these questions will require following the same children over time and combining parental reports with children's own accounts.

Moreover, although the previous study did obtain children's own responses, it focused on quantitative analyses (Matsuda et al., 2018). Given the high idiosyncrasy and complex variability of NP, qualitative analyses of children's own accounts will be essential, and future research should incorporate phenomenological methods designed for children (Philo, 2003).

5 Conclusion

In this study, we conducted a survey of parents to clarify the relationship between NP and children's fantasy play, using IC and fantasy orientation as indicators of everyday imaginative play. Contrary to our hypothesis that NP is a developmental extension of IC, past IC did not predict current NP. Instead, NP co-occurred with current IC: children with current ICs were about four times more likely to show NP. Furthermore, IC was related to school grade, gender, and fantasy orientation, whereas NP showed no relation to school grade or gender and only a weak association with fantasy orientation, suggesting the asymmetric structure depicted in Figure 3. The prevalence of parent-reported NP was lower than in previous research based on children's own responses, indicating that NP may vary in degree and that parental reports capture only its pronounced form (Matsuda et al., 2018). Future research should combine parental reports with children's own accounts to gain a deeper understanding of this complex and idiosyncratic phenomenon.
