Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Background and objectives: This study is the first to explore mechanisms of change and engagement experiences for consumers completing a tailored intervention involving imagery rescripting, replacement implementation intentions, and imagery rehearsal to target hypothesised mechansims of suicide imagery (Integrated skills for SUicide IMagery; I-SUIM). Design and Methods: Adult participants with suicide imagery (N = 8) completed two weekly baseline surveys, followed by 3-4 I-SUIM sessions with post-session measures, and a twoweek follow-up survey. Evaluation included qualitative interviews at follow-up and mixedmethods surveys collecting qualitative feedback and quantitative ratings of suicide imagery duration, coping, imagery meaning, distress, suicide urge, imagery features, and metacognitions.

Results: No participants dropped out of treatment, and there were no series adverse events.

All participants endorsed the intervention as beneficial. Most reported improved understanding of imagery precipitants and function, meaning, and safety plan use.

Improvements in other mechanisms were more variable, including changes to imagery features, defusion, emotion changes, and metacognitive shifts. Participants noted some challenges including temporary imagery salience and negative affect immediately following rescripting and difficulty practicing independently. Participants described the role of an experienced and empathetic therapist important to guide intervention and help them feel less alone.

Limitations: There was no control arm and the study was not designed to demonstrate efficacy.

Conclusions: I-SUIM appears safe and acceptable with qualitatively reported improvements.

However, the specific nature of improvements may vary between individuals, and future research is required to assess efficacy across a range of cognitive, emotional, and behavioural outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Suicide imagery is a commonly experienced but poorly understood risk factor for suicide.

Mental imagery involves internally experiencing sensory information without corresponding sensory input, including both external (e.g. visual experiences, smell, touch) and internal sensory modalities (e.g., interoception, kinaesthesia) (Kosslyn et al., 2001). Imagery about suicide can include mental representations of suicide behaviour sequences ('flash-forwards' imagery; Crane et al., 2012; Hales et al., 2011; Holmes, Crane, et al., 2007), wanted impacts of suicide on the self and others, fantasy-based mental escapes, memories of previous suicide attempts, and even discouraging imagery depicting unwanted impacts of suicide (Crane et al., 2012; Hales et al., 2011; Holmes, Crane, et al., 2007; Martin et al., 2026).

Suicide imagery is common (clinical prevalence estimated at 74%) (Lawrence et al., 2023) and is recognised as a clear risk factor for suicide behaviour (Lawrence et al., 2022). It is associated with greater risk of both having a detailed suicide plan and having previously enacted suicide behaviours, compared to verbal ideation (Lawrence et al., 2023; Lawrence et al., 2022; Wetherall et al., 2018). Researchers and clinicians have made repeated calls to develop and evaluate treatments for suicide imagery (De Rozario et al., 2021; Holmes, Crane, et al., 2007; Lawrence et al., 2022; Paulik et al., 2023) with some suggesting application of existing psychological interventions for problematic imagery, such as imagery rescripting (e.g., Paulik et al., 2023). Imagery rescripting is a brief psychological intervention that was originally proposed as an extension of cognitive behavioural therapy to address maladaptive core beliefs and schemas that may arise from childhood sexual abuse (Smucker et al., 1995).

It has since been validated for use across a range of conditions including other traumas, depression, and social anxiety (Kroener et al., 2023).

Two small pilot studies have evaluated the use of imagery rescripting for suicide imagery. Rahnama and colleagues (2013) treated adults (n = 13) with a history of suicide attempts using two to nine sessions of imagery rescripting based upon Smucker and Dancu's (2005) rescripting protocol for childhood trauma. The intervention group showed large significant reductions in suicide ideation compared to an undefined control group. Schmaal and colleagues (2026) treated inpatient young people (n = 11) with imagery rescripting (schema therapy protocol), which led to reductions in image frequency and ideation severity. These studies provide preliminary support that imagery rescripting may be useful in the treatment of suicide imagery.

Holmes and colleagues caution against the application of existing manualised treatments to novel settings without careful tailoring, suggesting that novel interventions should address theoretically and empirically implicated mechanisms. Despite promising findings, Rahnama et al. (2013) and Schmaal (2026) applied an imagery rescripting intervention that was originally developed for trauma-related imagery (Holmes, Arntz, et al., 2007; Valiente-Gómez et al., 2017) without substantial modification to address potential mechanisms of suicide imagery. An important distinction is that trauma flash-backs are intrusivelyexperienced and distress-eliciting (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), whereas suicide imagery may be deliberately evoked as well as intrusively experienced, particularly when the imagery is associated with attempt behaviour (Martin et al., 2026). Further, suicide imagery can be associated with positive feelings such as comfort and control (Crane et al., 2012; Martin et al., 2026; Ng et al., 2016) as well as negative experiences such as distress (Lawrence et al., 2023). Thus, suicide imagery may differ from trauma-related imagery in mechanistically important ways, suggesting a need to appropriately tailor interventions.

This study aims to provide a detailed exploration of consumers' phenomenological experiences when engaging in an imagery-based intervention tailored and co-designed for suicide imagery through a mixed-methods case series. Specifically, the study explores the potential mechanisms of suicide imagery and therapeutic change, as well as consumers' experiences of factors that may facilitate or impede engagement.

METHODS

Design

This study utilised an exploratory mixed-methods multiple baseline case series design (Yin, 2017) involving a two-week baseline, 3-4 intervention sessions (with pre-and post-session questionnaires), then a follow-up interview and survey 2-weeks post-treatment. No primary outcome was specified, as the core aim was to explore potential mechanisms of change that may be suitable for future evaluation. Data was collected between March and December 2025.

Lived Experience Engagement

Two Lived Experience Advisors were paid for their time in sharing decision-making regarding study design, materials, and safety protocols via a mixture of written material review and meetings with the lead researcher. These advisors previously contributed to the development of the intervention. One advisor (WC) elected to contribute to data analysis and manuscript preparation.

Participants and recruitment

Participants (N = 8) were recruited through online advertisements on social media, newsletters, and via snowball emails. Adults residing in Australia who experienced suicide imagery in the past month were eligible with referral and safety plan (Stanley & Brown, 2012) from a current therapist. After recruiting four participants, the criteria were broadened to allow referral from general practitioners if previous therapy was completed, and for if safety planning was completed at intake. Exclusion criteria included (1) protective imagery (subjectively perceived to reduce attempt risk) and ( 2) recent (past 3-months) unresolved crisis (e.g., intention, preparation, attempt, crisis support).

A sample of 8-12 participants was anticipated as required to yield adequate information power (Malterud et al., 2016) due to (a) dense sample specificity (purposive sampling and control of intervention exposure), (b) case analysis strategy of in-depth longitudinal evaluation through both written survey and transcribed oral data, and (c) excellent quality dialogue due to DR's qualitative interview experience but detachment from intervention delivery.

Materials

Interview guide

A semi-structured interview guide was developed by AM, PM, FS, and LS and reviewed by two lived experienced advisors (including WC) with recommended changes implemented.

The interview guide assessed recent risk (past-week preparations or intent to suicide), imagery experiences and safety plan use across treatment, and most (and least) helpful intervention components (S1).

Questionnaires

Demographics. A brief questionnaire assessed socio-demographic information at baseline including age, sex, gender, indigeneity, country of birth, and language use.

Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview-Revised (SITBI-R) (Fox et al., 2020).

Items evaluating lifetime history of ideation, plans, and attempts were used to characterise sample suicidality history. Most items have good test-rest reliability (κ > .69) excepting the ideation item (κ = .39), and all have excellent convergent validity with the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (Posner et al., 2011), (Kappa's > 0.74) (Fox et al., 2020).

Suicide Imagery Measures (Suicide Imagery and Coping Survey (SICS); Suicide Imagery and Features Scale (SIFS); (Suicide Imagery Metacognitions Survey (SIMS), unpublished scales).

This study piloted initial iterations of the SICS, SIFS, and SIMSscales under development by the study team. SICS items numerically estimate frequency and duration of suicide imagery and frequency of coping strategy use, and rate interference on a 7-point Likert scale (1= not at all;7 = completely). The SIFS asks participants to rate pastweek frequency of suicide imagery features (Martin et al., 2026) on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = never;7 = always). The SIMS assesses six metacognitive beliefs about suicide imagery on a 7-point scale (1= completely disagree to7= completely agree).

Post-session surveys. Open-response questions assessed session content, most (and least) helpful aspects, and general feedback. Therapists also recorded intervention strategies and participant ratings of imagery meaning, coping confidence, distress, and urge to suicide on a scale from0 = not at all, to10 = the strongest ever felt.

Intervention

Integrated skills for SUicide IMagery (I-SUIM) is a 3-4 session adjunctive intervention that was developed by investigators AM, PM, LS, and FS in collaboration with two lived experience advisors (including WC). I-SUIM includes the following core components as well as optional formulation-indicated modules. Intervention begins with assessment and formulation of a target image, then creation of an explicit replacement implementation intention (Adriaanse et al., 2011) outlining typical stressors and coping/safety plan strategies to address needs (e.g., "when I feel isolated, instead of staying on the couch and leaning into my suicide imagery, I will practice my TIP skills, then call my friend Susan. If this doesn't work, I will call Lifeline"). Participants' target images and psycho-emotional needs are detailed in Supplementary 2.

Rescripting begins with a stem depicting a typical stressor or 'activating event' for the target image, followed by rescripting including updating of meaning and addressing activated unmet psycho-emotional needs. This optionally includes positive future imagery. I-SUIM rescripting then interweaves imaginal rehearsal of the replacement implementation intention (Andersson & Moss, 2011; Divine & Astill, 2025), with motivational emphasis upon imagined benefits (both hedonic and goal-directed) of enacting coping behaviours (Andrade et al., 2025). The final session includes relapse prevention.

Procedure

The project was conducted in accordance with approved ethics protocols (University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee approval # 7650). Online surveys were conducted using the REDCap platform, while videoconferencing (Zoom v.6) was used for screening, intervention, and interviews. Following referral, potential participants completed electronic consent, a brief pre-screening survey, then a screening interview with a clinical researcher (AM or LS). Enrolled participants completed two weekly baseline surveys before commencing intervention with an experienced clinical psychologist (AM, LS, or GP) who attended fortnightly supervision. At baseline 1, participants completed demographic and SITBI-SR scales. The SICS was completed at each baseline and intervention session as well as 2 weeks follow-up. The SIFS and SIMS were completed at initial baseline 1, session 3, and 2 weeks follow-up. Interviews were conducted at 2 weeks follow-up by DR, audiorecorded, and transcribed. Participants were reimbursed for their participation following posttreatment interviews with a $50 e-gift card.

Analysis

Qualitative data

Qualitative interview transcripts then post-session survey data were analysed following six phases of reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021) using NVivo 12 (detailed in S3 alongside reflexivity statements).

The core analysis team (DR, AM, and WC) utilised audit trails, repeated discussions throughout sense-making, and the application of reflexivity to maintain rigour.

Quantitative data

Quantitative data pertaining to demographic information and suicidality history were collated and reported descriptively. Quantitative ratings of belief in unhelpful meaning, confidence using coping strategies, distress, and suicide urge were summarised descriptively, and effect size (Cohen's d) confidence intervals calculated to provide preliminary effect size estimates for future studies. Quantitative ratings on the SICS, SIFS, and SIMS were summarised descriptively using medians (due to outlying data points), and individual participant responses plotted.

Note that participants 4 and 7 reported substantial increases to known imagery triggers at session 4 and follow-up respectively, which corresponded with increased duration at these timepoints. Note also that in session 3, participant 5 elected to deliberately evoke additional imagery of the unwanted impacts of suicide on their family as a deterrent to suicide, which was followed by increased duration in session 4 and follow-up. There were overall low frequencies of suicide crisis requiring safety plan use, leading to floor effects (S4).

Ratings of Target Imagery Meaning, Coping, Distress, and Suicide Urge

Table 3 displays sample average ratings of image outcomes prior to any rescripting intervention compared to after the final rescripting session, alongside effect size estimates. These suggest medium to very large reductions in the strength of the unhelpful meaning of target imagery, and small to very large increases in participant confidence implementing the coping strategies from their safety plans. The effect size confidence intervals were wide due to the small sample, so point estimates need to be interpreted with caution.

Figure 2 displays plots of individual participant ratings of target imagery meaning, coping confidence, distress, and suicide urge (plot of sample medians available in S3). Seven participants displayed reductions in unhelpful meaning over time. Four demonstrated a partial return in meaning strength during the period between rescripting sessions, but these tended to reduce following repeated rescripting. Most participants demonstrated modest increases in coping confidence and decreases in distress. Although two participants demonstrated decreases in suicide urge over sessions, for most this remained stable and low (around 0-2/10 urge). Within individual sessions, ratings of both distress and suicide urge often increased between pre-rescript (prior to any experiential work) and mid-rescripting (after evocation of their distressing 'stem' image), before decreasing post-rescripting.

Suicide Imagery Features

Median ratings of the frequency of suicide imagery features (rated out of seven) are displayed in Table 4 and plots of individual participant ratings in Figure 3. At baseline, ratings of imagery detail (e.g., specific methods, date/time, location), feasibility of imagined methods, and realistic alignment with real-world details were relatively frequent (5 = 'most of the time') for most participants. About half the sample showed reductions in the frequency of imagery with feasible methods, detail, being dead, enacting methods, suicide behaviour but including barriers, and negative impacts on the self. Frequency of realistic alignment with real-world details remained high for most.

Metacognitive Beliefs

Median ratings of metacognitive beliefs about suicide are displayed in Table 5 and individual participant plots in Figure 4. There was substantial variability between participants. Baseline intrusivity was high but reduced for about half of participants. Baseline endorsement of imagery risk was low to moderate, but increased for about a third of participants. Disengage difficulty appeared to reduce for the third of participants with initially high ratings.

DISCUSSION

This mixed-methods case series explored consumer experiences of a tailored intervention for suicide imagery, with the primary aim to identify potential mechanisms of change for evaluation in future research. The study expanded previous research (Rahnama et al., 2013; Schmaal et al., 2026) by identifying a broad range of potential mechanisms of therapeutic change among a sample of adults with relatively severe and longstanding suicidality. Few constructs demonstrated reliable improvement across participants. However, all participants reported improvements on at least one construct, and all endorsed the intervention as beneficial. The study also identified several process considerations for facilitating engagement.

Potential Mechanisms of Suicide Imagery and Therapeutic Change

Almost all participants reported improved understanding of imagery precipitants and function and perceived control. Consistent with hypothesised mechanisms of imagery rescripting (Arntz, 2012), I-SUIM was associated with average reductions in unhelpful imagery meanings, estimated to be of at least moderate to very large effects. Consistent with the use of implementation intention (Andersson & Moss, 2011) and imagery rehearsal strategies (Andrade et al., 2025), almost all participants qualitatively reported increases in safety plan strategy use. Average improvement in coping confidence was estimated to be at least small to a very large effect. Although quantitative estimates of safety plan strategy use were stably low, this item was anchored to infrequent crisis contexts. In contrast, qualitative data suggested increases in safety plan strategy use earlier in trajectories to pre-empt crisis. Future studies might explore therapeutic change across insight and positive coping strategies more broadly beyond worst-point crisis.

There was variability in the specific aspects of imagery that demonstrated change. Around half reported change to concrete imagery features (e.g., less lethal methods depictions) while others reported improved psychological distancing from their imagery or metacognitive shifts (e.g., increased sense of control). Changes to emotional responses similarly varied to include reduced negative emotions (e.g., distress) for some and improved positive emotions (e.g., self-compassion, empowerment) for others. Therapeutic improvements may therefore manifest idiosyncratically, cautioning against the use of limited universal outcome metrics.

Future studies may instead consider classifying participants as responders based on improvement on any among a range of outcomes.

Only half of participants demonstrated reductions in imagery duration, with a few demonstrating increases alongside increases in external stressors. This aligns with broader research demonstrating that fluctuates in response to contextual drivers such as housing, finances, and workplace stressors (Gallagher et al., 2025). Future studies may consider tracking fluctuations in contextual stressors alongside clinical outcomes.

Engagement Factors

The study also aimed to explore consumer experiences of engaging with the intervention process. Engagement was high, with all participants completing all planned intervention sessions despite increased contextual stressors for some, compared to drop-out rates between 10-14% for other brief interventions (Andreasson et al., 2016; Comtois et al., 2011) and 24-48% for longer interventions (Linehan et al., 2015). This suggests that the intervention was acceptable and engaging to participants, despite the relative severity of the sample. Some challenges were identified including activation of difficult emotions and transient increases in imagery salience following rescripting, which once participant acknowledged was associated with processing previously avoided stressors and suicide imagery in treatment. Studies of imagery-related interventions for trauma reliably report that small subsets of participants may experience transient increases in distress and trauma symptoms that are not associated with poor outcomes or drop-out (Burger et al., 2023; Kullberg et al., 2025; Larsen et al., 2016). It is also important to note that imagery rescripting requires deliberate activation of 'hotspots' of difficult images and emotion for optimal treatment response (Arntz, 2025). Consistent with this, the participants (1, 2, 6, and 8) who endorsed this transient increase were among those with larger reductions in suicide image duration, unhelpful meaning, overall distress, and suicide urge. Although these transient symptom increases may be expected among a subset of participants and are not indicative of poor outcomes, it is nonetheless important that clinicians discuss this possibility during informed consent.

Participants also described therapist-related factors important for engagement, including the need for an experienced therapist to guide the intervention and provide emotional validation.

Participants also noted the value of discussing their imagery with a trained therapist, often for the first time. These findings suggest that careful work may be required to translate the I-SUIM intervention to non-contiguous intervention formats such as online modules.

Limitations

Limitations inherent to the study's design include lack of control arm, small sample unpowered for inferential evaluation of efficacy, and no post-treatment evaluation of suicide behaviours. Full-powered randomised controlled efficacy trials are required to draw conclusions regarding treatment impact. Further, this sample experienced chronic suicidality, with an average duration approaching 20 years. It is unclear how the intervention may be experienced by individuals with more recently onset suicide imagery.

Measures used to evaluate suicide imagery duration and safety plan strategy use were also limited. Although sessions targeted only a single suicide image, items assessing imagery duration enquired regarding suicide imagery in general (most reported a range of imagery on intake). If intervention effects did not generalise to non-targeted suicide imagery, these benefits may have been obscured in broad outcome data. Conversely, participants often presented with narrow safety plans which were elaborated on during treatment to include a broader range of coping strategies. However, items assessing safety plan use were anchored to crisis points where the specific safety plan was used. Thus, quantitative estimates may not have captured instances where broader coping strategies were used, or strategies applied prior to crisis. Future studies may consider evaluating change in targeted imagery, as well as implementation of broader coping strategies prior to suicide crisis.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary support that I-SUIM provides a safe and acceptable brief intervention for suicide imagery among adults with chronic suicidality, with qualitatively reported improvements for all participants. The nature of treatment improvements may be variable between individuals, and further research is required to evaluate treatment efficacy and the impact of I-SUIM upon a range of cognitive, emotional, and behavioural outcomes.
