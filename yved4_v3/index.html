How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

While a reader might imagine the narrator's and characters' voices in a written book, a spoken narrative solidifies the relationship between the narrator and the text. Here, we report two experiments investigating how perceived narrator-to-story fit affects narrative listening enjoyment and other listening outcomes. In a first experiment (N=400 participants), we manipulated the gender-related congruence between the expected and heard narrator of four spoken narratives in a willingness-to-wait paradigm. We found that listeners were more likely to continue listening to congruent than incongruent stories, but did not enjoy them more or comprehend them more accurately. However, we observed a dissociation in the observed effects of perceived narrator-to-story fit: specifically, higher individual evaluations significantly increased enjoyment, while both higher individual and (aggregated) mean fit ratings predicted greater motivation to continue listening. This suggested that crowd-sourced data may be less effective than personal taste when predicting narrative listening enjoyment. Indeed, a second experiment using a larger number of stories showed that participants' enjoyment of spoken narratives is most strongly predicted by within-sample individual fit ratings, but not by withinor between-group aggregate ratings. Our findings provide evidence to support personalisation of narration as a way to promote enjoyment of spoken narratives and, potentially, audiobooks.

Introduction

Book language typically features more advanced vocabulary and greater syntactic complexity, and exposure to books supports the development of both linguistic and non-linguistic abilities, including skills such as emotion processing and theory of mind (Nation et al., 2022). Yet, recent years have seen a decline in the levels of reading for pleasure, with large proportions of children and adults who do not consider themselves to be readers (Bone et al., 2025; Clark et al., 2025; Cremin & Scholes, 2024; Spaulding et al., 2025). This presents the risk that large and increasing proportions of people are missing out on the benefits of engaging with books.

Recent findings have underlined the importance of reward systems for engaging with the benefits of book language, by showing that greater enjoyment of a written or audio book excerpt predicts both increased comprehension of the book, and a greater motivation to continue reading or listening to it (Bains et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2025; McGettigan et al., 2026). This motivates a need to investigate the factors that might enhance enjoyment, as potential levers for promoting cognitive engagement with books.

Audiobooks offer an alternative to book reading, while preserving access to the language of written books. A crucial distinction between written and spoken versions of narratives is that while the written form leaves the narrator's voice to the reader's imagination, an audiobook defines it. This opens up the possibility to use audio design as a lever to enhance enjoyment and engagement: Previous work has shown that greater narrator pleasantness and familiarity are both associated with increased enjoyment of spoken narratives (McGettigan et al., 2026), and other work has shown that acoustic features of narration (such as speaking rate) can predict narrative absorption (Lange et al., 2022). However, less is known about how the relationship between a voice and the specific text it is narrating -or the narrator-to-story "fit"might impact the listening experience as well as related outcomes such as comprehension.

The current study aimed to investigate this by measuring how variation in narrator-to-story fit impacted listening enjoyment, comprehension, and motivation to continue listening (measured as willingness to wait).

There are several ways in which a heard narration might be perceived to match or mismatch with the linguistic content of a narrative, in terms of both the speaker's perceived person characteristics (e.g. age, sex/gender, personality traits, first language / nationality, social class) and/or their delivery style (e.g. speaking rate, voice quality, affective prosody). However, voice gender presents itself as the strongest candidate for a first investigation of narrator-to-story fit.

As a speaker property, binary sex judgements from adult voices can be performed with high accuracy and from very brief exposures (Owren et al., 2007), due to the substantial sexual dimorphism in human voice fundamental frequency (related to pitch) and formant frequences (related to timbre or vocal size); acoustic variations within male and female voices further give rise to stereotyped inferences about speakers that are at least partly mediated by percepts of relative masculinity and femininity (Ko et al., 2006). Gender also has arguably the largest existing evidence base in relation to the interaction of (perceived) voice properties with cognitive and linguistic processes. For example, in an auditory version of the Stroop effect, listeners were slower to label the gender of a female speaker speaking the word "father" (incongruent) than "mother" (congruent) or "person" (neutral) (Sommers & Danielson, 1999).

Similarly, the processing of grammatical gender of spoken words is primed by congruence in the sex of the speaker (Casado et al., 2023). Other work has shown that the congruence between the gender of a talker's voice and the gender stereotypicality of their utterance modulated P600 evoked responses, indicative of impacts on the integration of speaker properties with sentence meaning and stereotypical beliefs (Lattner & Friederici, 2003).

In the current study we therefore sought to manipulate narrator-to-story fit via the correspondence between the expected gender of an audiobook narration and its actual heard narration. To do this, we first ran a pilot study in which we obtained ratings for the expected gender of the "ideal narrator" of twenty book extracts, and from these selected examples yielding strong expectations of either a male or a female narrator voice. In a first main experiment (Experiment 1), these stories were combined with either a male or a female voice to generate gender-congruent and gender-incongruent narrations for a willingness-to-wait paradigm that measured reward as self-rated enjoyment of the narrative and motivation as the decision to wait (or not) in order to hear more of the narrative. Finding that the categorical gender congruence manipulation in Experiment 1 was a less effective predictor of listening enjoyment than individual evaluations of narrator-to-story fit, we ran a second experiment (Experiment 2) to further investigate fit ratings as a predictor of enjoyment, comparing the predictive power of within-participant (i.e. trialwise), within-group, and between-group estimates.

Experiment 1

This study tested whether a categorical manipulation of congruence between the gender of a narrator and the linguistic content of the story being narrated could modulate the experiences and outcomes of listening, using a willingness-to-wait task.

Methods

Participants

Four hundred participants were recruited via the online recruitment platform Prolific.ac.

Participants were invited to the study based on the following inclusion criteria: aged between 18-50 years old, with English as their first language, having spent of their time in the UK before turning 18, and without hearing difficulties or dyslexia. We further required that participants had a minimum approval rating of 95% on Prolific and a minimum of 10 previous submissions on the platform.

An a priori sample size calculation in G*Power (Faul et al., 2009) used the observed effect size of 0.208 (based on the within-participants effect of Pleasantness on Enjoyment in Experiment 2 of McGettigan et al., 2026) with an alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.8. This suggested a total sample of N=184 participants. A larger target sample of 400 was however chosen to match the total number of trials (i.e. observations) in Experiment 2 of McGettigan et al., (2026), which was 1600 (N=80 participants x 10 trials x 2 repeated-measures conditions).

In the current study, each participant encountered 2 trials in each of the 2 experimental conditions.

Three participants asked for their data to be removed from the analysis, in response to a debrief question inviting participants to report honestly (and without penalty) if they had not completed the tasks properly in line with the instructions. These participants were removed with replacement. Five additional participants were removed for choosing to wait on all 4 trials of the willingness-to-wait paradigm (following Bains et al., 2023). This left a final sample of 395 participants (200 female, 195 male, Mean age 35.9 years (s.d. 8.4 years)), all of whom passed the in-task vigilance checks. The study was approved by the local ethics committee at UCL Speech, Hearing and Phonetic Sciences (SHaPS-2023-CM-038), and all participants provided informed consent prior to completing the experiment. All participants who completed the experiment were rewarded £2.25.

Results of the pilot experiment, Experiment 1 data and analysis script are available as open data via the Open Science Framework: https://osf.io/8sjpm/overview?view_only=7b966a7bc1514b9e8c43f9afdebcdded. The study was not preregistered.

Materials

The stimuli comprised 8 spoken narrative excerpts (2 sequential excerpts from each of 4 books), which were generated from texts used in Jones et al. (2025).

The 4 chosen books were selected using a pilot study, in which 40 adult participants (16 female, 24 male, Mean age 36.0 years (s.d. 7.3 years), recruited using the same inclusion criteria as above, were asked to read the text of the first excerpt from 20 different books and instructed to imagine the ideal narrator for each text. Having read each extract, participants provided ratings of the imagined narrator's gender (on a 0-100 scale from "very masculine male" to "very feminine female") and age (from 0-100 years old). They also rated each extract for how much they enjoyed it (from1 = not at all to9 = extremely) and how likely they would be to buy the book from which the extract was taken (from1 = not at all to9 = extremely).

Using the gender rating data, 2 female-expected and 2 male-expected excerpts were chosen whose ideal narrators yielded gender scores that were significantly lower (male) or higher (female) than the scale midpoint (50): the selections were validated via significant two-tailed one-sample t-tests, which confirmed large effect sizes (Cohen's d > 0.8) for all selected stories.

Further constraints on selection were that the mean expected age of the narrators should be relatively similar across all 4 books (to mitigate against age (in)congruence as a confound with the manipulation of interest), and that they should produce relatively similar mean enjoyment and purchase likelihood ratings. See Table 1 for details of the final selections. Each pair of book excerpts was sequential, such that the second excerpt could be presented in trials where the participant chose to wait to hear more of a given book. Comprehension questions accompanying each audiobook in Experiment 1 were the same as those used in Jones et al. (2025).

Spoken audio versions of each book excerpt were produced as one WAV file per excerpt, using text-to-speech synthesis in ElevenLabs (elevenlabs.io). One female-sounding voice identity ("Elizabeth -Professional British Narrator") and one male-sounding voice identity ("Christopher -Smooth, calm, and very English") were selected from voices that were available via ElevenLabs for community use. Both synthetic voices had been created using ElevenLabs' Professional Voice Clone tool, which produces a high-accuracy digital replica of a human identity based on a recommended 1-3 hours of original human voice recordings.

Once generated, the audiobook clips were downsampled to 22050Hz, loudness-normalised (EBU R 128), and converted from WAV to MP3 for presentation within the online experiment.

Design & Procedure

The main willingness-to-wait task comprised 4 experimental trials (one per audiobook) presented in random order. Of these trials, 2 featured a gender-congruent narration (e.g. a male-expected story read in a male-sounding voice) and 2 featured a gender-incongruent narration (e.g. a male-expected story read in a female-sounding voice). The assignment of voice (Christopher or Elizabeth) to each story was counterbalanced across 4 versions of the experiment. The experimental trials were interspersed with 2 vigilance trials in fixed positions (after the 1 st and 3 rd experimental trials), in which the participants were required to choose the number stated by a synthetic voice (1 female-sounding, 1 male-sounding; both distinct from the main trial narrator voices).

Participants recruited from Prolific were directed to the experiment on Gorilla Experiment Builder (Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020) where they read the study information sheet and provided informed consent. Each participant was assigned to one of the 4 versions of the experiment.

Each experimental trial had the same structure (Figure 1): After presentation of a fixation cross, the participant heard the first spoken audio excerpt from a book lasting 30-40 seconds.

When the audio clip finished playing, the participant rated their enjoyment of the excerpt using a 9-point visual analogue scale (where1 = "hated it" and9 = "loved it"). The next screen presented a written comprehension question (as used by Jones et al., 2025), which the participant answered by selecting from 4 choices. Next, the participant indicated whether they had heard or read the book before. Finally, the participant was asked if they would like to wait to hear some more of the book. If they chose to wait, the second audio excerpt would play following a delay of 4.5 seconds (with participants having the option to move on to the next trial at any point during this second clip). If they chose to skip, the next trial began after 500ms.

After all 4 trials, participants were presented again with the first excerpt from each of the 4 books. Here, they were instructed to rate how suitable they found the voice as a narrator for each of the excerpts, where suitability was further defined as whether the narrator's voice was a good fit to the content of the excerpt. Suitability was rated from 0-100, where0 = not at all suitable and100 = extremely suitable. To facilitate this decision, each audio narration was accompanied by the text of the excerpt onscreen. Participants did not have to listen to the whole clip before making their rating.

After rating suitability for all 4 stories, the participants were asked which of the 2 narrator voices sounded more pleasant to them, with the response options "male voice", "female voice", and "no preference". Participants were then invited to, if relevant, report any technical difficulties and make further comments about the experiment. Finally, the participants were given the opportunity to recommend removal of their data from the analyses, without penalty, if they felt they had not completed the tasks properly (i.e. by following all the task instructions and engaging with the tasks attentively).

Data analysis

Data were analysed with (generalised) linear mixed models in RStudio with lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), using the afex package (Singmann et al., 2016) to estimate main effects via likelihood ratio tests. Initial random-effects structures were maximal, with effects removed in the presence of convergence failures, singular fits, and/or boundary solutions. Models used a binomial error distribution and logit link function to model binary response outcomes. Full model tables (prepared using lme4 and sjPlot) were used to report estimates / odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals. Categorical predictors were coded using sum (deviation) contrasts.

Ratings of Enjoyment and suitability (henceforth Fit) were coded as numerical variables.

Responses to the Comprehension questions were scored as 1 (correct) or 0 (incorrect).

Waiting was coded as 1 (wait) or 0 (skip). The Enjoyment and Fit variables were mean-centred across participants for inclusion in mixed models, and all trials where the participant reported having previously heard or read the book were also removed (7 trials in total, from 7 unique participants).

Results & Discussion

The mean enjoyment rating of the initial book excerpts was 5.60 (out of 9; SD = 1.27) for gender-congruent narrations and 5.55 (SD = 1.24) for gender-incongruent narrations. Mean comprehension accuracy was 75.9% for gender-congruent narrations and 76.5% for genderincongruent narrations. Participants waited to hear more of the book on a total of 155 trials for the gender-congruent narrations and 108 trials for the gender-incongruent narrations; this corresponded to mean waiting rates of 19.6% and 13.8%, respectively. Mean post-hoc ratings of narrator-to-story Fit were 77.87 (out of 100; SD = 14.9) for gender-congruent narrations and 48.43 (SD = 23.4) for gender-incongruent narrations. Participants' expressed preferences in terms of narrator voice pleasantness were evenly distributed across the three options (female voice, male voice, no preference; χ²(2) = 0.309, p = .857). Among participants who expressed a preference, a binomial test showed no difference in preferences for the female versus the male voice (p = .804).

Effects of Congruence on Enjoyment, Comprehension, and Waiting

Congruence had no effect on enjoyment (Estimate = 0.06 [95% CIs: -0.22 -0.37], χ² = 0.21, p = .648) or the likelihood of answering the comprehension question accurately (OR = 0.98 [95% CIs: 0.77 -1.25], χ² = 0.03, p = .868). However, participants were significantly more likely to wait when the narration was gender-congruent (OR = 2.43 [95% CIs: 1.37 -4.37], χ² = 12.74 p < .001; Figure 2). An exploratory analysis indicated that trialwise enjoyment was still highly predictive of greater likelihood of waiting in the current experiment (OR = 2.53 [95% CIs: 2.10 -3.04], χ² = 112.16, p < .001), as has been shown in previous similar studies.

Overall, these findings align with the human reward and motivation literature, which shows that while "wanting" (here, waiting to hear more of the book) usually tracks "liking" (here, enjoyment), these can still be independently modulated (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2008; Berridge & Robinson, 1998). In the current experiment, listeners' enjoyment of a book was predictive of their motivation to continue listening to it, but at the same time only listening motivation was enhanced by greater gender congruency between the book's narration and its content. This observation could be explained via the difference between the immediate experience of listening to each book's first excerpt and the motivational salience attached to the book on the basis of that excerpt: for incongruent stories, the violation of gender stereotypes may add some processing cost to the integration of the excerpt's meaning with the medium of delivery, for example via attempts to revise and repair what has been perceived (Lattner & Friederici, 2003; Porkert et al., 2024). While this cost may be insufficient to modulate the conscious experience of enjoyment during listening, it may reduce the likelihood of sustained listening due to the increased cognitive cost of integrating the voice with the spoken information.

Effects of Narrator-to-story Fit on Enjoyment, Comprehension, and Waiting

A more parsimonious explanation for the results could be that while the experimental manipulation of gender congruency alone was insufficient to modulate listeners' conscious experience of the spoken narrative, an alternative measure of overall narrator-to-story "fit" might be more effective in capturing variance in enjoyment. The results of the validation analysis found that gender congruence was a highly significant predictor of participants' posthoc ratings of narrator-to-story fit (Estimate = 29.36 [95% CIs: 26.90 -31.82], χ² = 344.62, p < .001; Figure 3), therefore listeners were sensitive to the experimental manipulation.

Exploratory models using fit ratings to predict responses in the willingness-to-wait task revealed that narrator-to-story fit ratings were indeed predictive of listener behaviour in the willingness-to-wait task, but -like the effects of gender congruence -the predictive effects were not uniform across dependent variables. For Enjoyment, while consensus Fit ratings were not predictive of story enjoyment ratings (Estimate = 0.00 [95% CIs: -0.00 -0.01], χ² = 2.81, p < .094), the results showed that enjoyment was higher when the individual listener rated the item's Fit as higher than the item mean (Estimate = 0.01 [95% CIs: 0.01 -0.01], χ² = 57.28, p < .001; Figure 3B). In contrast, greater likelihood of waiting was significantly predicted by both higher consensus Fit (Estimate = 1.02 [95% CIs: 1.01 -1.02], χ² = 18.24, p < .001; Figure 3C) and higher individual Fit ratings (Estimate = 1.02 [95% CIs: 1.01 -1.03], χ² = 18.78, p < .001; Figure 3C). Neither individual nor consensus Fit variables predicted answering the comprehension question accurately (Mean fit: Estimate = 1.00 [95% CIs: 0.99 -1.01], χ² = 0.06, p = .805, Trialwise fit: Estimate = 1.00 [95% CIs: 0.99 -1.01], χ² = 3.14, p = .727).

The finding that individual "taste" in narrator-to-story fit significantly predicted both greater "liking" and "wanting" suggests that the relationship of the narrator to the book content can indeed shape both listeners' experience of a spoken narrative as well as its incentive salience to motivate continued listening. Crucially, however, consensus on fit -like the overall gender congruency manipulation -only significantly predicted motivation. This profile of findings raises the question of how the assignment of a narrator to a book might operate at scale. On the one hand, the evidence that the "wisdom of the crowd" could be used to nudge behavioural decision-making and extend engagement with a book is an encouraging finding, as greater exposure to books is linked to greater benefits (Mol & Bus, 2011). However, if the goal of narrator-to-story matching is enjoyment, given the power of perceived hedonistic pleasure to itself incentivise motivated behaviour, then the evidence presented here suggests that narrator assignment may require additional listener-specific considerations.

However, there are some important considerations that limit interpretability of the findings.

Here, listeners were exposed to a relatively small number of trials and, due to counterbalancing, the consensus ratings of fit were necessarily established based on subsets of participants who heard the same combinations of story and narrator rather than using the overall group consensus. That neither the categorical gender congruence manipulation nor post-hoc fit ratings could predict comprehension may also reflect ceiling effects in the dependent variable, given two of the questions yielded mean accuracy scores exceeding 85%.

Thus, in order to better understand the predictive power of perceived narrator-to-story fit on listener experience and listening outcomes, and to test the robustness of the effects found in Experiment 1, it is necessary to investigate the impact of fit across a larger number of stories, independently of any additional experimental manipulation, and across truly independent listener groups.

Experiment 2

This experiment focused on narrator-to-story fit as a continuous predictor of narrative listening experiences and outcomes. Directly addressing the limitations of Experiment 1, we collected novel ratings of enjoyment and narrator-to-story fit for a set of 20 spoken narratives presented to listeners in a single, female-sounding voice. The ratings were used to test the predictive power of individual taste and consensus ratings on enjoyment within the same sample, and separately, to test the effects of consensus narrator-to-story fit ratings on enjoyment, comprehension, and motivation using a pre-existing dataset from a willingness-to-wait task using the same set of audio excerpts (McGettigan et al., 2026, Experiment 1).

We predicted that, within-sample, individual taste in narrator-to-story fit but not consensus fit ratings would predict enjoyment. Across-samples, we predicted that consensus narrator-tostory fit would significantly predict motivation, but not enjoyment or comprehension.

Methods

Participants

Fifty participants (25 female, 25 male, Mean age 36.9 years (s.d. 8.5 years)) were recruited via the online recruitment platform Prolific.ac using the same inclusion criteria as for Experiment 1).

The stimuli comprised 20 spoken book excerpts (i.e. the first excerpt from each of 20 stories),

Design & Procedure

Participants recruited from Prolific were directed to the experiment on Gorilla Experiment Builder (Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020) where they read the study information sheet and provided informed consent.

The main task comprised 20 experimental trials (one per book), presented in random order.

Each trial of the experiment included a 1-second fixation cross followed by audio presentation of the book excerpt. When the audio clip finished playing, the participant rated their enjoyment of the extract using a 9-point visual analogue scale (where1 = "hated it" and9 = "loved it").

They then rated the narrator-to-story fit using a 0-100 scale (where0 = not at all suitable and100 = extremely suitable). Finally, participants were shown the book title and author onscreen and asked to indicate whether they had heard or read the book before. Two vigilance trials, placed after the 6 th and 15 th stories, required the participant to select the onscreen number corresponding to the number spoken in an audio instruction produced in a male-sounding voice. Participants were given the opportunity to take a short a break after the 10 th story.

Following the main task, participants were invited to, if relevant, report any technical difficulties and make further comments about the experiment. Finally, the participants were given the opportunity to recommend removal of their data from the analyses, without penalty, if they felt they had not completed the tasks properly.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed with (generalised) linear mixed models in RStudio with lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), using the afex package (Singmann et al., 2016) to estimate main effects via likelihood ratio tests. Initial random-effects structures were maximal, with effects removed in the presence of convergence failures, singular fits, and/or boundary solutions. Models used a binomial error distribution and logit link function to model binary response outcomes. Full model tables (prepared using lme4 and sjPlot) were used to report estimates / odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals.

Ratings of Enjoyment and Fit were coded as numerical variables. Additional variables Fit_consensus and Fit_individual represented the mean ratings of Fit per unique story (i.e. 20 unique values) and the participant's trialwise deviation from that mean, respectively. The Enjoyment and Fit variables were mean-centred across participants for inclusion in mixed models, and all trials where the participant reported having previously heard or read the book were also removed (9 trials in total, from 5 unique participants).

The first model tested the within-sample effect of Fit ratings as predictors of Enjoyment in the main experiment. The bobyqa optimizer (maxfun = 10000) was used in response to a convergence warning.

The remaining models tested the between-samples effect of consensus fit ratings on enjoyment (2), the likelihood of answering the comprehension question accurately (3), and the likelihood of waiting to hear more of the book (4). For this purpose, independent willingnessto-wait data per book were obtained from a sample of 86 participants (38 female, 48 male; mean age = 35.7 years, SD= 8.6 years), who completed the task as part of a previous study (for a full report of the methods and results, see Experiment 1 of McGettigan et al., 2026).

These data comprised trialwise enjoyment ratings (1-9; mean centred for analysis), comprehension accuracy (1 = correct,0 = incorrect), and waiting decision (1 = wait,0 = skip).

Results

Within-sample effects of Narrator-to-story Fit on Enjoyment

Mean Fit ratings per book were not predictive of enjoyment ratings from the same participant sample (Estimate = 0.02 [95% CIs: -0.01 -0.04], χ² = 2.04, p = .153), but enjoyment was significantly higher when the listener rated the item's Fit as higher than the item mean (Estimate = 0.03 [95% CIs: 0.02 -0.04], χ² = 39.05, p < .001; Figure 4). This profile of results aligns with the within-sample analyses reported for Experiment 1 of the current study. Between-samples effects of Narrator-to-story Fit on Enjoyment, Comprehension, and Motivation When modelling consensus ratings of Fit per story across participant samples, we found no evidence of significant predictions for enjoyment (Estimate = -0.01 [95% CIs: -0.03 -0.02], χ² = 0.32, p = .574), comprehension (OR = 1.00 [95% CIs: 0.95 -1.04], χ² = 0.02, p = .879), or motivation (OR = 0.97 [95% CIs: 0.94 -1.01], χ² = 1.88, p = 1.70). While this pattern of findings aligns with the null effects seen for consensus manipulations of gender congruency in Experiment 1, the findings are partly contradictory for motivation, as this was significantly predicted by greater gender congruence and greater (within-sample) consensus fit ratings in Experiment 1.

General Discussion

The current study presents two novel experiments investigating how the fit between the sound of a narrator and the content of the book being narrated influences the listener's experience and their behaviours in response to the story. Overall, the findings show that while aspects of fit can significantly predict how much listeners enjoy and engage with a story, these effects are neither consistent across measures, nor across listener samples. Specifically, while both a between-sample, consensus-derived categorical manipulation of narrator-to-story gender congruence as well as within-sample consensus ratings of narrator-to-story fit could significantly predict listeners' motivation to continue listening to a story, listening enjoyment was much more dependent on listener-specific impressions of fit. Listening comprehension was consistently insensitive to any measure or manipulation of narrator-to-story fit.

A key outcome of the current findings is that enjoyment and waiting -or, "liking" and "wanting" (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2008) -were differentially sensitive to manipulated or rated variation in narrator-to-story fit. While both ratings of enjoyment and the choice to continue engaging with a book were expressed as conscious decisions on the part of the listener, the latter was nonetheless sensitive to the "wisdom of the crowd". In Experiment 1, the categorical manipulation of gender-congruence was determined based on an independent group of readers' ratings of the "ideal" narrator's gender. The findings showed that pairing a book that gave those readers a strong expectation of a male or female narrator with an audio narration categorically matching that expectation was sufficient to increase engagement, via more frequent waiting decisions, in an independent group of listeners. Similarly, consensus-based mean ratings of overall narrator-to-story fit collected from the group of listeners in Experiment 1 significantly predicted the same sample's waiting decisions in response to gender-congruent and gender-incongruent narrations. Mechanistically, the dissociation between enjoyment and motivation in Experiment 1 suggests that categorical manipulations of gender congruence (and the related perception of narrator-to-story fit) do not operate directly on hedonistic pleasure responses but may otherwise affect the assignment of value to the book and, consequently, the strength of the incentive to continue listening. This may come about due to the increased cognitive cost of integrating a narrative with an unexpected narrator (e.g. Lattner & Friederici, 2003), rather than due to an incongruent narration being less enjoyable per se.

The sensitivity of behavioural motivation to gender congruence presents the intriguing possibility that there are aspects of audiobook narration that could be selectively designed to extend listening engagement, thereby increasing exposure to books and their potential benefits (Mol & Bus, 2011). However, it is important to note that the robustness of consensus data was limited in the current study, as Experiment 2 found that consensus ratings of narratorto-story fit for a larger set of 20 stories did not predict waiting decisions in a fully independent participant sample. There are a number of possible reasons why effects did not generalise across these two samples. First, the presence of two narrators in Experiment 1 may have been sufficiently more salient to engage listeners with narrator identity in the willingness-to-wait task than the single female-sounding narrator in Experiment 2. Second, the gender congruence manipulation in Experiment 1 may have been more effective in modulating the ease of integration of information during listening, in line with previous findings of neural and behavioural sensitivities to apparent conflicts between the perceived gender of a speaker and the meaning of the spoken message (Casado et al., 2023; Lattner & Friederici, 2003; Porkert et al., 2024; Sommers & Danielson, 1999). For example, one excerpt with first-person narration that explicitly mentions being 35 weeks pregnant yielded post-hoc narrator-to-story fit ratings of 82.4 for the female-sounding narrator and 25.7 for the male narrator. In contrast, the range of mean suitability ratings for all 20 books in Experiment 2, which were narrated in a female-sounding voice, ran from 51.9 (for an excerpt about a men's prison) to 74.8 (for the aforementioned excerpt about a pregnant person). A final consideration is that the listeners in Experiment 1 were providing post-hoc ratings of narrator-to-story fit, and thus their ratings may not be fully independent of the decisions they made within the willingness-to-wait task (i.e. "I previously waited to hear more of this one, so I should rate it as highly suitable"). Here, the nature of the waiting data are instructive: with a completely free choice to "wait" or "skip", the overall likelihood of waiting can be low (Bains et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2025; McGettigan et al., 2026) and in the current study 53% of participants in the final sample did not wait on any trial, yet these same participants showed significant sensitivity to the gender congruence manipulation in their narrator-to-story fit ratings (Estimate = 27.9 [95% CIs: 9.22 -46.58], χ² = 263.09, p = .02001; model included random slopes and intercepts by participants and books).

In conclusion, it is most likely that consensus estimates of narrator-to-story fit can be impactful on motivated behaviour when the range or contrast of fit is sufficiently salient to disrupt the integration of the voice and the spoken message.

In contrast to motivated behaviour, measures of listening enjoyment showed a much greater dependence on the individual listener's evaluation of the narrator-to-story fit. Specifically, participant-specific trialwise deviations from mean fit ratings were significantly able to predict trial-specific ratings of story enjoyment in both Experiment 1 and Experiment 2: when a participant rated a narrative higher than the aggregate rating, they enjoyed it more. In contrast, the aggregate (or consensus) rating was independently unable to predict within-sample enjoyment, in either experiment. This is a clear demonstration of the importance of personal taste in shaping the experience of narrative listening. The finding aligns with our previous findings that listeners enjoy narratives more, and are more willing to continue listening, when they are narrated in voices that are personally familiar to them (i.e. their own voice, the voice of a friend; McGettigan et al., 2026) -personally-familiar and personally-valued voices have separately been shown to implicate reward-related brain circuitry (Abrams et al., 2016 (Abrams et al., , 2019 (Abrams et al., , 2022;; Kanber et al., 2025) and motivated behaviour in comparison with less valued familiar and unfamiliar voices (Kanber et al., 2025). In our previous study of narrative listening (McGettigan et al., 2026), which used voice identity cloning to replicate voice identity, we also found that enjoyment of book excerpts was significantly predicted by the extent to which the listener reporting liking the sound of the familiar narrator's voice. Taken together, our current findings and those of relevant previous studies emphasise the personal nature of affective and hedonistic experience when listening to voices. If enjoyment is a crucial lever with which to promote engagement with narrative listening, these findings in turn suggest the potential importance of personalising the listening experience, to yield gains in engagement via boosts in enjoyment.

However, it is not the case that enjoyment is completely insensitive to consensus-based intervention. In Experiment 2 of McGettigan et al. (2026), the experimental design directly targeted the perceived pleasantness of the narrator voice, regardless of its relationship to the text being spoken, and used a manipulation based on independent aggregate ratings. The study -which used the same stories as the current Experiment 2 -found that there was a significant and selective boost to the rated enjoyment of stories when these were delivered in a more pleasant-sounding voice. This finding indicates that narrative listening enjoyment is not entirely personal. Mechanistically, some voices are rapidly evaluated as more attractive than others, and with very good agreement across listeners (Lavan, 2023; Lavan et al., 2024; Mileva & Lavan, 2023). The experience of a pleasant or attractive voice may therefore hold some level of intrinsic value that can generalise across listeners. In the context of narrative listening, the simple effect of vocal pleasantness on enjoyment may also explain the observation that some audiobook listeners will even tolerate listening to poor quality books simply because of their love of the narrator (Spjeldnæs & Karlsen, 2024). Given the demonstrated positive relationship between enjoyment, comprehension, and motivation during listening (McGettigan et al., 2026), targeting narrator pleasantness or attractiveness may be one means by which audiobook creators can enhance listening experiences and outcomes when a better "fit" to the story is not available (and there is no functionality to allow listeners to make their own choices).

Our findings further contribute to developing theoretical accounts of narrative listening. The Model of Listening Engagement (MoLE) considers the interplay of engagement (recruitment of resources), motivation (willingness to recruit those resources), and experiences (enjoyment, boredom, effort) in narrative listening (Herrmann & Johnsrude, 2020). The MoLE posits that listening motivation can influence both engagement and experiences, while experiences can only directly modulate motivation (and not engagement). Our core willingness-to-wait measures repeatedly align with this proposal: increased situational enjoyment of a narrative significantly predicted the decision to wait in Experiment 1 of the current paper, and in all three experiments of McGettigan et al. (2026). Our further findings in relation to both narrator voice identity and its relationship to the narrative's content then suggest how the properties of a narrative itself -i.e. qualities external to the listener -can shape this underlying mechanism.

Here, it is perhaps of greatest relevance to the development of the model that adjustments to narrative voice identity can dissociate in their impacts on listening experiences (here, enjoyment) and motivation (here, the willingness to continue listening).

The current study immediately presents several pathways for further investigation. One unresolved question is the degree to which narrator-to-story fit "breaks through" into the situational listening experience. On the one hand, our findings show that a relatively drastic categorical manipulation of gender congruency yielded variation in perceived narrator fit sufficient to impact enjoyment and engagement (Experiment 1) but this did not replicate to a wider story set and different narrator-story pairing (Experiment 2). Future work should explore whether a wider range of aggregate estimates of fit would indeed continuously predict independent listeners' enjoyment and engagement across a larger number of narratives.

Another dimension worth exploring is the degree to which the listener's experience of narratorto-story fit might depend on their explicit consideration of fit during situational listening: In Experiment 2, listeners were aware that they should rate both enjoyment and fit per trial, and therefore may have paid greater attention to fit as a component of their enjoyment. Although this cannot explain the dissociation between individual and consensus fit ratings within that same experiment, it would be beneficial to compare the within-group findings from Experiment 2 to an experiment in which participants first listen to and rate all stories for enjoyment and then separately consider narrator-to-story fit. However, these relationships between perceived fit and experienced enjoyment and engagement would still be estimated retroactively. An altogether more suitable approach, and one that may lend itself more cleanly to application, would be to examine narrator-to-story fit in the context of allowing listeners to proactively choose or design a narrator for their narrative listening experience. Bains et al. (2025) found that offering readers a choice of book title or genre was sufficient to significantly boost their situational enjoyment of the chosen book's synopsis, compared with having no choice. This may be related to increased agency over the experience, which could also apply to having a choice of narrator voice -for example, previous work has shown that choosing a voice to represent the self is associated with stronger perceptual prioritisation of that voice compared with being assigned a self-voice (Payne et al., 2021). However, the act of choosing (or designing) a narrator also allows the listener to freely and non-prescriptively consider their individual needs and preferences in relation to the listening experience. This could include the fit between the perceived narrator and the book genre or sample content, and/or more global considerations of what sound "good" in terms of voice attractiveness or other perceived attributes like masculinity or accent. We predict that offering listeners the opportunity to choose a narrator should increase enjoyment, with concomitant boosts to comprehension and waiting, compared with having no choice -within this, there would then be opportunities to determine whether listener choices that boost enjoyment map well onto individual or aggregate estimates of concepts including narrator-to-story fit.
