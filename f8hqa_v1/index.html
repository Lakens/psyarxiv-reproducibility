Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

Stroke risk is assessed using factors such as hypertension, diabetes, smoking and atrial fibrillation. However, these factors describe sources of risk but do not reveal how long-term vascular, metabolic and cardiac stress has affected the brain. Structural MRI can show accumulated brain injury, whereas functional MRI reflects dynamic brain states and may provide complementary information about vulnerability. Their temporal sequence and predictive value require longitudinal validation. This Review evaluates brain imaging features as candidate stroke-risk markers and proposes the central autonomic network as a mechanistic framework linking brain function with cardiac rhythm, blood pressure, inflammation and metabolism.

Overview of structural and functional brain-imaging markers relevant to ischemic stroke risk and the proposed CAN framework for stroke vulnerability.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke remains a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide, with approximately 7.8 million new cases in 2021. 1 Current prevention and risk stratification largely rely on established vascular and cardiac risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, dyslipidaemia and atrial fibrillation, [2] [3] together with clinical risk models derived from these factors. [4] [5] [6] However, these measures primarily capture an individual's risk exposure and do not directly reflect the cumulative effects of long-term vascular, metabolic and cardiac stress on the brain. The brain itself may therefore contain information about stroke susceptibility that is not captured by conventional clinical measures.

Brain MRI provides a window into these risk-related cerebral phenotypes. Structural MRI markers, including cerebral small-vessel-disease burden, brain atrophy and white-matter microstructural injury, reflect accumulated brain damage, and several have been associated with subsequent stroke in longitudinal studies. [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] By contrast, functional MRI can characterize more dynamic aspects of brain function, including spontaneous activity, functional connectivity, network organization and neurovascular coupling. However, existing functional imaging studies have largely focused on populations with elevated or related stroke risk, and the reported abnormalities vary substantially in anatomical distribution, network involvement and direction of effects. No stable, specific and reproducible functional imaging phenotype of stroke risk has yet emerged, and direct prospective validation against first-ever incident stroke remains lacking. [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] Given this heterogeneity, the challenge is not only to identify reproducible regional abnormalities, but also to determine whether these functional alterations can be interpreted within a physiologically grounded and prospectively testable mechanistic framework. The central autonomic network (CAN) is one such candidate framework because it participates in the regulation of cardiac rhythm and heart rate, blood pressure, inflammation and metabolic homeostasis, all of which are closely related to stroke susceptibility. [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] This Review summarizes structural MRI markers supported by longitudinal stroke-outcome data, then evaluates functional MRI phenotypes observed in stroke-risk states, and finally examines whether the CAN can serve as a mechanistically driven candidate framework for understanding the relationship between these functional alterations and stroke-related physiological processes, while generating testable hypotheses for prospective stroke-risk research.

Search strategy and selection criteria

We conducted a structured literature search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science from database inception through July 2026 to identify studies examining brain imaging in relation to ischemic stroke risk, supplemented by screening the reference lists for relevant articles. Eligible studies primarily examined structural or functional brain imaging in relation to stroke risk or stroke-related high-risk conditions, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, and cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD). For structural imaging, priority was given to prospective and longitudinal studies with stroke outcomes; for functional imaging and CAN-related evidence, key clinical, physiological, and mechanistic studies were also included.

Structural Brain Predictors of Stroke Risk

Structural MRI characterizes brain tissue integrity and the burden of cerebral small vessel disease and currently represents the most mature component of brain-imaging research on stroke prediction. 7, 11, [23] [24] Relevant markers can be grouped into total brain volume, imaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease, and white matter microstructural integrity (Table 1). These measures primarily reflect the cumulative effects of previous or ongoing vascular risk exposure within the brain and may, to some extent, index the brain reserve available to withstand subsequent ischemic injury.

Total brain volume (TBV)

The assessment of total brain volume (TBV), which combines gray and white matter, is commonly used as a global structural marker of brain integrity. 25 Reduced TBV is widely regarded as an imaging marker of brain atrophy and may reflect the cumulative effects of chronic ischemia and vascular injury. 26 Lower TBV is associated with cSVD, 26 and may also reflect the cumulative impact of vascular risk factors, such as chronic hypertension 27 and inflammation. 28 Earlier studies reported that individuals with higher vascular risk scores had smaller TBV. 29 In the Framingham Heart Study, each standard deviation decrease in total cerebral brain volume (TCBV) was associated with a 51% higher risk of incident stroke (HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.13-2.01). 7 This suggests that brain atrophy may reflect a cumulative burden of vascular injury and may serve as a proxy for overall brain health and resilience.

White matter hyperintensities (WMH)

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) are areas of increased signal intensity in the white matter on T2-weighted or FLAIR MRI and are commonly regarded as imaging markers of cSVD. 24 Pathologically, they are thought to reflect chronic ischemic injury and may also involve demyelination and blood-brain barrier disruption. [30] [31] Prospective studies consistently show that WMH burden is associated with both incident and recurrent ischemic stroke. 8, 32 In the Cardiovascular Health Study, annual stroke incidence was 2.8% in individuals with severe WMH versus 0.6% in those with minimal WMH. 8 Similarly, in a large cohort of 7,101 patients with ischemic stroke, higher WMH volume quartiles were associated with a stepwise increase in 1-year risk of recurrent ischemic stroke (HR 1.03, 1.37, and 1.61 across quartiles vs. the lowest). 32 Notably, WMH characteristics beyond total burden may also be clinically relevant. In the SMART-MR Study, WMH volume, type, and shape were associated with long-term risk of mortality and ischemic stroke. 33 Together, these findings indicate that WMH burden and morphology capture clinically relevant aspects of cSVD-related brain injury and may contribute to stroke risk stratification.

Cerebral microbleeds (CMB)

Cerebral microbleeds (CMB) are small, round or ovoid hypointense lesions seen on T2*weighted or susceptibility-weighted MRI and are generally regarded as imaging markers of cSVD. 24 They are thought to reflect hemosiderin deposition after prior microscopic bleeding. 34 In the population-based Rotterdam Study, cerebral microbleeds were associated with an increased risk of stroke overall, whereas microbleeds at non-Cerebral-Amyloid-Angiopathy (CAA)-related locations were associated with ischemic stroke. 9 This distinction is critical: CMB in deep or infratentorial regions (e.g., basal ganglia, brainstem) are more likely to reflect hypertensive arteriopathy, while lobar CMB are more often linked to CAA. 34 A higher CMB burden was associated with greater stroke risk, supporting a dose-response relationship between microvascular injury and future cerebrovascular events. 35 This suggests that CMB may reflect microvascular fragility and a heightened risk of both hemorrhagic and ischemic events, possibly due to impaired vascular autoregulation and endothelial dysfunction.

Lacunes

Lacunes are round or ovoid, fluid-filled cavities measuring 3-15 mm in diameter in the deep gray or white matter, brainstem, or pons, and are generally regarded as neuroimaging markers of cSVD. 24 They are thought to represent the chronic stage of a prior small subcortical infarct or, less commonly, a small hemorrhage. Evidence suggests that a greater lacune burden, particularly the presence of multiple lacunes, is associated with an increased risk of recurrent ischemic stroke. 10 In a Danish cohort of 786 patients, single silent lacunes were associated with a numerically higher but non-significant risk of recurrence (HR 1.53, 95% CI 0.67-3.49), whereas multiple lacunes were associated with a significantly higher risk of recurrent ischemic stroke (HR 2.52, 95% CI 1.25-5.09). 10 This dose-response relationship underscores the cumulative impact of small vessel injury on stroke vulnerability.

White matter microstructural integrity

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is widely used to characterize white matter microstructural integrity. 36 Commonly derived measures include fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), axial diffusivity (AD), 37 and the more recent peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD). [38] [39] These indices are sensitive to subtle abnormalities in normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) 40 and are thought to reflect alterations related to myelin and axonal integrity as well as white matter organization. 41 Because DTI can detect microstructural injury before macroscopic WMH becomes visible, it may serve as a sensitive early marker of cSVD. 38, 40 Evidence from large population-based cohorts, notably the Rotterdam Study, has shown that lower FA and higher MD within NAWM are associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke, independent of age, blood pressure, WMH volume, and other vascular risk factors. 11 Recent genetic evidence further supports the relevance of white matter microstructural pathways to ischemic stroke risk. In a UK Biobank Mendelian randomization study, blood pressure was found to influence ischemic stroke risk partly through diffusion-related IDPs in white matter tracts, particularly the anterior limb of the internal capsule and the superior fronto-occipital fasciculus. 42 (Figure 1) Longitudinal evidence suggests that baseline NAWM microstructural abnormalities may predict subsequent periventricular WMH expansion. In a follow-up study, lower FA and higher MD in NAWM were associated with new periventricular WMH voxels, supporting the value of DTI measures for identifying white matter regions vulnerable to future WMH growth. Structural MRI markers have a relatively substantial longitudinal evidence base: white matter hyperintensities, greater lacune burden, cerebral microbleeds, and white matter microstructural abnormalities have all been associated with subsequent cerebrovascular events. [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] Overall, these measures have relatively good measurement stability and clinical accessibility, and imaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease have relatively wellestablished definitions and reporting standards. 24 Their main limitations are that most reflect tissue injury that has already occurred and have limited etiological specificity. Future research should move beyond repeatedly establishing associations with stroke and determine whether individual or combined structural markers provide independent, stable, and clinically actionable incremental value beyond traditional risk models.

Functional Brain Imaging in Stroke Risk

Whereas structural MRI reflects cumulative tissue injury, functional MRI (fMRI) characterizes dynamic aspects of brain function -local spontaneous activity, functional connectivity and network organization, and neurovascular coupling -and thus may capture information not evident structurally. To date, however, no study has acquired functional MRI (fMRI) in stroke-free individuals and shown that it predicts first-ever incident stroke. The available evidence comes almost entirely from cross-sectional comparisons in populations with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, atrial fibrillation, transient ischemic attack (TIA), or cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD), or from associations with blood pressure, structural injury, or cognition rather than stroke. [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [44] [45] [46] [47] [48] [49] [50] [51] [52] Three features recur across this literature and should be kept in mind throughout: findings are anatomically heterogeneous and directionally inconsistent; the measures are derived from the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal and are therefore sensitive to cerebral hemodynamics and systemic physiology as well as to neural activity; [53] [54] and reproducibility is constrained by limited test-retest reliability and by analytic choices such as parcellation, motion correction, and physiological-noise removal. [54] [55] [56] [57] These constraints, more than any single regional finding, explain why no reproducible functional phenotype of stroke risk has yet emerged.

Local Spontaneous Activity

Resting-state measures of local activity capture distinct properties of the BOLD signal and are not interchangeable: the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) indexes fluctuation amplitude, fractional ALFF (fALFF) its relative spectral contribution, and regional homogeneity (ReHo) local temporal synchronization. [58] [59] [60] Studies in hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and cSVD report abnormalities in all three, but neither the anatomical distribution nor the direction of change is consistent. 12, [44] [45] [46] [61] [62] [63] [64] [65] [66] [67] The precuneus illustrates the problem: reduced ALFF has been reported in subcortical ischemic vascular disease and in lacunar infarction with mild cognitive impairment, 12, 61 increased precuneus ALFF in leukoaraiosis without cognitive impairment, 62 and type 2 diabetes studies disagree even on the sign of change. [64] [65] The fALFF and ReHo literatures are equally unstable -a single-centre study found hypertension-related fALFF abnormalities whereas a UK Biobank analysis (2,054 hypertensive vs. 1,724 controls) identified no hypertension-specific pattern, 44, 67 and TIA ReHo findings range from reduced prefrontal-cingulate synchronization to no significant group difference in a larger sample. 46, 66, 68 Because these measures are cross-sectional and many participants already had cerebrovascular injury or TIA confirmed by imaging, an increase or decrease cannot be assigned a consistent pathophysiological meaning; they are best regarded as candidate state phenotypes rather than individual-level predictors.

Functional Connectivity and Network Topology

Functional connectivity -the statistical association between regional BOLD time series, assessed by seed-based, independent-component, or graph-theoretical approaches, and not equivalent to anatomical connectivity or directed information transfer [69] [70] [71] -is altered across hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and atrial fibrillation. [47] [48] [49] The most informative evidence links connectivity to continuous vascular exposure: in more than 30,000 UK Biobank participants, whole-brain connectivity was associated with prior, current, and future blood pressure, involving prefrontal, insular, cingulate, precuneus, and cerebellar nodes across default-mode and frontoparietal systems, 72 and in CARDIA, cumulative systolic exposure related to executive-control-network connectivity, WMH burden, and executive function. 73 In cSVD, a systematic review of 44 studies described default-mode, attention, frontoparietal, and salience network alterations, with graph-theoretical abnormalities in efficiency, path length, and nodal organization. 16, [74] [75] [76] However, the networks implicated and the direction of change vary across studies, no reproducible disease-specific signature has emerged, 16 and the outcomes have been blood pressure, brain injury, or cognition -not first-ever stroke. Whether connectivity or topology predicts incident stroke beyond clinical risk factors and structural MRI is therefore untested.

Neurovascular Coupling

Neurovascular coupling indexes the matching of neural activity to local blood-flow supply, estimated in humans from task-evoked responses or from resting-state cerebral blood flow (CBF)-ALFF, CBF-ReHo, or CBF-connectivity coupling, [77] [78] and is mechanistically closer to vascular vulnerability than isolated activity or connectivity measures. A meta-analysis reported reduced coupling with greater small-vessel-disease and WMH burden (26/29 and 25/28 studies), 79 though it pooled fMRI, transcranial Doppler, and other techniques and so supports the general mechanistic conclusion rather than any single fMRI measure; altered regional CBF-connectivity matching has also been reported in individuals with WMH. 51 Impaired coupling is not confined to established disease: among 31,934 UK Biobank participants without known cerebrovascular disease, higher cardiovascular risk was associated with lower task-evoked responses, 80 and abnormal coupling has been reported in type 2 diabetes. 52 The limited longitudinal data -for example, reduced ReHo-CBF coupling in the insula over five years in 27 patients with diabetes, tracking memory rather than stroke 15 -cannot establish predictive value. Because coupling is also shaped by blood pressure, heart rate, respiration, CO₂, and vasoactive medication, 54, 81 it remains uncertain whether observed abnormalities represent early dysregulation or a consequence of existing injury; it too should be treated as a candidate phenotype.

Functional Imaging and Stroke Prediction

Taken together, systemic vascular risk gives rise to functional brain phenotypes that fMRI can detect, but the evidence is cross-sectional and associational and does not show that any measure identifies which individuals will later have a stroke. Establishing predictive value would require functional MRI acquired in stroke-free individuals at baseline, first-ever stroke as the prospective outcome, adequate within-individual stability, and demonstrable incremental value beyond established risk factors and structural MRI. Until that evidence chain exists, local activity, functional connectivity, and neurovascular coupling should be regarded as candidate phenotypes of brain vulnerability rather than validated predictors of stroke.

Central Autonomic Network and Stroke Risk

Existing functional imaging studies have not identified a stable, specific, and reproducible phenotype of stroke risk. This may be because stroke susceptibility is not determined by functional abnormalities in a single brain region but reflects the long-term dysregulation of multiple physiological processes -heart rate and cardiac rhythm, blood pressure, inflammation, and metabolism. The central autonomic network (CAN) participates in the central regulation of all four and links brain function with peripheral physiological activity. [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] We therefore propose the CAN not as an established stroke-specific signature but as an organizing framework -a way to specify, a priori, which regions to examine and why. The subsections below set out the biological plausibility of this proposal: that CAN circuits regulate cardiac autonomic tone, blood pressure, inflammation, and metabolism, each independently linked to stroke. We stress at the outset that this plausibility is not evidence of prediction, since no study has explicitly related CAN-derived imaging measures to incident stroke, and the links assembled below are associational.

Definitions of the CAN vary across conceptual and neuroimaging frameworks. Across neuroimaging meta-analyses, the most consistent convergence is observed in the bilateral insula and midcingulate cortex, which are treated here as the imaging-defined core, while the inferior parietal lobule forms part of an extended functional network. [82] [83] Broader conceptual and neuroimaging frameworks additionally include the amygdala, medial prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortices, hypothalamus, hippocampal formation, mediodorsal thalamus, precuneus, cerebellum, periaqueductal gray, parabrachial complex, nucleus tractus solitarius, and ventrolateral medulla.

The CAN and cardiac autonomic regulation

Under physiological conditions, the CAN coordinates parasympathetic and sympathetic outflow through interconnected insula, cingulate cortex (ACC), hypothalamus and brainstem circuits, contributing to cardiovascular autonomic regulation and heart-rate variability (HRV). 17, [21] [22] Within this network, the insula is a key cortical region for autonomic cardiovascular control, as shown by insular cortex stimulation studies. 84 Clinically, insular involvement has been associated with reduced HRV and a higher risk of arrhythmias or other adverse cardiac manifestations. 85 Rs-fMRI studies in stroke high-risk populations, particularly in cSVD, have shown alterations in intrinsic networks, including salience-network regions such as the insula and ACC, as well as altered local spontaneous activity reflected by changes in amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) and regional homogeneity (ReHo). 16, 68 HRV-fMRI coupling provides a more direct functional readout of autonomic-central interactions. 86 Prospective population-based evidence links peripheral autonomic imbalance -higher resting heart rate and lower heart-rate variability -to incident stroke, 87 establishing that autonomic dysregulation carries stroke risk. This peripheral evidence does not, however, show that central, CAN-derived imaging measures predict stroke: it motivates, rather than validates, their prospective evaluation.

The CAN and blood pressure regulation

Beyond heart rate and rhythm regulation, the CAN is also involved in central blood pressure control. Early electrical stimulation studies in epilepsy patients showed that insular stimulation could induce bradycardia and depressor responses. 84 A later electrical stimulation study in patients with epilepsy suggested a direct role of the insula in central blood pressure regulation. 88 Rs-fMRI studies further demonstrated that functional connectivity among CAN-related regions, including the insula, anterior cingulate cortex, thalamus, amygdala, and brainstem, was associated with baroreflex sensitivity, supporting a close link between CAN network state and blood pressure regulatory function. 89 Furthermore, in APOE ε4 carriers, lower resting-state CAN functional connectivity strengthened the association between beat-to-beat blood pressure variability and cSVD burden, whereas CAN connectivity did not mediate this relationship. 90 Meanwhile, higher blood pressure variability has been shown to predict subsequent stroke independently of mean blood pressure and to be associated with greater cSVD burden, particularly WMH. 91- 92 Taken together, these observations are consistent with a link between altered CAN functional connectivity, blood-pressure instability, and small-vessel brain injury; whether this constellation carries information about future stroke, beyond blood pressure itself, remains to be tested prospectively.

The CAN and inflammatory regulation

The CAN is also involved in the central regulation of inflammation. Vagus nerve stimulation has been reported to inhibit peripheral inflammatory markers, including TNF, IL-6, and IL-1β. 93 In a previous study, lower HRV was also associated with higher levels of C-reactive protein and interleukin-6, suggesting a link between autonomic regulation and systemic inflammatory burden. 94 At the functional imaging level, higher peripheral IL-6 levels were associated with stronger functional connectivity of the subgenual ACC within the DMN. 95 In addition, a double-blind randomized study showed that acute systemic inflammation induced by low-dose lipopolysaccharide increased resting-state connectivity between the left anterior insula and the left midcingulate cortex. 96 Inflammation is also linked to cSVD, with higher CRP and IL-6 levels being associated with greater WMH burden. 97 Prospective evidence has further shown that higher circulating IL-6 levels are associated with an increased risk of incident ischemic stroke. 98 Collectively, these findings suggest that altered inflammatory regulation may represent one pathway linking CAN-related alterations to cSVD-related WMH and increased stroke susceptibility.

The CAN and metabolic regulation

The CAN-related circuits are also involved in the central regulation of metabolic homeostasis. For example, in patients with refractory obesity, hypothalamic deep brain stimulation has been reported to acutely increase resting metabolic rate and sleep energy expenditure, suggesting that CAN-related circuits may contribute to energy balance regulation. 99 Meanwhile, in patients with type 2 diabetes, an rs-fMRI study found increased degree centrality in the right anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, suggesting stronger functional connections with other brain regions. Stronger connectivity was also observed between the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal regions. 100 In addition, at the structural level, metabolic syndrome has been associated with a greater WMH burden, whereas type 2 diabetes has been linked to cortical thinning and reduced brain volume. [101] [102] [103] In addition, large longitudinal studies have shown that type 2 diabetes is associated with an increased risk of first stroke, with poorer glycemic control associated with higher stroke risk. 104 Overall, alterations in CAN-related circuits may be associated with cSVD-related brain injury and increased stroke risk, partly through their relationship with altered metabolic regulation.

Taken together, current evidence suggests that CAN dysfunction may provide a biologically plausible mechanistic framework linking autonomic, vascular, inflammatory, and metabolic dysregulation with cSVD-related brain injury and stroke susceptibility (Figure 3). However, the role of the CAN in stroke susceptibility remains largely inferential, and prospective studies directly linking CAN-related functional alterations to incident stroke are still lacking. CAN-related functional measures should therefore be regarded as candidate markers requiring prospective validation rather than established predictors of stroke risk.

Before considering how such markers might be validated and translated into clinical practice, it is useful to place them within the broader landscape of imaging biomarkers in neurological disease.

Positioning Brain-Based Stroke Prediction in the Wider Biomarker Landscape

The proposition that brain imaging can index the risk of a systemic vascular event is rare in stroke medicine, but it is not without precedent. The closest analogy is Alzheimer's disease, where imaging biomarkers have traveled the full distance from descriptive correlates to a biologically defined framework that now guides trial enrollment and therapeutic decisions. [105] [106] That trajectory is instructive in three respects.

First, biomarkers gained traction only once they were anchored to an explicit mechanistic model rather than accumulated as a list of group differences. The amyloid-tauneurodegeneration construct succeeded because each marker was assigned a defined position in a hypothesized causal sequence. 107 The CAN framework proposed here is intended to provide a similar organizing framework for stroke: it specifies a priori which regions should be evaluated and why, and it makes falsifiable predictions-that CANrelated functional measures should relate to autonomic, blood pressure, inflammatory, and metabolic phenotypes, and should carry predictive information beyond established clinical risk factors and structural cSVD burden.

Second, progress depended on methodological harmonization. Standardized data acquisition, common quantitative scales, and shared multicentre infrastructure converted heterogeneous findings into comparable measurements. 105, 108 Stroke research has achieved something similar for structural small-vessel-disease markers, where consensus reporting standards permit WMH, lacunes, and microbleeds to be compared across cohorts. 24 No equivalent standard exists for resting-state functional measures, representing one major obstacle to translation.

Third, progress also depended on establishing the temporal ordering of biomarkers. The corresponding task here is to determine whether CAN functional alterations precede autonomic and hemodynamic instability, which in turn precede accelerating small-vessel injury, or whether the sequence runs in the opposite direction.

Two differences from the neurodegenerative setting shape what success would look like.

Stroke is an acute, partly stochastic event with competing extracerebral causes, not a slowly progressive proteinopathy; and much of its risk is already captured by established clinical instruments. The benchmark for a brain-based marker is therefore not diagnostic classification but demonstrable incremental value over existing risk scores and established structural imaging markers.

Translational Outlook: Toward CAN-Informed Precision Stroke Prevention

If the framework is supported by future prospective studies, its practical consequence may be that stroke risk assessment could incorporate a measure of central regulatory state alongside conventional vascular and cardiac variables. Four developments would be required.

Multimodal imaging integration.

Individual markers are unlikely to be sufficient on their own. A composite CAN-informed phenotype could combine structural burden (WMH volume and shape, lacunes, microbleeds, PSMD), white matter microstructure along CANrelevant pathways, resting-state connectivity within the prespecified cortical CAN node set, and perfusion-based neurovascular coupling. Non-canonical regions should be analysed separately rather than added post hoc to the core node set. Because these modalities capture complementary aspects of brain vulnerability, including accumulated damage, current network state, and vascular responsiveness, their combination may be more informative than any single index and could be summarized in a compact form analogous to established brain-age or global-burden scores.

Data-driven imaging signatures. Machine learning offers a means of deriving such composites empirically rather than by expert weighting, and of detecting spatial patterns that region-of-interest analyses miss. 109 Realizing this will require training on cohorts with prospectively ascertained incident stroke rather than on case-control contrasts, since the latter risk learning the consequences of established disease. Given the modest sample sizes typical of functional imaging studies, external validation in independent cohorts, explicit calibration assessment, and interpretability constraints that keep models anatomically accountable are essential; otherwise apparent performance will not survive replication.

Coupling central imaging with continuous peripheral physiology. Imaging characterizes the central state at a single time point, whereas the pathophysiology proposed here is dynamic. Heart rate variability, baroreflex sensitivity, and beat-to-beat blood pressure variability provide the corresponding peripheral readout, and HRV-fMRI coupling already offers a direct index of central-autonomic interaction. 86 Wearable and ambulatory technologies extend this to continuous, real-world monitoring of rhythm and autonomic tone, 110 allowing a static central phenotype to be paired with longitudinal peripheral output. This combined central-peripheral autonomic phenotype, rather than imaging alone, may therefore represent a promising strategy for future translational studies.

Incorporation into clinical risk models. The decisive test is whether such a phenotype improves prediction beyond established instruments and structural MRI markers. [4] [5] [6] This requires evaluation in prospective cohorts using discrimination, calibration, reclassification, and decision-analytic measures, with attention to whether reclassification occurs in individuals for whom management would actually change. Potential future applications to be tested include stratifying blood-pressure management in patients with high blood pressure variability and CAN dysconnectivity, selecting candidates for extended rhythm monitoring after cryptogenic stroke, 111 and identifying individuals in whom inflammatory or metabolic pathways contribute disproportionately to risk.

Finally, the framework implies an intervention-based test that imaging association studies cannot provide: if CAN dysfunction contributes causally to stroke vulnerability, then interventions that modify central autonomic regulation should alter intermediate phenotypes such as blood pressure variability, inflammatory burden, and arrhythmia propensity in a predictable direction. Such studies would help determine whether CANrelated functional alterations are merely markers of an underlying risk state or mechanistic contributors to its development, moving the CAN from a framework that organizes existing observations to one that generates testable therapeutic hypotheses.

Conclusion

Brain imaging may provide information about stroke susceptibility that is complementary to conventional clinical risk factors. Structural MRI markers reflect accumulated vascular brain injury and are supported by a relatively substantial longitudinal evidence base, whereas functional measures, including local activity, functional connectivity, network organization, and neurovascular coupling, capture more dynamic alterations associated with vascular risk and related cerebrovascular conditions. However, current functional imaging findings remain heterogeneous, and no stable or reproducible functional phenotype of stroke risk has yet been established.

Within this context, the CAN is proposed not as an established stroke-specific imaging signature, but as a mechanistically grounded framework linking central functional states to cardiac autonomic regulation, blood pressure, inflammation, and metabolism. The key question is therefore whether CAN-related functional measures can provide information beyond conventional vascular and cardiac risk factors and structural MRI markers. Addressing this question will require prospective studies in stroke-free populations that directly evaluate incident stroke and test the stability, reproducibility, and incremental predictive value of CAN-related measures. If validated, such measures could contribute to a more integrated assessment of stroke susceptibility.
