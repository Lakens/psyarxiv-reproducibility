Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

As this is a meta-analysis of published studies, no new ethical approval or consent was required.

• Conducted during a replication crisis, this meta-analysis updates prior work, synthesizing 182 effect sizes from 4,089 infants.

• Infants show a consistent preference for prosocial over antisocial agents, with a metaanalytic proportion of 0.65.

• Preference for prosocial agents is robust across age, measure type, stimulus format and scenario, but moderated by sample size.

• No clear evidence of publication bias was detected.

[div-01]

Over the past two decades, a substantial number of studies have examined infants' and toddlers' responses to prosocial agents, who help, comfort, or act fairly, versus antisocial agents, who hinder, harm, or act unfairly. This line of research was spearheaded by Hamlin et al. (2007), who presented 6-and 10-month-old preverbal infants with a scenario in which a central character, a wooden circle with large googly eyes, attempted to climb a hill.

Infants were then introduced to a prosocial agent, who assisted the climber, and an antisocial agent, who obstructed it. When choosing between agents, most of the infants selected the prosocial helper rather than the antisocial hinderer. This initial evidence was followed by numerous studies that extended the finding from a helper-hinderer distinction within the "hill" paradigm (e.g., Hamlin, 2013; Hamlin et al., 2010) to a broader preference for prosocial, morally good agents over antisocial, morally bad ones. Research has documented early-emerging preferences for agents who return resources rather than take them (e.g., Dahl et al., 2013; Hamlin and Wynn, 2011; Scola et al., 2015), distribute resources fairly rather than unfairly (e.g., Burns and Sommerville, 2014; Geraci and Surian, 2011; Lucca et al., 2018), and comfort or protect others from aggression rather than harm or ignore them (e.g., Biro, 2023; Buon et al., 2014; Kanakogi et al., 2017).

An early-emerging capacity to distinguish and prefer prosocial over antisocial agentsthe focus of the present meta-analysis-is regarded as a key piece of evidence in debates on whether the foundations of human morality are built-in and present before the second birthday, equipping infants to navigate their social world, or whether they emerge only through extensive socialization and, potentially, language development beyond infancy (Hamlin, 2015b; Laurence and Margolis, 2024; Thomsen and Carey, 2013). Contributing to the debate are also studies that examine infants' and toddlers' expectations about others' behavior. This research shows that children under two years of age are sensitive to violations of several foundational moral principles (Baillargeon et al., 2026; Ting et al., 2020), including harm avoidance (Ting and Baillargeon, 2021), fairness-both distributive (e.g., Meristo and Surian, 2013; Sloane et al., 2012) and procedural (Surian and Margoni, 2020)-as well as ingroup support (e.g., Jin and Baillargeon, 2017; Pun et al., 2021), authority, which defines moral obligations for leaders and subordinates (Margoni and Thomsen, 2024; Margoni et al., 2018; Stavans and Baillargeon, 2019), and reciprocity (Jin et al., 2024). However, the present metaanalysis is restricted to studies examining whether infants and toddlers exhibit a preference for prosocial over antisocial agents-that is, whether they display early-emerging, intuitive evaluations that may serve as precursors and continue to underlie adult moral judgments.

Critically, accumulating evidence of replication failures-including a notable multi-lab attempt-challenges claims of robust early-emerging preferences for prosociality. To start, two studies attempting to replicate the seminal findings by Hamlin and colleagues failed to reproduce the effect: Salvadori et al. (2015) did not replicate Hamlin and Wynn (2011)'s results, and Schlingloff et al. (2020) reported similar null findings when revisiting Hamlin et al. (2007). A number of studies have also failed to detect a reliable preference for prosociality across different paradigms and scenarios (Cowell and Decety, 2015; Holvoet et al., 2018 Holvoet et al., , 2019;; Licht et al., 2023; Nava et al., 2019; Scarf et al., 2012; Surian and Margoni, 2020; Tan and Hamlin, 2022, 2024; Vaporova and Zmyj, 2020; Wu et al., 2025). Unpublished work further contributes to this picture, with several reports of null findings or failed replications (e.g., Abramson et al., 2016; Hamlin, 2016 Hamlin, , 2025;; Woo and Hamlin, 2016; Yuen and Hamlin, 2021). Not all of these null findings, however, necessarily indicate genuine replication failures.

Some have been reinterpreted in light of methodological differences (Woo et al., 2025); for example, Scarf et al. (2012)'s results were later argued by Hamlin (2015a) to stem from variations in the stimuli used. Others may simply reflect variability expected from sampling error, as discussed in Margoni and Shepperd (2020). Nevertheless, concerns regarding the robustness of infants' preferences for prosociality remain well-founded. This is exemplified by the recent ManyBabies large-scale, multi-lab collaboration (Lucca et al., 2025), which failed to reproduce the original effect reported by Hamlin et al. (2007), finding that only about 49% of infants (total N = 302 in the social condition) preferred the helper over the hinderer. This recent large-scale null result substantially reinforces existing concerns and signals that the field is experiencing a moment of crisis (Open Science Collaboration, 2015). In this context, meta-analyzing the available data presents a timely opportunity to address unresolved questions. Unlike most meta-analyses, which are typically conducted when enthusiasm in the field is high and a critical mass of positive findings has accumulatedconditions that are likely to inflate effect size estimates (ter Schure and Grünwald, 2019)-the present meta-analysis is conducted precisely amid growing uncertainty. As such, it offers a chance to obtain a more realistic, less inflated estimate of the phenomenon of interest.

The Present Meta-Analysis

The present meta-analysis is a https://aspredicted.org/vjn6-73k3.pdf update and extension of the 2018 metaanalysis (Margoni and Surian, 2018), which synthesized published and unpublished studies retrieved up to 2017 and estimated a publication-bias-adjusted proportion of infants preferring prosocial over antisocial agents at 64% (95% CI [60, 69]). This new meta-analysis updates that estimate and examines potential moderators of heterogeneity across studies, with three main goals.

First, our main objective is to provide an updated estimate of the phenomenon of interest by not only incorporating published and unpublished evidence that appears after 2017, but also by including studies employing preferential looking paradigms alongside manual reaching tasks, which were the sole focus of the Margoni and Surian (2018)'s analysis.

Preferential looking studies were excluded from the original meta-analysis because only a limited number were available at the time, and their exclusion helped preserve methodological homogeneity. Since then, however, the use of visual preference measures has grown substantially, partly reflecting the practical constraints on in-person testing imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic. Because both manual and visual preferences are routinely interpreted as indexing sociomoral preferences in this literature, we included both approaches in the present meta-analysis. This broader scope also allowed us to extend the age range to include infants younger than four months, who were previously excluded. As in Margoni and Surian (2018), we test for publication bias and examine whether published studies report system-atically larger effects than unpublished ones, despite the growing ease of publishing null findings, particularly when studies are preregistered or involve direct replication attempts.

Second, in this meta-analysis, which uses a large dataset comprising about 4,000 participants, we examine whether the age of infants and toddlers (0-32 months, spanning the first 2.5 years of life) moderates their preference for prosocial agents. This question is central because it allows us to evaluate competing theoretical accounts. Finding age-related increases in prosocial choices would support the view that social experience and, possibly, early language development shape infants' sociomoral preferences. In contrast, a rationalist account of the acquisition of prosocial preferences-while fully compatible with developmental change and the influence of social experience-would be particularly strengthened by the absence of significant age effects, together with evidence that infants and toddlers overall show a preference for prosociality. In the present context, such a pattern would suggest that a propensity to prefer prosocial agents emerges very early in life, with limited social experience, and is potentially mediated by domain-specific learning mechanisms (Laurence and Margolis, 2024). More broadly, the presence or absence of developmental change provides a critical test of whether sociomoral preferences are shaped primarily through experience or reflect an early, stable predisposition, thereby further informing the debate between competing theories on the origins of human moral judgment.

Third, we examine several additional moderators to assess the generalizability and consistency of the effect. These include (a) sample size, (b) scenario type (fair/unfair, help/hinder, give/take, other scenarios), (c) measure type (reach, help, visual preference), (d) stimulus format (live shows of real events, movies of real events, movies of cartoons), (e) type of agents presented (human confederates, puppets, shapes), (f) whether a prompt was used to elicit preferences, (g) laboratory affiliation (Hamlin and collaborators, Geraci and collaborators, independent labs), (h) study location, and (i) preregistration status.

(a) We begin with sample size, which is expected to influence the precision of the effect estimates. Small samples can reduce estimate accuracy and are more susceptible to publication bias, potentially inflating estimates. In contrast, large, well-powered tests provide more precise estimates. Consequently, if the true effect is smaller than suggested by the full set of studies, we might expect an inverse relationship between the sample size and the strength of the observed effect. More broadly, examining sample size helps evaluate the reliability and robustness of the overall meta-analytic estimate.

(b) Next, we test whether effect sizes vary across the main scenario types (fair/unfair, help/hinder, give/take, and other scenarios such as comforting/ignoring). Notably, one of the key findings of Margoni and Surian (2018) was that the type of scenario moderated the preference of the infants and toddlers, with help/hinder scenarios yielding significantly weaker effects than give/take scenarios, by nearly 10 percentage points in the average estimate. We therefore test whether this pattern replicates in the present meta-analysis, which also incorporates the data from the large-scale multi-lab replication attempt by Lucca et al. (2025), a study that relied exclusively on a help/hinder paradigm and did not yield significant effects. (c) Additionally, we examine the impact of measure type, that is, whether infants' preferences were inferred from manual choices, helping decisions, or visual attention. Visual preference measures are a novel addition in this meta-analysis, not included in Margoni and Surian (2018). We specifically explore whether visual measures confer an advantage over manual reaching measures, which were employed in most prior failed replications.

Three further factors relate to procedure design. We test whether (d1) scenario delivery mode (movies vs. live shows) and (d2) scenario presentation format (cartoons vs. real events, irrespective of delivery mode) moderate the effect. However, because no studies employed live cartoon shows, we coded a single (d) stimulus format moderator with three levels: live shows of real events, movies of real events, and movies of cartoons. Infants may be predisposed to attend more to real-life social interactions, given that our evolutionary history primarily exposed us to three-dimensional, real-world agents rather than two-dimensional representations. Consequently, live shows or videos of real events might facilitate better processing and understanding. Conversely, cartoons could offer an advantage because their simplified, less perceptually complex stimuli may reduce cognitive demands and make the social interactions easier to follow. While the Margoni and Surian (2018) analysis did not find evidence for an effect of these moderators, we revisit this question using a substantially larger dataset. (e) We also test whether the type of agents presented to infants (human confederates, puppets, or foam shapes) moderates the effect. Based on the reasoning outlined above for the predictions regarding scenario delivery mode and presentation format, interactions involving human confederates might be expected to produce the strongest effects, followed by puppets and foam shapes. (f) Last, we test whether providing infants and toddlers with a prompt to express their preference facilitates task engagement and overall produces stronger effects compared to when no prompt is given.

Next, we investigate potential moderators related to study provenance and preregistration. (g) We test laboratory affiliation (Hamlin's group versus independent labs) as moderator. Given that K. Hamlin initiated this research area and contributed a substantial portion of the data, which might also be affected by a winner's curse effect-i.e., the tendency for initial studies to systematically overestimate true effect sizes (Gelman and Loken, 2014)-and considering several failed replication attempts (reviewed above), we assess whether effect sizes are reliably smaller in studies conducted by independent labs. We exploratorily also considered affiliation with A. Geraci, who has also emerged as a prolific contributor to this field. (h) Moreover, we assess the geographical generalizability of the effect by examining study location, coded by continent (North America, South America, Europe, Asia, and Oceania). (i) Finally, we exploratorily test whether preregistration status (preregistered vs. not preregistered) moderates the effect, as preregistered studies may yield more reliable estimates. More broadly, evaluating the impact of all the methodological factors outlined above provides insight into the robustness and generalizability of infants' preferences for prosociality over antisociality.

Method Literature Search and Inclusion Criteria

The identification of relevant studies was undertaken (between spring and summer 2025) through a systematic search of PsycINFO, MEDLINE, and Google Scholar, conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines (see Figure ?? for the corresponding PRISMA flow diagram). An abstract-level search was performed employing the keywords infant* or toddler* and moral*, help*, hinder*, harm*, fair*, comfort*, prosocial* or prefer*. We thus conducted a search for relevant studies published up to 2025. Studies from the original metaanalysis were directly incorporated, previously excluded studies were re-evaluated under the revised inclusion criteria, and additional eligible studies were identified through database searches. Studies were included according to the following criteria: (a) The study evaluated infants' choices using a manual task (which could involve a manual choice paradigm, a selective helping paradigm, or a paradigm in which children were encouraged to provide goods to one of two characters) or a preferential looking task. We did not include violation of expectation studies. Namely, we included only studies that could reveal infants' sociomoral assessments or preferences (Choi and Luo, 2023), rather than their expectations (measured via the violation-of-expectation paradigm; Margoni et al., 2024). (b) Infants were presented with an opportunity to indicate a preference between a "morally good" and a "morally bad" character. In this context, a morally good character could be depicted as a helping agent, a fair distributor, a giver, or an agent who provides comfort to others or intervenes in their defense when they are subjected to aggression. Conversely, a morally bad character could be portrayed as a hindering agent, an unfair distributor, a keeper/taker, or an agent who disregards others in distress. (c) The sample consisted of infants and toddlers between 0 and 36 months of age. Relative to the original 2018 meta-analysis, the present meta-analysis differs in two main aspects. First, studies employing preferential looking paradigms have been included. Second, the age range has been extended to cover the first four months of life, which were previously excluded.

Following the main search on the databases, we conducted a supplementary screening of the reference lists of the retrieved articles to identify any additional relevant published studies. We also sought unpublished data by issuing calls on relevant online listservs (i.e., COGDEVSOC, DEVEUROPE, ICIS-listserv) and by directly contacting the most prolific authors in the field. This search yielded a total of 65 studies (representing an increase of 39 studies compared to the original meta-analysis) encompassing k = 183 effect sizes (152 published, 31 unpublished), with an aggregate sample of 4,090 unique participants, 2,846 more than in the previous meta-analysis. Note that two studies (k = 3 effect sizes) were initially identified as unpublished but were coded as published in the final analyses because they appeared in print before the analyses were finalized, and that one effect size was excluded because it was based on a single participant (n = 1), precluding the computation of a smallsample corrected effect size. Thus, the final analytic dataset comprised k = 182 effect sizes.

A detailed list of the included studies is provided in the online Supplemental Material (SM) uploaded on https://osf.io/r4av3/?view_only=aa69cf6c08884058b0cc72a75d55347c and a visual summary is presented in the forest plot displayed in Figure 1.

Coding and Effect Size Calculation

Two coders (the study's authors) who were aware of the meta-analysis objectives, examined each article and independently coded its methodological attributes and reported outcomes. Each evaluator also assessed whether the experiments satisfied the inclusion criteria. Initial inter-rater agreement averaged 99% across all coded variables, with discrepancies subsequently resolved through discussion (approximately 30 discrepancies were resolved). In line with the procedures of the original meta-analysis and the preregistration, we coded the following 13 variables. (1) Published status: Coded as whether the study had been published.

(2) Age: We coded both the sample mean age (in days) and, consistent with the original meta-analysis, a binary classification of young versus old; the young category was defined as a mean age ≤ 12 months, 29 days. (3) Sample size. (4) Scenario type: Coded according to the type of event presented to infants or toddlers, including helping vs. hindering, fair vs. unfair (distributive) actions, giving/returning vs. taking objects, or other sociomoral interactions (e.g., harm/ignore vs. comfort) (for each scenario type, we included only cases in which agents with at least positive versus at least negative intentions were contrasted, excluding cases in which the outcomes contradicted the agent's intentions). ( 5) Measure type: We coded infants' responses into three categories: reach (if infants were prompted to reach for one of the agents or an object offered by them), help (if infants were encouraged to give a treat or share with one of the agents), or visual (if the preference was inferred from looking time within a preferential-looking study). ( 6) Scenario delivery mode: Coded as live show or movie (e.g., Hamlin et al. (2007)'s wooden hill version was coded as a live show). ( 7) Scenario presentation format: Coded as real events (live shows or staged movies with puppets or human actors) versus cartoons. Because no studies employed live cartoon stimuli, the scenario delivery mode and presentation format variables were combined into a single stimulus format moderator with three levels: live shows of real events, movies of real events, and movies of cartoons. (8) Agent type: We coded whether infants were prompted to show a preference or were presented with two options and their preference was assessed via looking time, using experimenters, puppets, or foam shapes as the agents (three levels). ( 9) Prompt type: We coded whether participants were prompted to choose or attend to one of the agents using the question "Who do you like?" (or a similarly worded prompt), with other questions or instructions, or with no questions or instructions. (10) Lab affiliation: Given that K. Hamlin initiated this research area and provided a substantial portion of the data, and considering that some direct replication attempts have failed (e.g., Lucca et al., 2025; Salvadori et al., 2015; Schlingloff et al., 2020), we coded whether the study was conducted in her laboratory. This is again consistent with the procedures of the original meta-analysis.

We also exploratorily coded affiliation with A. Geraci and collaborators vs. independent laboratories. (11) Study location: Coded by continent of data collection (North America, South America, Europe, Asia, Oceania). This variable was included on an exploratory basis. (12) Preregistration status: We coded whether the study was preregistered or not. (13) Dependent Variable: Last, we recorded, for manual-reaching studies, the number of participants showing a manual preference for the prosocial agent; for preferential-looking studies, we coded whichever of the following were reported or available: average looking times toward the prosocial and antisocial agents, corresponding t-test values, and/or the percentage of time spent looking at the prosocial agent. The dataset is openly available and uploaded on https://osf.io/r4av3/?view_only=aa69cf6c08884058b0cc72a75d55347c.

All statistical analyses were carried out in R using the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010). Before conducting the analyses, each outcome was converted into Cohen's d and its corresponding variance. These values were computed following three steps, depending on what information was available in each experiment. For studies with manual choice outcomes ("reach" or "help"), we converted the log odds ratio of choosing the prosocial agent to Cohen's π , and Var(d) = 1 p + 1 1-p , where p is the proportion of infants choosing the prosocial agent (Hasselblad and Hedges, 1995).

For studies with looking time outcomes, we used t-statistics where available, with d = t √ n . When unavailable, we used the mean and standard deviation for s, the proportion of looking time toward the prosocial agent, with (Borenstein, 2009). A few studies only reported the number of participants who looked more to the prosocial agent; in those cases, we used the same calculation as the binary manual choice outcomes. Many of the included effect sizes had small sample sizes, for which d is a biased estimate; thus, we used Hedges' small-sample correction, transforming Cohen's d into Hedges' g (Hedges, 1981).

For interpretability, all figures (except Figure 2) present Hedges' g backtransformed into raw proportions (infants or toddlers expressing a preference for the prosocial agent divided by sample size). To do so, the odds ratio was recovered as OR = exp g • π √ 3 , then converted to a proportion via p = OR 1+OR . The same transformation was applied to the confidence interval bounds of g. Results

Average Point Estimate

We begin by calculating an average point estimate of the effect size for infants' preference for prosocial over antisocial agents, reporting both Hedges' g and its back-transformation into raw proportions to improve interpretability. The included studies contributed a total of k = 182 effect sizes to analyze (dataset available at https://osf.io/r4av3/?view_only=aa69cf6c08884058b0cc72a75d55347c). We used a random-effects meta-analysis to account for heterogeneity across studies, with a nested random structure of participant group within paper. We report all estimates with their 95% confidence intervals.

A forest plot is shown in Figure 1; note that the forest plot displays effect sizes aggregated by paper for concision, but all analyses treated each effect size separately. The estimated meta-analytic effect size was g = 0.34 [0.26, 0.41]. When back-transformed to raw proportions, this effect size corresponds to 0.65 [0.61, 0.68]. A significant Q-test confirmed the presence of heterogeneity, Q(181) = 627.92, p < .001. The estimated variance across papers was σ2 = 0.04, and the estimated variance across participant groups within papers was σ2 = 0.07. Given this variability between studies, we proceeded to examine potential moderators to identify factors that might account for differences in infants' preferences across experiments.

Publication Bias

An initial analysis examined publication status (published vs. unpublished) as a potential moderator of effect size. Figure 2 displays a funnel plot of the effect sizes. No significant moderation was observed, Q M (1) = 0.86, p = .353. We assessed evidence of publication bias (Ferguson and Brannick, 2012) using the k = 154 published effect sizes.

We first used a generalization of Egger's test (Egger et al., 1997), regressing the effect sizes on their standard errors; publication bias was indicated if the intercept was significantly different from zero. We found evidence of publication bias, with the intercept estimated as -0.31 [-0.59, -0.03], p = .032. However, as Egger's test may be invalid for odds ratio outcomes (due to the correlation between log odds ratios and their standard errors), we also ran a generalization of Peters' test (Peters et al., 2006), regressing the effect sizes on the inverse sample sizes; publication bias was indicated if the slope was significantly different from zero.

In this case, we did not find evidence of publication bias, with the slope estimated as 1.22 [-1.08, 3.51], p = .299.

Moderator Analyses

To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, we considered a range of moderators relating to the sample (age, sample size), experimental design and procedure (scenario type, measure type, stimulus format, agent type, prompt type), provenance (laboratory affiliation, country), and preregistration. We first examined the associations among these moderators.

Figure 3 shows a correlogram, using Spearman's correlation coefficients for associations be-tween two numeric variables, the eta coefficient from an ANOVA for associations between a numeric and a categorical variable, and Cramér's V for associations between two categorical variables. The correlogram indicates moderate associations among some variable pairs. Nonetheless, because there were no large associations, we retained all moderators in subsequent analyses, although we recommend caution when interpreting individual coefficients.

Additionally, there was insufficient information to fully determine the prompt type for nine effect sizes (arising from five papers). We inferred the most plausible prompt type on the basis of the methods text and knowledge about other comparable studies conducted by the same research group.

We fit two hierarchical meta-regression models. The first was a main-effects model that included all moderators simultaneously as fixed effects, thus estimating the association of each moderator with effect size while adjusting for the remaining moderators. The second was an interaction model containing theoretically motivated interaction terms among selected moderators. Both models included random intercepts of participant group nested within study. Post-hoc pairwise contrasts were evaluated using Tukey's HSD correction. In the following sections, we first present the results of the main-effects model, followed by the interaction model.

We next investigated two key design-related moderators, namely scenario type and measure type. Scenario type (four levels: fair/unfair, give/take, help/hinder, other) did significantly moderate the effect size, Q M (3) = 8.28, p = .041, although no pairwise contrasts survived Tukey's HSD correction, as shown in Figure 4C. Procedure Design Features

We further investigated three potential moderators related to procedure design. First, we collapsed the scenario delivery mode and presentation format into a single stimulus format moderator (three levels: live shows of real events, movies of real events, cartoon movies).

This moderator was not significant, Q M (2) = 4.21, p = .122, as shown in Figure 5A. The estimated proportion of children preferring the prosocial agent was 0.69 [0.63, 0.74] in studies employing live shows of real events, 0.63 [0.57, 0.68] for movies of real events, and 0.60 [0.53, 0.66] for cartoon movies.

Agent type (three levels: experimenters, puppets, foam shapes) did not significantly moderate the effect size, Q M (2) = 0.82, p = .663, as shown in Figure 5B. The estimated proportion of children preferring the prosocial agent was 0.62 [0.52, 0.71] in studies in which infants and toddlers expressed a preference between two experimenters, 0.64 [0.58, 0.69] in studies using puppets as agents, and 0.66 [0.61, 0.71] in studies using shapes.

Additionally, prompt type (three levels: question "Who do you like?", no prompt, other instructions) also did not significantly moderate the effect size, Q M (2) = 0.01, p = .994, as shown in Figure 5C.

The estimated proportion of children preferring the prosocial agent was 0.65 [0.60, 0.69] in studies that explicitly asked the question "Who do you like?", 0.65 [0.58, 0.71] in studies employing no prompt, and 0.64 [0.58, 0.70] in studies using alternative question wordings or instructions.

Interaction Effects

It is possible that the moderators had effects that were non-independent. Thus, we considered three possible sets of interaction effects in an extended model. First, the response measure used may have depended on the stimulus format and the scenario (e.g., preferential looking is more common with movies than with live shows). Second, the scenarios used may differ depending on the laboratory affiliation, as some laboratories may prefer research questions related to particular scenarios. Third, the age of the children may have affected the choices of scenario, measure, and stimulus format (e.g., looking time measures tend to be used with younger infants who are less able to reliably reach). Only one interaction effect was significant: the interaction between scenario and Geraci's lab (Q M (2) = 6.03, p = .049; all other p > .061). We then examined the simple contrasts across scenarios within laboratory affiliation; none of the simple contrasts remained significant after Tukey's HSD correction.

Discussion

The present preregistered meta-analysis provides an updated estimate of infants' and toddlers' preference for prosociality over antisociality. This analysis refines the estimate originally reported by Margoni and Surian (2018) and was undertaken amid a replication crisis, particularly following the large-scale failed replication by the ManyBabies collabo-ration (Lucca et al., 2025). Across 182 effect sizes, we estimated that 65% [61%, 68%] of infants and toddlers (0-32 months) preferred a prosocial agent, corresponding to a Hedges' g = 0.34 [0.26, 0.41]. No clear evidence for publication bias was detected. Notably, the estimated proportion remained virtually identical to the publication-bias-adjusted estimate reported in the previous meta-analysis, which was 64% [60%, 69%]. We will return later to the interpretation of what a proportion of 0.65 may signify. For now, we note that, compared to the previous meta-analysis, the present one incorporated 121 new independent effect sizes and 2,845 additional participants. Importantly, these additions comprised numerous positive findings alongside the several failed replications reported in recent years, while also broadening the evidence base to include visual-preference paradigms. Yet, because measure type (preferential-looking vs. manual-reaching) did not moderate the magnitude of the effect, the remarkable stability of the updated estimate appears to be driven primarily by the coexistence of both successful and unsuccessful replications, rather than by the inclusion of a wider range of methodological approaches.

Whereas we found no clear evidence of publication bias, no indication that published studies reported larger effects than unpublished ones, and no indication that preregistration status moderated the effect size, the present meta-analysis revealed a possible effect of sample size. Specifically, when including outliers (i.e., four effect sizes associated with n ∈ [171, 442]), experiments with larger samples yielded smaller effects. This finding might temper the apparent stability of the updated estimate relative to the prior meta-analysis.

Because larger samples provide more precise estimates, the observation that greater precision is associated with smaller effects raises the possibility that the current estimate may still be inflated relative to the true population parameter. However, this association between sample size and effect size was attenuated and no longer reached conventional levels of statistical significance after excluding outliers (p = .088), while retaining the same negative direction.

Consequently, additional large-scale investigations will be needed to determine whether this pattern is robust.

Robustness of the Estimated Effect

A central finding of the current meta-analysis is that age (5-981 days) did not moderate infants' and toddlers' preference for prosocial agents. This result suggests that general developmental changes during the first two years of life, as well as the social input infants receive during this period-central to empiricist accounts of social preference development-do not substantially influence infant sociomoral preferences. The absence of age effects, together with the observation that a significant proportion of both younger and older infants show a preference for prosocial over antisocial agents, indicates that a propensity to favor prosociality emerges early and with limited social experience, consistent with rationalist accounts of social development (Laurence & Margolis, 2024). In other words, sociomoral preferences may be acquired through domain-specific learning mechanisms and reflect stable, potentially biologically prepared tendencies (Cosmides & Tooby, 1992). Additionally, we found no evidence of cross-cultural variability: study location (North America, Europe, Asia, Oceania) did not moderate the effect size, which may further support rationalist accounts.

More broadly, these data offer a concrete empirical benchmark for theories of the origins and early development of human moral cognition.

We also found no clear evidence that scenario type (fair/unfair, give/take, help/hinder, other) moderated the effect size. Although help/hinder scenarios yielded a descriptively lower average estimate (0.62) than other types-particularly give/take and other scenarios (0.70 and 0.74 respectively)-no pairwise contrasts survived Tukey's HSD correction. Consequently, the significant effect-size difference between give/take and help/hinder scenarios reported in the 2018 meta-analysis was not fully replicated. This null result is notable, as reproducing that difference could have offered a partial explanation for the nonsignificant findings of the recent ManyBabies replication (Lucca et al., 2025), which used a help/hinder paradigm. Another null finding concerned measure type. Unlike the previous meta-analysis, the current analysis included not only studies using manual reaching or selective helping measures, but also experiments assessing visual preference to test whether preferential-looking paradigms yield larger effects than manual-reaching measures. No such effect was observed, indicating that effect sizes were consistent across measurement types-a pattern broadly consistent with evidence that looking and choosing often reflect related underlying processes (Hagihara et al., 2021; Hendrickson et al., 2015).

Several other potential moderators yielded null effects, highlighting the consistency of the effect size across these factors. First, we examined whether the presentation format of the stimuli (live shows of real events, movies of real events, or cartoon movies) moderated the effect size. One possibility is that live presentations yield stronger effects than video-based presentations because infants may be especially attuned to real-life social interactions, reflecting an evolutionary history dominated by three-dimensional and real-world agents. Relatedly, the video deficit effect suggests that young children often learn less from video than from comparable live presentations (Strouse & Samson, 2021). Live interactions may therefore facilitate more effective processing and understanding of social behavior. In addition, movie-based tasks (as in the recent failed replication by Lucca et al., 2025) may require infants to establish a correspondence between agents previously seen on screen and those subsequently encountered in the live test phase, and, more broadly, agents introduced in live shows may be experienced as more immediate and socially meaningful interaction partners than agents introduced via video, thus facilitating the effect. Consistent with these possibilities, live shows were descriptively associated with stronger effects (0.69) than either type of movie presentation (0.63 and 0.60 for real events and cartoons, respectively). However, stimulus format did not significantly moderate the effect size, indicating that these differences were only descriptive. Consequently, any interpretation of this pattern remains speculative.

Second, two potential procedural moderators were examined: the type of agents used to assess infants' preferences (experimenter, puppet, or geometric-shape agents) and type of prompt employed (question "Who do you like?", no prompt, or other instructions). Neither moderated the effect size, a pattern consistent with the previous meta-analysis. Third, contrary to the previous meta-analysis, which reported an effect of laboratory affiliationspecifically that K. Hamlin's group produced larger effect sizes than independent laboratories when considering help/hinder and give/take effect sizes-the current meta-analysis found no such evidence. However, an exploratory analysis based on the concept of "research allegiance" (Munder et al., 2013), which classified effects reported by B. Woo, a former student of K. Hamlin who may employ similar experimental protocols and methodological practices, as part of Hamlin's group, instead suggested larger effects in Hamlin-affiliated laboratories, with approximately 70% of children preferring the prosocial agent compared to 61% in independent laboratories. The mixed evidence for this moderating effect likely partially reflects more recent published and unpublished work from Hamlin's lab reporting null effects (e.g., Hamlin, 2025; Tan and Hamlin, 2022, 2024; Yuen et al., 2023), which would attenuate the previously observed difference between Hamlin's group and other laboratories. By contrast, an exploratory analysis of Geraci laboratory affiliation, motivated by Geraci's emergence as the second most prolific contributor to this literature after Hamlin, revealed a significant moderating effect, with studies from the Geraci lab yielding larger effect sizes (75%) than studies from other laboratories (63%). Given that much of the evidence on infants' preferences for fair versus unfair agents specifically originates from this laboratory, this finding highlights the importance of additional independent replications to establish the robustness and generalizability of the effect across research groups.

Insights From the Average Point Estimate

We estimated that 65% of infants and toddlers preferred prosocial over antisocial agents. How should this figure be interpreted? To begin, this proportion does not fall below the publication-bias-adjusted estimate reported in the previous meta-analysis, yet it is markedly lower than the original effect size reported by Hamlin et al. (2007), where all 6-month-old infants and the vast majority (88%) of 10-month-olds preferred the prosocial agent. At the same time, our meta-analytic estimate diverges substantially from several failed replications, most notably Lucca et al. (2025). It is worth considering some potential reasons for the surprising deviation in results between the present meta-analysis and their multi-lab replication. First, Lucca et al. ( 2025) used a single methodological paradigm-the hill helping/hindering paradigm using shape agents displayed via video. Thus, the failed replication represents one specific set of stimuli under one methodological procedure, and it is possible that the observed null results may not generalize to other stimuli and procedures.

Second, the substantial between-lab heterogeneity observed in Lucca et al. (2025) suggests that there may be other population-or lab-related variance that remained to be explained despite their unified stimuli and procedure (e.g., variation in how labs "warmed up" with infants); as such information was not captured by the multi-lab replication (nor, indeed, by most of the studies in our meta-analysis), it is difficult to predict how such variation may affect the generalizability of the findings. Third, the discrepancy between our meta-analytic estimate and the null finding in Lucca et al. (2025) could be considered in light of a broader methodological pattern in psychology. Kvarven et al. (2020) compared 15 psychological phenomena for which both a meta-analysis and a large preregistered multi-laboratory replication were available and found that meta-analytic effect sizes were significantly larger than replication effect sizes in 12 of the 15 cases, with meta-analytic estimates being, on average, nearly three times as large. Thus, divergence between meta-analytic and replication-based estimates is not unusual. At the same time, neither approach can be assumed to provide a uniquely accurate estimate of the underlying effect. Large-scale replications minimize concerns about publication bias and selective reporting but typically examine a single operationalization under highly standardized conditions, whereas meta-analyses aggregate evidence across a broader range of populations, stimuli, and procedures while remaining vulnerable to biases and heterogeneity in the underlying literature. Consequently, differences between the present meta-analysis and Lucca et al. (2025) may reflect not only discrepancies in effect-size estimation but also differences in the scope of the phenomena being captured.

Taken together, these observations suggest that it would be premature to dismiss evidence for an early-emerging preference for prosociality. Such a conclusion would rely primarily on null results from individual experiments, while neglecting the aggregate evidence synthesized here. Pooling all available data reveals that slightly more than three out of five infants prefer prosocial over antisocial agents. Interpreting this point estimate requires considering what manual choice or visual attention measures actually capture. This is not a trivial task. However, we believe that one of the most informative aspects of these tests is that-together with converging evidence from violation-of-expectation studies (e.g., Jin and Baillargeon, 2017; Margoni et al., 2018; Sloane et al., 2012; Ting and Baillargeon, 2021)-they suggest that infants possess a rudimentary moral sensitivity that enables them to distinguish between morally positive and negative actions.

Accordingly, rather than viewing these paradigms solely as tests of prosocial preference, they may first be understood as demonstrating that infants reliably distinguish between agents-evidenced by non-random choices or differential attention. Building on this foundation, two further questions arise. First, do these paradigms reveal a true preference rather than curiosity or avoidance? Although manual reaching and looking-time measures are typically interpreted as indicators of preference, they might alternatively reflect general curiosity toward one agent or an adaptive tendency to approach agents perceived as less threatening or relatively more beneficial-interpretations that partly even depend on how "preference" is conceptually defined. Second, to what extent does the estimated mean proportion of 0.65 conceal individual differences in infants' and toddlers' responses? At present, the available data do not permit a definitive answer (although, see Tan et al., 2018). Future theoretical and empirical work is needed to investigate whether infants exhibit systematic individual differences in their attraction to prosocial versus antisocial agents, or in their relative curiosity toward these characters (see Nighbor et al., 2017). And further work is needed to uncover the cognitive mechanisms that lead children to ostensibly prefer one of the two agents.

Conclusion

The current meta-analysis estimates that 65% of infants and toddlers (0-32 months) prefer prosocial over antisocial agents. This estimate appears robust to publication bias and is generally consistent across a range of potential moderators. Notably, it is not influenced by age or study location, findings that lend support to rationalist accounts of moral sense acquisition. However, when including outliers, the effect size was moderated by sample size, with larger studies reporting smaller effects. This pattern tempers enthusiasm for the estimate, which, despite numerous recent failed replications, remains largely consistent with the previous meta-analysis. The present work extends that earlier synthesis and provides an updated perspective in the context of the ongoing replication crisis.
