How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited (currently, a warning is provided regardless of whether the replication/reproduction study is already cited).

The module requires that the reference has a DOI. If you run the ref_doi_check module in a pipeline before this, it will use the enhanced DOI list from that module, otherwise it will only run on references with existing DOIs.

It is possible the original study was cited for other reasons than the empirical claim tested, or that the replication/reproduction in the FLoRA database is for only one of the studies in the paper, and not the study the authors discuss.

Abstract

Bipolar disorder (BD) is marked by extreme mood fluctuations, yet the computational mechanisms underlying this instability remain poorly understood. Recent computational accounts suggest that mood instability may arise from altered reward prediction error (RPE) processing, yet little is known about the factors that modulate this process. Volatility inference-the ability to track environmental change-may be particularly relevant as it modulates the influence of prediction errors on belief updating. However, it remains unclear whether BD is characterized by altered volatility inference.

We investigated whether BD showed altered volatility inference across distinct environmental structures using decision-making tasks and computational modeling. Individuals with bipolar I disorder (BD-I; N = 32) and healthy controls (HC; N = 40) completed two tasks: a probabilitytracking task (PTT) with predetermined contingency changes across stable and volatile phases and a probabilistic reversal learning task (PRL) with performance-contingent reversals.

The BD-I group showed reduced performance and switched choices more frequently than the HC group across both tasks. Computational modeling further revealed heightened volatility beliefs in the BD-I group, reflected by a higher prior belief about volatility in the PTT and higher tonic volatility in the PRL, together with greater behavioral stochasticity across both tasks. These computational alterations were not associated with current manic or depressive symptom severity.

These findings suggest that BD-I shows deficits in adapting to volatile environments, potentially arising from trait-like computational alterations characterized by heightened expectations of environmental change and noisy belief-to-action mapping, which may provide candidate mechanisms through which decision-making abnormalities contribute to mood instability.

1. Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a chronic mood disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of mania and depression (Grande et al., 2016). Despite mood instability being a central symptom in BD, the mechanisms underlying these extreme mood fluctuations remain poorly understood. BD has also been widely associated with abnormalities across multiple facets of reward-based decision making, including altered reward sensitivity, reward learning and adaptation to changing reward contingencies (Johnson et al., 2012; Whitton et al., 2015; Long et al., 2022). In light of recent computational accounts of mood, such alterations in decision making may provide valuable insights for understanding the mechanisms underlying mood instability in BD.

Recent computational accounts have suggested that mood is shaped not simply by objective outcomes, but by the discrepancy between prior expectations and experienced outcomes, as captured by reward prediction errors (Rutledge et al., 2014 (Rutledge et al., , 2015)). This perspective highlights a close link between mood and decision-making processes, whereby iterative learning shapes expectations and choices influence the subsequent outcomes individuals experience, ultimately shaping mood dynamics (Eldar et al., 2016; Blain and Rutledge, 2020). Building on this computational view of mood, frameworks that model decision-making processes to identify the generative processes underlying mood have emerged as a promising avenue for developing mechanistic accounts of mood disturbances in affective disorders, including BD (Mason et al., 2017; Zavlis et al., 2026).

Computational studies in BD have identified disruptions in decision-making processes, including noisy belief-to-action mapping (Yechiam et al., 2008; Lasagna et al., 2022; MacDonald et al., 2025) and abnormalities in reward prediction error (RPE) processing or belief updating (Ossola et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2024). Moreover, beyond simply identifying such alterations in BD, recent work has suggested that disruptions in these processes may be related to mood fluctuations. Crucially, recent studies have proposed that the sum of recent RPEs, or environmental momentum, may reflect mood and bias subsequent reward perception, thereby altering future RPEs and forming a feedback loop between mood and reward processing, and amplification of this feedback process has been proposed as a computational mechanism underlying mood instability in BD (Eldar and Niv, 2015; Mason et al., 2017; Moningka and Mason, 2024). These findings suggest that disruptions in reward-related decisionmaking processes may actually contribute to mood fluctuations, highlighting the importance of identifying which components of decision making are altered and relevant to mood dynamics in BD. However, given the relatively early stage of computational approaches in BD, several potentially important components remain underexplored.

One such component is the inference of how rapidly the environment changes, commonly referred to as environmental volatility (Behrens et al., 2007). Given the continually changing nature of the real-world environment, accurately tracking volatility is critical for adaptive behavior, and disruptions in this process have been implicated across a range of psychiatric conditions and symptoms including psychosis (Deserno et al., 2020), anxiety (Hein et al., 2021) and borderline personality disorder (Henco et al., 2020). Given that inferred volatility can shape expectations of the world by determining how strongly prediction errors influence belief updating (Nassar et al., 2010; Mathys et al., 2011 Mathys et al., , 2014) ) and that recent accounts propose that mood dynamics are shaped by prediction errors (Rutledge et al., 2014 (Rutledge et al., , 2015;; Eldar et al., 2016), altered volatility inference may therefore contribute to mood instability. In line with this possibility, two recent studies using computational modeling of probabilistic reversal learning have investigated whether BD shows alterations in beliefs about environmental change, yet the findings are inconsistent. Ivanova et al. (2025) found higher prior and overall beliefs about environmental volatility in euthymic BD, whereas MacDonald et al. (2025) found no evidence that BD held elevated reversal beliefs that reward contingencies would reverse.

Despite these mixed findings in computational studies, consistent behavioral patterns observed in previous studies may provide indirect evidence for heightened volatility beliefs in BD. Previous studies have reported impaired adaptation in BD, often accompanied by increased choice switching, when learning associations in probabilistic environments (Minassian et al., 2004; Pizzagalli et al., 2008; Ryu et al., 2017; Urošević et al., 2018) or in environments where reward contingencies change over time (Roiser et al., 2009; Ivanova et al., 2025; MacDonald et al., 2025). Given that elevated choice switching has been associated with heightened expectations of environmental change in humans (Schiffer et al., 2017; Deserno et al., 2020), these behavioral patterns may provide indirect support for the possibility that BD is characterized by overestimated environmental volatility. However, this interpretation remains partly speculative, as most previous studies did not computationally dissociate the potential underlying mechanisms. Specifically, it remains unclear whether this heightened switching behavior arises from overestimated environmental volatility or reflects greater noise in belief-to-action mapping as reported by MacDonald et al. (2025), or both. Nonetheless, findings that individuals with BD show greater sensitivity to recent feedback, particularly when it conflicts with prior reinforcement history (Pizzagalli et al., 2008; Ryu et al., 2017), may suggest that elevated behavioral variability may not be fully explained by choice randomness. Computational approaches are therefore needed to dissociate these two possibilities, enabling us to test whether such behavioral patterns could be explained by heightened volatility beliefs in BD.

Another crucial question is whether alterations in volatility inference vary with current mood symptoms of mania and depression. However, few studies have directly examined associations between symptom severity and measures indicative of altered volatility inference, with most available evidence coming from euthymic samples. For mania, greater residual manic symptoms were associated with lower uncertainty about reward contingencies in a changing environment (Ivanova et al., 2025), contrary to what would be expected under heightened volatility beliefs. On the other hand, Moningka and Mason (2024) reported that residual manic symptoms were associated with stronger modulation of insular-striatal connectivity by momentum-biased RPEs, potentially reflecting a greater tendency to interpret recent reward momentum as a signal that the environment is changing. For depression, studies in euthymic samples have found no evidence that reversal-learning performance varies with depressive symptom severity (Gorrindo et al., 2005; Wegbreit et al., 2016). In a computational study examining a broader range of mood symptom severity, reversal beliefs were unrelated to either manic or depressive symptoms (MacDonald et al., 2025). Taken together, the relationship between mood symptoms and volatility inference in BD remains unclear, which may be attributable to the scarcity of studies or the limited range of mood symptom severity in previous samples.

Here, we aimed to examine whether bipolar I disorder (BD-I) shows alterations in volatility inference using computational modeling across two well-established probabilistic learning tasks-a probability-tracking task (PTT) and a probabilistic reversal learning task (PRL)-which differ in the sources and patterns of environmental change. By examining whether patterns of volatility inference and group differences are consistent across distinct environmental structures, we aimed to characterize these alterations more comprehensively while better reflecting the complexity of the real-world environment. We hypothesized that BD would show reduced behavioral performance in both tasks, consistent with previous findings (Gorrindo et al., 2005; Roiser et al., 2009; Wegbreit et al., 2016; Ivanova et al., 2025; MacDonald et al., 2025), underpinned by heightened volatility beliefs, as reported in a previous study (Ivanova et al., 2025). We additionally explored associations between volatility beliefs and a broader range of manic and depressive symptom severity.

2.2 Procedures

All study procedures were approved by the Indiana University Human Subjects Institutional Review Board. Following the diagnostic interviews and mood symptom assessments described above, all participants completed two behavioral tasks (Fig. 1) and several self-report measures relevant to BD. Self-report measures included the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; Beck et al., 1996), Behavioral Inhibition System/Behavioral Activation System scales (BIS/BAS; Carver and White, 1994), Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11; Patton et al., 1995), and Hypomanic Personality Scale (HPS; Eckblad and Chapman, 1986). IQ (Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence [WASI-II]; Wechsler, 2011), working memory (Spatial Span task; Wechsler, 1997a), and information processing speed (Digit Symbol Test; Wechsler, 1997b) were also assessed.

2.3 Behavioral Tasks

Participants completed two probabilistic learning tasks characterized by distinct sources and patterns of contingency change (i.e., reversals): a probability-tracking task (PTT; Behrens et al., 2007) with predetermined contingency changes across stable and volatile phases and a probabilistic reversal learning task (PRL; Hampton et al., 2006) with performance-contingent reversals. In both tasks, participants repeatedly chose between two options that probabilistically yielded rewards, while they were informed that one option was more advantageous at any given time and that the relative advantage could reverse during the task. Task order was counterbalanced across participants (see Supplementary Material for additional task details). In the PRL, the chosen option could yield either a gain or a loss of 25 points. The better option yielded gains with 80% probability and losses with 20% probability, with the opposite contingencies for the worse option. Reversals were performance-contingent: after four consecutive selections of the better option, the reversal probability was 25% on each subsequent trial and increased to 100% after eight consecutive selections. Participants completed two or three blocks, with each block consisting of 200 trials. In the PTT, the options displayed the reward magnitude that could be earned, which varied across trials independently of the underlying reward contingency. The task comprised a stable phase (120 trials) in which the reward contingency of the better option remained constant at 75% and a volatile phase (180 trials) in which the better option had an 80% reward contingency and its identity reversed every 30 or 40 trials at predetermined points. Phase order was counterbalanced across participants, and each participant completed one block of 300 trials.

2.4.1 Computational Modeling

To characterize participants' trial-by-trial belief-updating processes, we used the Hierarchical Gaussian Filter for binary inputs (HGF; Mathys et al., 2011 Mathys et al., , 2014)), a well-established computational framework that describes how an agent learns about an uncertain quantity from binary inputs. The HGF is a perceptual model that assumes that an agent has an internal generative model of the environment, represented by a hierarchy of environmental states 𝑥 (𝑘) , with k indexing trials. For example, in the 3level HGF, the model is represented by a hierarchy of 𝑥 1 (𝑘) , 𝑥 2 (𝑘) and 𝑥 3 (𝑘) , where 𝑥 1 (𝑘) (level 1) represents the binary input, 𝑥 2 (𝑘) (level 2) represents the latent tendency toward a particular category (i.e., how likely option 1 is to yield a positive outcome on trial k), and 𝑥 3 (𝑘) (level 3) represents environmental log-volatility. Beliefs about these states are represented by probability distributions, whose means (𝜇 1 , 𝜇 2 , 𝜇 3 ) reflect current belief estimates and whose variances (𝜎 1 , 𝜎 2 , 𝜎 3 ) reflect their uncertainty. 2.4.2. Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses of demographic, clinical, behavioral variables and modelderived parameters were conducted using R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025). Behavioral and computational analyses included 64 participants (28 BD-I and 36 HC) for the PTT and 70 participants (31 BD-I and 39 HC) for the PRL. To compare behavioral measures between BD and HC across task condition, mixed-design ANOVAs were performed using the afex package (Singmann et al., 2025). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the emmeans package (Lenth and Piaskowski, 2026), with Holm's correction for multiple comparisons. Both partial eta-squared (𝜂 𝑝 2 ) and generalized eta-squared (𝜂 𝐺 2 ) were reported as measures of effect size for ANOVAs. Comparisons of group-level parameters of BD and HC were conducted within a Bayesian framework, following Ahn et al. (2017). Specifically, posterior distributions of the between-group differences were obtained for each parameter by subtracting HC grouplevel posterior samples from the corresponding BD samples and were evaluated using 95% highest density intervals (HDIs). A group difference was considered credible when its 95% HDI excluded zero.

3.1 Behavioral Analyses 3.1.1 Task Performance

Task performance was defined separately for each task to reflect its specific characteristics. In the PTT, task performance was assessed using accuracy, defined as the proportion of correct choices across trials. In the PRL, task performance was assessed using reversal counts, defined as the number of reversals in each block. For the PTT, a 2 (group) × 2 (phase) mixed ANOVA was conducted to investigate whether accuracy differed between groups and across stable and volatile phases. Only the main effect of group was significant, F(1, 62) = 8.92, p = .004, 𝜂 𝑝2 = .126, 𝜂 𝐺2 = .104, with higher accuracy in the HC group (M = 0.79, SD = 0.12) than in the BD group (M = 0.70, SD = 0.12); the estimated marginal mean (EMM) difference (BD -HC) was -0.09, 95% CI [-0.153, -0.0304], t(62) = -2.99, p = .004 (Fig. 2A). For the PRL, a 2 (group) × 3 (block) mixed ANOVA was conducted to examine whether reversal counts differed between groups across the three blocks. Only the main effect of group was significant, F(1, 65) = 21.28, p < .001, 𝜂 𝑝2 = .247, 𝜂 𝐺2 = .200, with higher average reversal counts across the three blocks in the HC group (M = 16.36, SD = 2.40) than in the BD group (M = 13.14, SD = 3.83); the EMM difference (BD -HC) was -3.49, 95% CI [-5.00, -1.98], t(65) = -4.613, p < .001 (Fig. 2D). Collectively, the results indicated reduced performance in the BD group on both tasks, suggesting difficulty learning and adapting to dynamic environments with different patterns of change.

3.1.2 Switching Behavior

The switching rate was calculated as the proportion of trials on which participants chose an option different from that chosen on the preceding trial. To further examine the effects of the types of prior trials (i.e., correctness and outcome) on switching, additional analyses were conducted by calculating the switching rates conditional on these trial characteristics. In the PTT, a 2 (group) × 2 (phase) mixed ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group, F(1, 62) = 13.22 (p < .001), 𝜂 𝑝2 = .176, 𝜂 𝐺2 = .162, and a group x phase interaction, F(1, 62) = 5.65 (p = .02), 𝜂 𝑝2 = .083, 𝜂 𝐺2 = .021. Post hoc comparisons showed higher switching rates in the BD group than in the HC group during both the stable (EMM difference [BD -HC] = 0.121, 95% CI [0.032, 0.21], p = .009) and volatile phases (EMM difference [BD -HC] = 0.184, 95% CI [0.097, 0.27], p < .001), with a larger group difference during the volatile phase (difference in EMM contrasts [stable -volatile] = -0.06, 95% CI [-0.116, -0.010], p = .02, Fig. 2B). Next, to investigate whether group differences in switching rate varied depending on the outcome (win vs. lose) and correctness (correct vs. incorrect) of the previous trial, a 2 (group) × 2 (outcome) × 2 (correctness) ANOVA was conducted. The analysis revealed a significant threeway interaction in the PTT, F(1, 62) = 7.85, p = .007, 𝜂 𝑝2 = .112, 𝜂 𝐺2 = .006. Post hoc analyses showed that the BD group exhibited higher switching rates in all PTT conditions, with the largest group difference following a correct-lose trial (EMM difference [BD -HC] = 0.232, 95% CI [0.118, 0.346], p < .001) (Fig. 2C). The analogous PRL analysis revealed the same overall pattern: a significant three-way interaction, F(1, 68) = 12.45, p < .001, 𝜂 𝑝2 = .155, 𝜂 𝐺2 = .010, with the largest group difference following a correct-lose trial (EMM difference [BD -HC] = 0.224, 95% CI [0.120, 0.327], p < .001) (Fig. 2F). Overall, the BD group switched choices more frequently than the HC group in both tasks. These group differences were particularly pronounced after correct-lose trials, which may reflect difficulty maintaining previously formed beliefs in the face of recent contradictory feedback, potentially in line with elevated volatility beliefs.

4. Discussion

Our findings showed that BD-I exhibited reduced performance and elevated switching behavior, underpinned by computational signatures of heightened beliefs about environmental volatility and increased behavioral stochasticity in volatile environments. By demonstrating that these alterations were consistent across two environments with distinct sources and patterns of environmental change, our study provides evidence that heightened volatility inference may characterize BD. Moreover, the altered volatility belief was not associated with current manic or depressive symptoms. Together, these findings suggest that BD shows deficits in adapting to a volatile environment, potentially arising from heightened expectations of environmental change and noisy belief-to-action mapping, which may represent trait-like rather than state-dependent characteristics varying with current mood symptoms.

Our findings replicated previously reported abnormalities in decision making in BD. The impaired performance and elevated switching behavior observed in the current study are consistent with behavioral patterns previously reported among individuals with BD in uncertain environments (Minassian et al., 2004; Pizzagalli et al., 2008; Roiser et al., 2009; Ryu et al., 2017; Urošević et al., 2018; Ivanova et al., 2025; MacDonald et al., 2025). While confirming these well-established patterns, the current study dissociated the computational mechanisms underlying them via computational modeling. Specifically, our findings suggested that computational alterations previously reported in BD, including greater behavioral stochasticity (Yechiam et al., 2008; Lasagna et al., 2022; MacDonald et al., 2025) and heightened beliefs about environmental volatility (Ivanova et al., 2025), may both contribute to these behavioral patterns. Notably, whereas previous evidence regarding volatility inference in BD has been unclear, the present findings provide converging evidence for heightened volatility inference by demonstrating this pattern across two distinct environmental structures and in a sample with broader symptom variability, extending previous evidence from a euthymic sample examined in a single environment (Ivanova et al., 2025). Together, the present study integrates previously disparate behavioral and computational findings while adding converging evidence for heightened volatility inference, a relatively underexplored computational alteration in BD, providing a more unified account of aberrant decision making in BD.

Beyond accounting for aberrant decision making, the current finding of heightened volatility inference may also provide a nuanced account of mood instability when integrated with previously proposed computational models of mood and BD. Recent computational frameworks propose that mood reflects discrepancies between expectations and outcomes as captured by RPEs (Rutledge et al., 2014 (Rutledge et al., , 2015;; Blain and Rutledge, 2020). By shaping expectations that deviate from the true states of the world, heightened volatility beliefs in BD may generate larger prediction errors and potentially contribute to mood fluctuations. Furthermore, heightened volatility inference may influence mood by modulating the recursive interaction between environmental momentum and reward perception proposed in the momentum-bias model (Eldar and Niv, 2015). According to the model, environmental momentum, formalized as a sum of recent RPEs and proposed to reflect mood state, can bias subsequent reward perception (i.e., reward is experienced as more or less rewarding when recent outcomes signal the environment is improving or deteriorating). As this biased reward perception shapes subsequent RPEs, this process can form a recursive feedback loop, with greater momentum bias having previously been associated with hypomanic traits and BD (Eldar and Niv, 2015; Moningka and Mason, 2024). Under Bayesian accounts of learning, heightened volatility beliefs increase uncertainty about lower-level beliefs and thereby increase the influence of recent prediction errors on belief updating (Behrens et al., 2007) as formalized in the HGF (Mathys et al., 2011 (Mathys et al., , 2014)). Accordingly, heightened volatility beliefs in BD may increase sensitivity to recent RPEs, potentially strengthening the accumulation of recent RPEs into environmental momentum and thereby amplifying the feedback loop that may give rise to extreme mood fluctuations; however, this mechanistic link remains to be tested empirically. Future studies could use designs that jointly measure volatility inference and environmental momentum to test whether volatility inference can be incorporated into the existing momentum-bias model of BD.

The current findings may have several clinical implications. First, heightened volatility beliefs may provide a possible account of clinical phenomena observed in BD, such as why specific life events (e.g., goal-achievement or goal-striving) precede increases in manic symptoms (Johnson et al., 2008) or episode onset (Nusslock et al., 2007). By increasing sensitivity to incoming evidence, heightened volatility beliefs may lead individuals with BD to interpret ordinary life events as overly salient signals of environmental change (e.g., the environment has become more favorable or opportunity-rich), thereby potentially facilitating manic symptoms or transitions into mood episodes. Second, relationships between volatility inference and clinical characteristics may offer additional clinical insights. In the current study, volatility inference did not vary with manic or depressive symptom severity. Together with findings from a euthymic BD sample (Ivanova et al., 2025), our finding raises the possibility that heightened volatility inference may represent a trait-like characteristic of BD that can potentially serve as a computational marker, particularly in the differential diagnosis with unipolar depression. In light of recent computational findings suggesting that lower expectations of environmental change may serve as potential markers of depression (Duda et al., 2026), future studies should further test specificity of elevated volatility beliefs in BD by directly comparing unipolar and bipolar depression. The positive association between BAS and volatility beliefs in HC participants is also noteworthy. BAS, which reflects sensitivity and responsiveness to reward, has been proposed as a key vulnerability factor for BD and has prospectively predicted BD onset (Alloy et al., 2012). The present finding may point to a computational process underlying heightened BAS sensitivity: heightened volatility beliefs may increase reward responsiveness by causing reward outcomes to be interpreted as more salient (i.e., signaling environmental change), although this possibility requires further investigation.

Although the current findings provide new insights into computational mechanisms underlying mood fluctuations in BD, several important questions remain. First, it remains unclear whether heightened volatility inference develops over the course of BD, represents a pre-existing vulnerability, or both. Ivanova et al. (2025) proposed that a history of extreme mood episodes may establish higher prior beliefs about environmental volatility in BD, which may parallel the kindling or sensitization hypothesis, whereby mood episodes become increasingly likely to be precipitated by minor life events as the illness progresses (Bender and Alloy, 2011). This possibility is consistent with an illnessprogression account, whereas the association between BAS and volatility beliefs in HC participants in the current study may provide indirect evidence of a pre-existing vulnerability, given that BAS has been a key vulnerability factor to BD (Alloy et al., 2012). Future longitudinal studies could clarify the origin of heightened volatility inference by tracking it in BD or high-risk groups. Second, an important extension would be to examine whether altered volatility inference is specific to beliefs about the external environment or also extends to beliefs about internal states or self-beliefs. BD has been associated with differences in self-beliefs across mood states (Bentall et al., 2005) and labile self-esteem even during remission (Knowles et al., 2007). These fluctuations in self-beliefs may reflect altered belief-updating processes, as suggested in a recent computational study in which overly sensitive updating of self-efficacy in response to goal progress may give rise to manic-like behavior (Li and Radulescu, 2024). Future studies could test whether such alterations in self-belief can be explained by sensitive belief updating based on heightened volatility beliefs about the self or internal states. Third, further work could directly test how elevated volatility beliefs relate to moment-to-moment mood dynamics, thereby evaluating our proposed account of how volatility beliefs may contribute to mood fluctuations. One possible approach is to repeatedly sample momentary mood during a task and relate these reports to model-derived trial-wise estimates of volatility or uncertainty, leveraging paradigms from Blain and Rutledge (2020). Furthermore, future studies could combine this laboratory-based approach with computational modeling of real-world mood dynamics. Notably, Pulcu et al. (2022) identified elevated affective volatility as a prominent feature of real-world mood dynamics in BD. Future studies could examine whether laboratory-derived computational parameters, such as elevated volatility beliefs identified in the present study, are associated with this real-world affective volatility and whether incorporating these parameters into models of mood dynamics could improve their predictive accuracy.

Finally, it should be noted that the present findings alone may not fully explain all aspects of mood dynamics in BD. For example, although heightened volatility beliefs may help account for transitions into mood episodes, they may appear to contradict the persistence of mood within an episode. If heightened volatility beliefs increase sensitivity to recent outcomes and promote belief updating, then why do mood and behavior remain rigid in BD despite the mood-incongruent evidence during episodes (i.e., the persistence of depressive mood despite success cues during a depressive episode or excessive goal-directed activity during mania despite failure cues)? One possibility is that heightened volatility beliefs and within-episode mood rigidity are not mutually exclusive, with volatility inference operating in a mood-congruent manner such that the environment is inferred to be changing more rapidly when recent outcomes align with the current mood state. For example, during mania, positive RPEs may be interpreted as stronger evidence that the environment is rapidly improving, whereas negative RPEs may be more salient in depression. This could produce asymmetric, valence-dependent belief updating that preferentially amplifies evidence congruent with the ongoing mood episode while attenuating the influence of incongruent evidence, further shaping environmental momentum and thereby exaggerating biases in reward perception. Future research should examine whether volatility inference varies across mood episodes and how it relates to the momentum-bias account, thereby contributing to a more integrative computational model of mood instability in BD that better captures clinically relevant phenomena.

5. Conclusion

We demonstrated that BD-I exhibited heightened beliefs about environmental volatility, accompanied by noisy belief-to-action mapping that may underlie impaired adaptation and elevated switching across dynamically changing environments. These computational alterations did not vary with current manic or depressive symptom severity, suggesting that they may represent relatively stable characteristics rather than mood-dependent features. Together, these findings contribute to the mechanistic understanding of aberrant decision making in BD by identifying computational alterations in both belief updating and action selection, proposing candidate mechanisms through which decisionmaking abnormalities may contribute to mood instability.
