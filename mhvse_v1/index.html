How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

People routinely make decisions about how to express their internal psychological states to the outside world. These communication choices can systematically influence the meaning observers extract about the internal states of others. In psychological science, this is frequently observed in the way people respond to self-report surveys. Quantitative cognitive models of these communication choices, such as the Latent State and Response Style (LASARS) model, translate continuous psychological states into Likert-type responses. In doing so, the model can separate internal experiences (latent states) from preferences for (or against) certain response options in self-report scenarios. A key assumption of the LASARS model is that response style reflects a stable individual characteristic that generalises across contexts. The present study tested this assumption by examining whether response styles remain consistent across theoretically distinct psychological domains. A sample of 145 adults completed four survey instruments assessing sensation seeking, thinking styles, dark personality traits, and perceived control. We compared a model in which response style parameters were estimated separately for each instrument with a more parsimonious model in which response style was assumed to be identical across all instruments. Response style parameters exhibited strong cross-instrument correlations, exceeding the magnitude of correlations observed among latent states. Furthermore, constraining response style to be shared across instruments produced virtually identical predictions of observed scores and latent state estimates while reducing model complexity substantially. These findings provide strong evidence that response style is a stable, domain-general characteristic. They also support the conclusion that people use similar decision strategies to express their internal psychological states across the different constructs they may wish or be asked to communicate.

Introduction

Survey responses are influenced not only by respondents' underlying latent statesfoot_0 , but also by the choices they make when communicating those states (Schwarz, 1999). Commonly described as response styles, individuals often exhibit preferences for or against particular response options, regardless of the substantive content of the question being asked (Paulhus, Vazire, et al., 2007).

For example, extreme and midpoint responding occur when respondents show a tendency to favour or avoid the endpoints or centre of a response scale, whereas acquiescent responding reflects a general tendency to agree with survey items irrespective of their content (Batchelor & Miao, 2016; Nadler et al., 2015; Smith, 2004). These systematic patterns of responding can introduce bias into observed data, obscure meaningful individual differences, and ultimately affect the validity of substantive conclusions drawn from survey responses (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001; Paulhus, Vazire, et al., 2007; Schauberger & Tutz, 2022).

The Latent State and Response Style (LASARS) model (Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, & Hawkins, 2026a) provides a means of separating variation attributable to latent states from variation attributable to response style, thereby offering a clearer understanding of the constructs being measured and the circumstances under which response style emerges. This ordinal-probit model assumes that continuous latent states are discretised by a set of thresholds to produce ordinal responses on Likert-type scales. Response style is captured through transformations of these thresholds, altering the manner in which the latent continuum is partitioned into response categories. The LASARS model incorporates measures of three response behaviours, namely a preference for centre options, for odd options (on a 5-point scale) and directional bias, which combine to characterise a broad range of both canonical and idiosyncratic response tendencies (for full details, see Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, & Hawkins, 2026a).

The LASARS model achieves this separation by assuming that response style remains stable across latent states. Consistent with this assumption, research suggests that response styles possess an enduring, trait-like component that is stable over time and generalises across measurement contexts. For example, acquiescent, disacquiescent, midpoint, and extreme responding have been shown to exhibit substantial temporal stability (Danner et al., 2015; Weijters et al., 2010; Wu, 2025) for periods as long as eight years (Wetzel et al., 2016). Evidence also indicates that response styles are not tied to particular items, scales, or latent constructs, with tendencies such as acquiescent (Danner et al., 2015) and extreme (Kieruj & Moors, 2013; Wetzel et al., 2013) responding appearing consistently across different measures and domains. Together, these findings suggest that response styles reflect relatively stable individual differences that can systematically influence observed responses. Indeed, recent applications of the LASARS model to existing data sets have provided further evidence for the stability of response style (Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, & Hawkins, 2026a).

Analysing data collected by Huang et al. (2024), which repeated impulsivity measurements separated by three-month intervals, we found that response style was highly consistent over time and at least as stable as the latent states themselves. Similarly, the LASARS model revealed that response style demonstrated stronger convergence across multiple measures of impulsivity than the purported impulsivity constructs those measures were intended to capture (Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, & Hawkins, 2026a). In this way, the manner in which respondents express their underlying psychological states may be more consistent across measures than the latent states the surveys are designed to capture, even when the instruments differ in the number and labelling of their response options. This finding is noteworthy because it suggests that the reliability and convergent validity commonly observed across psychological measures may reflect not only overlap in latent states, but also consistency in the ways individuals respond to rating scales.

However, whether response style remains stable across different psychological domains remains an open question. This poses a more demanding test of the LASARS model, as the consistency observed within a single construct could alternatively reflect shared variance between latent states and response style, or construct-specific measurement artefacts. Although our recent findings suggest that response style is highly stable over time and across different measures of impulsivity (Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, & Hawkins, 2026a), other item-level modelling approaches have indicated that certain response behaviours may vary across tasks and response formats (Ames, 2022; Bolt & Meng, 2025). Consequently, the extent to which response styles reflect domain-general individual differences, rather than context-dependent response tendencies, remains an important issue for establishing the validity and comparability of psychological measurement. Furthermore, clarifying the range of contexts over which response style remains stable could inform the development of response style theory and provide clearer guidance regarding the analysis and interpretation of Likert-scale data.

In this paper, we investigate whether response style remains consistent across different psychological domains and, moreover, whether that consistency is sufficient to justify the assumption that individuals adopt the same response strategies across survey contexts. We first demonstrate that response style is remarkably similar across a diverse range of instruments.

Guided by this finding, we then tested a more stringent psychological assumption: that people have the same response style across surveys. We found that this more parsimonious approach neither diminishes the model's ability to accurately describe observed responses nor alters the substantive conclusions drawn from the data. These findings provide support for the proposition that response style reflects a stable, domain-general characteristic of respondents.

Method

A total of 145 participants were recruited through Prolific Academic and completed four surveys: the Arnett Inventory of Sensation Seeking (AISS; Arnett, 1994), the Rational Experiential Inventory (REI; Pacini and Epstein, 1999), the Short Dark Triad (SD3; Jones and Paulhus, 2014) and the Spheres of Control Scale (SOC; Paulhus and Christie, 1981; Paulhus and Van Selst, 1990) in a randomised order during June 2026. Participants also completed the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS; Lovibond, Lovibond, et al., 1995) following the primary measures; however, the DASS data are not relevant to the present study and were not analysed. Participants received £2.85 for their participation, and the median completion time was 20 minutes. Ethical approval was granted by the University of Newcastle Human Research Ethics Committee. The sample comprised 75 females and 70 males residing across 24 countries, with a mean age of 32.31 years (SD = 11.69).

The four surveys detailed in Table 1 were selected because they assess psychological domains that are theoretically distinct from one another, and have at least some reverse-coded items. The AISS measures sensation seeking through preferences for new (novelty) and exciting (intensity) experiences (Arnett, 1994), whereas the REI (Pacini & Epstein, 1999) assesses individual differences in thinking styles, distinguishing between thought-based processing (rational) and intuitive (experiential) processing preferences. Following the recommendations of Spittal et al. (2002), we adopted a hybrid measure of the SOC, which assesses perceived control across personal, interpersonal, and sociopolitical domains. We used the Interpersonal Control (IPC) and Sociopolitical Control (SPC) subscales from the SOC-1 (Paulhus & Christie, 1981) and the Personal Control (PC) subscale from the SOC-3 (Paulhus & Van Selst, 1990) which produces a clearer three-factor structure than either of the independent versions while remaining highly reliable (Spittal et al., 2002). The SD3 measures three aversive facets of personality -Machiavellianism, Narcissism and Psychopathy. The original survey contains nine items for each of the three subscales, however, due to an administrative error, data from two machiavellianism and one psychopathy items could not be used in this analysis. To facilitate direct comparison of response style across instruments, the response options for all measures were standardised to a common five-point Likert scale with the labels1 = Disagree,2 = Slightly Disagree,3 = Neutral,4 = Slightly Agree, and5 = Agree. To test different assumptions about the consistency of response styles across different survey instruments, we tested two parameterisations of the model. In the first, which we call the 'Separate' Model, we assume that people approach different surveys with different response strategies. Under this specification, latent states were estimated for each construct measured across the four surveys, yielding a total of 10 latent state parameters (cf. rows in Table 1), while each of the four surveys was assigned its own set of three response style parameters (centre, odd, and directional responding), resulting in 12 response style parameters overall. Thus, the Separate model comprised 22 person-level parameters in total. In the second model, which we call the 'Shared' model, we assume that people adopt identical response strategies across all survey instruments. Under this specification, the same 10 latent state parameters estimated by the Separate model were retained, but only a single set of three response style parameters was estimated and assumed to apply across all four survey instruments, resulting in a total of 13 parameters.

For both models, parameters were estimated using the Particle Metropolis within Gibbs (PMwG) sampling algorithm (Cooper et al., 2024; Gunawan et al., 2020). Sampling consisted of 3,000 iterations of 50 particles for all three PMwG stages. All analyses were conducted using samples from the final sampling stage only. Standard multivariate normal priors with mean zero and an identity covariance matrix were specified for all person-level parameters. The target particle acceptance rate was set to .95. Reported correlations were derived from the estimated covariance matrix. For additional guidance on selection of sampling details, see Kuhne et al., 2026.

Contrasting the two LASARS models allowed us to directly test whether domain-specific response style parameters (measured by the Separate model) meaningfully improve model performance beyond a domain-general representation of response behaviour (measured by the Shared model). To evaluate the extent to which response style parameters could be shared across instruments, we generated 100 posterior predictions for each participant from both the Separate and Shared models and averaged these across sampling iterations. We then compared these predicted scores with the observed scale scores calculated with the conventional scoring method to test the respective models' abilities to reproduce observed responses.

We also ran preliminary checks to ensure that our changes to the survey response formats did not affect their normative interpretation. To do this, we used what will henceforth be referred to as the 'conventional scoring method', whereby reverse-coded items are scored and responses are summed to create a scale score. Although no widely accepted normative values exist for the REI, the observed scores fell within the range reported in previous research (McLaughlin et al., 2014).

Similarly, scores on the SD3 were consistent with those reported in other samples (Latham & Stephenson, 2025), as were all three SOC scales (Spittal et al., 2002). The intensity AISS scores were comparable, though our sample's novelty scores were generally higher (M = 24.20 from a possible 50) than previously reported group means (M = 24.76 from a possible 40). Descriptive statistics for the sample based on conventional scoring procedures are included in the Supporting Information.

Results

Figure 1 shows the correlations between scales when scores are calculated using the conventional sum-scoring approach. Although these scales were selected because they measure theoretically unrelated domains, several moderate and theoretically explainable correlations are evident. The weak-moderate relationship between the narcissism and SOC scales, for example, likely reflects the heightened perceptions of success and competence in both personal and social domains that are characteristic of individuals with stronger narcissistic tendencies (Back et al., 2013).

However, several statistically significant correlations between instruments remain (as indicated by the asterisks in Figure 1) that are not readily explained by existing theory. One plausible explanation is that conventional sum-scoring conflates latent state expression with response style.

As a result, some of the observed associations may reflect similarities in response behaviour rather than genuine overlap between the underlying psychological constructs.

Response Styles Are Stable Across Domains

To examine the stability of response style across psychological domains, we first fitted a version of the LASARS model in which each survey instrument was assigned an independent set of response style parameters (the "Separate" model). The left-hand panel of Figure 2 presents the distribution of correlation coefficients between equivalent response style parameters across all survey instruments. For instance, the centre preference parameter estimated from the REI was correlated with the centre preference parameter estimated from each of the other three surveys, and each correlation is represented in the boxplot. Across all instruments, response style parameters showed strong positive correlations, with posterior means ranging from .51 to .87.

The strongest relationships were observed between the centre preference parameters estimated from different surveys (all correlations above .74), whereas directional responding parameters tended to exhibit somewhat weaker, although still substantial, associations (.51 to .63). A matrix with all scale correlations is included in the Supporting Information. The 95% credible intervals for all like-for-like response style correlation coefficients excluded 0, providing strong evidence that individual differences in response style generalise across psychological domains with remarkable consistency.

Importantly, Figure 2 shows that the correlations between response style parameters were stronger overall than those between latent states. Even latent states within the same survey instrument (centre panel of Figure 2) were generally less strongly correlated than the response style estimates, with correlations ranging from .18 to .66 and 8% with 95% credible intervals that included zero. Similarly, and perhaps unsurprisingly given that the instruments were selected to capture relatively distinct psychological constructs, correlations between latent states across different survey instruments were weaker still, ranging from -.18 to .62. More than half of these cross-instrument correlations (54%) had 95% credible intervals that included zero. Taken together, this result suggests that people's response strategies are remarkably similar across different survey instruments, even when the surveys measure relatively distinct psychological constructs.

It should be noted that the correlations shown in Figure 2 provide a cleaner estimate of latent state relationships than those presented in Figure 1, as they are free from the potentially confounding effects of response style. Once the influence of response style is removed, some latent state correlations are attenuated whereas others are strengthened (compared with conventional scoring method scores); however, the overall pattern of relationships and the substantive conclusions remain largely unchanged. Where stronger associations are observed between model scores, they typically occur between conceptually related constructs or are readily explained by existing theory and are also present between conventionally calculated scores in Figure 1, suggesting that the latent state modelling primarily strengthened existing associations rather than altering the substantive conclusions. For example, both REI scales, which capture distinct thinking styles, exhibited moderate correlations with the SOC scales which is plausible given that both measures relate, albeit in different ways, to an individual's perceived competence and sense of agency in navigating their environment. Consistent with this interpretation, previous research has reported moderate associations between locus of control and thinking styles, although within different task contexts and theoretical frameworks than those examined in the present study (Başol & Türkoglu, 2009). Hence, despite selecting these surveys because they were expected to be largely unrelated, some degree of theoretical overlap is evident among certain subscales, which is difficult to avoid when assessing a broad range of latent states within a single study. Overall, however, the pattern of latent state correlations suggests that the measures capture largely distinct constructs while exhibiting theoretically coherent overlap where meaningful substantive relationships would be expected.

Response Styles Are the Same Across Domains

Given the strong consistency of response style observed across psychological domains which are mainly distinct from one another, it may be reasonable to assume that individuals approach different survey instruments with the same underlying response preferences, at least when response formats are held constant.

This comparison allowed us to assess whether the additional complexity of estimating instrument-specific response style parameters yielded meaningful gains in predictive performance. If the two models exhibit comparable predictive ability, the Shared model would be preferred on the grounds of parsimony, suggesting that a common response style process adequately characterises responding across instruments. Discussion

In this paper, we examined whether decisions about communicating latent states are consistent across surveys measuring theoretically distinct psychological constructs. We first demonstrated that, when response style parameters were estimated separately for each instrument, the resulting response style estimates were remarkably strongly correlated across tasks, despite the weak relationships observed between the latent states themselves. We then evaluated the stronger assumption that individuals adopt the same response strategies across instruments by comparing a model with instrument-specific response style parameters to a more parsimonious model in which response style was shared across all measures. Despite the substantial reduction in model complexity, there was virtually identical latent state identification between the Separate and Shared models, and the substantive conclusions drawn from the two were indistinguishable for all but one participant-latent state combination. Taken together, these findings provide strong evidence that response style reflects a stable, domain-general tendency that can be modelled parsimoniously without compromising predictive performance or the interpretation of latent states.

These findings are consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating that response styles exhibit substantial stability across both time (Weijters et al., 2010; Wetzel et al., 2016) and measurement contexts (Danner et al., 2015; Kieruj & Moors, 2013; Wetzel et al., 2013). The present work extends previous applications of the LASARS model to measures of impulsivity (Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, & Hawkins, 2026a) by showing that response style remains highly consistent even across instruments assessing theoretically distinct psychological domains. At first glance, these findings may appear to contrast with evidence from item-level modelling approaches (Ames, 2022; Bolt & Meng, 2025), which suggest that response behaviour can vary according to item and task characteristics. However, the LASARS framework adopts a broader perspective, conceptualising response style as a three-dimensional signature of responding.

Although this signature may not be perfectly invariant across contexts, it appears sufficiently stable to be meaningfully characterised at the person level and to capture substantial variation in response tendencies across instruments. Our results suggest that, although item and task characteristics may influence responding to some extent, these influences are generally insufficient to produce meaningful changes in the underlying response preferences that drive substantive conclusions about latent states, at least under the conditions tested here.

These findings have interesting theoretical implications for the study and understanding of response style. Specifically, they suggest that response style is sufficiently consistent across distinct psychological domains to be treated as a common underlying tendency rather than a construct-specific artefact. This result provides further support for the theoretical position that response style represents a meaningful source of individual differences (Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, & Hawkins, 2026a) and that the LASARS model's assumption of stability is appropriate, even in circumstances where the survey instruments measure largely theoretically distinct psychological constructs. From this perspective, response style may capture information about enduring psychological dispositions in its own right, rather than simply reflecting measurement error or random response variability, as has been previously assumed (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001). Such an interpretation opens several interesting theoretical avenues for future research, including the possibility that response style is linked to broad personality characteristics, cognitive processing preferences, motivational factors, or habitual strategies for interacting with rating scales. For example, Grimmond et al. (2025) found that extreme responders tended to be higher in all Big-Five personality traits (openness, extraversion, agreeableness and conscientiousness) except neuroticism, in which they were lower. Understanding these relationships may ultimately provide insight not only into how people respond to surveys, but also into stable aspects of individual psychological functioning that are expressed through response behaviour.

These findings also have important practical implications for the measurement and analysis of Likert-scale data. Our results suggest that, when response formats are equivalent, it is both theoretically and empirically justifiable to assume that individuals bring the same underlying response preferences to different survey instruments. Broadly, the ability to estimate response style jointly across multiple instruments may help mitigate limitations associated with imperfect scale design. For example, information about a respondent's response style obtained from a well-designed instrument, such as one with a balanced mixture of positively and negatively worded items, may assist in distinguishing response style from latent state variation in measures where aspects of response behaviour are more strongly confounded with construct measurement (e.g., directional responding; see Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, and Hawkins, 2026a).

When adopting the LASARS model, specifically, future applications can employ a more parsimonious specification in which a single set of response style parameters is shared across measures, substantially reducing model complexity without compromising inference. This simplification also makes the model easier to apply in practice, as data from multiple instruments can be combined and analysed simultaneously within the lasars jamovi module (Grimmond, Hawkins, et al., 2026a) or R package (Grimmond, Hawkins, et al., 2026b). Further details on implementation can be found in Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, and Hawkins (2026b). In this way, modelling response style across instruments may improve both the precision and interpretability of latent state estimates.

Despite these implications, several limitations should be acknowledged. Previous research has shown that characteristics of the survey instrument, such as the number of response categories, the labelling of response options, item wording, and overall scale structure, can all affect how respondents use rating scales (Kusmaryono et al., 2022; Leung, 2011). Consequently, response style may reflect not only stable individual differences but also systematic features of the response format itself. Because all instruments in the present study were administered using an identical response format, the current design cannot fully disentangle these sources of variation. Although previous LASARS-based research suggests that response style remains largely consistent across tasks that differ in their response-option labels and category structures (Grimmond, Brown, Gronau, & Hawkins, 2026a), the extent to which this stability persists when both psychological domain and response format vary simultaneously remains uncertain. Future research should, therefore, investigate the generalisability of response style across distinct constructs while systematically manipulating response formats. Such work would provide a more stringent test of the domain-generality of response style and help identify the boundary conditions under which the assumption of response style stability remains appropriate.

It also remains unclear whether these findings generalise beyond the types of individual differences measures examined in the present study. While the current results suggest that response style is highly stable across a range of psychological trait measures, other forms of self-report assessment may place different demands on respondents and, therefore, elicit different response behaviours. For example, measures of preferences, confidence judgements, or attitudes may engage distinct cognitive processes, while surveys addressing sensitive topics may be more susceptible to socially desirable responding, impression management, or deliberate misreporting (Krumpal, 2013). Such factors could weaken the consistency of response style observed across tasks and limit the generalisability of the present findings. Future research should, therefore, investigate the stability of response style across a broader range of measurement contexts, including preferential judgements, confidence ratings, and attitudes towards socially sensitive issues, to better understand the conditions under which response style can be considered a domain-general individual characteristic.

In conclusion, the present findings provide strong evidence that response style is a stable and domain-general characteristic that extends across survey instruments assessing theoretically distinct psychological constructs. When response style was estimated separately for each instrument, the resulting parameters exhibited remarkable consistency across tasks, despite the weak relationships observed between the latent states themselves. Moreover, assuming a common response style across instruments produced virtually identical person-level latent state estimates and predictive performance while substantially reducing model complexity. Together, these results support the central assumption of the LASARS framework that response style reflects an enduring aspect of how individuals interact with rating scales, rather than a task-specific source of measurement artefact. More broadly, the findings suggest that response style should not be viewed solely as a nuisance variable to be controlled, but as a meaningful individual difference worthy of investigation in its own right. We, therefore, encourage researchers to move beyond treating response style as a source of error and instead routinely measure, model, and report it alongside substantive psychological constructs.

Respondents are assumed to answer survey items by sampling from their latent state distribution and selecting the response option associated with the bin in which that sample falls. Under this framework, survey responses are determined by two components: the location of the latent state distribution and the placement of the response thresholds. The mean of the latent state distribution (µ) serves as a proxy for an individual's level on the underlying construct, with higher values corresponding to higher trait levels and a greater likelihood of selecting higher response options. Response style is captured by the locations of the response thresholds, the placement of which is governed by three response style parameters, each representing a distinct aspect of responding. Higher values of the centre preference parameter reflect greater overuse of middle response options. The directional preference parameter captures asymmetry in responding, with higher values indicating a tendency towards selecting options on the right-hand side of the scale. Finally, the odds preference parameter describes non-uniform use of response options, reflecting a general tendency to favour a combination of outer and middle options which, for a 5-point scale, corresponds with a preference for odd-numbered response options (i.e., 1, 3, and 5).
