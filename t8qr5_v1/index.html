What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Knowledge tasks (KTs) are commonly used in medical device human factors (HF) testing to assess participant understanding that cannot be evaluated through direct observation. However, it is unclear whether presenting KTs orally or in writing influences participant behavior or task performance. This study compared oral and written KT prompt presentation in 38 lay adults who each completed 10 KTs for two consumer medical devices. Participants responded verbally in both groups and had access to the device instructions for use (IFU). The Written KT delivery resulted in statistically higher accuracy than the Oral delivery for the blood pressure monitor (65% vs. 47%) and nebulizer (85% vs. 72%), as well as greater frequency of IFU consultation (64% vs. 32% and 65% vs. 36%, respectively). The Written KT Group also had longer pre-response and total task times and fewer moderator interventions, whereas response duration did not differ between groups. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly examine KT prompt delivery in medical device HF testing. The findings indicate that oral and written KT prompts may lead to different results and participant behavior. Accordingly, whether KT prompts are presented orally or in writing should be treated as a deliberate study design choice, standardized within studies, and clearly reported.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1 Knowledge tasks in medical device human factors evaluation

Human factors (HF) validation testing is intended to demonstrate that intended users can use a medical device safely and effectively under conditions that generalize to actual use. In this context, the user-interface (UI) includes controls and displays, packaging, labeling, instructions for use (IFUs), training, and other sources of information that may shape user behavior (U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA], 2026). Many aspects of device use can be evaluated by directly observing participants perform representative tasks. Other critical aspects of safe and effective use, however, depend on information or understanding that may not produce an observable action during the test session. This could include understanding warnings, contraindications, maintenance or cleaning requirements, environmental limitations, or other important instructions.

Knowledge tasks (KTs) can be used to address this problem. Rather than observing how a participant performs a physical action, evaluators use direct questioning to assess whether participants understand information needed for safe and effective use. In this sense, KTs are simply questions the researchers ask the participant. UI components evaluated through KTs may include the instructions for use (IFU), quick start guide, device labeling, and training (FDA, 2026). Thus, KTs evaluate the user's "knowledge" based on how the user perceives and processes the information communicated through the UI (FDA, 2026). Because KT data can contribute to conclusions from HF validation testing, the administration of the KTs and the validity of the respective data is important. Existing regulatory guidance addresses several aspects of KT design and administration, including the use of open-ended and neutrally worded questions or "prompts". However, it does not specify whether KT prompts should be provided as a spoken question from the moderator (i.e., orally) or delivered in writing. Published HF validation studies and FDA-reviewed validation materials frequently describe participants as being asked KT questions by a moderator or interviewed using structured KT questionnaires, indicating that moderator-administered oral questioning is a common approach in practice (Adelman et al., 2019; Gwathmey et al., 2026; FDA, 2025). Informal and anecdotal discourse among the medical device HF community suggests that both oral and written modalities are used. However, KT administration procedures are not consistently reported in enough detail to determine how frequently oral versus written prompts are used.

A KT question delivered orally or in writing may appear to be a minor procedural choice because the wording of a question can remain identical. In practice, however, oral and written KTs create different task conditions that likely influence participant behavior. A moderator may read each question aloud, a participant may read the question from a card or screen, or both approaches may be combined. With oral delivery, the KT is transient once it has been spoken unless it is repeated. With written delivery, the KT can remain visible and can be revisited while the participant considers the question or consults the IFU. Oral KTs delivered by a moderator also occur within a live social interaction, whereas written KTs can be reviewed more independently. Thus, even when the wording is held constant, changing how a KT question is presented changes what information remains available to the participant during the task.

1.2 Why prompt presentation may matter

Because oral and written KT delivery has not been studied directly, there is no single established framework for predicting how the two approaches should differ. However, relevant research suggests two broad ways in which KT delivery could influence participant behavior. One concerns how information is processed and remains available during the task, and the other concerns the social and conversational context in which participants are asked to respond.

1.2.1 Information availability and processing

One reason to expect differences is the continued availability of the question. When task-relevant information remains externally available, people can return to that information rather than relying entirely on memory. This use of the environment as an external source of information is commonly described as cognitive offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Experimental work indicates that externally available information can support immediate task performance, although reliance on external information can have different effects later on subsequent memory (e.g., Grinschgl et al., 2021). These findings do not establish that written KTs necessarily reduce cognitive load or that oral delivery overloads working memory. Rather, they suggest that the availability of information can change how people use memory and external resources during a task. This may be particularly relevant for KTs, where participants may need to maintain the purpose of a question while deciding whether to consult an IFU and while searching for relevant information. A written KT remains available throughout this process, allowing participants to revisit the question before, during, or after consulting the IFU, whereas an oral KT does not provide the same persistent reference once it has been delivered. This difference in availability and persistent access could therefore affect both information-seeking behavior and task performance by allowing participants to return to what is being asked while they search, evaluate information, or verify an answer. On this basis, written KTs may be associated with greater consultation of relevant IFU information and, potentially, greater KT accuracy.

Other research on presentation modality provides a different prediction. When oral information is brief and can be processed as it is presented, distributing information across auditory and visual channels can improve performance compared with presenting both sources visually (Ginns, 2005). Applied to a KT, this could favor an oral question paired with a visual IFU. However, such effects depend on the characteristics of the task and the information being presented. Leahy and Sweller (2011), for example, showed that an audiovisual advantage could diminish or reverse when spoken information became difficult to maintain because of its transient nature. Medical device KTs differ from the instructional tasks typically examined in this literature because the spoken question may have ended before substantial searching of external reference material begins. Research comparing reading and listening similarly suggests that neither modality is uniformly superior and that performance differences depend on the type of information and whether participants can control the pace of presentation (Clinton-Lisell, 2022). Thus, oral presentation may be advantageous when a question can be readily processed before it disappears, whereas written presentation may be advantageous when continued access to the question is useful during the task.

1.2.2 Social and conversational context

A second reason to expect differences concerns the social and conversational context of administration. Research comparing self-administered and interviewer-administered surveys has found differences in several aspects of responding. Self-administered questionnaires have generally produced less socially desirable responding and somewhat greater response consistency, whereas interviewer-administered approaches have tended to reduce missing responses and can produce more detailed answers to complex open-ended questions (Bowl-ing, 2005; de Leeuw, 2005). Importantly, no single mode was consistently superior, and de Leeuw (2005) noted that mode differences can reflect several factors at once, including how information is presented and whether an interviewer is involved. Although survey questionnaires differ substantially from medical device KTs, this work illustrates that the presence of another person in question administration can affect how the participant responds. Accordingly, an orally presented KT differs from a written prompt not only in sensory modality but also in the moderator's involvement during question delivery.

Within this social context, oral and written prompts may also establish different conversational expectations about when a response should begin. Spoken conversation is characterized by rapid turn-taking with relatively little silence between speakers, and people can begin preparing responses before a question has finished (Stivers et al., 2009; Magyari et al., 2017). Silence following a spoken question can also carry conversational meaning, and faster response preparation may compete with continued processing of the question (Bögels et al., 2015 (Bögels et al., , 2018)). When a moderator asks a KT aloud and then waits for a response, the interaction may therefore implicitly encourage the participant to begin speaking rather than remain silent while continuing to consider the question or consult available reference material. Written delivery may not establish the same turn-taking expectation because silent reading and consideration are inherent to the task. Although this possibility has not been tested directly in medical device KTs, it provides another reason why otherwise identical oral and written KTs could produce different pre-response behavior.

Taken together, these bodies of research suggest that oral and written KT delivery may not be behaviorally equivalent. From an information-processing perspective, persistent written KTs may be advantageous when participants need to maintain the question while consulting external reference materials. From a social and conversational perspective, oral KTs may influence how and when participants begin responding and may increase reliance on the moderator during the task. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, nor do they provide a single prediction across all outcomes. Rather, they suggest that changing how an otherwise identical KT question is delivered could alter information-seeking behavior, response behavior, and ultimately task performance.

1.3 Problem Statement

Importantly, these possibilities are drawn primarily from research outside medical device KT testing. Direct empirical evidence comparing oral and written delivery of equivalent prompts in HF testing is currently lacking. Consequently, it remains unknown whether KT delivery affects the performance or behavior observed during KTs. This represents a methodological gap because, if oral and written KTs produce systematically different responses or patterns of information use, the two approaches may not be interchangeable methods of administering the same KT. If oral KTs are commonly used in practice, even modest effects of KT delivery could become a recurring source of method-related variation in validation data, potentially affecting participant behavior, moderator involvement, and the interpretability of KT performance. Understanding whether such differences occur is therefore important for interpreting KT results and for determining whether KT delivery should be considered when designing and reporting HF validation studies.

This study examines whether delivering KTs orally or in writing affects participant behavior and task performance. Adult lay participants completed KTs for two consumer medical devices in either an oral-delivery group, in which the moderator presented each question orally, or a written-delivery group, in which the question was presented and remained visually available. All participants completed KTs for both products. Participants in both groups responded orally and had access to the device IFUs. Therefore, the experimental manipulation concerned KT presentation rather than response modality. Based on the literature reviewed above, we expected the delivery method to influence how participants approached and performed the KTs. More specifically, because written KTs remain available throughout the task, we predicted that written presentation would result in greater consultation of reference material (i.e., IFU) and higher KT accuracy than oral presentation. We also examined the time required before responding to assess whether KT delivery influenced how long participants considered the question or consulted available information before beginning their response. We predicted that this duration would be longer for participants provided with written KTs compared to oral KTs. Lastly, state anxiety was assessed as a secondary and exploratory outcome because oral KTs may differ from written presentation in its social, conversational, and evaluative context, potentially affecting both the participant's experience and perceived expectation to respond. As this anxiety measure was exploratory, no formal prediction was made. Together, these measures were used to determine whether different methods of delivering the same KT question influence participant behavior and task performance.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Participants

Thirty-eight lay adults (19 oral, 19 written) completed the study. Participants completed a recruitment screener and were recruited to provide variation in age, education, sex, prior participation in usability studies, and experience with home-use blood pressure monitors and nebulizers. Eligible participants were at least 18 years old, able to read and communicate in English, and able to provide informed consent. The participants were recruited from the general public to represent the user population of consumer and home-use medical devices. The sample ranged in age from 27 to 77 years (M = 50.8, SD = 15.6) and comprised 22 women and 16 men. Educational attainment ranged from high school to graduate degrees. Health literacy, assessed with the Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine-Short Form (REALM-SF; range 0-7), was uniformly high (M = 6.58, SD = 0.60), with 24 of 38 participants scoring the maximum. The oral and written groups were comparable in age (M = 52.6 vs. 48.9 years), sex distribution, and health literacy (see Table 1).

The study was approved by Castle Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. Procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.2 Devices and Materials

Two commercially available consumer medical devices were used: Braun ExactFit 2 Connect Upper Arm Blood Pressure Monitor and a HYDRA-NEB Nebulizer. Saline solution was used in place of medication during simulated use of the nebulizer. The devices were selected to represent different levels of operational and informational complexity while remaining within the over-the-counter and home-use category relevant to lay users. Each device was accompanied by its manufacturer instructions for use (IFU), which were physically present and available to the participant throughout that device's KT block.

Ten KTs were developed for each device (see Appendix A). Questions addressed information contained in the device labeling, including warnings and precautions, contraindications, operating requirements, cleaning and maintenance, and storage. Each KT had predefined success criteria specifying the information required for a correct response. The wording of each question was identical for oral and written groups. In the Oral Group, the moderator delivered the question from a standardized script. In the Written Group, the same question was presented on a 5.5 x 8.5-inch (14.0 x 21.6 cm) printed task card in 12-point Arial font. The task card remained available to the participant throughout the KT.

The device and IFU remained physically available during the KT portion of the session. Participants were not explicitly instructed to consult the IFU for individual questions. Rather, they were informed that materials in the study environment remained available to them. A moderator guide was used to standardize instructions and question delivery. Study sessions were recorded using fixed audio and video equipment to support behavioral coding and timing measurements.

Health literacy was assessed using the Rapid estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine-Short Form (REALM-SF), a seven-item word-recognition measure scored from 0 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater health literacy (Arozullah et al., 2007). State anxiety was assessed using the five-item state version of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S-5; Zsido et al., 2020). Each item is scored on a scale from 1 to 4. The items are summed, which yields a total sum score ranging from 5 to 20, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. The STAI-S-5 was administered before device interaction and again immediately after completion of both KT blocks.

2.3 Procedure

Individual study sessions were conducted in a usability laboratory configured to approximate a home-use setting and lasted approximately 60 minutes. Room layout, lighting, device placement, and recording-equipment placement were standardized across sessions. Participants were assigned to the Oral or Written KT group stratified by sex to maintain comparable sex distributions across groups.

After providing informed consent, participants received a standardized orientation explaining that the purpose of the session was to evaluate device information and assessment methods rather than to test the participant. Participants were instructed to behave as they would if they were in their home and were told that there was no need to rush. Participants then completed the baseline state-anxiety measure, the REALM-SF, and background questions concerning prior experience with blood pressure monitors and nebulizers.

For the first device, participants received the device, packaging, and IFU and completed a simulated-use task. Because KT administration always takes place after simulated-use tasks during HF validation studies, it was important to establish a similar context. For the blood pressure monitor, the simulated-use activity involved preparing and positioning the cuff and initiating a measurement. For the nebulizer, participants prepared and assembled the device using saline solution but did not complete an actual treatment. Given the purpose of the study, simulated-use performance was not an outcome of the present study and was therefore not assessed.

Participants then completed the 10 KTs for the first device. In the Oral Group, the moderator read each question aloud from the standardized script and the participant responded verbally. In the Written Group, the participant read each question from the task card and responded verbally. The device materials, including the IFU, remained available throughout the KT block. Moderator interactions, IFU consultation behavior, KT responses, and response timing were recorded.

The same procedure was then repeated for the second device. Device order was counterbalanced across participants, and KT order within each device was counterbalanced using 10 predefined KT sequences. Following completion of both KT blocks, participants completed the second state-anxiety assessment. The session concluded with a standardized debrief that allowed the moderator to explore incorrect responses, perceived difficulty, stress or frustration, and any confusion regarding task expectations.

2.4 Data Analysis

Analyses were conducted in R 4.6.0 (R Core Team, 2026). The analysis dataset contained one observation for each participant, device, and KT. KT-level observations were summarized separately for each participant and device. KT accuracy and IFU consultation were calculated as percentages across the 10 KTs, pre-response time, response duration, and total time were averaged across KTs, and moderator interventions were summed across KTs. Summed total time was calculated by adding the total times for all 10 KTs when complete timing data were available. The analytic dataset contained 38 participants, with 19 participants in each KT group.

KT accuracy was determined using predefined task-specific success criteria. Responses containing all required critical elements and no contradictory misconception were coded as successful. Partially correct or incorrect responses were treated as unsuccessful. Accuracy for each participant and device was calculated as the percentage of the 10 KTs answered successfully.

IFU consultation was coded for each KT using four observational categories: (1) did not look at the IFU, (2) looked at the IFU but did not seemingly read it, (3) looked at and seemingly read the IFU silently, and (4) looked at and read the IFU aloud. For the analysis, categories 3 and 4 were classified as IFU consultation, whereas categories 1 and 2 were classified as no consultation. Thus, the consultation outcome reflected whether the participant read information from the IFU, rather than whether they merely looked toward it. For each participant and device, consultation rate was calculated as the percentage of the 10 KTs during which IFU consultation occurred.

Three timing measures were derived for each KT. Pre-response time (PT) was defined as the interval from prompt onset to the beginning of the participant's verbal response. Prompt onset was defined as the start of verbal question delivery in the Oral Group and the moment the written prompt was placed in front of the participant in the Written Group. Response duration (RD) was the interval from response initiation to response completion, and total time (TT) was the interval from prompt onset to response completion. RD was examined as a secondary timing, or a specificity, measure to determine whether timing differences were specific to the pre-response period. Because response modality was held constant across groups, no systematic difference in RD was expected. Mean PT, RD, and TT were calculated separately for each participant and device across available KTs. Total KT-block time was also calculated as the sum of TT across the 10 KTs. This measure was included only when timing data were available for all 10 KTs for that participant-device combination.

Moderator interventions were coded for each KT as present or absent. An intervention was recorded when the participant requested moderator involvement beyond the standardized question delivery, such as repetition of the question, clarification of the question, or other assistance needed to continue responding. For each participant and device, the number of KTs with at least one moderator intervention was summed across the 10 KTs, yielding a count from 0 to 10. State anxiety was measured before the device tasks and after completion of both KT blocks. A post-minus-baseline change score was calculated to characterize changes in state anxiety over the study session. Because anxiety was secondary to the primary KT outcomes, it was treated as an exploratory measure.

The originally planned parametric analyses comprised 2 (prompt: Oral, Written) × 2 (device: blood pressure monitor, nebulizer) mixed ANOVAs, with prompt as a between-participant factor and device as a within-participant factor. Separate ANOVAs were conducted for accuracy, IFU consultation, PT, RD, TT, summed total time, and moderator interventions, using the original outcome scales without transformation. Each analysis included participants with observations for both devices for the relevant outcome. State anxiety was analyzed using a 2 (prompt) × 2 (time: pre-task, post-task) mixed ANOVA. The prompt × time interaction tested whether change differed between prompt groups. Omnibus effects were summarized using F statistics, p values, and partial eta squared. P values were considered statistically significant at p < .05.

Assumption checks included Shapiro-Wilk tests, Levene's tests, and visual inspection of distributions. Because these checks identified violations, nonparametric analyses were also conducted using two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests within each device and a Mann-Whitney U comparison of anxiety change scores between groups. The parametric and nonparametric results were examined for agreement in direction and interpretation. The nonparametric results produced the same overall pattern as the primary parametric analyses. Thus, the parametric results are reported for interpretability.

4. Discussion

This study examined whether oral and written delivery, of otherwise equivalent KTs, produced different patterns of behavior and performance during medical device usability testing. Across both devices, the Written Group resulted in higher KT accuracy, greater IFU consultation, longer pre-response and total task times, and fewer participant-requested moderator interventions. In contrast, response duration was similar across KT groups, and there was no clear evidence that state anxiety changed differently between groups. Taken together, these findings indicate that oral and written KT prompts may not be interchangeable approaches to administering the same question. Rather, written KTs lead to higher KT accuracy, slower pre-response time, and greater use of available reference materials. The pattern of results is consistent with differences in how information remains available and is used during the task and the social and conversational context created by the method of KT delivery. One of the primary findings from this study was that participants in the Written Group consulted the IFU more frequently and performed better on the KTs. Specifically, KT mean accuracy was approximately 18 percentage points higher for the blood pressure monitor and 13 percentage points higher for the nebulizer under Written KTs. IFU consultation was approximately 32 and 29 percentage points higher, respectively, in the Written Group. This pattern is consistent with the literature on cognitive offloading and persistent external information (Risko & Gilbert, 2016; Grinschgl et al., 2021). Rather than relying solely on their memory of the original KT prompt, participants in the Written Group had continued physical access to the question throughout the KT. Therefore, participants could return to the question while deciding whether to consult the IFU and while searching or evaluating information. The greater IFU consultation observed in the Written Group is therefore consistent with the possibility that persistent access to the question supported a different information-seeking strategy. Intuitively, it makes sense that participants who use the available reference materials to answer the question, as opposed to not using any reference materials at all, would have a greater chance of answering the KT correctly. Im-portantly, however, the present data do not establish a causal relationship that greater IFU consultation led to the increase in KT accuracy, despite the observed advantages for both the KT accuracy and IFU consultation rate in the written group.

Broader research on presentation modality also provides a perspective on these findings. Auditory information presented alongside visual information can sometimes improve performance by distributing information across sensory channels (Ginns, 2005). Applied to a KT, this could favor an oral question paired with a visual IFU. However, this advantage can diminish when spoken information is transient but remains relevant as the task continues (Leahy and Sweller, 2011). In other words, spoken or oral information may be less advantageous when people need to refer back to the information after it has been presented. This may be particularly relevant for KTs because participants need to maintain the question's meaning while potentially deciding whether to consult the IFU, searching the material, evaluating information, and formulating a response. In the Written Group, the KT remained available throughout this process, allowing participants to return to the question as needed. In the Oral Group, participants instead had to retain the question or request that the moderator repeat it. The greater participant-requested moderator involvement observed in the Oral Group is also consistent with this possibility.

The timing results further clarify how KT prompts affected participant behavior. Preresponse time was longer in the Written Group for both devices, whereas there was no clear evidence of a difference in response duration between groups. Because pre-response time was measured from prompt onset to the initiation of the participant's oral answer, this pattern is consistent with participants in the Written Group spending more time before responding while reading the KT question, deciding whether to consult the IFU, and searching or evaluating reference information. This interpretation is supported by the greater IFU consultation observed in the Written Group. Participants began responding after an average of approximately 14 seconds in the Oral Group versus 25 seconds in the Written Group for the blood pressure monitor, and 13 versus 19 seconds for the nebulizer. Together with the lower IFU consultation rates under Oral KTs, this pattern suggests that participants more often responded from information already available to them rather than first consulting external reference material. The absence of a corresponding difference in response duration further suggests that the additional time observed within the Written KT group occurred primarily before an answer was initiated rather than during verbal response production. This pattern is consistent with the broader cognitive-offloading account (Risko & Gilbert, 2016; Grinschgl et al., 2021).

A second possible explanation is that differences in the social and conversational context of the KT administration may also have contributed to the observed pattern (Bowling, 2005; de Leeuw, 2005; Magyari et al., 2017; Stivers et al., 2009). In the Oral Group, the moderator asked a question and then waited for the participant to respond. This interaction resembles ordinary conversational turn-taking. That is, once one person has asked a question, the other person is generally expected to provide a response. Prior research has shown that people can begin preparing responses before a question has finished (Stivers et al., 2009; Magyari et al., 2017). Silence following a spoken question can also carry conversational meaning, and faster response preparation may compete with continued processing of the question (Bögels et al., 2015 (Bögels et al., , 2018)). Additionally, prior work has shown that interviewer-administered questions can alter response behavior (Bowling, 2005; de Leeuw, 2005). Thus, when the moderator asked a KT aloud and then waited for a response, participants may have experienced an implicit expectation to begin speaking rather than remain silent while continuing to consider the question or consult the IFU. Written KTs may not create the same turn-taking expectation because silent reading and consideration are inherent to the task. The shorter pre-response times observed within the Oral Group are consistent with this possibility. Although state anxiety did not differ clearly between groups, anxiety scores were already low and showed evidence of a floor effect, potentially limiting sensitivity to subtle social or evaluative differences.

Written KTs also required more time overall during KT administration. Across the 10 KTs, participants in the Written Group spent an average of approximately 458 versus 294 seconds on the blood pressure monitor and 330 versus 234 seconds on the nebulizer, corresponding to approximately 2.7 and 1.6 additional minutes per 10-KT block, respectively. These differences should not necessarily be interpreted as equivalent increases in the total duration of a HF validation session. Participants in the Written Group also demonstrated higher KT accuracy, which, during an actual HF evaluation, could reduce the number of unsuccessful KTs requiring subsequent debriefing and root-cause exploration. In practice, therefore, some or all of the additional time spent considering written KTs could potentially be offset by reduced time needed to debrief unsuccessful responses later in the session. The present study did not measure debrief duration on a task-by-task basis and therefore cannot determine whether total KT administration and debrief time would differ between modalities. Even without such an offset, however, the observed increase of approximately 1.6 to 2.7 minutes per 10 KTs suggests that the additional time required for written KTs may be a relatively modest logistical consideration.

KT delivery also influenced the amount of participant-requested moderator involvement. Participants requested fewer moderator interventions under Written than Oral KTs for both devices. These interventions occurred when participants asked the moderator to repeat or clarify the question or requested other assistance needed to continue responding. Moderators did not independently intervene beyond the standardized KT administration. Because participants could revisit a written question on their own, persistent access to the prompt likely reduced the need to ask the moderator to repeat or clarify it. This finding may be methodologically important for two reasons. First, additional moderator involvement creates more opportunities for variation in how KTs are administered, which could in turn reduce standardization and potentially affect the reliability or validity of the resulting data. Second, more participant-moderator interaction may reduce the extent to which the session reflects independent interaction with the device and labeling. HF validation testing places considerable importance on creating test groups that are representative of actual device use. Although KTs inherently involve moderator-participant interaction and may therefore be less representative of actual use than simulated-use tasks, additional moderator involvement beyond standardized question delivery may further reduce that representativeness. In particular, repeated requests for clarification or repetition introduce interactions that would not typically be available when a user independently encounters the device and its labeling.

4.1 Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the experimental manipulation differed along several related dimensions. Oral KTs were auditory, transient, and delivered by the moderator, whereas written KTs were visual, persistent, self-paced, and could be revisited throughout the KT. The present study therefore cannot determine whether the observed differences were driven primarily by information persistence and availability, sensory modality, moderator involvement, or some combination of these factors. This limitation also applies to the possibility that the two groups created different conversational expectations. As discussed above, participants receiving an oral KT may have experienced a greater implicit expectation to begin responding immediately once the moderator finished speaking, whereas participants receiving a written KT may have been more comfortable remaining silent while considering the question or consulting the IFU. Although the longer pre-response times observed in the Written Group are consistent with this possibility, the study was not designed to measure perceived pressure to respond or conversational turn-taking, and the timing results cannot establish that these factors contributed to the observed differences. Future research that independently manipulates prompt persistence and moderator involvement, or directly assesses perceived pressure to respond, could help distinguish these potential mechanisms.

Second, the sample was relatively small and consisted of lay users completing KTs for two consumer medical devices. Although the direction of several effects was consistent across devices, confidence intervals around the effect-size estimates were relatively wide, and generalizability to other devices, more complex KTs, or professional-user populations remains uncertain. Health literacy was also high in the present sample, which may limit generalizability to populations for whom understanding or retaining KTs could be more difficult. Finally, the absence of a clear difference in state anxiety should be interpreted cautiously. Baseline anxiety was already low, suggesting limited sensitivity to relatively subtle social or evaluative differences between oral and written KTs.

Future research should more directly separate the mechanisms that differ between oral and written KTs. For example, including a group in which KTs are presented both orally and in writing could help distinguish the effects of auditory presentation from those of persistent access to the question. In practice, this combined approach may also be particularly relevant because moderators delivering a KT orally while simultaneously providing the question in writing is a legitimate option. Comparing oral-only, written-only, and combined delivery would help determine whether the observed advantage of written KTs is primarily attributable to persistent access, presentation modality, or both. More detailed behavioral measures could also clarify how participants use the prompt and IFU during KTs, including when they begin searching, whether they return to the question, and which information they inspect before responding. Eye-tracking measures may be particularly useful for characterizing these information-seeking patterns and testing whether differences in visual behavior help explain the observed differences in KT performance. Future work could also directly assess perceived pressure to respond or conversational expectations to examine the social and interactional explanation more directly. Replication across a broader range of devices, KT complexity, user populations, and health-literacy levels would also help determine how broadly the present findings generalize.

5. Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly examine the effects of KT prompt delivery within medical device HF testing. Adult lay participants completed KTs for two consumer medical devices under either oral or written KTs that remained available throughout the task, while participant-response was held constant. Participants in the Written KT Group had higher KT accuracy, greater IFU consultation, longer pre-response time, and less reliance on moderator intervention, while response duration remained similar between groups. Together, these findings indicate that oral and written prompts may not be interchangeable approaches to administering the same KT and that the delivery method can influence both participant behavior and the performance observed during testing. These effects may reflect multiple features of KT administration, including the continued availability of written information and differences in the social and conversational context surrounding when participants begin responding. The present study cannot distinguish among these mechanisms, and future research should examine them directly. Nonetheless, how KT questions are delivered should be treated as a deliberate study design choice, standardized within studies, and clearly reported. Although the present findings do not establish that written KTs are universally preferable, they provide initial evidence that persistent written KTs may support greater use of available reference information and higher KT performance during medical device HF testing.

6. Impact Statement

This study provides initial evidence that how knowledge task prompts are delivered can influence participant behavior and performance in medical-device human factors testing.
