Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Participants had to flexibly adjust their behaviour on value-incongruent trials where the stimulusresponse association learned during training was no longer appropriate.

In fact, two recent studies have concluded that when participants are instructed to withhold responses for some stimuli, interference from S-R associations can only be observed for highly consolidated mappings shaped by lifelong real-world reinforcement such as green-go and red-stop (Ceceli et al., 2020; Du & Haith, 2023).

Participants are required to learn the specific outcome associated with each stimulus during training because only some outcomes are valuable during each block.

During the test phase, the value of associated outcomes could change and participants had to choose whether to respond to stimuli on the basis of the associated outcome value.

After half of the trials, the participants were then asked to recall and respond to one of the digits.

In total, 63 participants were recruited from the University of Amsterdam participant website.

At the start of every block, during both training and test phases, participants were instructed on which ice creams they should be collecting in that block using an outcome value instruction screen.

Participants had to select the two ice creams that were valuable in the upcoming block and if they got it incorrect then the outcome value instruction screen and test were repeated until they were correct.

Once the vehicle started moving, the participant had 600ms to push the spacebar if they wished to collect the ice cream outcome that was associated with that stimulus.

If the participant had pushed the space bar to collect the ice cream within the allotted 600ms, the skateboarder moved down to intercept the vehicle as it approached the right-hand side of the screen.

If the participant had not responded within the initial 600ms (before the ice cream appeared) then the vehicle and ice cream continued off screen and the skateboarder missed the intercept point.

Participants were given 300ms to remember the numbers as well as their relative physical size and their relative location.

After completion of the SORT trial, participants were prompted on 50% of trials to report either the number larger in size or value, by pressing a left or right key that corresponded to the location of the digit in question (see Figure 2C).

Participants had 1000ms to make their response and were told ‘too late’ if they did not respond in time, but no feedback was given about the accuracy of their response.

In both conditions, participants were told they would earn an additional point when making the correct response and lose a point for the incorrect response.

In the forward digit-span test the participant was required to repeat each sequence back exactly as it had been read, in the backwards test the participants had to repeat the sequence in reverse order.

In the sequencing task the participant had to repeat the sequence in order of size.

This answer was highlighted and then participants were required to answer one question on how difficult it would be to change that response (“This response is something … that would be difficult to change”), followed by the four questions from the SRBAI e.g.

Participants were told that they were going to play the “Sneaky Skateboard Game” where they were playing as a skateboarder and had to collect as many ice creams (and points) as possible.

Prior to the instrumental training phase participants completed a short demo with discriminative stimuli (vehicles) that did not appear in the real task.

Participants were told that the outcomes would no longer be visible because the drivers put an advertisement on top, and that the pizza’s they had to collect differed from the first part of practice (i.e. some were devalued and some upvalued).

In total, participants completed 36 training blocks, resulting in a total of 576 training trials and 72 stimulus-response repetitions per stimulus.

Every 6 blocks, participants were allowed to take a break of 30 seconds and could resume the task at any time during that period by pressing the space bar.

Next, participants were instructed that, just like before, they would be informed at the beginning of each block as to which ice creams they needed to collect.

Participants were also told that they were still earning points for collecting valuable ice creams (and losing points for collecting not-valuable ice creams).

When participants had completed the four test blocks, they were informed as to the total points they had won.

As expected, participants were better on Stroopcongruent trials (25% of memory trials where the numerical value and physical size of the digit-to-be-reported were compatible; accuracy of 76%, SEM: 5%) relative to the Stroopincongruent trials (75% of memory-trials, mean accuracy 64%, SEM: 4%).

Participants were less accurate on both devalued trials (where they had to now inhibit the Go response) and upvalued trials (where a Go response was now required), relative to trials where the value of the outcome signalled by the cue was congruent with that during training.

Participants were more accurate on Stroopcongruent trials (25% of all memory trials; mean accuracy of 83%, SEM: 3%) relative to the Stroop-incongruent trials (75% of all memory trials; mean accuracy of 75%, SEM: 4%).

Participants were less accurate on both devalued and upvalued trials relative to still-not-valuable and still-valuable trials respectively.

In the slips-of-action task, a variant of the outcome devaluation paradigm, all outcomes are valuable during the instrumental learning phase, meaning that participants are not required to learn the S-O associations to perform well (Buabang et al., 2021; De Houwer et al., 2018).

We did not observe any effects of working memory load on the valuable Go trials at testspecifically, there was no indication that load impaired performance on upvalued trials (where participants had to carry out a response in the presence of a stimulus that had been trained as NoGo).

Specifically, following instrumental training, Wood et al. (2023) presented participants with an outcome (labelled ‘goal-directed cue’ that participants were instructed to respond to; O-R) and following a delay, an irrelevant ‘stimulus cue’ (which could trigger either a congruent or incongruent response; S-R).

Increased slips of action for overtrained stimuli may be observed when participants are under working memory load, thus providing direct evidence for stronger habits, as predicted by the associative dual-process model.

In total, 69 participants were recruited from the University of Amsterdam participant website, following the recruitment protocol of Experiment 1.

Six participants were excluded from all analyses (see results) leaving 63 participants in the analysis.

Including all six possible block combinations of stimuli signalling two valuable and two not-valuable outcomes is different to our previous study where participants had blocks in which the same four stimuli were repeated in alternating fashion(Watson et al., 2023).

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Despite our familiarity with the concept of habits, eliciting and measuring habitual "slips of action" experimentally in humans has proven to be difficult. A possible explanation is that participants in psychological experiments actively recruit goal-directed control and therefore make few habitual slips-of-action in the presence of stimuli signalling devalued outcomes.

In the current experiment we used the Symmetrical Outcome Revaluation Task in combination with a working memory load in an attempt to tip the balance from goal-directed control to habitual control. During the instrumental learning phase, participants learned to make a Go response to stimuli signalling valuable outcomes (and points) while not responding (NoGo) to stimuli signalling not-valuable outcomes. During the subsequent test phase, the outcomes signalled by the stimuli were either value-congruent with training (stillvaluable and still-not-valuable), or value-incongruent (devalued and upvalued). Participants had to flexibly adjust their behaviour on value-incongruent trials where the stimulusresponse association learned during training was no longer appropriate. For half the participants, a concurrent working memory load was imposed during the test phase. In line with our preregistered hypotheses, participants showed evidence for habitual slips of action across two experiments, but those under working memory load showed increased habitual responding (specifically failures to inhibit prepotent Go responses in the presence of stimuli signalling devalued outcomes). This central finding suggests that a working memory load can be used to reveal habits in humans.

Introduction

The term 'habit' is used frequently in everyday language to refer to behaviours that are learnt via behavioural repetition and carried out relatively automatically. Influential accounts of habit in human behaviour consistently emphasise the role of stable cue-response associations, automaticity, and reduced reliance on deliberative goal evaluation (de Wit & Dickinson, 2009; Gardner et al., 2024; Mazar & W. Wood, 2018; Verplanken & Orbell, 2003; W. Wood & Rünger, 2016). Sometimes these habits are adaptive (e.g., regularly going to the gym at the same time each day) but at other times they can be maladaptive (e.g., regularly eating chocolate on the couch at the same time each day).

The associative dual-process model of behaviour defines habits as responses (R) that are elicited by contexts and stimuli (S), regardless of the current desirability of the outcome (de Wit & Dickinson, 2009; Dickinson, 1994 Dickinson, , 2016)). These can be contrasted with goal-directed actions, driven by anticipation of and current desire for the outcome (O). Goal-directed behaviour allows for flexible behavioural adjustments when goals change, but at the same time may be relatively cognitively demanding because the expected outcome requires evaluation before the corresponding action is carried out (O-R) 1 . In contrast, habits are mediated by S-R associations, such that these responses are triggered by contexts or stimuli without evaluation of the current desirability of the consequences and can be carried out quickly with relatively little cognitive effort. Importantly, the dual-process account does not require either form of control to be regarded as the default; rather, behaviour may reflect the relative contribution of S-R and outcome-mediated processes, with the balance between them influenced by the conditions under which behaviour is generated.

In the lab, S-R habits have been extensively studied in both rodents and humans using the outcome-devaluation paradigm (Adams, 1982; Balleine & O'Doherty, 2010; Dickinson, 1985). In the human version, subjects learn that different responses earn different outcomes during the initial instrumental training phase (e.g., a left key press leads to chocolate, a right key press to popcorn). One of these outcomes is then devalued,commonly through instructed devaluation, satiation, or by adding bitter solutions to food. Sensitivity to outcome devaluation is then assessed in an extinction test. If the participant flexibly reduces responding on the key that leads to the devalued (relative to the valuable) outcome, then behaviour is under goal-directed control (O-R). By contrast, a failure to reduce responding on the key associated with the devalued outcome is interpreted as an S-R habit. However, revealing human habits in the lab using the outcome devaluation paradigm has proven to be challenging. Healthy participants perform exceptionally well in the outcome devaluation test (de Wit et al., 2018; Morris et al., 2015; van Timmeren et al., 2020; Watson, van Wingen, et al., 2018; Watson et al., 2023). Although they do make some erroneous stimulus-elicited "slips of action" towards devalued outcomes (relative to valuable outcomes), in general they are quite capable of flexibly adjusting their behaviour after changes in outcome value (Watson, van Wingen, et al., 2018).

There are several factors that could tip the balance towards goal-directed control rather than habits in human outcome devaluation tasks. One obvious possibility is that the amount of training offered to participants is typically brief; less than one hour in a single session. This single-session training may result in relatively weak S-R associations that are easier to overcome when the outcome is no longer desirable. However, recent studies that have used extensive training protocols (across days or weeks) with humans and examined short-versus long-trained stimuli have not found evidence that longer relative to shorter training leads to increased 'slips of action' at test following devaluation (de Wit et al., 2018; Du & Haith, 2023; Luque et al., 2020; Pool et al., 2022; Watson et al., 2023). In fact, two recent studies have concluded that when participants are instructed to withhold responses for some stimuli, interference from S-R associations can only be observed for highly consolidated mappings shaped by lifelong real-world reinforcement such as green-go and red-stop (Ceceli et al., 2020; Du & Haith, 2023). Theoretically, however, simple motor S-R associations should be quickly acquired and strengthened on every trial of instrumental training that is followed by reinforcement (Dickinson, 1985 (Dickinson, , 2016;; Dickinson et al., 1995). Slips of action, albeit infrequent, suggest that S-R associations have been learned and are competing for behavioural control (Dickinson, 1994).

Another potential reason that participants make few overt slips of action for devalued outcomes is that goal-directed control may re-emerge during the test phase, obscuring the measurement of S-R habits. It has been proposed that the context switch between training and test phases in human outcome-devaluation tasks may cause goal-directed processes to re-emerge to control responding during the test phase, thus reducing the likelihood of erroneous slips of action (de Wit et al., 2018; Watson & de Wit, 2018; W. Wood et al., 2022). Time pressure is often used to prevent participants from slowing down and reexerting goal-directed control during the test phase (Buabang, Köster, et al., 2023; Frölich et al., 2022; Hardwick et al., 2019; Hennessy et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2024; Luque et al., 2020; Rafei et al., 2026). However, studies using outcome devaluation tests have so far not found clear evidence that participants under time pressure make more overt slips-of-action (i.e. failures to inhibit previously-learned responses) than participants not under time pressure (see also : Du & Haith, 2023; Martínez-López et al., 2025; Rafei et al., 2026).

Another reason that laboratory studies with outcome devaluation tasks likely underestimate the expression of habitual control, is that they are usually performed in the absence of distraction and competing cognitive demands. Under those conditions, participants may be perfectly capable of keeping current goals and action-outcome contingencies active in working memory. Indeed, some of the conditions under which habits typically emerge in everyday life involve some form of depletion of cognitive resources. To illustrate, a previous study examined whether depletion of cognitive resources would lead to an increase in reallife habitual behaviours (Neal et al., 2013). Across a series of experiments, participants first reported on their baseline habitual behaviours and were then surveyed again on days where cognitive resources were assumed to be depleted (e.g., during exam weeks or when asked to repeatedly use their non-dominant hand throughout the day). Participants reported engaging more frequently in habitual behaviours (e.g., eating certain types of food) on 'depletion' days compared to 'ordinary' (non-depletion) days. These findings provide important ecological evidence that limitations on cognitive resources are linked to greater reliance on habitual action selection. However, self-report measures of habit frequency cannot directly dissociate habitual control from changes in goals, motivation, or opportunity. Outcome devaluation paradigms therefore complement this approach by offering a rigorous test of habitual control, allowing behaviour to be assessed when outcome values are experimentally manipulated, and other factors held constant.

While the effect of a working memory load on test performance in the outcome devaluation paradigm has not been investigated yet, several strands of indirect evidence suggest that this is a promising approach to revealing habits in an experimental setting. Firstly, disruption to working memory has been seen to reduce the ability to exhibit flexible declarative knowledge in an associative learning task (Foerde et al., 2006) and bias behaviour toward repeating prior responses in a voluntary task switching paradigm (Demanet et al., 2010).

Previous work with the two-step task has found that a concurrent working memory load reduces model-based control (Otto, Gershman, et al., 2013). Secondly, previous research has suggested that stress tips the balance from goal-directed control towards habits (Fournier et al., 2017; Hartogsveld et al., 2020; Schwabe & Wolf, 2009 , 2010), which has been suggested to be mediated -at least in part -by the negative impact of stress on cognitive resources, including working memory (Schwabe & Wolf, 2013). Indeed, stress specifically impairs to sensitivity outcome devaluation in individuals with low working memory capacity (Quaedflieg et al., 2019), and working memory capacity protects model-based control, a computational counterpart of goal-directed control, from the effects of stress (Otto, Raio, et al., 2013). However, evidence for stress-induced shifts from goal-directed to habitual control is not consistent, with recent work with the outcome devaluation paradigm failing to replicate earlier findings (Buabang, Boddez, et al., 2023; Smeets et al., 2023).

Such inconsistencies may point to important boundary conditions governing the relative expression of goal-directed and habitual action control, highlighting the need for further investigation. Finally, individual differences in baseline working memory capacity have been related to the ability of participants to exert goal-directed control and flexibly adjust behaviour following changes in outcome value in this task (Watson et al., 2023).

In the present study, we therefore investigated directly whether introducing a concurrent working memory load during the test phase shifts the relative expression of goal-directed control and habits in the outcome devaluation task. To this end, we used the Symmetrical Outcome Revaluation Task (SORT: Watson et al., 2023), which incorporates both outcome devaluation and outcome appreciation (i.e., upvaluation), to study the balance between goaldirected and habitual control. Participants learn during the instrumental training phase the correct responses that should be made in the presence of discriminative stimuli in order to earn specific outcomes (and points). Half of the outcomes are valuable, requiring that they be collected (a "Go" response), and the other half are not valuable, meaning that the participant should withhold responding ("NoGo"). Participants are required to learn the specific outcome associated with each stimulus during training because only some outcomes are valuable during each block. As a result, participants cannot maximise their earnings in the first few blocks through simple S-R associations ("to this stimulus I respond") -instead they need to monitor the values of the outcomes they are earning and are initially forced to learn the S-O-R associations ("this stimulus signals outcome Y, which is currently worth points so I should respond"). However, the extensive repetition of consistent S-R associations across training blocks ensures that S-R associations are concurrently learned.

During the test phase (conducted in extinction in which no feedback is given), discriminative stimuli are presented, and participants must quickly decide whether to respond (for valuable outcomes) or to inhibit responding (for not-valuable outcomes). They are instructed that some outcome values change compared to the training phase. The symmetrical design of the task means that the value of outcomes associated with half of the stimuli changes relative to training (see Figure 1A). For these stimuli, the associated outcome is now either devalued (in contrast to training when they were always valuable) or upvalued (now valuable when they were never valuable during training), and previously learned S-R associations can compete for behavioural control. As a baseline control condition, the other half of stimuli have outcomes that do not change value between training and test. As such, the S-R mappings can be relied upon during the test phase.

Importantly, performance on still-not-valuable trials, where participants can rely on learnt S-R "NoGo" associations, is contrasted with performance on devalued trials -which also require response inhibition but lack this benefit of consistent S-R mappings. This therefore controls for an individual's propensity to withhold responding during the test phase, ensuring that poorer performance on devalued trials is not simply due to a greater propensity to (not) respond. Instead, a selective impairment on devalued trials reflects greater difficulty using updated outcome information to flexibly adjust behaviour. Likewise, performance on still-valuable trials can be compared to upvalued trials -both require a Go response, but that was only the learned response for the stimuli signalling still-valuable outcomes.

Furthermore, although habit is often conceptualised in terms of errors of commission, it is also theoretically possible that learned S-R associations contribute to errors of omission when particular stimuli were consistently associated with withholding a response (i.e. a NoGo response; Jahanshahi et al., 2015; Kühn & Brass, 2010). Thus, when a stimulus previously associated with a non-valuable outcome now signals a valuable outcome, responding may require greater reliance on goal-directed control to overcome the previously learned NoGo response. With the SORT, it has been shown that learnt S-R associations induce errors during incongruent trials of the test phase, despite participants having intact explicit knowledge of the appropriate S-O relations (Hennessy et al., 2025; van de Vijver et al., 2023; van Timmeren et al., 2023; Watson et al., 2023).

We used a between-groups design to examine whether working memory load during the test phase would impact goal-directed control and allow S-R associations to be more prominent.

Participants first underwent instrumental training to learn the outcomes associated with discriminative stimuli and to acquire consistent responding towards the 50% of stimuli associated with valuable outcomes. During the test phase, the value of associated outcomes could change and participants had to choose whether to respond to stimuli on the basis of the associated outcome value. For participants in the memory-load group, each test trial began with two numbers with varying features that needed to be remembered. The test trial of the SORT was then completed. After half of the trials, the participants were then asked to recall and respond to one of the digits. Requiring responses on only half of the memory trials ensured that the proportion of responses in the memory test was consistent with the overall proportion of Go responses (i.e. 50%) across the SORT. For participants in the no-memoryload group, no numbers were displayed before each trial. However, on half of the SORT trials they were prompted to press a left or right key after they had completed the trial. We assessed explicit knowledge of all task contingencies at the end of the experiment and also measured working memory capacity, to examine whether individual differences in working memory capacity would affect performance in the task (particularly under working memory load).

Our preregistered hypotheses were that increased reliance on habit responding would be reflected as reduced accuracy on value-incongruent test trials (devalued and upvalued) relative to value-congruent trials (still-not-valuable and still-valuable, respectively). We also expected that these effects would be exaggerated for those under memory load with poorer performance on the devalued (and upvalued) trials relative to participants in the no-memoryload group. Finally, we expected a negative correlation between baseline working memory capacity and our indices of habit, and expected this effect to be more pronounced under memory load.

Methods

All measures and manipulations are disclosed in the present manuscript. The aims, hypotheses, methods and analyses were preregistered prior to data collection (osf.io/yrh5p).

Participants

We aimed to test until we had 32 participants in each group. A pre-registered sample size of 64 was arrived at based on the effect size (f = 0.16) found in a previous study using a between-groups working memory load manipulation and a reinforcement learning task used to index goal-directed (model-based) control (Otto, Gershman, et al., 2013, Experiment 2).

A power analysis carried out in G*power (Faul et al., 2007) suggested that for an interaction between trial type and memory-load group, a total sample size of 56 subjects would be needed based on effect size of f=0.16, an alpha level of .05, power of 0.8 (with G*power defaults of 0.5 for correlation between repeated measures and 1 for non-sphericity correction). Based on previous studies, we assumed that we would need to exclude around 15% of participants and thus aimed to test at least 32 in each group.

In total, 63 participants were recruited from the University of Amsterdam participant website. Advertisements stated that the study investigated whether people fall back on learned habits when experiencing higher working memory load, and that they were going to play a game in which they played a hungry skateboarder and had to collect ice-creams. Eight participants (13%) were excluded from all analyses (see results), leaving 55 participants in the analysis. Demographic information for the remaining participants can be seen in Table 1.

Participants received either €10 or course participation credits for taking part. The Psychology Ethics Committee of the University of Amsterdam approved the study.

Stimuli and materials

Symmetrical Outcome Revaluation Task. The SORT was programmed in Presentation® (Version 20.0, Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc) and consisted of an instrumental training phase and subsequent outcome-revaluation test phase. As can be seen in Figure 1A, eight different vans (each with a different coloured shape) were used as discriminative stimuli and four popular Dutch ice creams functioned as instrumental outcomes (Cornetto, Magnum, Rocket and Calippo). The assignment of ice creams to the four outcomes was randomised across participants. Each ice-cream was associated with two vans, once as a valuable and once as a not-valuable outcome, across separate blocks (see Figure 1A).

At the start of every block, during both training and test phases, participants were instructed on which ice creams they should be collecting in that block using an outcome value instruction screen. Two ice creams were shown highlighted in green (the valuable ice creams) and two were highlighted in red (the not-valuable ice creams; Fig 1B). The position of the valuable ice creams (left or right) was randomized across participants. This was immediately followed by the outcome value memory check. Participants had to select the two ice creams that were valuable in the upcoming block and if they got it incorrect then the outcome value instruction screen and test were repeated until they were correct.

During each training trial, a road was depicted upon which one of the eight discriminative stimuli moved from left to right during the course of a trial (1200 ms total, see Figure 1C).

At the beginning of the trial a cartoon skateboarder was also presented at the top of the screen. Once the vehicle started moving, the participant had 600ms to push the spacebar if they wished to collect the ice cream outcome that was associated with that stimulus. At 600ms (when the vehicle was halfway across the screen) the associated ice cream appeared on top of the vehicle. If the participant had pushed the space bar to collect the ice cream within the allotted 600ms, the skateboarder moved down to intercept the vehicle as it approached the right-hand side of the screen. The ice cream was then left behind with the skate boarder as the vehicle continued, passing off screen at 1200ms. If the participant had not responded within the initial 600ms (before the ice cream appeared) then the vehicle and ice cream continued off screen and the skateboarder missed the intercept point. Regardless of whether participants collected the ice cream or not, no direct feedback was given about the accuracy of the (non-)responsefoot_1 . Instead, the final screen (containing the skateboarder either with the collected ice cream or without) simply paused for 600ms. Although participants did not receive trial feedback, they earned one point for each valuable ice cream that was collected and -1 points for each not-valuable ice cream that was collected (performance feedback was provided at the end of each block). Each trial was followed by an intertrial interval with a random duration between 500 and 1500ms, during which just the background images were presented. Each block of instrumental training consisted of 16 trials in which two stimuli associated with valuable ice creams and two stimuli associated with not-valuable ice creams were each presented four times in total. With a total of eight stimuli, there were six possible combinations of training blocks, each containing two valuable and two not-valuable stimuli. These six possible combinations of blocks were displayed in random order, across each six blocks (for a total of 36 blocks)foot_2 . Across training blocks therefore, each ice cream was equally often valuable as not valuable. Crucially, however, the value assigned to each van stimulus (and accordingly, the associated 'Go' or 'NoGo' response) was consistent throughout the entire training phase allowing for the development of S-R associative links.

The test phase trials were very similar, with each block beginning with instructions as to which ice creams should be collected. The critical test phase manipulation was that all stimuli now appeared in the same block (i.e. both stimuli associated with an ice cream appeared whereas during training they had never appeared in the same block). Because all the vans appeared intermixed during the test phase, it meant that the value of the signalled outcome was congruent with the training value for half of the stimuli but incongruent for the other half. This resulted in four different trial types per block of the test phase (as depicted in Figure 1A). Congruent trials were either 'still-valuable' (the stimulus consistently signalled a valuable outcome across both training and test) or 'still-not-valuable' (stimuli that were consistently signalling a not-valuable outcome across training and test). By contrast, incongruent trials could be either 'upvalued' (stimuli that had consistently signalled a notvaluable ice cream during training, but now signalled a valuable ice cream during test) or 'devalued' (stimuli that had always signalled a valuable ice cream during training, but now signalled a not-valuable ice cream). Within each test block, each of the eight stimuli were presented four times, with trial order randomized per 16 trials (32 trials total per block).

During the test phase the associated ice cream was never shown to the participant (to prevent further learning during the test phase). Instead, the ice cream was replaced by a generic banner saying "ice cream" (see Figure 2B). Additionally, each test-phase block ended with an outcome value memory check, to test whether the participants correctly remembered which outcomes were (not-)valuable during that block.

Numerical Stroop Task /Control Task

Working memory load was operationalised through the numerical Stroop task (Waldron & Ashby, 2001). For those in the memory-load group, each outcome-revaluation task test trial began with two digits presented at the top of the screen in black boxes (see Figure 1F). These digits differed in their value (ranging from 1-9) as well as differing in their physical size (font size 40 -100 points, see Figure 2C). The size and value of the two digits were incongruent in 75% of trials (referred to as 'Stroop-incongruent'). Participants were given 300ms to remember the numbers as well as their relative physical size and their relative location. After completion of the SORT trial, participants were prompted on 50% of trials to report either the number larger in size or value, by pressing a left or right key that corresponded to the location of the digit in question (see Figure 2C). On the remaining trials, no response was required. This ensured that Go and NoGo responses were equally balanced across the memory task, matching the response distribution used in the SORT. Participants had 1000ms to make their response and were told 'too late' if they did not respond in time, but no feedback was given about the accuracy of their response. Participants in the nomemory-load group performed a control task (Figure 2D). Here, the black boxes appeared at the beginning of each trial for 300 ms but no digits were presented. To control for key pressing, participants in the no-memory-load group were instructed after 50% of SORT trials to push either the left or right key. In both conditions, participants were told they would earn an additional point when making the correct response and lose a point for the incorrect response.

Digit Span.

As a measure of working memory capacity, a variant of the digit span test from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-Third Edition (Wechsler & Psychological Corporation, 1997) was administered orally. The experimenter read out a series of digits of increasing length at the rate of one digit per second. In the forward digit-span test the participant was required to repeat each sequence back exactly as it had been read, in the backwards test the participants had to repeat the sequence in reverse order. In the sequencing task the participant had to repeat the sequence in order of size. Sequences started from three digits to a maximum of eight digits (two sequences of each length were included, 12 in total). Each test was stopped if participants made two consecutive errors. Participants received one point for each correct answer with a higher score indicating higher working memory capacity. We used the backwards span task as the measure of working memory capacity. The backwards span has good reliability and consistency and is closely related to performance on other working memory tasks (Waters & Caplan, 2003).

SRBAI.

The Self Report Brief Automaticity Index (Gardner et al., 2012) asks participants to rate their agreement with four statements on a scale ranging from 1 "strongly disagree" to 7 "strongly agree". The items correspond to the degree to which a behaviour begins "before I realise I am doing it", is done "automatically", "without having to consciously remember" and "without thinking". The SRBAI has good reliability and validity (Gardner et al., 2012).

As in our previous study (Watson et al., 2023) we adapted the SRBAI to measure automaticity of stimulus-Go and stimulus-NoGo responses at the end of the instrumental training phase. On each trial participants saw one of the eight vehicles used as discriminative stimuli in the task. They had to indicate whether the correct response for that stimulus had been press or not press. This answer was highlighted and then participants were required to answer one question on how difficult it would be to change that response ("This response is something … that would be difficult to change"), followed by the four questions from the SRBAI e.g. "This response was something I did automatically". Participants used a mouse to respond on a VAS scale ranging from 0 (completely disagree) to 100 (completely agree). This process was repeated for each stimulus and the mean score was taken across the four SRBAI items, calculated separately for the NoGo and Go stimuli.

Procedure

The experiment took place in a plain lab room. Participants were told that they were going to play the "Sneaky Skateboard Game" where they were playing as a skateboarder and had to collect as many ice creams (and points) as possible. The participant with the most points at the end of the study received cinema vouchers. All participants started with 50 points.

Prior to the instrumental training phase participants completed a short demo with discriminative stimuli (vehicles) that did not appear in the real task. During the demo participants learned that they should press the space bar in order to collect valuable pizza but should not respond when a vehicle associated with a not-valuable pizza was present. After one block of the demo-training, they were also instructed about the test-phase. Participants were told that the outcomes would no longer be visible because the drivers put an advertisement on top, and that the pizza's they had to collect differed from the first part of practice (i.e. some were devalued and some upvalued). After conducting one block of demotest, they then began the instrumental training phase with ice creams.

In total, participants completed 36 training blocks, resulting in a total of 576 training trials and 72 stimulus-response repetitions per stimulus. Every 6 blocks, participants were allowed to take a break of 30 seconds and could resume the task at any time during that period by pressing the space bar. At the end of each block participants saw a screen with information on their performance, e.g. "75% correct PRESSES for ice creams that you DID have to collect. 65% correct NO PRESSES for ice creams that you DID NOT have to collect. 1 press that was too late. You earned a total of 64 points!" After the training phase, participants then answered the four questions from the SRBAI and the effort question for each discriminative stimulus that had appeared in the training phase.

At the beginning of the test phase, participants received instructions about the Numerical Stroop task (or control task) that they would be performing concurrently during the test phase. They first completed two practice blocks with the Numerical Stroop task (or control task) and the same pizza scooters and outcomes as during the test-phase demo. They were told that they should focus on performing well on the 'number game' and not worry about collecting the correct pizzas. Next, participants were instructed that, just like before, they would be informed at the beginning of each block as to which ice creams they needed to collect. However, they would have to rely on what they had already learned in the experiment because they would not be told on each trial which ice cream had been earned.

Instead, the vans delivering the ice creams had put an advertisement board on the roof and it was no longer possible to see the ice cream that each was carrying. Participants were also told that they were still earning points for collecting valuable ice creams (and losing points for collecting not-valuable ice creams). They were instructed that during the final phase all the vans would appear intermixed in each block. They then completed four blocks of test trials (total of 128 trials total). At the end of each block participants received feedback about their performance.

When participants had completed the four test blocks, they were informed as to the total points they had won. Finally, they were asked to complete the demographics questionnaire.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis commenced after all data collection had been completed. We first compared the memory-load and no-memory-load groups to ensure that they did not differ significantly in age, gender distribution, baseline working memory capacity or explicit recall of the associative structures underlying the task (i.e., performance on the final tests of associative S-O knowledge).

For the analysis of the training data, the data were collapsed across each two blocks (within which each stimulus was shown 8 times), thus reducing the 36 blocks to eighteen blocks. To be consistent with our previous study using the SORT (Watson et al., 2023) we deviated from the preregistration and used accuracy rather than response rates as the dependent variable in the analysis. For valuable trials, response rates and accuracy are essentially the same metric, whereas on not-valuable trials Go responses are counted as errors. This inconsistency between accuracy and response rates on not-valuable trials creates an unnecessary interaction between response rates and value in the response rate analysis, hence we chose to use accuracy instead. By coding no-responses as accurate on NoGo trials the redundant four-way interaction in the test phase analysis is eliminated. In addition, the expected metric of habitual responding should therefore be reflected as a main effect of congruency (less accurate on devalued relative to still-not-valuable and less accurate on upvalued relative to still-valuable outcomes). The effect of memory-load on habitual responding should be reflected as a 2-way interaction between congruence and memory-load (with lower accuracy on devalued and upvalued trials for the memory-load group relative to the no-memory-load group).

Percentage accuracy during the training phase (i.e. number of responses made during the 600ms response window divided by number of trials * 100) was calculated separately for trials where stimuli signalled valuable versus not valuable outcomes. This was analysed using repeated measures ANOVA with within-subject variables of block (1-6) and outcome value (valuable, not valuable) and the between-subject variable of memory-load group (memory-load vs. no-memory load). For the test phase, accuracy data (% of correct responses made in the 600ms response window) were collapsed across blocks and an ANOVA was used with the between-subject variable of memory load (memory-load group vs. no-memory load group) and within-subject variables of outcome value on the current test block (valuable, not valuable) and congruence between training value and test value (congruent, incongruent). The combination of the latter two variables gives the four trial types indicated previously: still valuable, still not valuable, upvalued and devalued. All Greenhouse-Geisser p-values are reported from the ANOVAs with the original degrees of freedom. In the case of significant interactions, the highest-order interaction is reported.

Finally, we conducted a (preregistered) regression analysis. As in our previous work, we calculated two difference scores for baseline-corrected performance on value-incongruent trials. To calculate this 'habit score', we subtracted accuracy on devalued trials from stillnot-valuable trials and subtracted upvalued trial accuracy from that of still-valuable trials.

Greater difference scores represent more conflict on incongruent trials due to learned S-R associations. These difference scores were then centred and used as the dependent variables in two regression models to determine whether the relationship between baseline working memory capacity and performance on the task was stronger under working memory load.

Preliminary analyses were conducted to ensure that assumptions of normality, linearity, multicollinearity and homoscedasticity were not violated. Normal probability plots and scatterplots of the standardized residuals were checked visually for violations of assumptions (including deviation from normality).

Participants were less accurate on both devalued trials (where they had to now inhibit the Go response) and upvalued trials (where a Go response was now required), relative to trials where the value of the outcome signalled by the cue was congruent with that during training. The expected two-way interaction between value-congruency and memory-load group did not reach significance, F(1,54) = 3.7, p = 0.061, ηp² = 0.06, however as pre-registered, we analysed the results from the test phase separately for the not-valuable (NoGo) and valuable (Go) trials.

For the not-valuable trials (still-not-valuable and devalued), there were significant main effects of value-congruence, F(1,53) = 137.1, p <0.001, ηp² = 0.72, and memory-load group, F(1,53) = 9.2, p = .004, ηp² = 0.15. The expected interaction between these two was also significant, F(1,53) = 7.4, p =0.009, ηp² = 0.12. As hypothesised, participants under memory load were less successful at inhibiting responses on (value-incongruent) devalued trials than participants not under memory load, t(53) = 3.7, p < .001, d = 1.0. The groups did not differ significantly in performance on (value-congruent) still-not-valuable trials, t(53) = 1.9, p = .062, d = 0.5 (see Figure 3). For valuable trials (still-valuable and upvalued) there was a significant main effect of valuecongruence, F(1,53) = 57.9, p <0.001, ηp² = 0.52, such that participants performed better on value-congruent (still-valuable) trials. Contrary to our hypotheses, however, there was no significant main effect of memory-load group, F<1, p = .579, ηp² = 0.005, nor interaction between memory-load group and value-congruence, F<1, p = .599, ηp² = 0.005 for valuable trials.

Working Memory Regression Analyses

We conducted two pre-registered regression analyses to observe whether habitual responding in the task (i.e. larger difference scores for still-not-valuable minus devalued and still-valuable minus upvalued) would be associated with poorer baseline working memory capacity (as indexed by the digit span backwards task), and whether this relation was more pronounced in individuals who performed the task under working memory load.

We first entered the NoGo habit score (accuracy on still-not-valuable minus devalued) as the dependent variable in a hierarchical regression model. We then entered the baseline digitspan backwards score (centred) and the memory-load group assignment at Step 1, followed by the interaction term at Step 2. Preliminary analyses were conducted to ensure that assumptions of linearity, multicollinearity, normality and homoscedasticity were not violated (strongest intercorrelation = .68, minimum tolerance value = .53, maximum Mahalanobis distance= 7.6, maximum Cook's distance = 0.50). Normal probability plots of the standardized residuals did not suggest major deviations from normality and a scatterplot of residuals did not indicate violations of assumptions.

The final model was significant and the total variance explained was 20%, F(3,54) = 4.4, p = 0.08. However, adding the interaction term at the second step did not improve the model, R squared change = .01, F change <1, p = .395. Both memory-load group (β = 0.34, p = .013) and working memory capacity (β = -0.37, p = .038) were significant in the final model, but the interaction term was not significant, β = 0.15, p = .395. This analysis suggests that individuals with higher baseline working memory capacity were better at inhibiting the learnt S-R response on devalued trials (after controlling for baseline NoGo responses on still-not-valuable trials), regardless of whether a working memory load was imposed during the outcome-revaluation test.

We repeated the analysis for the Go habit score (accuracy on still-valuable minus upvalued).

However, while the relations were in the same direction, the model failed to reach significance after either step, Fs<1.2, ps>.313 and none of the variables were significant in either model (β = -.31 to .11, ps>.103).

Exploratory Correlational Analyses

In line with our previous study (Watson et al., 2023) we explored correlations between behavioural indices from the SORT test phase after confirming normal distribution of all variables (Shapiro-Wilk's test of normality).

We first calculated two baseline-corrected habit scores for performance on still-not-valuable trials relative to devalued trials and performance on still-valuable trials relative to upvalued trials. As observed in our previous study (Watson et al., 2023) these difference scores correlated significantly with one another, r(53) = .373, p = 0.005, suggesting that individual differences in adapting behaviour following changes in outcome value contributed to performance across both upvalued and devalued trials.

We tested whether participants in the memory-load group traded off performance in the SORT test phase for accuracy on the numerical Stroop task. Working memory performance was positively correlated with accuracy on both congruent trials, r(24) = .398, p = 0.044 and incongruent trials, r(24) = .569, p = 0.002. These positive correlations suggest that participants did not trade-off accuracy on the working memory task for performance on the SORT.

Finally, participants reported stronger automaticity on the SRBAI when making Go responses to stimuli signalling valuable outcomes (mean: 67.6, SEM = 2.8) relative to NoGo for not valuable outcomes (mean: 63.9, SEM = 2.7), however this difference did not reach statistical significance, t(54) = 1.51, p = 0.068, dz = .20. We examined whether participants reporting stronger automaticity when responding to stimuli signalling valuable outcomes during training would show increased slips of action during the test phase but the correlation between the SRBAI-Go-automaticity score and the baseline-corrected habit score for performance on still-not-valuable trials relative to devalued trials was not significant, r(53) = -.15, p = .270. Likewise, self-reported automaticity for NoGo responding during training (i.e. in the presence of stimuli signalling not-valuable outcomes) was not significantly related to performance during test on still-valuable trials relative to upvalued, r(53) = -.006, p = .966.

Experiment 1 Discussion

In Experiment 1, we used the SORT with a between-group working memory manipulation to assess whether imposing a memory load at test would impair goal-directed control and force reliance on S-R strategies compared to participants not under working memory load.

As expected, participants made more errors on value-incongruent trials, but the critical effect emerged on NoGo (devalued) trials. Those under working-memory load were significantly impaired at withholding previously learned Go responses to stimuli signalling devalued outcomes (relative to those who were not under memory load), consistent with our preregistered predictions. This finding indicates that taxing executive resources can reveal habitual responding in humans as reflected by increased slips of action towards for devalued outcomes, despite being counterproductive (leading to loss of points).

By contrast, working-memory load did not interfere with performance on not-valuable trials at test. Regardless of working memory load condition, participants made more errors on value-incongruent (upvalued) trials relative to value-congruent (still-valuable) trials.

Importantly, errors on devalued and upvalued trials likely reflect different underlying mechanisms: failures to inhibit a prepotent (inhibiting a previously learned Go response) response on devalued trial versus failures to generate a novel response on upvalued trials. As such, these error types need not be equally sensitive to working memory load (Jahanshahi et al., 2015; Martínez-López et al., 2025; Rafei et al., 2026), a theme we return to in the general discussion.

Regression analyses confirmed that increased habit tendencies (specifically failures to withhold prepotent responses) were greater under load, after accounting for baseline NoGo performance. Baseline working-memory capacity also contributed independently (similarly for the memory load conditions), replicating prior work showing that lower capacity is associated with stronger habit expression (Watson et al., 2023). This convergence across studies suggests that individual differences in cognitive resources contribute to the ability to adjust behaviour following changes in outcome value.

Importantly, participants accurately identified currently valuable outcomes at the start of each block, and explicit knowledge of stimulus-outcome contingencies was high and equivalent across memory-load groups. This indicates that impaired performance on devalued trials was unlikely to reflect simple deficits in outcome value or contingency knowledge. Instead, behaviour was no longer driven by the anticipation and evaluation of behavioural outcomes under working memory load, in line with the idea that well-learned S-R associations drove responding.

Given that the primary effect was concentrated in the devalued NoGo condition, Experiment 2 was subsequently performed to replicate this finding.

Experiment 2

Experiment 2 aimed to replicate the findings of increased reliance on learned S-R associations when goal-directed control was taxed during the test phase. We expected to replicate the (pre-registered) finding from Experiment 1, that those under working-memory load would be significantly less able to inhibit previously learned Go responses to stimuli signalling devalued outcomes (relative to those who not under memory load).

To complement the primary analysis, we conducted a Bayesian t-test analysis in JASP.

Bayes factor for the group difference in accuracy on still-not-valuable minus devalued trials (i.e. our habit index) was computed to examine support for the alternative versus null hypothesis. The Bayes factor indicated moderate evidence for the directional alternative hypothesis, that reliance on habit would be stronger for those under concurrent memory load (BF+0 = 4.27).

General Discussion

In the present study we investigated whether a working memory load during an outcomerevaluation test could shift the balance between dual processes away from goal-directed control and allow habits to be more prominent. To this end, we trained and tested participants on the SORT, with half receiving a concurrent working memory load task during the test phase. Overall, participants made habitual slips-of-action on valueincongruent trials where the outcome value signalled by the stimulus had changed relative to training, requiring an adjustment of the learned response. In line with our pre-registered hypotheses, we found across two experiments that those under working memory load performed significantly worse on the devalued test trials. That is, participants in the memory-load group were impaired at inhibiting the learnt (Go) response to a stimulus that signalled a devalued outcome. This central finding suggests that a working memory load can be used to reveal habits in humans; specifically, counterproductive responding for outcomes that are no longer valuable.

Working memory load also tended to affect performance on still-not-valuable trials, and the difference between groups was significant in the combined analysis. However, both the final Bayesian analysis and the regression analysis from Experiment 1 demonstrated that even after controlling for NoGo performance on still-not-valuable trials, increased habit tendencies (specifically failures to inhibit prepotent Go responses) during the test phase were higher in those who were under working memory load. Baseline working memory capacity also contributed independently to the regression model in Experiment 1 (after controlling for working memory load group assignment). In line with our previous study, increased habit tendencies were associated negatively with baseline working memory capacity (Watson et al., 2023). Overall, these findings were as expected: in situations where executive control functions were taxed, habits for devalued outcomes were more prominent.

Importantly, explicit knowledge of S/R-O associations at the end of the test was very high and there was no difference between memory-load groups. The fact that poorer performance on value-incongruent trials was observed despite excellent explicit knowledge of the S-O relations, suggests that impaired behavioural flexibility on the value-incongruent trials cannot be attributed to diminished knowledge of the signalled outcome. Instead, we argue that the memory load compromised the ability of participants to keep current goals and R-O contingencies active in working memory, and thereby increased reliance on S-R habits. The fact that participants must learn the association between stimuli and outcomes in order to maximise reward during the training phase is an advantage of the SORT relative to the commonly used slips-of-action task (de Wit et al., 2012; Gillan et al., 2011). In the slips-of-action task, a variant of the outcome devaluation paradigm, all outcomes are valuable during the instrumental learning phase, meaning that participants are not required to learn the S-O associations to perform well (Buabang et al., 2021; De Houwer et al., 2018). However, this issue is resolved in the SORT, meaning that poorer performance on value-incongruent trials cannot be attributed to poor S-O knowledge but likely reflects competition between goaldirected and habitual control at test.

We did not observe any effects of working memory load on the valuable Go trials at testspecifically, there was no indication that load impaired performance on upvalued trials (where participants had to carry out a response in the presence of a stimulus that had been trained as NoGo). This null effect was replicated in Experiment 2. These findings could suggest that NoGo training did not result in the formation of 'inhibition habits' (Jahanshahi et al., 2015), or at least S-NoGo associations may have been weaker then S-Go. Relatedly, participants in the current study reported slightly higher automaticity for Go responses directed toward valuable stimuli during training relative to NoGo responses, although SRBAI scores following this brief period of training did not predict behavioural adaptation at test (see also : Mazar & W. Wood, 2022; Watson et al., 2023). Furthermore, a fMRI study with the SORT failed to find evidence for 'inhibition habits': while it succesfully identified neural correlates of the acquisition of instrumental Go responses, there were no comparable changes in neural activity over the course of No-Go training (van Timmeren et al., 2023).

Taken together, these findings suggest that superior performance on still-valuable trials compared to upvalued may reflect mostly the advantage of having already formed a S-Go habit for the still-valuable trials, rather than interference by a No-Go habit on the upvalued trials. Following this same line of reasoning, a working memory load may have failed to affect performance on upvalued trials (more than still-valuable) because -in contrast to the devalued trials -the new target response did not conflict with an established habit.

By directly constraining working memory at the point of action selection, we disrupted goaldirected processes and shifted behaviour toward habitual control, in contrast to a recent study in which a retention delay between stimulus presentation and response selection did not significantly disrupt online goal representations (K. M. J. Wood et al., 2023).

Specifically, following instrumental training, Wood et al. (2023) presented participants with an outcome (labelled 'goal-directed cue' that participants were instructed to respond to; O-R) and following a delay, an irrelevant 'stimulus cue' (which could trigger either a congruent or incongruent response; S-R). The authors reasoned that participants would find it difficult to maintain the goal-directed cue in working memory across the delay, thereby increasing errors on incongruent trials. However, this study reported that across two experiments neither the delay nor the inclusion of a distractor task during the delay increased reliance on S-R over O-R associations at test. Taken together, these studies clarify the parameters of working memory load required to disrupt goal-directed control during test.

Our findings add to studies attempting to increase reliance on stimulus-response associations under conditions of time pressure (Buabang, Köster, et al., 2023; Frölich et al., 2022; Hardwick et al., 2019; Hennessy et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2024; Luque et al., 2020; Rafei et al., 2026), and stress (Buabang, Boddez, et al., 2023; Fournier et al., 2017; Hartogsveld et al., 2020; Schwabe & Wolf, 2009 , 2010; Smeets et al., 2023). Our results also complement those of Neal et al. (2013) by providing converging experimental evidence under controlled laboratory conditions. Whereas Neal et al. demonstrated ecologically that cognitive depletion induced "in the wild" is associated with greater self-reported engagement in habitual behaviours, our results show that directly taxing working memory at the point of choice can shift action selection away from goal-directed control and toward habitual responding. Following similar logic, imposing a memory load during training may promote an early reliance on S-R associations. Consistent with this, stress manipulations applied during training have been shown to impair goal-directed control in outcome devaluation tasks (Schwabe & Wolf, 2009; but see: Smeets et al., 2023) and to bias behaviour toward model-free control in reinforcement learning paradigms (Gershman et al., 2014). Future studies could add working memory load during training, to examine whether this promotes habit formation rather than only expression, while measuring contingency knowledge to determine whether this may be driven by stronger S-R learning or by impaired responseoutcome learning. In addition, the dependence of habits on working memory availability could be further tested by varying the load of the working memory manipulation.

The results of the present study, where habits were more prominent when goal-directed resources were taxed by a working memory load, offer a promising new avenue for the investigation of the expression of habits. In a previous study, we attempted to demonstrate direct evidence for S-R associative links through an overtraining manipulation (Watson et al., 2023). During training on the SORT, participants learned to respond to some stimuli up to seven times more often than to other stimuli. However, we did not find evidence of increased slips of action at test for long-trained stimuli signalling devalued outcomes relative to moderately-trained stimuli (Watson et al., 2023; see also: de Wit et al., 2018; Pool et al., 2022; Luque et al., 2020). We argued that goal-directed control dominated performance at test, obscuring the stronger habits that had built up for the long-trained stimuli during extended training (as reflected also in higher automaticity for long-trained Go stimuli). The results of the present study, where working memory load promotes habitual control when goal-directed resources were taxed, support this notion. That is -in the absence of working memory load or distraction -humans appear to be capable of readily shifting back to a goaldirected strategy when needed -perhaps not surprising when situated in an artificial laboratory environment and receiving explicit instructions about task-related changes that occur. Future studies should combine the within-subjects overtraining manipulation with the memory-load manipulation. Increased slips of action for overtrained stimuli may be observed when participants are under working memory load, thus providing direct evidence for stronger habits, as predicted by the associative dual-process model.

Finally, we should note that while (S-)O-R associations are often used in the associativelearning literature to denote goal-directed behaviour that is mediated by an explicit representation of the outcome and its current value, there is evidence for S-O-R associations that can underlie inflexible habit-like behaviour that is mediated by the anticipation of the outcome, but not by the evaluation of its current value (Corbit et al., 2007; de Wit & Dickinson, 2009; Watson, Wiers, et al., 2018). Specifically, in (outcome-specific)

Pavlovian-instrumental transfer (PIT) studies, Pavlovian stimuli that signal certain outcomes through stimulus-outcome (S-O) associations bias (separately trained) instrumental responses towards those same outcomes. This PIT effect is thought to be mediated by S-O-R associations, or in other words: the Pavlovian stimuli activate responses via a shared outcome representation. Interestingly, this PIT effect can take place even when the outcome is devalued (Corbit et al., 2007; Watson, Wiers, et al., 2018), suggesting that the instrumental response is selected based on anticipation of the outcome, but not on the evaluation of its current value. As such, action selection via these indirect S-O-R associations meets the belief criterion of goal-directed action, but not the motivational criterion (de Wit & Dickinson, 2009; Heyes & Dickinson, 1990). This is therefore a potential alternative route (S-O-R instead of S-R) to cue-driven behaviour that is insensitive to the current value of outcomes -in general (Watson & de Wit, 2018) and in the context of the SORT (see also Watson et al., 2023). When applied to the current study, memory load may have impaired participants' ability to evaluate the anticipated outcome and use this to inform their choices, rendering it similarly dependent on indirect S-O-R associations where the outcome representation basically functions as another sensory cue triggering the instrumental response (see e.g., Trapold & Overmier, 1972 for a related perspective).

This alternative account is also consistent with proposals that action slips can arise from incorrect retrieval of an outdated R-O association or outdated outcome value (Aarts & Dijksterhuis, 2000; Buabang et al., 2021; Buabang, Köster, et al., 2023; Custers & Aarts, 2010; De Houwer et al., 2023; Kruglanski & Szumowska, 2020; Moors et al., 2017; but tendencies. We note, however, that even if action slips reflect the retrieval of incorrect goals or outdated outcome representations, these representations are themselves activated by stimuli and thus these accounts are less distinct than is sometimes assumed (De Houwer et al., 2023; Moors et al., 2017). Future research that can more directly distinguish between these mechanisms will be important for determining the basis of habitual behaviour following outcome revaluation under cognitive load.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that a working memory load shifted the balance of performance in an outcome-devaluation test from goal-directed control to habits. While stimuli triggered slips of action towards no-longer-valuable outcomes in both memory load groups, participants who were under working memory load were relatively impaired at flexibly adjusting behaviour on the basis of changed outcome value, rendering them more dependent on learnt habits. These findings suggest that during standard test conditions -in human research -goal-directed control dominates responding. Imposing a working memory load renders test conditions more similar to real-life conditions, in which people often fall back on their habits when their working memory is taxed: e.g., because they are distracted, tired, stressed, or when they are multitasking. The results of this study support the idea that researchers interested in studying (human) habits should tax goal-directed control to increase the ecological validity of the outcome-revaluation paradigm and thereby render it more sensitive to reveal the force of habits.

Although we assessed participant's explicit knowledge of current outcome values (and thus the correct response) throughout the test phase, this was not done under time pressure or working memory load. It is therefore not possible to rule out the possibility that during the test phase, incomplete or outdated S-O-R associations led to the observed performance under cognitive load. Moreover, although the reduced accuracy on devalued trials under working-memory load is consistent with reduced outcome sensitivity, performance approached chance rather than showing a systematic bias towards the previously learned response. Although there is debate regarding the precise representational structure underlying goal-directed control (e.g., whether it is best characterised as S-O-R or S-R-O), we use the term O-R here as shorthand to denote behaviour that is mediated by an explicit representation of the outcome and its current value, in contrast to direct S-R control.

Not providing points feedback on each trial encourages participants to monitor the outcomes they earn rather than simply relying on the points as an indication of whether they made the correct response. This differs to the procedure outlined in our previous experiment, where points feedback was provided on each trial(Watson et al., 2023).

Including all six possible block combinations of stimuli signalling two valuable and two not-valuable outcomes is different to our previous study where participants had blocks in which the same four stimuli were repeated in alternating fashion(Watson et al., 2023). However, the disadvantage of the alternating two block procedure is that participants can quickly learn which two stimuli they should be responding for, thus potentially impairing the learning of S-O associations (particularly the not-valuable S-O associations).
