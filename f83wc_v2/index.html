Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Calls for greater precision and mathematization in psychology are often motivated by the hope that they will help overcome the field's theoretical problems. While mathematics undoubtedly contributes to scientific progress, this paper questions the privileged status often granted to mathematization as an ideal of scientific development. First, it examines the tradeoffs of precision, arguing that making a theory more precise can sometimes change what is being studied, reduce its functionality, or lend unwarranted authority to its claims. It also considers examples of successful psychological approaches that do not rely on mathematization. Second, the paper introduces the Mathematization Bind. Under a realist standard, mathematical precision cannot, by itself, establish that a theory has moved closer to reality. Under a pragmatic standard, the value of mathematics depends on what it enables us to accomplish, relative to the goals of inquiry. Neither perspective justifies granting mathematics a privileged status as a measure of scientific progress. The paper also questions the tendency to regard an idealized image of physics as the model of scientific maturity. It concludes that the appropriate role and degree of mathematization in 2 psychology should be determined by the problems it enables researchers to solve, rather than by expectations about what a mature science ought to look like.

1. Introduction

What I want to do is to change your attitude. I want you to sense chaos where at first you noticed an orderly arrangement of well behaved things and processes.

-Paul Feyerabend (quoted in Hacking, 2000, p. 28) Psychology has no shortage of methodological reform movements. In the wake of the replication crisis, the field's attention has increasingly shifted beyond statistical practice to a deeper concern: perhaps psychology also has a theory problem (Eronen & Bringmann, 2021; Glo: ckner & Fiedler, 2025; McPhetres et al., 2021; Robinaugh et al., 2021), and many psychological theories are only weakly connected to the hypotheses used to test them (Fried, 2020; Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019; Scheel et al., 2021).

One proposed response to the theory crisis is to adopt a more structured methodology, often involving formal modeling to clarify and evaluate theoretical assumptions (Borsboom et al., 2021; Devezer et al., 2021; Robinaugh et al., 2021). The reasoning behind such proposals is rather straightforward. Ambiguities or inconsistencies in a theory are far less likely to be overlooked when it is expressed in an exact language than when it is left to the vagueness of verbal description. Danielle Navarro's (2021) defense of mathematical psychology offers one of the clearest and most attractive versions of this position. Drawing on Shepard's law of generalization and its Bayesian descendants, Navarro argues that mathematical formalism can both clarify theoretical commitments and support generalization across contexts. A mere verbal formulation of Shepard's law, which would state that the probability of generalizing a learned response from one stimulus to another decreases exponentially with their psychological distance, is not particularly useful in practice. This essay accepts much of that argument.

The problem begins when a fruitful methodological consideration is inadvertently elevated into an ideal. Greater precision can come to be treated as an improvement by default, with mathematical formulation representing its most developed form. One need not explicitly claim that all sciences should eventually become physics, if they can. It is enough to treat vagueness as something theories should continually overcome, or precision as something that should always be maximized. The present argument challenges this expectation, rather than precision, formalization, or conceptual refinement themselves. None of this disputes the need for better theory development and conceptual refinement, for instance along the lines discussed by Eronen and Bringmann (2021). Psychology should also make use of the full range of conceptual tools available, rather than relying on "statistical rituals" (Gigerenzer, 2018). If this is all that proponents of formalism and mathematical psychology are suggesting, then we are all in broad agreement. This essay, however, is particularly concerned with the possibility of a stronger but tacit claim: that mathematization is an essential part of any genuinely scientific practice and that a field should increasingly resemble physics as it develops. This ideal may draw some of its appeal from a broader view that regards familiar psychological categories as obstacles to progress, and thus sees their replacement with more scientific concepts as a promising way forward (Churchland, 1981; Tu: mkaya, 2022). The concern here is less about whether such an outcome is possible and more about the "wouldn't it be nice if it were" part. The essay takes issue with both this way of seeing science and the underlying desire for such an outcome among some academics in the first place.

This position, of course, is rarely stated in such blunt terms, and it would be unfair to attribute it wholesale to Navarro, Borsboom and colleagues, or many others mentioned in this essay. Several of these authors explicitly recognize limits and trade-offs. Nevertheless, the broader argument developed here considers how the admiration for mathematization may draw some of its appeal from a family of realist pictures of science in which progress consists, at least partly, in increasingly accurate representations of a mind-independent reality.

The essay begins by examining some of the tradeoffs of increasing precision, including how it can change what is being studied or come at the expense of functionality. It also considers whether mathematization is necessary or sufficient for scientific success. The argument proceeds from a broadly pragmatist perspective, although the purpose here is not to defend pragmatism against its philosophical alternatives. It then examines the realist expectations underlying mathematization, using the history of the aether to question whether mathematical success necessarily brings us closer to reality. Finally, it challenges the tendency to treat an idealized image of physics as the model of scientific success against which psychology should be judged.

2. The Tradeoffs of Precision

Increasing precision often offers genuine advantages, but it can also come with tradeoffs.

This section examines how greater precision may sometimes change what is being studied

or otherwise come at the expense of a theory's usefulness for the purposes at hand.

2.1 From concepts to specifications

There are clear advantages to mathematical expression at least in cases such as Shepard's law. In simple terms, Shepard's law describes how what we learn about one stimulus generalizes to other stimuli. The more psychologically different two stimuli are, the less we tend to generalize from one to the other. For example, if someone learns that one particular dog is dangerous, they may readily generalize that to a similar-looking dog, but not to a canary. As Navarro (2021) discusses, it is the mathematical version, which specifies precisely how generalization declines with psychological distance, that allows for more exact predictions, among other advantages. These advantages, however, come from specifying the phenomenon in a particular way, which in turn requires practical choices about what goes into the model. Guest and Martin (2021) argue that computational modeling forces researchers to examine and specify their intuitions, but also acknowledge that converting a verbal account into a model constrains the dimensions it can span. This demand for clarity is one of the advantages of formalization, although any attempt at conceptual clarification is a valuable step. For present purposes, however, if the goal is to understand or explain a phenomenon, the process of converting a verbal account into a model can sometimes introduce a series of problems.

When there are discrepancies between an initial, often coarse-grained notion and a more fine-grained operational one, a decision eventually has to be made about which of the two should give way. This becomes particularly important if the goal is to understand and explain a phenomenon assumed to exist in a mind-independent reality. Researchers may continue with an operational definition because it works well with the available methods and theoretical framework, or they may continue trying to modify their models to better capture what the initial notion was meant to describe.

Sometimes the latter effort will succeed, and sometimes it will not. The initial concept may itself turn out to have been misguided. But the opposite error is also possible: researchers may struggle to capture a roughly correct initial concept within their mathematical framework and conclude that the concept itself is defective, rather than considering the limitations of their methods. They may then abandon the original concept in favor of a narrower definition that is easier to accommodate within their existing framework.

Once this happens, making the resulting model more precise or more fruitful does not recover what was lost. It may simply produce an increasingly successful account of the narrower phenomenon that the available framework made easier to study in the first place.

Unless realists are prepared to aggressively exclude from the list of real things that which cannot be neatly expressed in mathematical form, they must acknowledge the possibility that mathematization can sometimes lead researchers away from the very phenomenon they originally set out to understand.

On the other hand, if the case for mathematization is then limited to areas where doing so already works exceptionally well, then it becomes less of a general argument for mathematization and more of an obvious point: use math where math works.

A related problem is that translating an initial concept or theory into mathematical form can produce several different formulations, potentially leading researchers in different directions. Van Rooij and Baggio (2020) for instance, despite strongly favoring formalization for making theories more precise, acknowledge that the same verbal theory can be formalized in different ways, potentially resulting in different theories and predictions. This possibility is specially important here, although perhaps for reasons that go beyond their argument.

Different formalizations may generate different predictions, allowing us, at least in principle, to distinguish between them through further empirical testing. But there is no guarantee that empirical testing will eventually settle the differences between competing accounts, at least not without some help from pragmatic considerations (Rahimian, 2026).

Theories may differ in their underlying concepts and assumptions, or even in what they take to be the phenomenon under investigation, as is often the case in consciousness research (Irvine, 2013; Rahimian, 2022).

What follows from this depends partly on the researcher's end goal. If the goal is to understand and explain, perhaps motivated by a realist account of scientific activity (i.e. something about science reflects a true structure in reality "out there"), then the question arises of which of these mathematical accounts, if any, should be taken as describing that reality, and why.

The rather optimistic expectation that accumulating sufficient empirical evidence will eventually identify the correct account may itself stem from this very realist picture. But the existence of a mind-independent reality, even if granted, does not establish that our theoretical alternatives must eventually become empirically distinguishable, much less that the most empirically successful account must be the one that correctly represents it. Even when one formulation proves more successful, the further question remains whether it adequately captures the phenomenon originally under investigation, rather than merely providing a successful account of something else. The examples discussed in the following sections should help illustrate these problems further. This raises a question closely related to the classic problem of underdetermination (Stanford, 2023; Tulodziecki, 2017), which concerns not only existing alternatives but also possible ones that we have yet to encounter. Indeed, formal modeling alone cannot resolve the underdetermination that underlies the theory crisis (Oude Maatman, 2025).

2.2 Precision and the Boundaries of the Target

It is worth noting that precision itself can refer to different things, such as numerical exactness, explicit theoretical commitments, finer descriptive resolution, and possibly other definitions that different philosophers find noteworthy (Koberinski, 2022; Strunz, 2012). The notion of precision pursued in this essay mainly concerns concepts and constructs. The realist picture considered here is one in which increasing precision is taken to help our concepts correspond more closely to something in an external, mindindependent world. What that something amounts to can differ between different forms of realism. For the scientific realist, this might concern objects, properties, and relations (Psillos, 1999). For the structural realist, it would concern the structure and relations captured by our best theories, rather than necessarily the intrinsic nature of particular objects (Worrall, 1989). For the entity realist, it would concern entities whose existence is supported by successful experimental intervention, without necessarily accepting the broader theories describing them (Hacking, 1983), and so on.

Consider two reasons why a researcher might want to mathematize a psychological phenomenon or concept. The first is pragmatic. Mathematization might help us make better predictions, or accomplish something that was previously unfeasible. The second is representational. The researcher believes that there is something out there, with a stable structure that exists independently of our concepts and descriptions, and that increasingly precise mathematical specifications might progressively reveal that structure. In this latter case, mathematization has an additional appeal: even when its practical benefits are unclear, refining a mathematical model may still seem worthwhile if it promises a better understanding of what the phenomenon really is. This is not to say that a realist cannot accept that a concept becomes increasingly precise while still misrepresenting reality. It is to point out that the additional value attributed to precision here may come from the expectation that we are also gaining knowledge about something in an external reality, or at least getting closer to doing so. Without this expectation, precision loses some of its additional importance, with its value depending instead on the task at hand and other pragmatic considerations.

Ironically, a highly precise definition of precision would not change the argument made in this essay. What matters here is the general intuition behind increasing conceptual precision: the more closely a concept can latch onto something stable in the world without catching other things along with it, the better it has supposedly become. Think of a fishing hook with a specific bait, as opposed to a fishing net. A perfect precision trap, drawing from the world exactly what is sought and nothing else.

Not only is there no reason to believe that such specificity is possible, even in principle, without presupposing a pre-figured world (Chang, 2022), but the relationship between precision and success can sometimes run in the opposite direction. Even for a realist, precision has to be precision about the right thing. If making a concept more precise causes it to exclude relevant instances of the phenomenon under investigation, the resulting gain in specificity does not by itself amount to an improvement. There are cases in which deliberately reducing precision leads to better outcomes.

A subway diagram, for instance, helps passengers navigate partly because it discards geographic detail. Restoring centimeter-level spatial accuracy would make the diagram more precise while making it no more useful for navigation, and potentially less so. In such cases, reasonable imprecision is part of what makes the activity possible in the first place.

Increasing precision can also, at some point, simply cease to tell us more about what we are trying to understand. One can locate progressively smaller parts of a novel by identifying a chapter, page, sentence, and word. Eventually, however, the relationship can break down. A letter or particle of ink belongs to the physical text without containing any corresponding fraction of the story.

Of course, locating a smaller piece of text is not the same as making our understanding of the story more precise. One might go further and argue that true precision was never about narrowing the target in the first place, but about capturing exactly what is sought, neither more nor less. I shall return to this objection shortly. For now, the point is that in practice this distinction can easily be lost when we begin with a broad, familiar concept as we often do and attempt to clarify it by breaking it down into more precisely specified components.

2.3 Premature, Precise, and Prestigious

A label can be operationally precise yet conceptually empty or mainly irrelevant for practical purposes. One problem is that mathematical presentation may make research appear stronger even when it adds nothing substantive to the argument (Eriksson, 2012).

The critical positivity ratio provides a good example. Fredrickson and Losada (2005) proposed that human flourishing occurs above a specific ratio of positive to negative emotions. Using the Lorenz equations from fluid dynamics, they calculated a critical value of approximately 2.9, suggesting that people should experience roughly three positive emotions for every negative one in order to flourish. Brown et al. (2013), however, later showed that there was no adequate justification for applying these equations to positive and negative emotions, and that the mathematical derivation of the threshold of 2.9013 was fundamentally flawed.

What matters here is that the use of mathematics produced a high degree of precision through a fundamentally flawed mathematical procedure. The added precision cannot be justified, for instance, by pointing out that 2.9013 made the claim easier to test or falsify.

The problem was not that the proposed threshold turned out to be inaccurate and that, thanks to the specific predictions it generated, we were able to find that out. Rather, the mathematical procedure from which that precise value was derived was unwarranted in the first place.

I have no way of knowing whether Fredrickson and Losada's paper would still have been accepted without their attempt to apply Lorenz equations to a psychological concept and the "cool" impression the whole attempt might have made. But contrary to Navarro's (2021) belief that psychological journals sometimes reject papers if they are overtly mathematical, I believe that the mere presence of status markers, including advanced mathematics or statistics, or even expensive, exclusive devices (e.g. MEG as opposed to a humble scalp EEG), could increase the chances of an otherwise weak paper being accepted.

This remains an interesting open question that could be explored empirically. Both possibilities can unfortunately coexist.

2.4 Precision and Loss of Functionality

So far, I have argued that greater precision can sometimes change what a concept covers and lend authority to a model whose adequacy remains speculative at best. However, excess precision can also lead to a loss of functionality.

Consider a clinical guideline stating that patients with condition X often report feeling unusually hot. A patient might say "I feel hot", complain that the room is too warm, or repeatedly remove layers of clothing. The patient could even make a joke about global warming. A Martian practitioner following a strict lexical or behavioral rule would miss various instances of this symptom because it was looking for a very direct, precise report of "I feel hot".

Arguably, the ultra-precise version of the guideline would be easier to study systematically and quantitatively, since it would be almost fully independent of the clinician's interpretation. That study could even use a great research design and sophisticated statistics and yet have little relevance to real life.

One might wonder whether the clinician could confirm the symptom, for the sake of precision, by asking, "So, you are feeling unusually hot?". But this would merely move the problem one step back rather than solving it. The practitioner must first recognize the original remark or behavior as grounds for clarification. The demand for precision can therefore interfere with detecting the very instances that a coarser approach would have picked up better.

One might object that these examples mistake precision for narrowing a concept, or zooming in. After all, wouldn't true precision describe exactly what is sought, and nothing else? On this view, precision need not mean greater specificity. The appropriate level of precision could itself be relatively coarse-grained. For instance, if the task is to identify the sensation of feeling hot as a symptom of condition X, some flexibility and indeterminacy might be precisely what the task requires. The problem, however, is that the appropriate degree of precision is now being determined by how well a definition serves its intended purpose. A more specific definition that fails to serve its purpose is considered insufficiently precise, while a less specific one that works well can be called more precise.

But if this is what precision means, then saying that precision improves inquiry basically amounts to saying that something works well because it is specified in a way that makes it work well. This move also makes the claim difficult to challenge with counterexamples. Whenever greater exactness or specificity produces worse results, one can simply respond that this was not the right amount of precision. Unless the appropriate level of precision can be identified independently of the outcome, precision provides little guidance about how an inquiry should proceed. It becomes a retrospective label for whatever worked.

More importantly, this definition does not provide support for mathematization. If precision means achieving whatever degree of specification best serves a particular purpose, nothing follows regarding the form that specification should take. A verbal description or a mathematical model could each be more useful for one task and less useful for another. Defining whichever one works as appropriately "precise" therefore cannot establish that mathematical form is generally preferable, much less that increasing mathematization constitutes scientific progress. That would require a separate argument.

2.5 Fruitful without being mathematical

A related question is whether a framework can demonstrate its usefulness without having been formulated mathematically. Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) is a useful example of what a fairly non-mathematical framework can accomplish. At its core, it combines two seemingly opposing ideas: helping patients change patterns of behavior that are making their lives worse, while also teaching them to accept experiences that cannot simply be changed. In practice, this is organized around skills in emotion regulation, distress tolerance, mindfulness, and managing interpersonal problems (Linehan, 1993; Linehan & Wilks, 2015). Although standard DBT follows a structured treatment protocol, its underlying principles allow considerable flexibility in addressing the problem at hand. Systematic reviews have found that DBT can produce meaningful clinical improvements in people with borderline personality disorder, which is notoriously hard to treat (Crotty et al., 2024; Hernandez-Bustamante et al., 2024).

These achievements give the framework empirical and practical standing in its current form. A clinician who has just successfully treated a case of borderline personality disorder may not be primarily concerned with the fact that DBT is not sufficiently mathematical, that some of its concepts may be imprecise and overlapping (e.g. fear of abandonment), or that they may be incompatible with concepts derived from the models of a mathematical psychologist working on emotions. Moreover, it matters less whether DBT itself can one day become more mathematical and more precise in its terminology. It already works fine.

Together, DBT and the positivity ratio illustrate that mathematization, at least in an extensive amount, is neither necessary nor sufficient for scientific success. DBT illustrates the first half of this claim. Despite being comparatively non-mathematical, its concepts have been developed into testable interventions with clinically important results. Its scientific standing does not depend on first translating concepts such as distress tolerance or fear of abandonment into highly precise mathematical terms. The positivity ratio illustrates the other half. Here, mathematical form did produce precise and testable predictions, but the mathematical procedure responsible for that precision was itself unwarranted. Taken together, the two cases give us little reason to treat mathematization as an achievement in and of itself. Its value has to depend on what it actually contributes in the particular case.

3. The Mathematization Bind

Even if we set aside the preceding objections to precision, a further question still remains: why should becoming more mathematical count as a direction in which science, including the psychological sciences, ought to advance? The Mathematization Bind concerns two possible answers. If progress means getting closer to a mind-independent reality, advocates of mathematization must justify why it should bring us closer to that reality rather than simply assume that it does. If progress instead means improving what science enables us to predict or accomplish, then mathematization must be evaluated by its contribution to those outcomes. Neither answer gives mathematization a privileged status in advance.

3.1 The Aether and Mathematical Success

The dangers of pedestalizing mathematics go beyond overfitting or losing touch with reality in favor of "toy worlds", as elegantly acknowledged by Navarro (2021). Even in the positivity-ratio case, one could still point to errors in the mathematics and question whether plugging in Lorenz equations to calculate a threshold for human flourishing was warranted in the first place. But what happens when the mathematics is sound, the theory makes a definite prediction, and an experiment supports it? Can we then assume that the theory has correctly identified what exists, in part thanks to its mathematical form? The history of the luminiferous aether gives us reason to doubt that.

The aether example is highly important because researchers without a background in the relevant fields are rarely taught about aether and, more importantly, what it shows about science. The story is often framed as an illustration of Einstein's brilliance in introducing a paradigm shift in physics following the Michelson-Morley experiment, further demonstrating the triumph of science and suggesting that even when we (i.e. scientists) are wrong, further empirical testing and creativity will eventually point us in the right direction. This essay does not share that optimism.

For nineteenth-century wave theorists, light required a medium in which to travel, just as other waves did, much as sound travels through air. The aether was supposed to be that medium, extending through space and transparent matter. Since aether was included in many scientists' lists of real things, physicists would naturally investigate its properties and its relation to ordinary bodies. A moving body, for example, might carry the aether along with it or pass through it while leaving it at rest. Disagreements about these possibilities could also be expressed mathematically and tested experimentally.

Even corpuscular theories of light were not incompatible with aether. Newton, for example, suggested that light particles could create vibrations in a medium as they travelled through it. These vibrations would then affect the particles' motion, making them more likely to pass through a surface or be reflected by it at regular intervals. This offered a possible explanation for the repeating patterns of color seen in thin transparent films (Newton, 1730). The existence of the aether was therefore a common starting point for otherwise competing theories (Swenson, 1972).

Consider light passing through flowing water. If the water carried the aether along with it completely, then light traveling in the same direction should gain the full speed of the water, according to the ordinary rule for adding speeds. If the aether remained entirely unaffected by the water's motion, light should gain no additional speed. Fresnel proposed a third possibility in which transparent matter carried along only part of the aether. His partial-drag hypothesis predicted how much the speed of light would change depending on the material's refractive index, a measure of how much light slows down in a given material. The hypothesis thus provided a specific, testable prediction between the two extremes of complete dragging and no dragging (Cassini & Levinas, 2019; Stachel, 2005; Swenson, 1972).

In 1851, Fizeau tested this prediction by sending light through flowing water, comparing beams traveling with and against the flow. When the beams were recombined, changes in their interference pattern revealed the difference in their travel times. The effect agreed with Fresnel's formula rather than the prediction of complete dragging. Although Fizeau himself remained cautious about accepting the proposed mechanism (Cassini & Levinas, 2019), the partial-drag account had now more going for it than a plausible explanation or equations. It had also survived an experimental test. If the drag coefficient was taken to quantify a property of the aether with such mathematical precision, what did that precise quantification map onto? Precisely nothing. What followed was equally consequential for interpreting the role of mathematics when Lorentz later obtained the same coefficient within a theory in which the aether remained stationary (Cassini & Levinas, 2019). The question can be asked once more. When highly intelligent academics disagreed about properties of the aether for decades, each presenting reasonable mathematical and empirical evidence in support of their view, what were they disagreeing about, from the perspective of understanding a mind-independent reality and its precise structure? Precisely nothing.

A structural realist might respond that we should place our commitments in the mathematical relations that survive, rather than in things like the aether itself. But how could researchers have known where to place those commitments before knowing the outcome? Unless we have some independent way of making that distinction, this response risks becoming ad hoc (Vickers, 2017). Whatever survives is treated in hindsight as what the theory was really getting right, while everything that was abandoned is retrospectively dismissed as unimportant.

The problem should be clearer now. Physicists had precise mathematical predictions, explanations of how the aether supposedly worked, and experimental evidence supporting some of their theories. Although some mathematical formulas survived into successor accounts (Cassini & Levinas, 2019), others did not, and even those that survived did not preserve the physical claims originally attached to them. Most importantly, the luminiferous aether was widely accepted as a real physical entity, yet this fundamental assumption about the world was later rejected as false. Not approximately false or somewhat inaccurate, but completely false (Vickers, 2017). According to our current accounts, there is no such thing as the luminiferous aether.

Mathematics certainly helped formulate the successor theories, but it was also an integral part of the theories they replaced. The presence of mathematics alone could not tell researchers which account was supposedly closer to reality, nor could mathematical sophistication tell them in advance which parts of those theories would survive.

3.2 Mathematization Under a Pragmatic Standard

Suppose, now, that we judge a model by what it allows us to do, without worrying about whether it somehow corresponds to a deeper, underlying reality. The case for mathematics then becomes dependent on the task at hand. A mathematical model may be indispensable for one task and contribute very little to another. What matters is how much mathematization helps us achieve our goals in each case. The ability to express concepts and relations more precisely is not, by itself, a reason to prefer a mathematical approach.

Think of a simple analogy. If all you care about is going from location A to location B, whether the vehicle is automatic or manual matters only insofar as it affects that goal, and no further. You wanted to get to location B, and you got to location B. This also changes how we evaluate the absence of a unified mathematical framework.

Several useful practices may address different parts of a problem (or a family of similar problems) without sharing a single set of concepts. Their incompatibility does not cancel out what they each accomplish. Unifying them might make them more useful, but it might also require giving up some of the features that made them useful in the first place.

Whether such unity is worth pursuing therefore depends on its costs and consequences for inquiry, an entirely pragmatic and empirical concern (Rahimian, 2026). This is the bind. Under a realist standard, mathematization cannot, by itself, establish that a theory has moved closer to reality. Under a pragmatic standard, mathematics earns its value through what it allows us to accomplish, relative to the goal at hand. In neither case does becoming more mathematical constitute scientific progress by default.

One might concede that mathematics guarantees nothing while still maintaining that it generally gives us better science. The aether example shows that mathematical and empirical success can coexist with an entirely misguided account of what exists. DBT and the positivity ratio show that the use of mathematics and fruitfulness can come apart in both directions. To what extent mathematization improves psychological science therefore remains an empirical question. Across which tasks, compared with which alternatives, and judged by which outcomes? Indeed, if mathematical psychology did not exist, we might need to invent it (Navarro, 2021). However, any stronger claim requires serious justification that goes beyond good examples from physics and mathematical psychology and also considers good examples of non-mathematical psychology and bad examples of mathematical psychology.

3.3 Physics as a Model of Scientific Maturity?

Trafimow and Fiedler (2026) discuss the familiar concept of "physics envy", examining features that might distinguish physics from psychology, including empirical laws, parsimony, and falsifiability. Debates over these differences and psychology's supposed inferiority have persisted for decades (Howell et al., 2014; Meehl, 1967). Much of this discussion asks whether psychology can ever become as successful as physics, or whether the nature of what psychology studies makes it unreasonable to expect comparable achievements.

When the debate is framed in these terms, physics is already granted the superior position, but perhaps for the wrong (or at least questionable) reasons. Psychology's inferiority is, at best, tolerated and understood (for instance, due to the complexity of what it studies), in the hope that it might one day become more like physics: more unified, more mathematical, more objective. This essay rejects that framing and questions why scientific success should be judged by mathematical form (or any other kind of form), or by resemblance to physics, rather than by the ability to solve concrete, well-defined problems.

Under a pragmatic account, any advantage physics has over psychology derives from its capacity to generate useful practices that serve our purposes, not from any privileged access to a perspective-independent reality or a God's-eye view. The idea that science should aspire to reveal such a reality rests on a broadly realist conception of science. As long as serious objections to that realist picture remain unresolved (Chang, 2022; Stanford, 2023; Vickers, 2017), this ideal cannot simply be taken for granted as the ultimate measure of scientific progress. Consequently, once theory evaluation is stripped of such assumptions, the treatment of mathematization in psychology follows suit: there can be a too little, a just enough, and a too much.

4. Conclusion

From a pragmatic perspective, the question of whether psychology needs more mathematics cannot be answered in the abstract. It depends on what psychologists are trying to accomplish and what mathematics can contribute to those efforts. Researchers should not have to justify the absence of mathematics in an otherwise successful approach, just as its introduction should not automatically count as progress merely because it makes a theory more precise or brings it closer in form to the theories of physics. The same applies to precision, unification, and other characteristics commonly associated with scientific maturity. Their value depends on what they enable us to accomplish, relative to the problems we are trying to address.

Those who instead favor a realist standard must explain why mathematization should count as scientific progress beyond its practical contributions. The history of science offers many examples of mathematically sophisticated theories built on entirely mistaken assumptions about reality. What makes the present case different, and more importantly, how can we tell? Simply assuming that the world has a discoverable mathematical structure, and that greater mathematization therefore brings us closer to it, does not answer this question.

The point is not to lower our expectations of science, including psychology, but to reconsider what those expectations should be in the first place. Pragmatism offers a way of evaluating scientific progress without first (or ever) having to settle questions about whether our theories capture the true structure of reality, thereby avoiding endless theoretical battles that need not be settled for scientific inquiry to be fruitful. If the goal is to make psychology a more successful science, mathematical form is neither an end in itself nor an ultimate measure of success. It is one of the means available to us, which may prove indispensable in some cases, unnecessary in others, and even counterproductive in still other circumstances. What matters is whether we are making progress toward solving the problems that led us to develop these theories and methods in the first place.
