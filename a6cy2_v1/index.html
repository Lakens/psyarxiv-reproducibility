What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

description In Australia, women are diagnosed with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) at approximately twice the rate of men (AIWH, 2025). While this disparity is partly explained by sex-specific trauma exposure patterns, such as differences in trauma type and developmental timing (Eder-Moreau et al., 2022), these factors alone do not fully account for the elevated risk. Furthermore, gold-standard exposure-based psychotherapies for PTSD are only partially effective, with up to 70% of treated individuals relapsing within one year (Schottenbauer et al., 2008). A central challenge in improving treatment outcomes lies in understanding the sex-specific biological and contextual factors that shape symptom expression and the mechanisms underpinning psychotherapies, such as fear extinction learning.
Fluctuations in the female neuroendocrine system have been linked to PTSD vulnerability and symptom severity. Ovarian hormones, particularly oestradiol (E2) and progesterone, have been implicated as robust modulators of PTSD symptoms and outcomes (Ravi et al., 2019). For example, women with PTSD report heightened fear-related and avoidance symptoms during the late luteal/early follicular phases (i.e., low E2 and progesterone) compared to the early- to mid-luteal phases (i.e., higher E2 and progesterone; Nillni et al. (2015)). There is also evidence that the menstrual cycle affects learning and memory. For example, women under stress with high E2 levels (during late follicular phase) demonstrate better declarative memory learning and consolidation compared to women with low E2 levels (during early follicular phase; Antov and Stockhorst (2018)). Furthermore, women using oral contraceptives, which induce tonically low E2 levels, exhibit extinction memory deficits (Graham & Milad, 2013). Additional studies also show that women have enhanced extinction recall if they train when E2 levels are elevated (Kida, 2023). Despite this evidence, the menstrual cycle remains an overlooked factor in PTSD treatment. Given that fear extinction underpins exposure therapy, these early findings suggest that tailoring psychotherapeutic intervention to menstrual cycle phase could improve outcomes for women.
However, despite promising preliminary findings, there is evidence that the impact of menstrual phase on extinction retention may differ according to PTSD status. For instance, while low E2 is linked to higher PTSD symptoms and impaired threat extinction (Glover et al., 2012), women with PTSD have also shown impaired retention of extinction learning in the mid-luteal menstrual phase (i.e., when E2 and progesterone levels are high), which is an opposite pattern to observations in trauma‐exposed women without PTSD (Pineles et al., 2016). These discrepancies highlight the need to clarify how menstrual-related hormonal factors influence symptomatology and psychological processes specifically in woman with a formal PTSD diagnosis. Moreover, the extent to which laboratory findings have been translated into clinical practice, such as timing treatment delivery to hormonal phases or testing hormonal adjuncts, remains unclear.
To date, no comprehensive review has systematically mapped the literature examining menstrual cycle phase or ovarian hormone levels in relation to i) PTSD symptoms, ii) psychological processes and iii) psychotherapeutic treatment outcomes in woman with a formal PTSD diagnosis. It also remains unclear if and how emerging biological evidence has been operationalised in treatment design or delivery. Given the early stage and multidisciplinary nature of this research, a scoping review is the most suitable approach. By synthesising what is known and what remains to be understood, this review will inform future research directions and support the development of sex-specific, hormone-informed interventions for women with PTSD.

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Women are diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) at twice the rate of men, a disparity only partly explained by trauma exposure differences. Sex-specific biological factors, particularly cyclic fluctuations in estradiol and progesterone, have been associated with PTSD symptoms/related neuropsychological processes in healthy/trauma exposed women; however, the extent and clinical translation of this literature (if any) remain unclear in PTSD populations. This review aimed to map evidence examining associations between ovarian hormones/menstrual cycle phase and PTSD symptoms and/or related neuropsychological processes (e.g., extinction learning) in women with PTSD (Aim1), and/or associations with psychotherapy outcomes in PTSD/related anxiety disorders (Aim2).

A systematic search for peer-reviewed, empirical studies involving adult females was conducted across 6 databases, in accordance with PRISMA-ScR and JBI guidelines. From 2,464 results, 12 studies were included. Five studies addressed Aim 1 and seven addressed Aim 2. Hormonal operationalization was inconsistent, including continuous assays (n=5), menstrual cycle phase comparisons (n=3), and/or median split/other groupings (n=6), with estradiol most frequently measured. For Aim 1, PTSD symptom findings were inconsistent and inconclusive (n=3), whereas lower estradiol was more consistently associated with impaired fear extinction and habituation (n=2). For Aim 2, findings in anxiety-related disorders were mixed, with no study examining ovarian hormone status in psychotherapy outcomes for women with PTSD. Overall, evidence remains sparse, heterogeneous and largely cross-sectional. Findings do not yet support firm conclusions regarding ovarian hormone-PTSD symptom/neuropsychological interactions but highlight key research gaps and future directions to better understand these relationships and inform personalized PTSD treatments for women.

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating psychiatric condition that can develop following exposure to a traumatic event. Individuals with PTSD may experience intrusive recollections of the trauma, including sensory nightmares or flashbacks, alongside hypervigilance, sleep disturbance, impaired concentration, irritability, emotional withdrawal, and avoidance behaviors (APA, 2022; Yehuda et al., 2015). Post-traumatic stress disorder can arise following a wide variety of traumatic experiences (Bryant, 2019; Kessler et al., 2014; Yehuda, 2002), and the specific type and timing of trauma exposure can influence its course (Ressler et al., 2022; Santiago et al., 2013; Smid et al., 2009).

Globally, 1 in 25 individuals have experienced PTSD in their lifetime, highlighting the substantial public health burden associated with maladaptive processing of traumatic memory worldwide (Koenen et al., 2017).

Women are diagnosed with PTSD at approximately twice the rate of men (Kroeger et al., 2026).

Although this disparity may partly reflect sex differences in trauma exposure, the type of trauma experienced and exact timing of exposure (Eder-Moreau et al., 2022), these psychosocial factors do not fully account for the apparent sex-difference. Furthermore, whilst trauma-focused psychotherapies are the recommended first-line treatments for PTSD, between one third to one half of treated individuals do not respond to treatment and remain symptomatic (Keyan et al., 2024; Schottenbauer et al., 2008; Semmlinger et al., 2024). Emerging evidence suggests that sex-specific biological factors, particularly neuroendocrine mechanisms, may contribute to both PTSD vulnerability, expression and indeed the variability in treatment response. In particular, the ovarian hormones estradiol (E2) which is the most common type of estrogen, and progesterone (P4) have been implicated as modulators of PTSD symptoms and subsequent outcomes (Ravi et al., 2019). As these hormones fluctuate across the menstrual cycle, elucidating the apparent sex bias in PTSD may depend on a clearer understanding of menstrual cycle-related hormonal dynamics and their neurobiological influence.

During a natural menstrual cycle (Figure 1A), the early follicular phase (days 1-5) is characterized by low E2 and P4 levels, followed by the late follicular phase (days 6-14) where E2 levels increase to their maximum at ovulation at ~day 14 (Schmalenberger et al., 2021). After ovulation, the luteal phase (days 14-28) is characterized by an initial decline in E2, followed by an increase in both E2 and P4 during the mid-luteal phase before both hormones decline toward the end of the cycle (Hampson, 2020). These cyclical hormonal patterns are unique to naturally cycling women and differ under conditions such as hormonal contraceptive use (Pletzer et al., 2023). As illustrated in Figure 1B, hormonal contraceptives suppress endogenous ovarian hormone production and attenuate natural hormonal fluctuations by maintaining consistently low E2 and P4 while inhibiting the cyclical rise of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH; Chidi-Ogbolu and Baar (2019). Despite growing interest in the role of neuroendocrine factors in PTSD among women, menstrual cycle phase and/or ovarian hormone status remain largely overlooked in PTSD research and its treatment. Although women appear to derive at least comparable, and in some studies greater, short-term benefit from trauma-focused psychotherapy than men (Hiscox et al., 2023; Keyan et al., 2024), the mechanisms underlying this reported sex difference in treatment response remain unclear. Some proposed explanations are multifactorial, including differences in treatment engagement, with women more likely than men to initiate individual psychotherapy within six months of diagnosis and complete an adequate course of treatment (Valenstein-Mah et al., 2019). Differences in treatment utilization may also extend to other aspects of PTSD care, including medication prescribing patterns (Hadlandsmyth et al., 2022).

However, research in civilian populations remains limited, with an overrepresentation of veteran samples and a general paucity of direct evidence examining sex differences in PTSD treatment outcomes (Hiscox et al., 2023). Despite this seeming treatment advantage, women remain disproportionately affected by PTSD, and substantial variability remains in individual symptom trajectories and treatment response (Hiscox et al., 2023; Schottenbauer et al., 2008; Steenkamp et al., 2015). Thus, identifying biological factors that contribute to this variability among women may provide opportunities to further optimize PTSD treatment approaches, particularly given the low rates of diagnostic remission observed regardless of one's sex (Stefanovics et al., 2022). The cyclic fluctuations in ovarian hormones across the menstrual cycle may represent a potential source of within-person biological variability that may influence neuropsychological process cardinal to PTSD and traumafocused interventions alike (Knowles & Tolin, 2022), raising the possibility that treatment delivered during different hormonal states may not be biologically equivalent. Thus, optimizing the timing of existing interventions according to menstrual cycle phase or ovarian hormone status, or indeed utilizing hormonal adjuncts (Hammoud et al., 2020), may therefore represent a promising precision medicine approach to improve PTSD treatment outcomes for women. However, despite increasing interest in this concept (Bolea-Alamanac et al., 2018; Glover et al., 2015; Tang & Graham, 2020), the extent to which menstrual cycle phase or ovarian hormone status influences PTSD symptom expression, neuropsychological processes, and psychotherapy outcomes remains unclear in clinical populations.

In healthy/trauma-exposed populations, when considering learning and memory processes relevant to PTSD and its treatment, hormones such as E2 have been shown to play an important role. For instance, correlational studies demonstrate that higher E2 levels are associated with enhanced declarative memory learning and consolidation under high stress (Antov & Stockhorst, 2018), threat/fear conditioning and extinction learning (Hammoud et al., 2020), as well as extinction recall (Kida, 2023).

Consistent with this, women using oral contraceptives, which induce tonically low endogenous E2 levels, exhibit deficits in extinction memory (Graham & Milad, 2013). Estradiol also facilitates glutamatergic NMDA receptor activity (Hammoud et al., 2020) and exogenous administration modulates activation within prefrontal and fear-regulation networks during extinction learning and memory (Wen et al., 2021), providing a potential mechanistic account for the apparent role of E2 in learning and memory processes associated with PTSD.

Animal models provide further evidence that E2 is a biological modulator of extinction-related learning processes. For example, in female rodents, endogenous fluctuations in ovarian hormones across the estrous cycle influence conditioned fear extinction, with elevated hormone states associated with enhanced extinction learning and recall (Milad et al., 2009). In both women and female rats, E2 has also been shown to modulate activity within the medial prefrontal cortex and amygdala during fear extinction, suggesting that ovarian hormones influence recruitment of neural circuits involved in fear inhibition and memory updating (Zeidan et al., 2011). Conversely, inhibition of aromatase (the enzyme response for metabolizing testosterone into E2) in male rats impairs fear extinction, demonstrating that endogenous E2 signaling contributes directly to extinction processes (Graham & Milad, 2014). At a cellular level, endogenous E2 in mice regulates synaptic plasticity within the infralimbic medial prefrontal cortex through estrogen receptor-dependent modulation of glutamatergic transmission and NMDA receptor function (Galvin & Ninan, 2014), a potential mechanism through which E2 facilitates extinction (Graham & Scott, 2018). Extending the original work of Milad et al. (2009) and (Chang et al., 2009), experimental work in female rats has also demonstrated that acute E2 administration prior to extinction training enhanced subsequent extinction recall, providing further evidence that increased E2 availability can facilitate consolidation of extinction memories (Hartsgrove et al., 2026). Another recent cross-species study demonstrated that the P4:E2 ratio predicted fear extinction performance in both mice and humans, highlighting the potential importance of considering ovarian hormone profiles, but also relative proportions or balance of hormones, when examining extinction-related learning processes (Nabás et al., 2026). For an recent systematic overview of every study examining sex/sex hormones and fear extinction/exposure therapy, see Pestana et al. (2026).

Importantly though, despite this preclinical evidence, findings from clinical human populations appear more complex. While lower E2 and/or P4 levels have been associated with poorer emotional memory consolidation and extinction recall in healthy women (Hsu et al., 2021), women with PTSD have demonstrated impaired retention of extinction learning during the mid-luteal menstrual phase, when E2 and P4 levels are typically elevated (Pineles et al., 2016). These discrepancies suggest that ovarian hormone influences on related neuropsychological processes may differ between healthy and PTSD populations, highlighting the importance of examining associations stratified by PTSD status. This evidence also has important potential implications for psychotherapy, particularly exposure-based interventions that rely on extinction learning processes (Knowles & Tolin, 2022; Sep et al., 2023). That said, it remains unclear if and how emerging biological evidence has been operationalized in treatment design or delivery.

To date, no review has systematically mapped the literature examining menstrual cycle phase or ovarian hormone status in relation to PTSD symptoms, related neuropsychological processes, and psychotherapy outcomes in women with a PTSD diagnosis. Given the early-stage and multidisciplinary nature of this research area, a scoping review was deemed the most appropriate approach.

Objectives

The primary objective of this scoping review was to map and synthesize research in women with a formal PTSD diagnosis examining the relationship between menstrual cycle phase and/or ovarian hormone status and a) PTSD symptom expression, severity, or fluctuation (Aim 1a), or b) neuropsychological processes relevant to PTSD, such as extinction learning, extinction retention, and memory reconsolidation (Aim 1b). A secondary objective was to examine whether menstrual cycle phase or ovarian hormonal status influences psychotherapy outcomes in PTSD or related anxiety disorders, such as panic disorder and phobia (Aim 2). In doing so, we detail the extent of this literature, and how menstrual cycle phase, hormonal status, PTSD symptoms, and therapy/treatment outcomes have been measured across included studies, whilst identifying key gaps within the current literature.

This synthesis provides a systematic overview of the scope and nature of research in this emerging field, and highlights directions for future hormone-informed PTSD research and the downstream implications for treatment.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis (Aromataris et al., 2024), and the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (Peters et al., 2020; Tricco et al., 2018). The final review protocol was registered prospectively on Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/US7XT).

Search strategy

To identify potentially relevant studies, a systematic search of the following six electronic databases was performed in August 2025: PUBMED, Scopus, Web of Science, Medline (Ovid), PsychINFO, and ProQuest (One Academic). The final search strategy was initially developed using a structured two-step approach. First, a limited search of PubMed and PsycINFO was undertaken to identify relevant articles and refine the search terms. Titles, abstracts, and index terms of relevant studies were examined to identify keywords and text words commonly used in the literature. The second step involved using the results of the initial search to develop two complementary search strategies designed to address the review's dual focus. To validate the strategy, a set of key known relevant articles were identified, and the search was tested to ensure these articles were retrieved. While the core structure of the search remained consistent, minor adaptations were made for each database to account for specific syntax or indexing differences (e.g., truncation discrepancies, term limits). An experienced academic librarian was also consulted on the search strategy before implementation. An example of the full search strategy for PubMed is provided in Appendix A, and the PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Figure 2. In addition to database searching, we also conducted backward citation tracking and checked the reference list of related sources (e.g., reviews, protocols, or commentaries) discovered during the search process.

No additional studies were identified via these supplementary means. Nine corresponding authors were contacted seeking additional data. One responded supplying the requested data, two revealed that the requested data was published elsewhere (both publications were already captured in the search), three replied that the requested analyses were not available, and three did not respond to email enquiry.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

Upon completion of the literature search, all references were exported from respective databases into EndNote and then imported into Covidence for de-duplication as well as management of the screening processes. Prior to formal screening, a pilot exercise was conducted on the first 30 entries by two independent reviewers (C.T and K.M) to ensure consistent application of the inclusion criteria and resolve any discrepancies regarding eligibility criteria before continuing. Once high consistency was achieved across reviewers (> 90% agreement), we proceeded to screen all titles and abstracts independently in accordance with the outlined eligibility criteria. All potentially relevant articles were then retrieved in full and assessed for eligibility based on the pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (C.T and K.M). Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. Disagreements at any stage were resolved by consensus, or if needed, adjudicated by a third independent reviewer (A.B).

Peer-reviewed empirical studies were included if they were, published in English, included females with either a i. PTSD diagnosis and assessment of menstrual cycle phase and/or ovarian hormones in relation to PTSD symptomatology (Aim 1a) or related neuropsychological processes such as extinction learning, extinction retention, or memory reconsolidation (Aim 1b), or ii. diagnosis of PTSD or a related anxiety disorder with assessment of psychotherapy outcomes in relation to menstrual cycle phase and/or ovarian hormones (Aim 2). Studies were included if they provided an explicit measurement of ovarian hormones or menstrual cycle phase (via biological assay or self-report), reported on females (naturally cycling, using hormonal contraceptives, or in or beyond the menopause transition, provided hormonal status or cycle phase was measured). Studies were excluded if they involved females aged < 18 years, did not include a PTSD sample (Aim 1) or a PTSD/anxiety disorder sample (Aim 2), male-only samples, or only animal subjects. Due to the scoping nature of this review, no restriction on publication date, geographic location, cultural setting, upper participant age or study environment were applied. Review papers, book chapters, study protocols, theses, editorials or commentaries, and conference abstracts were not included, but were used in the search strategy to identify potentially relevant articles.

Data charting

Two data charting forms were developed in Microsoft Excel to determine which variables to extract.

The first form captured source and sample characteristics, including citation details, study context, and participant demographics (see Table 1), while the second form captured study methodology and key findings/concepts relevant to the review questions (see Table 2). Data from included studies were manually extracted by two independent reviewers (K.M and E.L) using the charting forms, which were subsequently verified and consolidated by a third reviewer (C.T). Between the time of registering the scoping review protocol and conducting the data charting, chart 2 was updated to include additional information pertaining to the type of trauma experienced by participants, and also more specific details regarding the exact day of the menstrual cycle/hormone concentration at the time of assessment/treatment (if reported), to further contextualize the included sources.

Menstrual cycle/ovarian hormones and PTSD-related symptoms (Aim 1a)

Hormones as continuous variables Only one study examined associations between ovarian hormone concentrations and PTSD symptom severity in women with PTSD. Adams et al. (2025) conducted a cross-sectional study including men and women with and without PTSD and reported non-significant associations between E2, P4, antimullerian hormone (AMH) and testosterone concentrations and PTSD symptom severity when analyses were restricted to women with a PTSD diagnosis.

Menstrual phase comparisons

Adopting a repeated-measures design, Nillni et al. (2015) examined menstrual cycle phase-by-PTSD status interactions in trauma-exposed women with subthreshold or full PTSD, reporting higher phobic anxiety symptoms (fear-and avoidance-related) during the early follicular phase compared with the mid luteal phase (t = 2.47, p = 0.022), corresponding to conditions characterized by relatively lower E2 and P4 levels.

Dichotomized "high" vs "low" hormone comparisons While Glover et al. (2012) primarily examined fear extinction processes in trauma-exposed women with and without PTSD using dichotomized E2 groupings, they also reported associations with PTSD symptom severity. Across the full sample, women with lower E2 concentrations reported greater PTSD symptom severity relative to women with higher E2 concentrations. However, no significant PTSD group × E2 interactions were observed for PTSD symptom scores.

Collectively, the role of ovarian hormone concentrations and menstrual cycle phase in PTSD symptom severity remains unclear, with inconsistent findings reported across only three studies, each employing differing methodological and hormonal operationalization approaches.

Menstrual cycle/ovarian hormones and PTSD-related neuropsychological processes (Aim 1b)

Despite searching for studies examining a range of neuropsychological processes relevant to PTSD, including extinction learning, extinction retention and memory reconsolidation (see Appendix A for the full search terms), the included studies exclusively investigated fear conditioning and extinction paradigms.

Dichotomized "high" vs "low" hormone comparisons Two cross-sectional studies examined PTSD-related fear conditioning and extinction processes using dichotomized high versus low E2 group comparisons, determined via median split (Glover et al., 2012; Sartin-Tarm et al., 2020). Glover et al. (2012) investigated fear extinction in trauma exposed women with and without PTSD and reported a significant interaction between PTSD status and E2 grouping on fear extinction indexed via fear-potentiated startle (F = 4.55, p < 0.05). Women with PTSD in the low E2 group demonstrated greater startle responding during extinction relative to trauma-exposed controls (F = 5.04, p < 0.05), whereas no such difference emerged in the high E2 group. More recently, Sartin-Tarm et al. (2020) investigated fear habituation during conditioning and extinction in women with PTSD related to interpersonal violence and reported a significant E2 × PTSD severity interaction on skin conductance response habituation (t = 3.18, p = 0.002). Specifically, lower E2 concentrations were associated with impaired fear habituation among women with greater PTSD symptom severity (t = 3.99, p < 0.001), whereas this association was not observed in the high E2 group. Collectively, findings from both studies suggest that lower E2 concentrations may associate with impaired fear habituation and extinction processes in women with PTSD.

Menstrual phase comparisons

Using a within-person repeated-measures design, Pineles et al. (2016) instead examined menstrual cycle phase differences in fear extinction retention among trauma-exposed women with and without PTSD.

Women completed fear conditioning and extinction procedures during both the early follicular phase and mid-luteal phase, corresponding to relatively lower versus higher E2 and P4 concentrations, respectively. A significant menstrual phase × PTSD interaction was reported for extinction retention (B = 0.42, p = 0.01). Specifically, women with PTSD demonstrated poorer extinction retention during the mid luteal phase, whereas trauma-exposed controls exhibited poorer retention during the early follicular phase. While on the surface these findings might suggest that higher ovarian hormone states may differentially influence fear extinction retention in women with PTSD relative to trauma-exposed controls, when assessing hormones as continuous variables, they reported that high P4 was associated with poorer extinction outcomes in the PTSD group, especially when paired with low E2.

Collectively, studies examining PTSD-related neuropsychological processes focused solely on fear conditioning, habituation, and extinction retention paradigms. Across both studies that split E2 into "high" versus "low" concentration groups, lower E2 was consistently shown to associate with impaired fear habituation and extinction processes in women with PTSD, however, when looking across menstrual phases, findings suggest more complex and potentially opposing effects for extinction retention. Besides Pineles et al's supplementary analysis, no other study within Aim 1b operationalized ovarian hormones as continuous variables, nor did they assess ovarian hormones other than E2.

Menstrual cycle/ovarian hormones and psychotherapy response (Aim 2)

Hormones as continuous variables Five studies examined outcomes using continuous hormonal measures, including testosterone (Hutschemaekers et al., 2020), E2 (Graham et al., 2018; Levy et al., 2021; Levy et al., 2024), as well as E2 and P4 (Li & Graham, 2020). Hutschemaekers et al. (2020) investigated endogenous testosterone concentrations as predictors of exposure therapy response in individuals with social anxiety disorder and reported a significant time × baseline testosterone interaction on social anxiety symptoms in women (F = 8.39, p = 0.006). Greater reductions in Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale scores across treatment were associated with lower baseline testosterone concentrations, although testosterone reactivity was not associated with treatment response, and no significant associations were observed for subjective fear ratings during or following exposure. Levy et al. (2024) examined E2 concentrations during singlesession exposure therapy across women with mixed anxiety disorders and reported non-significant associations with post-exposure distress (β = -0.06, p = 0.733) and recall distress (β = -0.16, p = 0.392). Graham et al. (2018) similarly examined E2 concentrations in relation to single-session exposure therapy outcomes, although this was instead investigated in women with spider phobia. Among naturally cycling women, higher E2 concentrations were associated with lower self-reported spider phobia symptoms immediately following treatment (r = -0.27, p = 0.04), although this association was not maintained at follow-up and was not observed for behavioral avoidance outcomes. Li and Graham (2020) then further examined endogenous E2 concentrations in women with spider phobia, this time using cognitive restructuring and considering the role of P4 concentrations. A significant time × P4 interaction was observed for behavioral avoidance outcomes on the BAT (F = 5.27, p = 0.024), with higher P4 concentrations associated with greater reductions in behavioral avoidance following treatment. Similarly, a significant group × P4 interaction emerged for post-treatment BAT performance (β = 0.16, p = 0.036). No significant associations were reported for E2 concentrations or self-reported spider phobia symptoms. Levy et al. (2021) examined women with various anxiety disorders undertaking cognitive restructuring and reported non-significant associations between E2 concentrations and cognitive restructuring quality (r = .01, p = .98), post-restructuring distress, heart rate, and electrodermal activity (all Bs < 0.021, all ps > .05). Collectively, findings from studies employing continuous hormonal measures suggest mixed associations between ovarian hormones and psychotherapy outcomes.

Dichotomized "high" vs "low" hormone comparisons Five studies examined psychotherapy outcomes using dichotomized hormone groupings or hormone manipulation approaches (Graham et al., 2018; Hutschemaekers et al., 2021; Levy et al., 2023; Levy et al., 2021; Levy et al., 2024). In addition to assessing hormones as continuous variables, Graham et al. (2018) also compared naturally cycling women with relatively high versus low E2 concentrations and women using hormonal contraceptives (low hormones) during exposure therapy for spider phobia.

No significant between-group differences were observed between naturally cycling high and low E2 groups across treatment outcomes. However, women using hormonal contraceptives demonstrated poorer treatment efficiency, lower behavioral approach scores following treatment and at follow-up, and completed fewer exposure tasks relative to naturally cycling women. Similarly, Levy et al. (2021)also compared high versus low E2 groups, classified using a median split. Consistent with their nonsignificant continuous hormone findings, no between-group difference was observed in cognitive restructuring scores (d = 0.50, p = .15), with non-significant group × time interaction effects reported for subjective distress, heart rate, skin conductance level, or skin conductance response (all ps > .05). Hutschemaekers et al. (2021) investigated exogenous testosterone augmentation during exposure therapy for social anxiety disorder and found that women receiving testosterone supplementation demonstrated greater reductions in subjective fear during exposure relative to placebo (F = 4.61, p = 0.037), particularly among those with higher endogenous baseline testosterone concentrations. No significant effects were observed for broader social anxiety symptom outcomes. Levy et al. (2023) examined high versus low E2 status during interoceptive exposure therapy for panic disorder and reported predominantly null findings across panic disorder severity, distress, and anxiety sensitivity outcomes. However, women in the low E2 group demonstrated greater reductions in skin conductance level from pre-to post-treatment relative to the high E2 group (F = 5.04, p = 0.034). Levy et al. (2024) then later investigated E2 concentrations during single-session exposure therapy across women with mixed anxiety disorders and reported largely non-significant effects of E2 on distress reduction and recall, consistent with the null findings also observed when E2 was operationalized as a continuous variable in this sample. However, exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that women with obsessivecompulsive disorder (OCD) and low E2 demonstrated greater reductions in distress immediately following exposure, whereas the high E2 group exhibited increased distress over the same interval. In contrast, low E2 concentrations in women with OCD were associated with increased distress at recall, whereas those with high E2 concentrations were associated with reduced recall distress.

Collectively, studies employing dichotomized hormone comparisons reported mixed or null findings across psychotherapy outcomes. While some evidence suggested that higher P4 and E2 concentrations or exogenous testosterone administration may benefit specific fear or symptom outcomes, effects were inconsistent across disorders, outcome measures, and assessment timepoints. In contrast to Aim 1 studies, no studies examining psychotherapy response employed menstrual cycle phase comparison designs.

Discussion

This scoping review mapped the available evidence examining associations between menstrual cycle phase and/or ovarian hormone status and PTSD symptom expression, related neuropsychological processes, and psychotherapy outcomes in women with PTSD. Despite growing interest in the role of the menstrual cycle/ovarian hormones in the context of PTSD (Green & Graham, 2022; Nillni et al., 2021; Peltier et al., 2024), only 12 studies met our inclusion criteria, highlighting the limited evidence available in clinically diagnosed PTSD populations. Indeed, much of the broader evidence base has focused on healthy/trauma-exposed populations, providing important mechanistic insights but nonetheless limiting conclusions regarding clinical presentations. To summarize, our findings did not support a consistent relationship between ovarian hormones and PTSD symptom severity outcomes in women with PTSD but did suggest that lower E2 concentrations were more consistently associated with impaired fear habituation and extinction-related processes. Notwithstanding, the clinical significance of these effects remain unclear, especially given that no study to date has empirically tested whether ovarian hormone status or menstrual cycle phase influences psychotherapy outcomes in women with PTSD, particularly with intensive PTSD treatment. Overall, the findings of this review were derived from a small number of heterogeneous studies employing diverse approaches to outcome measurement/selection, including hormonal assessment and menstrual cycle classification (or lack thereof). Collectively, the available evidence suggests that ovarian hormones may influence neuropsychological processes that are cardinal to PTSD (e.g., fear extinction), but whether this translates to treatment effectiveness remains elusive.

Ovarian hormones and PTSD symptom expression: limited evidence

Evidence examining associations between ovarian hormones and PTSD symptom-level outcomes was limited (Adams et al., 2025; Glover et al., 2012; Nillni et al., 2015) with each study employing different approaches to hormonal assessment, including continuous hormone concentrations, menstrual cycle phase comparisons, and dichotomized hormone groupings. Across these studies, findings did not demonstrate a consistent relationship between ovarian hormone status and overall PTSD symptom severity, suggesting that ovarian hormones may not directly influence symptom expression, though firm conclusions cannot be ascertained from such a small sample size. PTSD is a complex and heterogeneous disorder involving multiple symptom domains, including intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal (APA, 2022). It is therefore possible that ovarian hormones influence specific symptom dimensions or underlying biological processes rather than overall symptom severity scores, as were employed in the included studies. Furthermore, all studies examining symptom associations were predominantly cross-sectional, which does little to elucidate temporal change. It remains unclear whether ovarian hormone variation contributes to PTSD symptom fluctuation, reflects downstream consequences of PTSD-related stress physiology, or interacts with other biological and environmental factors. Longitudinal studies incorporating repeated hormone measurement and symptom assessment across the menstrual cycle are required to clarify these relationships.

Estradiol and fear-related learning processes: a potential mechanism underlying PTSD vulnerability and treatment response

Compared with symptom-level outcomes, more consistent patterns emerged regarding ovarian hormones and neuropsychological processes cardinal to PTSD, particularly fear conditioning, habituation, and extinction. Across studies employing dichotomized E2 concentrations, lower E2 levels were associated with poorer fear habituation and extinction-related processes among women with PTSD (Glover et al., 2012; Sartin-Tarm et al., 2020). These findings align with broader experimental literature demonstrating that E2 influences neural systems involved in fear regulation, including medial prefrontal cortex-amygdala circuitry, synaptic plasticity, and extinction memory consolidation (Hammoud et al., 2020; Milad et al., 2009). However, the current review does not support a straightforward interpretation that higher E2 menstrual states universally enhance fear extinction. In particular, Pineles et al. (2016) reported poorer extinction retention among women with PTSD during the mid-luteal phase, when both E2 and P4 are typically elevated. In contrast, trauma-exposed controls demonstrated poorer extinction retention during the early follicular phase, characterised by lower ovarian hormone concentrations.

Although these phase-based findings suggest that associations between menstrual cycle phase and extinction retention may differ according to PTSD status, they do not necessarily indicate that higher E2 is detrimental to extinction retention in women with PTSD. Indeed, supplementary analyses examining hormone concentrations as continuous variables revealed that higher P4 was associated with poorer extinction outcomes in the PTSD group, particularly when E2 was also low. This finding highlights the importance of considering the broader hormonal context rather than interpreting menstrual cycle phase as a proxy for E2 alone. It may also help reconcile the phase-based findings with evidence that progesterone can oppose oestradiol's facilitative effects on fear extinction in rats (Graham & Daher, 2016).

Progesterone is metabolized to the neuroactive steroids allopregnanolone (ALLO) and pregnanolone, which are potent positive allosteric modulators of GABA receptors, enhancing GABA-mediated inhibitory signaling and producing anxiolytic and antidepressant effects (Crowley & Girdler, 2014).

Prior work has shown that women with PTSD have lower ALLO levels, suggesting impaired metabolism of P4 (Pineles et al., 2018; Rasmusson et al., 2006). This altered pathway could potentially limit the expected anxiolytic and inhibitory effects of P4, particularly during the mid-luteal phase when P4 concentrations increase. Thus, reduced ALLO availability during the mid luteal phase in PTSD is thereby problematic and may also obscure the hypothesized beneficial effects of higher E2 and P4 concentrations on extinction retention in PTSD, highlighting the importance of considering hormone effects within the broader hormonal and neurobiological milieu of each menstrual cycle phase.

Collectively, these findings suggest that ovarian hormones may represent a biological moderator of PTSD-related learning processes, particularly extinction mechanisms relevant to psychotherapy. However, further work is required to determine whether laboratory-based hormone-related effects actually translate into clinically meaningful differences in treatment response.

Ovarian hormones and psychotherapy outcomes: an important gap in PTSD clinical evidence

A major gap identified by this review was the absence of studies examining ovarian hormone status or menstrual cycle phase in relation to psychotherapy outcomes among women with PTSD. This represents an important limitation, since many evidence-based PTSD treatments rely on learning and memory processes, including extinction learning, inhibitory learning, and memory reconsolidation (Knowles & Tolin, 2022; Sep et al., 2023), which were consistently associated with ovarian hormones here, and elsewhere (Hammoud et al., 2020; Kida, 2023). Although studies conducted in related anxiety disorders provide preliminary insights, findings remain mixed and difficult to interpret. Some studies suggested that higher E2 or P4 concentrations may be associated with improved behavioral or symptom outcomes following exposure-based interventions (Graham et al., 2018; Li & Graham, 2020). Conversely, other studies reported predominantly null or opposing findings across diagnostic groups and outcome measures (Levy et al., 2023; Levy et al., 2021; Levy et al., 2024). Variation in anxiety disorder populations, intervention paradigms, hormone assessment approaches, and follow-up durations further limits comparison between studies, nor are they mutually exclusive. Therefore, although mechanistic studies provide a rationale for considering ovarian hormones as potential moderators of treatment response, evidence is currently insufficient to support clinical recommendations regarding potential menstrual cycle timing of psychotherapy or hormone-informed PTSD treatment delivery.

Considerations for future hormone-informed PTSD research

A key priority identified by this review is greater methodological consistency in the assessment of ovarian hormones and menstrual cycle phase. The included studies varied substantially in their use of continuous hormone assays, median-split classifications, menstrual phase comparisons, contraceptivebased comparisons, and pharmacological manipulation (Figure 3), limiting cross-study comparison and making it difficult to determine whether apparently discrepant findings reflect genuine biological differences or methodological variation.

Future research should therefore prioritize rigorous menstrual cycle characterization using prospective cycle mapping alongside urinary LH testing and, where feasible, serum and/or salivary hormone measurement, rather than relying on retrospective self-reported cycle phase. Repeated hormone assessment across the menstrual cycle would allow endocrine state to be characterized continuously and in relation to the individual's cycle trajectory, rather than reducing hormone concentrations to arbitrary high-versus low-hormone categories using approaches such as median splits. Sampling during biologically defined phases (e.g., early follicular versus mid luteal) could additionally facilitate within-person comparisons of relatively low versus high E2 states while accounting for the broader hormonal changes occurring across the cycle. This approach may also provide a useful framework for investigating whether the timing of psychotherapy relative to E2 state influences treatment response. Given the potential for PTSD-related alterations in P4-to-ALLO metabolism to influence the interpretation of mid-luteal effects when P4 and E2 increase, an initial focus on the follicular phase may provide a more controlled approach to isolating the potential contribution of E2. For example, comparing delivery of intensive psychotherapy during early ("low E2") and late ("high E2") follicular phases could therefore help establish whether E2 state influences psychotherapy response before extending this work to other phases of the menstrual cycle.

In any case, a multi-hormone approach will ultimately be important, as E2 cannot be considered independently of the broader hormonal and neurobiological changes occurring across the menstrual cycle.

In order to effectively address the apparent gap in clinical translation, future randomized clinical trials should integrate rigorous menstrual cycle and endocrine assessment with validated PTSD outcomes to determine whether the menstrual cycle contributes to variability in treatment response. Establishing whether experimental hormone-related effects are reproducible and clinically meaningful will be essential before hormone-informed treatment timing can be considered a viable precision medicine approach for women with PTSD.

Limitations of the current scoping review

This is the first review to map the published empirical evidence to date examining the associations between menstrual cycle phase and/or ovarian hormone status and PTSD symptom expression, PTSDrelated neuropsychological processes, and psychotherapy outcomes in women with a formal PTSD diagnosis. While the review benefits from a systematic PRISMA-aligned approach, we acknowledge several limitations. First, although a comprehensive search strategy across six databases was undertaken, the overall evidence base identified was small, with only 12 studies meeting inclusion criteria. The limited number of eligible studies restricted the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the relationship between ovarian hormones, menstrual cycle phase, and PTSD-related outcomes. Second, substantial heterogeneity existed across included studies in participant characteristics, trauma exposure, diagnostic approaches, hormonal assessment methods, menstrual cycle classification strategies, and outcome measures, further limited direct comparison between studies. Additionally, many studies relied on single hormone assessments or dichotomized hormone classifications, which may not adequately capture dynamic ovarian hormone fluctuations across the menstrual cycle. Findings should therefore be interpreted as mapping the current evidence base rather than providing definitive estimates of hormone-related effects. Third, consistent with the purpose of a scoping review, this review aimed to map the extent and characteristics of available evidence rather than formally evaluate methodological quality or risk of bias. Although methodological limitations of included studies were discussed, formal critical appraisal was not undertaken. As this field develops and more rigorous longitudinal and intervention studies become available, systematic reviews and metaanalytic approaches will be required to determine the strength and consistency of observed associations.

Conclusions

This scoping review revealed no consistent relationship between ovarian hormone status and overall PTSD symptom severity but did show that lower E2 was more consistently associated with impaired fear habituation and extinction-related processes, suggesting a potential hormonal influence on neuropsychological mechanisms cardinal to PTSD. No studies have examined whether menstrual cycle phase or ovarian hormone status influences psychotherapy outcomes in women with PTSD, leaving the clinical significance of these neuropsychological findings unresolved. Future research should prioritize rigorous, within-person menstrual cycle mapping with repeated multi-hormone assessment to better characterize hormonal trajectories and their relationship with PTSD-related processes.
