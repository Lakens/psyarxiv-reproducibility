Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Evidence-based psychotherapies benefit many patients, but individual outcomes vary and guidance for personalizing treatment remains limited. The verbal theories underlying these treatments describe processes thought to produce improvement but often leave their strength, direction, functional form, and timescales unspecified. This limits theory-informed predictions about how a particular patient would respond to alternative treatments. Researchers have begun formalizing theories of psychopathology in mathematical or computational form to address these limitations. Formal theories make theoretical assumptions explicit, allowing precise and testable predictions to be deduced analytically or through computational models. When these models represent treatment effects and relevant individual differences, researchers can use patient measurements to inform model states and parameters, simulate alternative treatment configurations, and compare predicted outcomes. Empirical evaluation must distinguish the accuracy of outcome predictions, support for the proposed mechanisms, and whether modelguided selection or adaptation improves outcomes relative to other treatment selection strategies.

Translating this potential into practice requires aligning the theory with patient assessment, treatment delivery, and evaluation. We propose a roadmap for this process and describe conditions supporting the generation, delivery, and testing of formal theory-driven treatment recommendations. A worked example using a formal theory of panic disorder and a modular digital therapy illustrates the roadmap's early phases, including provisional model extensions that generate different treatment predictions for two simulated patients. We conclude by discussing challenges in theory development, patient matching, measurement, and empirical evaluation that must be addressed before these predictions can guide clinical care.

Introduction

Evidence-based treatments benefit many patients, but outcomes vary considerably and many patients do not achieve clinically meaningful improvement (Cuijpers et al., 2021; Loerinc et al., 2015). Therefore, clinicians are faced with a practical question: which treatment components should a specific patient receive, in what order, and at what dose? Although clinicians already adapt treatment to individual needs, we currently have limited evidence-based guidance to inform these decisions (Cohen et al., 2021).

Data-driven approaches to precision mental healthcare address this question by using patient characteristics to predict outcomes under different treatments. For example, the Personalized Advantage Index approach (DeRubeis et al., 2014) uses predictive models to compare a patient's expected outcomes under alternative treatments. However, such approaches depend on data that support the treatment comparisons of interest, and detecting gains from individualized treatment selection can require samples larger than those available in many clinical trials (Luedtke et al., 2019). A further challenge arises when personalization concerns combinations, sequences, or doses of treatment components that were not varied in existing studies. These are critical decisions for clinical practice, but their many possible configurations make direct empirical comparisons extremely challenging.

Formal theories offer a complementary way to generate treatment predictions. While verbal theories describe processes thought to produce symptom improvement, they often leave their strength, functional form, and timescale unspecified, limiting the predictions that can be deduced. Formal theories make these details explicit by expressing theorized mechanisms in mathematical or computational form (Robinaugh et al., 2024; Wang, Robinaugh et al., 2026).

Section 2. Key Elements Supporting Formal Theory-Driven Personalization

This section describes several key elements for supporting formal theory-driven personalized treatments. We use the term personalization to describe treatment decisions based on measurable patient-specific characteristics or processes, including relevant circumstances or contextual factors (Cohen et al., 2021). Such decisions may concern which components a patient receives, their sequence, or their dose-for example, whether cognitive restructuring precedes exposure or how much exposure practice is assigned. Formal theory-driven personalization offers a way to inform these decisions using predictions derived from explicitly represented mechanisms of psychopathology and treatment response.

Formal theory-driven personalization requires three things: (1) generating personalized treatment decisions from a formal model via simulation, (2) delivering the personalized treatment configurations in the real world, and (3) evaluating the resulting predictions, explanations, and clinical decisions empirically. Achieving these aims will involve harmonizing the formal theory with a candidate treatment and assessment procedures. Represent Individual Differences in Psychopathology Systems and Treatment Processes

To compare treatment options, a formal theory should represent both the processes underlying psychopathology and how treatment components are hypothesized to change them (Figure 1, Panel A). Because recovery from psychopathology may not simply reverse the pathway into disorder (Wang, Blanken, et al., 2026), a formal theory that explains onset or maintenance dynamics does not automatically explain treatment response, even when treatment components are included. The model should also accommodate individual differences in the hypothesized processes underlying psychopathology and treatment response, which can be represented through different initial states or parameter values within shared equations, rather than requiring a different model structure for every patient. Supplementary Figure S2 distinguishes variation in states and parameters from variation in model structure. For example, patients may differ in their willingness to attempt exposure or in the level of fear they believe they can tolerate before escaping. Representing these differences would allow researchers to examine whether exposure schedules that begin with lower-or higher-intensity exercises are predicted to produce better outcomes for a given simulated patient.

Match Patients to Variable Model Component States and Parameters

To inform decisions for actual patients, measurements need to be linked to the model's variable component states and parameters (Figure 1, Panel B). Patient matching uses these observations to estimate plausible values for the quantities that differ between individuals, identifying settings under which simulated patterns approximate the patient's data. For a real patient, the same observations may be consistent with several combinations of states and parameters. The relevant question is whether these plausible representations imply meaningfully different treatment choices. Depending on the quantities involved, matching may draw on questionnaire responses, repeated observations of symptoms and behavior, or features derived from passive data (e.g., collected via smartphone or wearable devices). Researchers can then simulate alternative treatment configurations for the same patient and compare their predicted outcomes. These comparisons may favor different configurations for different patients or for the same patient at different times. The same treatment may also be favored across patients despite heterogeneity in predicted outcomes.

Pair the Theory with a Treatment That Can Deliver Personalized Configurations

The treatment options compared in simulations also need to be deliverable in practice (Figure 1, Panel C). When predictions concern component selection, sequencing, or dosing, the treatment must allow those features to vary. Additionally, the treatment's active elements and delivery procedures should correspond to those represented in the model. Modular digital therapies offer one way to support this flexibility: standardized, standalone modules can be combined and sequenced differently across patients, with opportunities to vary the amount of between-session practice that is assigned (Cohen et al., 2025).

Observe What Each Patient Receives, Does, and What Changes

Researchers can use observations to support three related but distinct goals in formal theory-driven personalization (Figure 1, Panel D). First, evaluating outcome predictions involves comparing predicted and observed outcomes, such as symptom change, without necessarily measuring every modeled process. Second, evaluating the proposed explanation calls for observations of what patients receive, how they engage with active treatment elements, and how the targeted processes change (Cohen et al., 2023). For example, examining an explanation based on disconfirming feared outcomes involves assessing expectations before exposure, what occurred during the exercise, and subsequent belief change. Third, evaluating clinical utility requires testing whether using model-derived predictions to select or adapt treatment improves outcomes relative to a specified alternative decision strategy, such as clinician judgment, datadriven selection, or assigning the same treatment to all patients. This requires a specified decision rule and a design that supports the comparison. Within-treatment adaptation additionally requires timely observations to update predictions before the next decision. These observations may overlap with those used to evaluate mechanisms but are selected for their relevance to the treatment decision. We use the term personalization infrastructure to refer to the treatment-delivery and assessment capabilities supporting these goals. Section 3 presents a roadmap for developing these capabilities and empirically evaluating the approach.

Phase 1. Synthesize Existing Knowledge

The purpose of this phase is to synthesize what is known about the formal theory and intended treatment, identifying whether they can support treatment personalization in alignment with the researcher's goals and where further work may be needed. Researchers should examine the phenomena the theory aims to explain, the processes and individual differences it represents, and findings from existing simulations and empirical work. Reviewing the computational implementation and reproducing available simulations can clarify how the model generates its reported behavior. Treatment materials and delivery procedures should be considered alongside the model to identify how active elements and their formalized processes correspond (Cohen et al., 2023). Before proceeding to Phase 2, researchers should document areas of alignment, explanatory shortcomings, and relevant processes missing from the model or treatment.

Worked Example: Synthesizing the Panic Model and Digital Therapy

Our synthesis focused on whether prescribing different treatment configurations might benefit different patients receiving modular digital cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for panic disorder. We reviewed the Panic Model (Robinaugh et al., 2024), its treatment implementation (Ryan et al., 2025), and the digital therapy's panic modules (Cohen et al., 2025; Craske & Barlow, 2022). The original model reproduced individual differences in panic vulnerability and CBT efficacy in simulations but not moderate-severity panic disorder, and subsequent treatment simulations demonstrated heterogeneous responses. Throughout the example, "the published model" refers to this treatment-inclusive implementation before our extensions. Despite broad correspondence between the modeled treatment components and digital therapy content, the comparison revealed important differences in how exposure engagement and learning were represented.

The model links rapid changes in arousal, perceived threat, and escape with slower changes in avoidance and beliefs about the danger of bodily arousal and the ability to cope without escaping. These beliefs are represented by arousal schema and escape schema, respectively; we refer to escape schema as 'panic self-efficacy' for the remainder of this paper (Gallagher et al., 2013; McNally, 2002; Reiss et al., 1986). Cognitive components directly change these beliefs, whereas exposure increases arousal (interoceptive exposure) or places the simulated patient in a feared context (in vivo exposure). Exposure adherence and engagementquality parameters vary between patients but are determined by random draws and remain fixed within patients across treatment.

The digital therapy's exposure tools elicit expectations about feared outcomes before an exercise and reports of learning afterward. The published model instead updates arousal schema and panic self-efficacy using thresholds for fear and escape, without explicitly representing whether an exposure outcome differed from the patient's expectation (expectancy violation).

Patients can also choose exposure targets and durations in the digital therapy, whereas the model varies exposure modality and intensity. These mismatches informed the Phase 2 evaluation of whether the existing model could support the intended treatment comparisons and whether additional representations of learning and engagement were needed.

Phase 2. Specify the Formal Theory for Predicting Treatment Outcomes

The purpose of this phase is to determine whether the existing formal theory can support the intended treatment comparison and whether revisions are needed. Before proceeding, researchers should establish that the model represents the relevant processes and reproduces the treatment-response phenomena identified for evaluation, and that any revisions preserve the phenomena already reproduced by the starting model.

Specify the Treatment Question and Explanatory Account

Building on Phase 1, researchers should specify the treatment decision and a small set of phenomena the theory should explain. Phenomena are robust, empirically established features of the world, such as variation in outcomes among patients receiving the same treatment.

Researchers should make the proposed explanation for these phenomena explicit, drawing on existing psychological theory, clinical knowledge, and empirical research (Fried, 2020; Haslbeck et al., 2022). Personalization hypotheses can then specify how relevant individual differences might interact with treatment components, sequences, or doses. A new verbal account is needed only where the existing theory is deemed insufficient to explain why personalized treatment recommendations might emerge.

Evaluate and, if Necessary, Extend the Formal Theory

Researchers should first examine what the existing model predicts for the treatment comparison. This may require additional simulations and auxiliary assumptions about treatment delivery or the simulated population beyond those used in the model's original evaluation. For example, researchers can simulate alternative packages for individuals differing in theoretically relevant states or parameters. Parameter sweeps and sensitivity analyses across theoretically and empirically plausible values can establish which behaviors the model permits and how strongly predictions depend on particular assumptions (Haslbeck et al., 2022).

Different outcomes across patients do not necessarily imply different preferred treatments. If the model represents the relevant processes and reproduces the identified phenomena, but favors the same configuration across patients, this is an informative result within the treatments and patients examined and not by itself a reason to revise the theory. Failure to reproduce established treatment-response phenomena, or omission of processes needed to evaluate the comparison, is a different problem. When such discrepancies arise, researchers should use abductive inference to consider whether they reflect parameter settings, auxiliary assumptions, or the proposed explanation (i.e., the theory) itself. Revisions should faithfully represent the explanatory account, not simply produce different preferred treatments.

Researchers should evaluate the revised model against the intended treatment-response phenomena and the starting model's established successes. If shortcomings remain, further theory development is indicated before interpreting its predictions.

Worked Example: Comparing Exposure Packages

Treatment Question and Hypothesis

Our treatment question was whether patients would benefit differently from graded exposure delivered in alignment with a traditional fear-hierarchy approach versus a package emphasizing higher, varied intensities and additional support for inhibitory learning (Craske et al., 2014 (Craske et al., , 2022)). The phenomena we sought to explain included variable response to CBT for panic disorder (Loerinc et al., 2015), limited average differences between cognitive-and exposure-focused variants (Pompoli et al., 2018), few established moderators of relative treatment benefit (Porter & Chambless, 2015), and substantial, often early attrition from selfguided digital treatment (Linardon & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz, 2020).

We drew on inhibitory-learning accounts proposing that disconfirming feared outcomes establishes new learning that competes with, rather than erases, existing danger associations, and that its later expression depends on retention and retrieval in the current context (Bouton, 1993; Craske et al., 2014 Craske et al., , 2022)). Separately, an informative exercise cannot help through this pathway if it is never begun or is escaped before the expectation is tested. We hypothesized that differences in learning and participation could therefore change which exposure package produces the lowest cumulative symptom burden for a patient.

Inspecting the Published Model

We inspected the published treatment implementation (Ryan et al., 2025) in relation to the proposed comparison. Intensity perturbs arousal, but threshold-based learning does not explicitly represent how exercise-specific expectancy violation contributes to inhibitory learning.

Treatment adherence and engagement quality are determined by randomly drawn parameters that are fixed within patients. The published model therefore does not directly represent the inhibitory learning processes and interactions among treatment engagement, patient characteristics, and features of assigned treatment elements that we wished to examine. These conceptual limitations motivated the provisional extensions described below.

Provisional Model Extensions

We therefore provisionally extended the model to represent these learning and engagement processes while retaining its core panic system. Supplement S2 explains the principal mechanisms and key settings, and the computational implementation and full parameterization are documented in the accompanying codebase, which will be made available for download at https://github.com/nchoukas/PrecisionTreatmentRoadmap. In the extended model, completed exposures generate inhibitory learning when a feared outcome is expected but does not occur (Craske et al., 2014 (Craske et al., , 2022)). Safety behavior reduces the expectation available to be violated (Blakey & Abramowitz, 2016), while the quality of post-exposure processing affects how much learning a given violation produces (Craske et al., 2014 (Craske et al., , 2022)). Therefore, completing an exposure should not necessarily be expected to produce substantial learning if the feared outcome was not strongly expected or the experience was inadequately processed. This learning changes the effective arousal schema: how strongly the underlying danger belief influences threat, avoidance, and symptoms when learning is retrieved. Thus, symptoms can improve without erasing the original danger association (Bouton, 1993; Craske et al., 2014 Craske et al., , 2022)).

The extension also distinguishes acquiring learning from retaining and retrieving it (Bouton, 1993; Craske et al., 2014 Craske et al., , 2022)). Learning can be acquired during an exercise but poorly retained, or retained but weakly retrieved in an unfamiliar setting. Practice across varied contexts can broaden retrieval of learning (Shiban et al., 2013), whereas rehearsal and sleep quality may support its retention (Kleim et al., 2014; Pace-Schott et al., 2012). These distinctions allow experiences that produce similar initial learning to have different later effects.

Treatment adherence is represented separately from learning. We formalized an exposure willingness component, which represents a patient's readiness to attempt an exercise. Exposure willingness is distinct from panic self-efficacy, which concerns general confidence in coping with panic. Greater exposure willingness and panic self-efficacy increase the modeled probability of starting an assigned exercise, whereas greater expected distress, avoidance, and intensity of the assigned exposure decrease it. Additionally, we formalized a package demand component to represent the additional burden of completing structured exposures involving preexposure planning and post-exposure processing, which lowers the probability of starting an assigned exercise. These directions are informed by evidence linking willingness, panic-related appraisals and self-efficacy, avoidance, and treatment engagement with exposure participation and outcomes (Gallagher et al., 2013; Reid et al., 2017; Schønning & Nordgreen, 2021; Telch et al., 1989; Wheaton et al., 2018). Once an exercise begins, the panic and escape dynamics interact with distress tolerance, which reflects the patient's momentary belief about how much fear they can tolerate.

Finally, the extended model distinguishes exercises not started, started and escaped, and completed, with changing patient states influencing subsequent participation. In this formulation, an escaped exercise produces no feared-outcome expectancy violation through the inhibitorylearning pathway described above, and repeated noncompletion can contribute to treatment attrition, a broader challenge in digital mental health interventions (Linardon & Fuller-Tyszkiewicz, 2020). Within the model, escaping an exposure decreases momentary distress but increases arousal schema and decreases panic self-efficacy over time, by reinforcing beliefs that the situation was dangerous and that the individual was safe only because they escaped.

Phase 3. Build Personalization Infrastructure

The purpose of this phase is to develop the treatment-delivery and assessment capabilities needed to evaluate theory-implied predictions and design a study in which they will be used.

Before proceeding, researchers should specify how treatments will be delivered, how patient observations will inform the model, and which comparisons and analyses will address the study's research questions.

Develop or Adapt Treatment

Researchers should translate the treatment configurations examined in Phase 2 into deliverable procedures, specifying their active elements, sequence, timing, and amount of practice. The active elements and their intended effects should correspond to those represented in the model (Cohen et al., 2023). Where gaps remain, researchers may need to adapt existing materials or develop new content and delivery capabilities. For example, a modeled contribution of between-session practice calls for corresponding instructions and opportunities to practice. If the proposed procedures cannot be delivered, researchers should reconsider the treatment comparison or its representation in the model, rather than assume that a different intervention can be used to evaluate theory-implied predictions.

Design Measurement and Patient Matching

Researchers should specify how the model quantities relevant to their question relate to observable data. An observation mapping describes the measurements expected from a model state or trajectory, including response scale, timing, aggregation, measurement error, and missingness. Patient matching works in the other direction, whereby observations inform plausible states or parameters for an individual. Researchers should evaluate whether candidate measures capture the intended constructs (Flake & Fried, 2020), because simply rescaling a questionnaire total does not establish that it measures the corresponding model quantity.

Measurement timing should be carefully selected to capture the relevant quantities at the timescales upon which theory-implied predictions depend. Some relatively stable characteristics may be approximated using intake assessments, whereas rates of change or relationships among rapidly fluctuating states generally require repeated observations. One option for patient matching is simulation-based inference, which compares observed empirical patterns with those generated under candidate model settings (Cranmer et al., 2020). Researchers should use simulated observations to examine whether the planned measurements and estimation procedure support the treatment decision. Unique estimates of every quantity may be unnecessary if predicted treatment differences remain similar across plausible patient settings. When recommendations differ, researchers should quantify this uncertainty and specify whether to obtain additional observations, defer selection, or use a prespecified alternative decision rule.

Importantly, stable recommendations will still need to undergo empirical evaluation, as a misspecified model can make consistently incorrect predictions.

The research design should distinguish evaluation of predicted outcomes and process Before collecting data, we recommend simulating the planned study under its treatment, measurement, and research-design assumptions. Applying the planned analyses to simulated observations produces theory-implied features, such as symptom trajectories or treatment-group differences, that more closely represent empirical data and can thus be compared with corresponding empirical features once data are collected. When predictions and observations disagree, researchers should use abductive inference to consider plausible explanations involving the theory, treatment delivery, measurement, or research design (Haslbeck et al., 2022). Such analyses should be reported alongside the original comparison. A better match supports the documented departure as a possible explanation. However, different combinations of theory and measurement assumptions may produce similar observations, so improved agreement alone does not rule out competing explanations. A theory may explain important clinical phenomena without representing the processes needed for a particular treatment decision. Our review identified this problem for exposure learning and engagement. Matching Patients and Measuring What the Theory Represents

Patient matching raises a practical question: how much do researchers need to know about a patient to support a treatment decision? Different combinations of model states and parameters may reproduce similar observations yet imply different treatment responses. Phase 3 describes procedures for examining this problem. An important next step is to identify which observations distinguish decision-relevant quantities and whether uncertainty changes the favored treatment. Once evaluated across patients, these findings could inform a smaller, less burdensome set of measurements.

Connecting constructs to observations also remains a substantive research task (Flake & Fried, 2020). In the extension, a lower threat rating could reflect weaker danger beliefs, stronger retrieval of inhibitory learning, or both. Distinguishing these possibilities requires observations across relevant times and contexts rather than treating one rating as a direct measure of learning.

The model's single arousal component also raises questions about mapping diverse panic-related bodily experiences onto one quantity. In our pilot version, exposure tools are configured to collect reports of actual safety-behavior use; the remaining work includes evaluating how these reports and records of escape relate to model quantities. Quantitative ratings and written descriptions may inform measurement of learning (Cohen et al., 2023), and any human-coded or language-model-assisted ratings of those descriptions would require evaluation before being used to test mechanisms or guide treatment.

Building and Evaluating Personalization Infrastructure

Even with an existing model and treatment, aligning theory, delivery, measurement, and research design requires sustained collaboration across clinical, modeling, measurement, and implementation expertise. Shared model implementations, documented treatment-to-model mappings, and reusable measurement procedures could reduce duplication. Our planned pilot will collect pretreatment and longitudinal observations to evaluate the model and examine whether pretreatment assessments could support patient matching. Subsequent work should first establish whether these observations support informative treatment comparisons, then test whether a prespecified model-guided allocation rule improves outcomes relative to an alternative decision strategy. Support for the proposed mechanisms remains a separate empirical question.

Within-treatment adaptation further requires timely observations and evaluated procedures for updating predictions and deciding when to change treatment.

These developments also connect with patient digital-twin approaches that are being used in other areas of medicine, particularly models updated as patient observations become available.

Digital twins may use mechanistic, data-driven, or hybrid models, whereas formal theory-driven personalization derives predictions from explicit theories of psychopathology and treatment (Drummond & Gonsard, 2024; Katsoulakis et al., 2024). A formal theory could provide the mechanistic basis for a patient digital twin once matching, updating, and validation procedures are established. Our worked example illustrates simulations of hypothetical patients, not validated digital twins of actual patients.

Conclusion

Formal theories offer a way to derive treatment predictions from explicit accounts of psychopathology and recovery. Their clinical value depends on whether patients can be matched adequately, predicted treatments can be delivered, outcome and process predictions withstand empirical evaluation, and acting on those predictions improves care. Progress will require testing and refining these links, including learning when further personalization is not supported. The promise of this approach is therefore not personalization for its own sake, but a more explicit and testable basis for determining what treatment should be delivered, to whom, and when-and for learning when our theories are not yet sufficient to answer those questions.

Selecting Among Treatment Configurations

Generating treatment predictions does not by itself specify how to select treatment.

Researchers must define which configurations are clinically appropriate and feasible, along with the objective and time horizon for comparing them, choices our worked example illustrates but does not settle. Our worked example compared cumulative symptom burden over 12 weeks, rather than endpoint severity alone. When researchers wish to evaluate many different potential treatments, doing so for every configuration may become impractical, creating a need for search procedures that can be checked against broader comparisons. Selection also requires handling cases where predictions are effectively tied. Here, considering patient preferences, burden, or feasibility may provide additional criteria. Additionally, when uncertainty is large enough that it could reverse the ranking, selecting the configuration with the best point prediction may not be the most defensible choice. Developing and evaluating procedures that handle these cases is a necessary step before simulated treatment predictions can become usable recommendations.

Comorbidity, Context, and Intervention Targets

Disorder-specific models may omit interactions with processes associated with other disorders or the patient's circumstances. Connecting existing models or representing transdiagnostic processes offers one direction for addressing this limitation (Robinaugh et al., 2024). Relevant intervention targets may also lie beyond the individual, and selecting psychotherapy components alone may leave important problems unaddressed. A systems approach allows contextual factors (e.g., food or housing insecurity) to influence which interventions are considered, rather than treating them only as predictors of psychotherapy response.
