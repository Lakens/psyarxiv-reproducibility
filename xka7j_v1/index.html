Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

A key challenge in the behavioral sciences is the lack of formal models that capture individual differences in social interactions. At the same time, recent advances in artificial intelligence offer new opportunities to simulate and assess interpersonal behavior at scale. In this study, we introduce an agent-based modeling framework that uses large language models (LLMs) to simulate short-term social interactions. Within a dyadic interaction cycle, an LLM-based agent continually adjusts its interpersonal behavior (agency and communion) to its prior behavior, the partner's behavior, and the combined influence of behavioral defaults (differences in default behavioral tendencies) and interpersonal reactivities (differences in reactions to partner behaviors). We derive a mathematical model that formalizes how interpersonal behavior unfolds across the interaction, evaluate the model in simulations, illustrate how the framework expresses these interpersonal dynamics in natural language, and outline the framework's flexibility for simulating individual differences in practical applications across the behavioral sciences.

INTRODUCTION

Adequately describing, understanding, and predicting an individual's behavior during social interactions are fundamental tasks for the behavioral sciences [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] . How people perceive, respond to, and act toward others in social interactions largely defines their personality, drives individual development, and has short-, medium-, and long-term consequences for well-being, relationship qualities, and a variety of life outcomes 1, 4, [6] [7] [8] [9] . While individual differences in social interactions are a focal topic of the behavioral sciences with a range of theoretical frameworks and empirical findings [1] [2] [3] [4] 7, 8, 10, 11 , there is a lack of computational models that formalize how these differences dynamically emerge and manifest during interactions.

Among the most comprehensive and established frameworks in social interaction research is contemporary integrative interpersonal theory (CIIT) 3, 11, 12 , which conceptualizes personality expression as unfolding dynamically within social interactions. CIIT provides a coherent framework not only for general patterns of interaction dynamics such as the degree to which the behavior of interacting individuals is (dis)similar (complementarity) but also for describing, understanding, and predicting individual differences in social interaction. This includes both differences in people's baseline levels of interpersonal behavior (behavioral defaults) and differences in how they respond to others' behavior (interpersonal reactivities). Individuals vary substantially in both their behavioral defaults and their interpersonal reactivities 1, [13] [14] [15] [16] , and these differences critically shape how social interactions unfold.

Despite its extensive empirical foundation, CIIT lacks integrative formal models that unify these phenomena within a single framework and capture how they unfold dynamically between interacting individuals 1, 12, 17, 18 . Although there are conceptually rich descriptions regarding the mechanisms through which behavioral defaults and interpersonal reactivities contribute to differences in interpersonal behavior, these mechanisms remain mathematically underspecified. Agent-based modeling (ABM) is one important method for formalizing such mechanisms and simulating their dynamics [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] . However, the resulting interpersonal behavior in ABMs is abstract (i.e., only numbers) and lacks usability and ecological validity.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence offer novel opportunities to bridge this gap 20, 24, 25.

Embedding the principles of CIIT into an ABM framework powered by large language models (LLMs) makes it possible to simulate, assess, and manipulate social interactions with linguistic realism, systematic parameter variation, and scalability [24] [25] [26] . It enables formalizing how individual differences shape interpersonal dynamics while expressing those dynamics in natural-language interactions. CIIT conceptualizes personality as inherently relational: emerging, expressed, and understood within social interactions 2, 3, 11, 12, 27 and organizes personality expression along two orthogonal dimensions: agency and communion 11, 28 . Agency characterizes individuals in terms of influence, control, and mastery, ranging from dominant to submissive. Communion characterizes how individuals relate to others in terms of closeness, intimacy, and affiliation, ranging from friendly to hostile. These dimensions define the interpersonal circumplex (IPC), a circular structural model originating from Leary's 29 interpersonal circle and further developed to locate interpersonal traits, motives, and behaviors within a two-dimensional space 28, 30 . CIIT situates the components through which personality unfolds in real time (i.e., dispositions, situational perceptions, momentary goals, and self-related or affective processes) within this agency-communion space 3, 11, 12, 27 . In this study, we focus on behavior, through which these processes become observable in interactions. To describe how interpersonal behavior unfolds in "interpersonal situations" 2, 3, 27 , CIIT draws on the transaction cycle 31, 32 , a causal loop in which each individual's behavior, and their perception of the partner's behavior, reciprocally influence one another over the course of an interaction (Fig. 1). The transaction cycle provides both a conceptual foundation for modeling social interactions as a cyclical process and a mechanism for explaining moment-to-moment changes in interpersonal behavior. Despite its conceptual clarity, there are remarkably few formal models that build directly on CIIT, instantiate its transactional logic, and account for the unfolding of interpersonal dynamics. Westermann and Banisch 18 proposed a "relational state space" model that to our knowledge constitutes the first CIIT-consistent account to formalize interpersonal dynamics. They defined a tetrahedral (four-sided) space of dyadic relations and demonstrated how different configurations of affiliative approach and avoidance motives yield interpersonal patterns. Yet, their relational state space is a dyad-level construct which offers only limited possibilities for incorporating person-specific parameters at scale. To move toward models that integrate individual differences, it is essential to identify and formalize the phenomena that shape how interpersonal behavior evolves over the course of an interaction. Drawing on CIIT and empirical findings from social interaction research 6, 9, 15, [33] [34] [35] [36] , we focus on two key individual difference variables: behavioral defaults and interpersonal reactivities.

Behavioral defaults capture differences in the baseline behavioral level with which individuals enter social interactions and toward which their behavior tends to return over time. Within the IPC, behavioral defaults correspond to a person's typical location on the agentic and communal axes in the absence of strong situational influences, especially the interaction partner's behavior. Empirically, such defaults are typically approximated by individuals' characteristic distributions of behavioral states across situations (e.g., their average level of agency or communion across repeated observations), although observed behavior also reflects influences from the interaction partner. Previous studies have shown substantial individual differences in behavioral defaults across different kinds of behaviors in general 37, 38 and for agency and communion in particular 13, 33 . These differences are reliably measurable and highly stable over time 13, 37, 38 , whereas deviations from these defaults (i.e., "acting out of character") have been associated with negative outcomes such as reduced positive affect and increased psychological depletion 39 . This suggests that individuals systematically gravitate toward their behavioral defaults in the absence of strong partner signals 40, 41 . Beyond these differences in default behavioral tendencies, individuals also differ in how they react to the behavior of others during social interactions 1, 13, 14, 16, 34, 42, 43 . These interpersonal reactivities (also called contingencies) 15 refer to the extent to which individuals adjust their behavior in response to social characteristics of the situation, particularly the interaction partner's behavior 3, 11, 13 . Within CIIT, interpersonal reactivities have been examined mostly indirectly. General dynamics such as complementarity (a reciprocal pattern in which interpersonal behavior aligns with the partner's communion while opposing their agency 11, 35, 44, 45 ) are well studied, whereas reactivities appear mainly as psychopathological deviations from complementarity, as correlates of interpersonal dispositions, or as differences in perceiving others' agency and communion 14, 34, 43, 46 . They are, however, individual difference variables in their own right, shaping how people perceive others and adjust their own behavior in response 1, 13, 15 . Studies of reactivities to social situations show substantial individual differences, moderate reliability and consistency, and only modest overlap with behavioral defaults assessed via trait surveys 6, 9, 13, 15, 34 . Integrating both behavioral defaults and interpersonal reactivities is therefore an important step to describe, understand, and predict social interactions.

Doing so requires specifying the processes through which differences in interpersonal behavior unfold over time. In recent years, psychological science has increasingly emphasized formal models as a tool to make theoretical assumptions precise and to derive testable predictions 23, 47 . This approach is particularly promising for social interaction research 1, 18, 21, 41, 48 , where many theoretical frameworks provide detailed descriptions of key concepts and relationships in natural language but are not typically specified in mathematical terms 1, 2, 12, 17, 18, 23 . Existing efforts to model social interactions based on CIIT have primarily focused on specific phenomena, most notably complementarity 35, 44, 45, 49, 50 , and have not yet yielded fully formalized, generative, and predictive models of interpersonal dynamics, with Westermann and Banisch 18 providing a notable exception. Thus, despite the availability of theoretical blueprints and methodological tools for formal models of social interactions 10, 21, 22, 47 , the systematic implementation and evaluation of formal models of interpersonal behavior remains limited. There are likely multiple reasons for this gap, for example the limited availability of large-scale labeled datasets, challenges in reducing a complex phenomenon such as social interactions (where the relevant variable space is vast, multidimensional, and context-sensitive) to a tractable set of formalizable processes, and difficulties of recovering model parameters from empirical data 10, 47, 51.

One promising approach is ABM, a computational simulation method in which agents interact over time with one another and their environment, letting researchers reproduce dynamic social phenomena and refine testable theories in controlled settings 19, [21] [22] [23] . A limitation of classical ABM, however, is that agents exchange only numbers or heavily simplified patterns of interpersonal behavior. This keeps formal-model evaluation tractable but cannot capture the richness of interpersonal behavior, in particular the dynamics central to CIIT and the interpretation of language that underpins social interaction 20, 24, 25 , and it constrains both realistic simulations and human-model interaction. Newer paradigms begin to translate formal models into more ecologically valid interpersonal behavior (e.g., MovES 52 ), yet little prior work has connected a formal model of interpersonal behavior with a paradigm that enacts it in natural language.

LLMs offer a new way to connect formal models with ecologically valid social interactions at scale through sophisticated agents capable of simulating interpersonal behavior 20, 25, 26, 53 . Accordingly, large-scale platforms are emerging that model a variety of phenomena using LLM-based agents (e.g., SOTOPIA 53, 54 ), and LLM-based agent modeling has already been successfully applied to simulate a variety of social phenomena, such as decision making 25 , cooperation 53, 55 , or social network simulations 54 . Furthermore, a growing body of research has demonstrated that LLMs can adequately simulate personality differences, either through modifications to the underlying model architecture 56 or through carefully designed prompting strategies 57 . In parallel, LLMs have been shown to effectively assess personality differences, both when prompting off-the-shelf models 58, 59 and when employing models specifically fine-tuned for personality assessment 60.

Existing approaches to simulating differences in interpersonal behavior with LLMs are limited in two ways. First, most work implements personality expression as a fixed trait level 56, 57 and assesses it from one-time textual input 58, 59 or explicit self-descriptions 59.

Second, these simulations are rarely tied to a formal model of interpersonal behavior, which limits their theoretical value because the moment-to-moment transactions remain opaque.

Adequately simulating ecologically valid interactions and deriving insights for formal-model and theory development therefore require LLM-based simulations that capture and adapt to the partner's momentary interpersonal behavior and that connect an explicit formal model steering behavior across the interaction with the language capabilities of LLMs. Direct evidence remains limited, but early studies suggest LLMs can express and assess personality dynamically over an interaction: they can simulate complementarity through alignment in textual behavior 26 , track and update a partner's state across the interaction [61] [62] [63] , and adjust their own personality expression to situational characteristics 63, 64 . In this study, we aim to make three core contributions: (1) Introducing an overarching framework for LLM-based Agent Simulations of individual differences in Social Interactions (LASSI), in which each agent's interpersonal behavior is continually adjusted in response to the perceived behavior of the interaction partner.

(2) Developing and implementing a mathematical model that formalizes how two key individual difference variables (i.e., behavioral defaults, interpersonal reactivities) jointly shape an agent's interpersonal behavior and verifying its behavior in numerical simulations.

(3) Demonstrating how LASSI translates interpersonal dynamics into natural language and outlining applications across the behavioral sciences.

RESULTS

The LASSI framework

This study presents LLM-based Agent Simulations of individual differences in Social

Interactions (LASSI), a general framework for modeling social interactions by combining formal models of interpersonal behavior with LLM-based agents. LASSI is grounded in CIIT 3, 11, 12 and adopts the IPC as a validated structural representation of interpersonal behavior. It models social interactions as recursive cycles in which personality is expressed through acting, perceiving, and reacting (Fig. 1) 3, 11, 31, 32 ; see also social interaction units 1, 10 . At its core lies a dyadic interaction cycle between two agents, each either a human or an LLMbased agent (Fig. 2), who continually adjust their interpersonal behavior in response to the perceived behavior of their interaction partner. Each LLM-based agent consists of two distinct components: a generator, responsible for simulating interpersonal behavior, and an assessor, responsible for assessing the interpersonal behavior expressed by the interaction partner. This division maps onto the two separate but interrelated components of expressing and perceiving behavior that are central to humans in social interactions 10.

A central design principle of LASSI is to keep the formal modeling of interpersonal dynamics separate from the realistic language generation via the LLMs. The formal model defines how an agent's interpersonal state (i.e., agency and communion) evolves independently of any linguistic realization. An LLM-based generator renders a sampled interpersonal state as a concrete natural-language utterance, whereas an LLM-based assessor maps an observed utterance back onto agency and communion. Thus, the language model provides linguistic and contextual realism, usability through human users, and scalability, not the interpersonal dynamics themselves. LASSI's components can be evaluated separately: the formal model on its numerical output alone, the generator and assessor independently of the formal model, and the full pipeline end to end.

Prior to the interaction, each agent's behavioral repertoire is initialized for both IPC dimensions, agency and communion. Without prior interaction history or context, the repertoire's mean is set to the agent's behavioral default. A discrete state is then sampled from it to start the interaction. Based on this state, Agent-A's generator is prompted to express behavior aligned with the sampled state's position in the interpersonal space. The generator's system prompt combines a static part (the interaction context, background information, and instructions to remain in character) with a dynamic part that describes the agent's current states on both dimensions (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). The generator then simulates behavior that simultaneously aligns with the current conversational content and the agent's position in interpersonal space.

A new discrete state is sampled from the updated repertoire and informs the dynamic prompt, which guides the generator in producing behavior consistent with Agent-B's sampled state and the conversational content. A formal model of interpersonal behavior

A formal model governs the dynamics in LASSI (see Methods for details). It updates each agent's interpersonal behavior from turn to turn, each interpersonal dimension independently. An agent's behavior is modeled as draws from a behavioral repertoire, a probability distribution of width τ over a symmetric scale centered at zero. The mean of this repertoire is anchored at the behavioral default (BD) and shifted by the partner as the interaction unfolds. Interpersonal reactivity (IR) is a single parameter between -1 and +1 whose magnitude sets how much influence the partner's behavior has and whose sign sets whether the agent responds complementarily or against complementarity. At each turn the agent forms a partner-implied target. If IR is positive, that means mirroring (i.e., opposing) the partner on agency and matching the partner on communion. The agent blends this target with its default in proportion to |IR|, and then moves part of the way toward that blend at a relaxation rate λ. Behavior therefore shows turn-to-turn inertia, while relaxing back toward the default when the partner exerts little influence (|IR| near 0). Each agent is thus described on each interpersonal dimension by four parameters: BD, IR, τ, and λ.

Parameter recovery and data requirements

We next fitted the model back to the simulated data to see how well an agent's four parameters (BD, IR, τ, and λ) can be recovered and how recovery scales with the length of the interaction (20 to 160 exchanges; Fig. 4). Repertoire width was recovered best from about 80 exchanges onward, with a root mean squared error (RMSE) falling from 0.43 at 20 exchanges to 0.065 at 160 for agency (Supplementary Table S5). The direction of reactivity was also recovered well. Its sign was correct for 100% of agents from 20 exchanges onward when |IR| was at least 0.5, and for 79% to 96% of agents across the full range of IR.

Estimates of IR itself correlated with the true values at r = 0.78 to 0.81 at 20 exchanges and r = 0.92 from 80 exchanges onward (Supplementary Table S6). The BD was recovered with high correlations between true and estimated values (r = 0.81 to 0.92). Its RMSE was the largest of the four parameters, mainly because its range is wider (-2 to +2, compared with -1 to +1 for IR). Within a single interaction, an agent's average position reflects both its behavioral default and how strongly the partner shifts it away from that level, making the two difficult to disentangle.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we introduced LASSI, a framework for simulating social interactions and individual differences therein using LLM-based agents grounded in CIIT. By integrating behavioral defaults (BDs) and interpersonal reactivities (IRs) into a unified computational architecture, LASSI specifies how individual differences shape and are expressed in social interactions. Numerical simulations showed that cross-lagged complementarity and flux emerged as expected from the model, with complementarity tracking each agent's IR and flux increasing with |IR|. Parameter recovery was best for repertoire width (τ) and the direction of reactivity, moderate for the behavioral default (BD) and reactivity (IR), and weakest for the relaxation rate (λ). Integrating the formal model into the full LASSI pipeline illustrated how its states are expressed in individual messages and how the resulting interpersonal dynamics appear in natural-language interactions.

In the following, we outline three major advantages of the LASSI framework. First, its architecture is model-agnostic: it is not bound to any specific LLM, so current and future systems can be integrated, and it can be extended beyond text to voice, video, or virtual reality 66, 67 . Because core interpersonal dynamics recur across contexts 1, 33, 36, 49, 50 , the models developed within LASSI can, once validated, transfer across domains such as education, healthcare, psychotherapy, and organizations. Second, LASSI combines the tunable, in silico parameter manipulation of rule-based agent modeling with the linguistic realism of LLMs in one framework 20, 24, 25 . This allows human-agent and agent-agent interactions in natural language at scale, together with a systematic assessment of the reliability and validity of both the generator and the assessor and of how these properties depend on modeling choices, conversational context, and, in later iterations, multimodality. Researchers can also change the parameters at any point in an interaction, for example in response to specific events. Controlled in silico designs can thus isolate single parameters before resource-intensive human studies 19, 22, 68, 69 . This makes a wide range of phenomena tractable to simulate and probe, for example how personality similarity shapes negotiation, liking, or relationship satisfaction, whether introverts and extraverts get along 70 , how complementarity shapes interaction quality 44, 45, 50 , whether narcissism heightens sensitivity to agentic behavior 34, 46 , and how atypical reactivities relate to psychopathology and interpersonal dysfunction 2, 14, 43 . Third, the framework can host alternative narrative theories and formal models from personality science, clinical psychology, and neuroscience 10, 18, 40, 41, 48 , whose simulated behavior can be compared against large-scale human data to indicate which phenomena a given theory captures. Moreover, the present model can itself be calibrated on naturalistic interaction datasets and validated out of sample 71 , a process made increasingly feasible by rich multimodal corpora 51, 66.

The present study establishes that the formal model behaves as specified and that its states can be rendered in natural language, but it does not yet establish that these dynamics or linguistic realizations accurately reproduce human interactions. Validation against large-scale human interaction data is therefore a key next step. The model's current assumptions also motivate several promising refinements and extensions. The first concerns the temporal granularity of the dyadic cycle. Each iteration currently consists of one message exchange, with the behavioral repertoire updated only after the partner's message is processed. This is straightforward but may oversimplify real interaction 35, 45 . CIIT suggests that people adjust their behavior in response to both their own and their partner's actions 3, 11, 31 , so it may help to update after both messages, potentially with different parameters. In the numerical simulations, both agents responded to the partner's state from the previous exchange, producing complementarity across exchanges but not within them. In human interactions, however, complementarity has also been observed in behavior coded at short intervals. Rich or ambiguous behavior can also produce shifts on much shorter timescales, within a single message 35, 45 , which motivates event-based updates, such as detecting highly agentic or communal behavior, or time-based updates at fixed intervals. Evaluating behavior message by message can further mislead, since a neutral follow-up may be read as a change when it merely reflects prior behavior. More nuanced schemes could weight each message by its information content or prompt the models to infer the current state from the full interaction history.

A second extension concerns the assumption that behavioral defaults and interpersonal reactivities remain constant across interaction contexts. Reactivity can vary with the interaction partner, and complementarity can depend on the setting and on role status 4, 42, 49 . Context-specific parameters could capture this, though they complicate fitting and evaluation and should be introduced only where they yield meaningful gains in explanatory power. A final extension concerns the IPC itself, which we model with only the two orthogonal dimensions of agency and communion on a fixed set of scale values.

Incorporating additional rotations of the circumplex axes, such as the blends at 45-degree angles, and their interpersonal adjectives in the prompts for the generator and the assessor could capture finer nuances 28, 30 . Although agency and communion are defined as orthogonal, people do not always perceive or express them independently 72 , so future versions could add parameters that let one dimension influence the other. Representing a circular space on a square grid is also an approximation: distance from the neutral midpoint depends on direction, so a blend of high agency and high communion counts as more extreme than either alone. Polar or wrapped geometries, and finer or dynamically scaled values, are natural alternatives.

Beyond these refinements and extensions, LASSI offers practical applications across the behavioral sciences. Other personality variables, such as the Big Five or HEXACO, can be incorporated where relevant to a particular application. The models it hosts could transfer to work and organizational settings, such as negotiation, conflict resolution, and leadership training; healthcare, such as therapeutic conversations, diagnostic interviews, and clinician training; education, such as teacher training and classroom simulation; and broader sociotechnical applications, such as game design, virtual environments, and human-computer interaction. A particularly direct use is personalization training, in which trainees interact with agents that differ in interpersonal behavior and learn to recognize and adapt to those differences. Such training is already being applied in medical education 73 , where simulations train adaptive communication with patients of different interpersonal profiles, and similar uses look promising in psychotherapy training and other helping professions 74.

In sum, LASSI is a framework for simulating social interactions with LLM-based agents grounded in CIIT, in which each agent updates its interpersonal behavior in response to the perceived behavior of its partner. Its formal model specifies how complementarity, behavioral defaults, and interpersonal reactivities jointly shape behavior over time. Together this offers a bounded, transparent, and extensible foundation for formalizing interpersonal dynamics, and a basis for practical tools that simulate interpersonal behavior at scale.

Formal model specification

We propose a formal model of social interactions that steers how the interaction unfolds in the LLM-based interpersonal simulations. Specifically, the model formalizes how the agent's interpersonal behavior is updated from turn to turn on the basis of its prior behavior, the partner's behavior, and the influence of behavioral defaults and interpersonal reactivities. We use numerical simulations to examine how well the model's parameters can be recovered from model-generated interaction data and how recovery depends on the amount of data, and whether the model produces expected interpersonal dynamics (i.e., flux, complementarity). All assumptions the model makes are listed in the section "Formal model of interpersonal behavior" in the Supplementary Methods. However, three deliberate modeling choices are worth stating upfront. First, the updating depends only on the prior state and the partner's most recent behavior rather than the full interaction history. This keeps the model tractable, but limits it to short-term interactions and neglects longer-term history and relationship formation. Second, behavior lives on a finite grid. This makes the dynamics explicit, easy to inspect, and implementable in the LLM-based simulations with our dynamic prompting approach, at the cost of limiting granularity. Third, as implied by CIIT, agency and communion are modeled separately, but empirical work suggests that they can covary within individuals during social interactions 16, 72.

Behavioral defaults modeling

We conceptualized behavioral defaults as the baseline behavioral level with which individuals enter social interactions and toward which their behavior tends to gravitate in the absence of strong signals from the interaction partner. To represent this, we draw on dynamic systems approaches to personality and behavior, particularly models of attractor dynamics 40, 41 . In our model each agent has a baseline repertoire, the behavioral repertoire it returns to without partner influence. The baseline repertoire is represented as a bell-shaped probability distribution over the finite state grid, with its mean fixed at the agent's behavioral default (BD) and its width governed by τ. Thus, the BD defines the baseline to which the person's behavior returns, for example, more dominant than submissive, whereas τ determines how widely the person varies around this baseline. Without partner influence (i.e., at IR = 0), the agent's expected state relaxes back toward its BD across the unfolding interaction (i.e., an attractor tendency) 40, 41, 75.

Formal modeling summary

At each turn, the model updates an agent's expected interpersonal state in two steps. First, it combines the agent's BD with the partner-implied target according to |IR| to derive a desired state (equations ( 3) and ( 4)). Second, the agent moves only part of the way toward this state, with the size of that adjustment determined by the relaxation rate λ (equation ( 5)). The agent's baseline repertoire is then exponentially tilted to the updated mean, yielding its current behavioral repertoire while keeping the same width parameter τ. Each agent is therefore described on each interpersonal dimension by four parameters: its behavioral default (BD), interpersonal reactivity (IR), repertoire width (τ), and relaxation rate (λ).

Simulation procedure

In the following simulations, we addressed two questions: whether the model reproduces two expected interpersonal dynamics (flux as an individual-level dynamic, and complementarity as a dyad-level dynamic), and whether the model's parameters can be recovered from model-generated interaction data and how recovery changes with the amount of data. The simulations evaluated the formal model on its own, without any LLM or human interaction data, allowing its behavior to be examined separately from the surface realism of generated language.

For the simulation of interpersonal dynamics, all runs used a master seed of 42 and a seven-point grid from -3 to +3. Unless otherwise stated, λ was set to 0.5 and τ to 1. We chose 0.5 to balance adjustment toward the desired state with the turn-to-turn inertia that interpersonal behavior shows 45 . At λ = 1, the agent's next state would be independent of its previous state, whereas at small λ, it would adjust only slightly. A sweep across relaxation rates showed that the model behaves similarly at different rates and that no rate is preferable on internal grounds (see Supplementary Table S10). λ sets the scale of the reported complementarity coefficient, which approximates λ × IR (Supplementary Methods). We then ran the evaluated formal model inside the full LASSI framework, following the dyadic interaction cycle described above. The formal model steered each agent's interpersonal behavior over time, the generator translated the current interpersonal state into behavior, and the partner's assessor scored the resulting message on both dimensions. Formal model specification

State space

Interpersonal behavior on one dimension takes one of a finite number of values. Specifically, the state grid is symmetric and centered on zero: 𝑆 = $-(𝑁 -1) 2 , … , + (𝑁 -1) 2.

with 𝑁 odd, so that the grid has an exact midpoint 𝑠̄ = 0. All work reported here uses 𝑁 = 7, so the states run from -3 to +3. Thus, we explicitly constrain the range of interpersonal behavior an agent can express.

An agent's behavior at turn t is a probability distribution over the grid (the behavioral repertoire), and the behavior the agent expresses is one draw from this repertoire. This is the model's central representational choice because it separates two aspects that a single value cannot capture: the level of a person's behavior, and how widely it varies around that level. Accordingly, two agents with the same expected state can differ in how consistently their behavior stays near it, as determined by the width of the distribution.

Behavioral defaults modeling

The baseline repertoire is the distribution an agent falls back on when the partner exerts no influence. It is characterized by two of the four person parameters: the behavioral default BD, which is the baseline the person returns to without partner influence, and the repertoire width τ, which is how widely the person varies around it.

On a finite grid, however, the tail is truncated near the scale boundary, pulling the mean inward. The repertoire width (τ) is the second person parameter and determines how widely behavior varies around the expected state. The baseline repertoire is then re-tilted to have this new mean, and the behavior he expresses is a draw from the updated behavioral repertoire.

From an expected state to a distribution

The update determines where the mean of the repertoire should be, but it does not determine how the remaining probability mass should be distributed. To define the full repertoire, the model therefore selects, among all distributions with the required mean, the one that is closest to the agent's baseline repertoire in terms of Kullback-Leibler divergence:

This is the information projection of the baseline repertoire onto the mean constraint (Csiszár, 1975). The baseline repertoire is altered only as much as necessary to achieve the required mean.

Closed form

Specifically, the projection is given by an exponential tilt of the baseline repertoire: with the normalizer and the tilt fixed by the mean constraint.

Because this is a one-parameter exponential family, the derivative of the tilted mean is the tilted variance:

The tilted mean is therefore strictly increasing in the tilt, and it runs from the lower end of the grid to the upper end as the tilt runs over the real line. Thus, it is a bijection onto the open interval between the two ends, every strictly interior target mean is attained exactly at one finite tilt, and no approximation enters. At either end the only distribution with that mean is the point mass there, and that point mass is the projection. In simple terms, the model can therefore move the repertoire exactly to any mean within the scale with a unique amount of tilting.

We checked this guarantee; over the full set of simulations, across 1,272 scored cells, the largest difference between the realized mean and the intended mean was 1.0 × 10⁻¹², the tolerance within which a target counts as lying on a scale end; all other cells stayed below 1 × 10⁻¹³. Tilting moves the mean by moving probability mass, and near a scale end there is less room to move it into. The consequence is that the realized spread of expressed behavior falls as an agent is pushed toward an end, at a fixed τ.

The two quantities are therefore worth keeping apart when the parameters are read off a figure. The width τ is a property of the person and does not change within an interaction. This is a consequence of bounded behavior: as a person approaches the extreme of the scale, there is less room for variation, and the model captures this.

Choice of the base measure

For interior repertoires, projecting onto the baseline repertoire or onto the previous turn's behavioral repertoire gives the same answer, which we verified to within 1 × 10⁻¹³ over 200 random five-step chains. The tilted family is closed under tilting and the mean fixes the tilt uniquely, so the two routes coincide. However, they differ in exactly one place. After an agent has reached a scale end its distribution is a point mass, and the divergence from a point mass is infinite for every other distribution, so the second version would be trapped there for good. We therefore project onto the baseline repertoire.

Numerical solution

The tilt is found by safeguarded Newton iteration on the mean constraint, using the derivative identity above, with the bracket found by doubling outward from zero and a tolerance of 1 × 10⁻¹³ in grid units. No fixed bracket is used. The tilt required to move a baseline repertoire from its default to a given mean grows with 1/τ², without bound as τ shrinks, so any constant bracket silently rejects legitimate interior targets at some τ. Below τ ≈ 0.11 on a unit-step grid the bracket search can reject an interior target, and below τ ≈ 0.08 a large share of targets is rejected; all reported work uses τ ≥ 0.25. A BD exactly at a scale endpoint is excluded because it would make the baseline repertoire a point mass. As the baseline repertoire serves as the reference distribution for all subsequent projections, this would prevent the agent from moving away from that endpoint.

The search

We maximized the likelihood with a bounded quasi-Newton method. The expected state depends on reactivity through its magnitude |IR| and its direction d = sign(IR), so the derivative changes discontinuously at zero. Accordingly, we ran the search separately on each side of zero and kept the better optimum, with four random starting points per side, within bounds of -2.8 to +2.8 for BD, -1 to +1 for IR, 0.25 to 4 for τ and 0.05 to 1 for λ. Allowing the search to cross zero can otherwise cause the optimizer to stall or converge to the wrong branch.

How complementarity is measured is a separate choice from how the model generates behavior. The measure reported in the main text is the Level-1 equation of Kurzius et al. Predictors are centered on the person's own mean, as in the source. The first 20 of 60 exchanges were discarded as warm-up, coefficients were averaged over the 20 replicate dyads of a cell, and 157 of 48,400 fits whose design had a condition number above 10⁸ were dropped. Kurzius et al. had observers code behavior in 10-s intervals of dyadic interactions, which is close to the turn-by-turn states simulated here. They also multiplied the status coefficients by -1, so that higher values mean more complementarity on both dimensions, which is what multiplying by m does here.

We deliberately deviated from Kurzius et al. (2022) in two ways: We fitted one focal agent in one dyad by ordinary least squares, because within a condition every replicate dyad has the same true parameters and there is no between-dyad variance for a random coefficient to describe. The two partners of a dyad are also distinguishable by design, because each partner's parameters are set separately, so the pairwise indistinguishable structure of the source does not apply. We fitted each agent as the focal partner in turn instead.

Composite behavioral repertoire update

The agent first determines the state it would move to if it adjusted fully, weighting T against BD according to the magnitude |IR| of its reactivity:

With |IR| bounded between 0 and 1, this is a weighted average that always falls between BD and T. Interpersonal behavior is also autocorrelated from one moment to the next, so the expected state moves only part of the way toward that location, at the relaxation rate λ: 𝜇 -= (1 -𝜆)𝜇 -#4 + 𝜆 𝑞 - A small λ means strong inertia and slow adjustment. A rate of 1 would move the expected state fully to 𝑞 - each turn, so that it would no longer depend on the previous expected state. Combining these two steps gives the full update:

The three weights are (1 -𝜆), 𝜆(1 -|IR|), and 𝜆|IR|. They are nonnegative and they sum to one, so the updated expected state is a convex combination of the previous state, the default, and the target.

In plain terms, a convex combination is a weighted average whose weights are shares of one whole. At each turn the agent's expected state is therefore divided between three pulls, and the three shares always add up to one. The first share is inertia, that is, where the agent already was. The second is the pull back toward the agent's own behavioral default. The third is the pull toward the partner-implied target. This has two consequences: First, the expected state can never leave the range spanned by those three locations, so no rule is needed to keep behavior on the scale. Second, the parameters read directly as competing influences on one turn: a highly reactive person gives most of its adjustable share to the partner, a barely reactive person keeps that share for its own default, and a person with a low relaxation rate gives most of the turn to where it already was.

Behavior cannot be clipped

The updated expected state is a convex combination of three locations that all lie within the range of the grid, so it cannot leave that range. The simulations would have stopped on any excursion of more than 1 × 10⁻¹² outside the grid, and none did. The guarantee rests on two conditions that hold throughout this study and are worth stating, because they are properties of the scale. The complementarity direction m must be -1 or +1, and the scale midpoint s̄ must be the midpoint of the symmetric grid. Move the midpoint off center and the target can leave the grid: at a midpoint of +1 on the -3 to +3 grid, a partner at -3 implies a target of +5 for a complementary responder on agency.

Zero reactivity reproduces the baseline repertoire

At zero reactivity the update becomes 𝜇 -= (1 -𝜆)𝜇 -#4 + 𝜆BD. The behavioral default is the unique fixed point and the expected state returns to it geometrically at rate 1 -λ. For any λ, an agent that starts from its own baseline repertoire therefore never moves at all.

Level and breadth are separate parameters

At a fixed interior expected state, the width sets the spread and nothing else does: τ of 0.6, 1.0 and 1.5 gives realized standard deviations of 0.59, 1.00 and 1.41 at the midpoint of the scale. The narrowing described in Supplementary Table S9 is a separate effect and applies to position, not to the width parameter.

Summary

At each turn the model forms the partner-implied target from the partner's last behavior, blends it with the agent's own behavioral default (BD) in proportion to the magnitude of the interpersonal reactivity (IR), and moves the expected state part of the way toward that blend at the relaxation rate λ. It then tilts the agent's baseline repertoire to the new mean, retaining as much of its characteristic shape as possible. By construction, the realized mean equals the intended mean and behavior remains within the scale.

Simultaneous complementarity and behavioral stability

The simultaneous coefficient is essentially zero at every relaxation rate. In the simulations, both agents express a state and then update simultaneously. Each agent therefore responds only to the partner's previous expressed state. Conditional on the interaction history, the two same-turn draws are independent, and the mean dimension-aligned simultaneous coefficient across 1,210 scored cells was +0.0013. Under this update schedule, the model therefore produces essentially no simultaneous complementarity. This is an important limitation, because real interaction data can show simultaneous complementarity within short coding intervals; Kurzius et al. (2022) found it strongest within the same 10-s interval. The model represents such mutual adjustment across separate turns.

In the population regression on the latent expected states, the stability coefficient equals one minus the relaxation rate. For expressed states, it is strongly attenuated because the previous expressed state is only a noisy proxy for the previous latent mean (Supplementary Figs. S21-S24). At λ = 0.5, the mean stability coefficient was +0.036 for expressed states and +0.437 for latent expected states. The latter falls below 0.5 because the estimated coefficient on a lagged outcome is biased downward in short series (Marriott & Pope, 1954). The cross-lagged complementarity coefficient is less affected because its predictor is the partner's expressed state, which is the quantity the model directly responds to. Thus, a small stability coefficient in observed behavioral codes does not necessarily imply little underlying inertia.

Further complementarity measures

We computed two alternative complementarity scores on the expressed states and report them in Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3. Supplementary Table S4 gives their rank correlations with the cross-lagged coefficient from the main analysis across the reactivity plane. The first is the one-dimensional version of the distance score proposed by Woods and Wright (2024), defined as the negative distance from the complementary target and therefore bounded above by zero. The second is the correlation between an agent's state and the same target. It is signed, with +1 indicating perfect complementarity, 0 no association, and -1 perfect anti-complementarity. We report the correlation alongside the distance score because the distance score alone cannot separate an anti-complementary dyad from a weakly complementary one. Both scores compare an agent's expressed states with the complementary target, m times the partner's state, which is the same for every agent. An anti-complementary agent therefore receives a low score. Comparing each agent with its own partner-implied target T, which carries the agent's own sign d, would give every agent a high score, whatever its response direction.

Summary

At λ = 1, the model's update implies the Level-1 equation of Kurzius et al. (2022) exactly in the population regression, with 𝑏4 = 𝑏 ' = 0 and a dimension-aligned cross-lagged coefficient equal to IR. Below 1, the coefficient in the update is λ × IR. In the simulations, the fitted coefficients closely matched the expected value, with slopes ranging from 0.489 to 0.503 at λ = 0.5. The small deviations reflect sampling variability and the use of the previous expressed state instead of the latent state.

Scope and sources

This section documents the configuration of the two LLM-based agents used in the manuscript, Agent-A and Agent-B. It reproduces the assessor and generator prompts verbatim and lists the settings and parameters that govern the agents' behavior. The mathematical model behind those parameters, including its notation and derivation, is given in the section "Formal model of interpersonal behavior". The formal model represents interpersonal states on a centered grid from -3 to +3. For communication with the LLM components or for display, these internal states can be mapped onto equivalent alternative codings. In the generator, the seven states are represented by an index-based mapping from 0 for the lowest state to 6 for the highest state, with each index linked to a named behavioral anchor. The assessor instead returns an integer from 1 to 7. For reporting in the main text, both are mapped back onto the centered -3 to +3 grid (generator index minus 3; assessor score minus 4). From the second turn onward, both agents' assessors score each message; the update uses the partner's score, and the assessed scores shown in the main text are averaged across both assessors.

Everything below is extracted from the configuration files that were used to run the agents. Prompts are quoted unchanged. Headings, explanations and table captions are editorial.

The prompts are not stored as finished texts. They are assembled at runtime from named blocks, in the order given by the prompt_creation_order field of the respective component. This modular structure keeps the source files compact by avoiding repeated prompt text and makes the system flexible, as study-wide, agentspecific, and parameter-specific elements can be changed independently without rewriting complete prompts. Three levels of configuration contribute: • config.yaml at the study level holds the assessor configuration in full, the study-wide generator blocks, the two personality axes with their rule blocks and state contents, and the language model settings.

• config.yaml inside each agent folder holds the agent-specific generator blocks (description, priority rules, context, biography, situation) and the configuration of the feedback shown to a participant after an interaction.

• One template file per agent folder holds the three free parameters per axis: the behavioral default, the interpersonal reactivity and the repertoire width.

Agent-A is the agent configured in the folder "Bar David Meyer" and is called David Meyer in its own prompts; Agent-B is the folder "Bar John Doe" and is called John Doe. The two agents share one scenario, a first meeting in a bar, and differ in the three parameters per axis and in the five agent-specific generator blocks (Supplementary Table S1). The situation block is mirrored: David Meyer has only just been seated, whereas John Doe has been in the bar for a while and already has a drink.

In the rendering below, the blocks are shown in their assembly order and separated by line breaks. The configuration files specify the order and content of the blocks, but not the exact whitespace between them. The anchor representing an agent's current interpersonal state is inserted dynamically at runtime from the state contents defined for the personality axes. The assembled prompts shown below therefore include the nine fixed blocks in their assembly order but omit this dynamic state anchor.
