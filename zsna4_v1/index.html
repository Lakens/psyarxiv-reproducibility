How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Adolescent mental disorders affect approximately 15% of young people worldwide with a stark rise in mental health difficulties in secondary school age children over the past decade. Need for psychological support exceeds available provision and is exacerbated by staffing shortages. Schools are key settings for mental health and wellbeing (MHWB) support. This umbrella review synthesises evidence on school-based MHWB interventions in secondary schools, aiming to map and evaluate outcomes and intervention approaches on a tiered framework.

Following PRISMA guidelines, a systematic search of reviews on school-based MHWB interventions was conducted. Data were extracted on intervention type, delivery mode, and outcomes. Reviews including broader age ranges (4-25 years) were considered, with a specific focus on adolescents.

After screening 478 studies, 33 reviews published between 2014 and 2024 were included, covering programmes for adolescents aged 12-18 years. Social and emotional learning (SEL) programmes were most effective when implemented as whole-school approaches. Targeted interventions produced the largest gains for high-risk students, while universal programmes were more common in younger age groups. Preventive interventions delivered by specialists showed stronger effects, whereas universal programmes were more effective when delivered by school staff. Findings support a stepped-care model aligning MHWB support to student need. However, despite policy recommendations, implementation remains inconsistent.

Effective MHWB provision in schools requires culturally responsive, developmentally appropriate, and scalable approaches. A tiered framework matching support to student need is recommended. Future research should explore digital delivery, scalability, and sustainability of school-based MHWB initiatives.

Introduction

Within the education sector, a high proportion of post-primary schools face significant challenges in supporting student mental health and wellbeing (MHWB). Between 2009 and 2019, recorded common mental disorders (CMD) increased by 9.90%, while self-reported psychological distress rose more sharply by 19.33%, with the greatest increases seen among older adolescents (16-19 years) and those born after 1995, with symptom increases similar across genders but with substantially more recorded CMD rise in males (20.61%) than females (7.65%) (Dykxhoorn et al., 2025). Now more than ever, intervention is needed, and with funding shortages, these supports often come from charitable or private organisations.

Globally, 13% of YP have a MH diagnosis (Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health & UNICEF, 2022), with suicide as a leading cause of death among adolescents (Curtin & Garnett, 2023). In the UK, one in six YP face mental health (MH) challenges (Newlove-Delgado et al., 2023), with up to 50% of MH disorders emerging by age 14 (Kessler et al., 2005). Up to 75% of YP with MH concerns do not seek professional help (Calear et al., 2017), compounded by a global shortage of MH professionals when they do (Grande et al., 2023). MHWB challenges have also been intensified by the COVID-19 pandemic, accentuating the potential of schools in promoting MHWB (Montero-Marin et al., 2023; Butler et al., 2022).

Mental illness often peaks around age 15, with up to 75% of cases beginning before age 25, highlighting the importance of early intervention (Psychiatry, 2024). Young people with MH difficulties are at greater risk of poor academic performance, behavioural issues, absenteeism, suspension, and school failure (Reinke et al., 2020; Wigelsworth et al., 2017). Long-term consequences may include involvement with the legal system, unemployment, and reliance on social benefits (Aebi et al., 2014; Cefai et al., 2018), with lasting effects on physical and psychological wellbeing, productivity, and life satisfaction (Currie & Stabile, 2009).

In recent decades, global and regional attention to YP's MHWB has grown, with increasing efforts to understand and address these challenges. Globally, around 13% of 10-to 19-yearolds have a MH diagnosis (Keeley, 2021), and one in seven experience a MH disorder, with depression, anxiety, and behavioural issues among the leading causes of illness. Suicide is the third leading cause of death among 15-to 29-year-olds (WHO, 2024). In the UK, one in six young people experience MH difficulties, up from one in nine in 2017 (NHS, 2020), and 31% of 16-to 24-year-olds report symptoms of depression or anxiety, a 6% increase (Office for National Statistics, 2020).

According to Mind (2021), 96% of secondary school students in the UK said their MH affected their schoolwork, and 78% felt that school contributed to their difficulties. In Wales, over a quarter of young people reported not having MH support at school (Public Health Wales, 2019). In Northern Ireland, 12.6% of young people experience mood disorders, 25% higher than in England, with higher rates of anxiety and depression among adolescents (Bunting et al., 2020). In Ireland, MH disorders among young people are consistently higher than the EU average, with anxiety and depression accounting for nearly two-thirds of cases in 10-to 24-year-olds (Department of Children, Equality, Disability, Integration and Youth, 2023). Between 2019 and 2020, there was a 150% increase in young people receiving clinical psychology services, and a 50% rise in those identified as needing support.

Charities play an increasingly important role in supporting young people (YP) in schools by delivering evidence-based, stepped-care wellbeing programmes that promote early identification and intervention (mdeducational foundation, 2025). This preventative approach aligns with UK government priorities to expand early mental health support and reduce reliance on specialist services, as outlined in Transforming Children and Young People's Mental Health Provision (DoH, 2017) and the NHS Long Term Plan (NHS, 2019). By strengthening emotional resilience and coping skills at a universal and targeted level, charitable interventions may help prevent escalation to crisis-level need, thereby reducing referrals to intensive services such as Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services (CAMHS) and alleviating pressure on an overstretched NHS, while serving the wider public interest in young people's mental health and wellbeing (MHWB). This paper presents an international overview of youth mental health and wellbeing (MHWB) through an umbrella review of school-based interventions aimed at preventing mental health difficulties and promoting wellbeing. International evidence is synthesised to identify transferable, evidence-informed strategies that can inform researchers and practitioners working within secondary school settings in the UK and Ireland, where comparable education systems and policy priorities emphasise early intervention and prevention. The review therefore supports the integration and sustainable implementation of MHWB practices within these national contexts.

Despite the growing need, development and implementation of mental health and wellbeing (MHWB) strategies have largely focused on early years and primary school provision, with over 80% of programs targeting 4-to 12-year-olds (Cipriano et al., 2023; Durlak et al., 2011), highlighting a lag in innovation and provision for secondary school-aged pupils. Schools are ideally placed to deliver MHWB programs, fostering resilience, academic success, and prosocial behaviours (Durlak et al., 2011), and frameworks such as CASEL provide structured guidance for universal social-emotional (SE) learning, demonstrating medium-to-large positive effects on wellbeing, academic outcomes, and reductions in mood disorders and behavioural difficulties (Barry et al., 2017; Cipriano et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2017; CASEL, 2024). While there is global consensus on the importance of MHWB in schools, the specific policies and their implementation vary significantly across regions. International organisations such as the UN, UNICEF, and WHO provide overarching frameworks, while regional bodies, including the EU, and national governments develop tailored policies to address unique educational contexts (Margaretha et al., 2023). However, the efficacy of school-based interventions is mixed, and poorly targeted programs may inadvertently increase distress (Harvey et al., 2023; Stallard et al., 2013), underscoring the need for individualised approaches.

Preventative mental health programs in secondary schools, primarily grounded in cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), show small but positive effects (Caldwell et al., 2019; Dray et al., 2017), though comparative studies using other approaches are limited. Universal and indicated interventions produce similar short-term outcomes, with indicated programs demonstrating longer-term benefits (Corrieri et al., 2014; Werner-Seidler et al., 2017). Brief, targeted interventions addressing specific difficulties and skills are effective for adolescents (McDanal et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Schleider & Weisz, 2017) and across delivery modes (Dobias et al., 2021). Qualified facilitators enhance the impact of targeted MH interventions, while school staff achieve better outcomes delivering universal SE programs (Cohen et al., 2024; Cipriano et al., 2023c). To mitigate risk and optimise outcomes, an individualised stepped-care approach is recommended, distinguishing universal wellbeing promotion from selective and indicated mental health support based on student need (Neil & Christensen, 2009; Knight & Samuel, 2022). The highest tier involves referral to external organisations able to provide intensive, specialist interventions, ensuring students receive the right level of support at the right time while preventing unnecessary escalation. A multilevel, stepped-care model is therefore considered optimal, combining universal SE skill promotion with higher-intensity interventions for students requiring additional support (Caldwell et al., 2019).

The specific aims of this paper are to:

Examine the current state of mental health and wellbeing (MHWB) among adolescents within the broader mental health landscape;  Synthesise global perspectives on MHWB from policymakers, researchers, educators, practitioners, and private-sector stakeholders;  Review the literature on school-based MHWB interventions, outlining key strengths, limitations, and implementation challenges;  Identify core characteristics, implementation requirements, and evaluation strategies necessary for effective programme development and rollout, such as an evidenceinformed theoretical foundation, developmentally appropriate design, and a steppedcare structure; and  Inform recommendations for future research and practice in school-based MHWB.

This report provides a global evidence base using an umbrella review methodology (Belbasis et al., 2022), outlining how information was gathered, the current evidence on MHWB in school-aged children, its relevance to secondary school pupils, key insights into youth challenges, and how SE skills address these, concluding with actionable recommendations for stakeholders.

Defining Mental Health and Wellbeing

To address MHWB, it is important to understand its meaning. While definitions vary, the WHO describes health as "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity" (WHO, 1948), and mental health as "a state of wellbeing in which every individual realizes his or her own potential, can cope with normal stresses, work productively, and contribute to their community" (WHO, 2004). Schools are well-positioned to promote MHWB, with most young people attending and school environments exerting a stronger influence on development than any other institution, second only to the home (Reupert, 2019). Schools also provide structures that support development outside clinical settings (Domitrovich et al., 2010), allowing access to large numbers of youth at lower levels of need and those unlikely to seek clinical support. Social and emotional (SE) skills in schools serve as a protective mechanism and a low-level triage system, signposting those needing higher care to community or clinical services (Patalay et al., 2016).

Strong evidence shows that promoting social and emotional (SE) skills in YP can have longterm benefits for individuals, families, and communities, with wider impacts on educational, health, social, and economic outcomes when implemented effectively (Cipriano et al., 2023a; Clarke et al., 2015; Durlak et al., 2011; OECD, 2015; Weare & Nind, 2011). Research indicates that focusing on SE skill promotion may yield more lasting effects than interventions targeting prevention or reduction of negative outcomes (Barry & Dowling, 2015; Cipriano et al., 2023a Cipriano et al., , 2023c;; Durlak et al., 2011 Durlak et al., , 2022a;; Taylor et al., 2017). SE skills learning is defined as "the process through which children and adults acquire and effectively apply the knowledge, attitudes, and skills necessary to understand and manage emotions, set and achieve positive goals, feel and show empathy, establish and maintain positive relationships, and make responsible decisions" (CASEL, 2024).

Early US research on school-based social and emotional (SE) programs led to the establishment of the Collaborative for Social and Emotional Learning (CASEL) 30 years ago (CASEL, 2020). SE education is now internationally recognised as essential for promoting youth MHWB and enabling young people to reach their potential, contributing to long-term social, economic, and societal development (CASEL, 2024). SE learning (SEL) is a framework integrating social, emotional, and cognitive dimensions (Jones & Bouffard, 2012), which has evolved through research and practice, often structured around CASEL's five domains: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making (Durlak et al., 2023). Twelve major components have been identified to categorise skills and the environments in which SEL is most effective (Jones et al., 2021).

Policies and Frameworks

School-based MHWB provision is guided globally by international, regional, and national frameworks integrating MH promotion into education.

Internationally, the UN, UNICEF, WHO, and UNESCO emphasize MH in schools through global strategies and frameworks that advocate early intervention, whole-school approaches, and integration of social and emotional learning (Margaretha et al., 2023; UNESCO, 2018; UNICEF, 2022; WHO, 2021). Regionally, the EU and Council of Europe promote MH as a core educational priority, encouraging curriculum integration, staff training, and anti-bullying initiatives (Cefai et al., 2018; Grimonprez, 2020). Collectively, these frameworks reflect a continuum from universal principles to locally specific implementation, underscoring the need for contextually adaptive, equitable MHWB policies. Globally, more than 136 social and emotional learning frameworks organize competencies critical to holistic development (Berg et al., 2017; Jones et al., 2019) (see Figure 1). Jones et al. (2019) Brush et al. (2022) examined ten major global SEL frameworks (e.g., CASEL, EU, UNICEF, UN, World Bank, UNESCO) and found strong alignment with the six competency domains identified by Jones et al. (2019). However, Berg et al. (2017) noted that fewer than 10% of the 136 frameworks address developmental sequencing of competencies, highlighting a gap in age-specific research and application.

The US are the forerunners in provision and development of SE Skills for promotion of MH in schools with the predominance of the CASEL framework, which is increasingly being adopted worldwide (Barry et al., 2017; Berger et al., 2022; Cefai et al., 2018; Cipriano et al., 2023). Reviews and meta-analyses show that SE skills programs in schools produce medium to large positive effects on social, emotional, and psychosocial health (Cipriano et al., 2023; Durlak et al., 2011; Taylor et al., 2017). SE skills promote prosocial behaviour, improved MH, academic achievement, and decreases anxiety, depression, violence, substance use, and behavioural difficulties in YP (Clarke et al., 2015; Durlak et al., 2011; Korpershoek et al., 2016; Sklad eta al., 2012; van de Sande et al., 2019). Universal SE Skills promotion shows greater long-term benefit than approaches focussed on reducing negative outcomes (Barry et al., 2019; Durlak et al., 2011; Weare & Nind, 2011).

Studies involving elementary-school children (approximately 5-11 years) accounted for approximately 56%-57% of the studies reviewed by Durlak et al. (2011) and Cipriano et al. (2023c), compared with only 13% and 18.6% involving high-school pupils (approximately 14-18 years), respectively. With approximately half of MH problems developing before age 14 (Cefai et al., 2018), sustained support into adolescence is essential. Older adolescents' comparatively limited representation strengthens the case for developmentally appropriate MH provision throughout secondary education.

Methods

This study conducted a systematic search of meta-analyses, reviews of reviews, and systematic reviews (2014-2024) on the effectiveness of school-based interventions for young people's MHWB in secondary schools. An umbrella review was used to synthesize and compare this extensive evidence base, offering a high-level overview to inform practice, policy, and future research. This study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF): https://osf.io/s78ud/overview?view_only=0325d631d5524994a61a01094dbb6b50 Due to majority of studies focussing across primary and secondary schools, an age range of 4 to 25 years was used to improve catchment, evident from descriptives of papers included in the study (see Table 2). Four databases were chosen for searching academic sources, Medline, CINAHL, Scopus, ERIC, spanning January 2014 to June 2024. The PICO search strategy was used for selecting search terms (Richardson et al., 1995) and grouped into three main conceptual clusters: population, intervention, and outcomes (see Appendix 1).

The search process followed a clear and thorough approach to identifying relevant sources. It was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) protocol (Page et al., 2021) (see Figure 2). Grey literature was also included by reviewing websites of private organisations, government sources, and reference lists from selected studies (see Figure 2). Duplicate records were removed using RefWorks, and abstracts were screened for relevance. Studies that did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria were excluded (see Table 1).

The final selection of studies was based on several criteria: relevance to the review's focus, use of a strong search strategy, assessment of study quality, clear inclusion and exclusion criteria, a defined research question, and enough detail for replication. These criteria were adapted from the Education Endowment Foundation (2024) and Weare and Nind (2011). In total, 33 studies were included in the review (see Table 2).

Results

Overview

Table 2 summarises the characteristics of the studies included in this review, highlighting the diversity of intervention types, participant populations, and outcome measures, which provides context for interpreting the effectiveness and applicability of school-based mental health and wellbeing programmes. The included articles were synthesised through exploration of broader concepts, theories, and intervention types (see Appendix 3). Meta-analyses and systematic reviews consistently demonstrate that school-based (MHWB) interventions, both universal and targeted, are associated with improvements in adolescents' SE skills, prosocial behaviour, and academic engagement, alongside modest reductions in emotional and behavioural difficulties. Effect sizes are typically small to moderate and vary according to implementation fidelity, facilitator training, and the degree of integration within school systems (Barry & Dowling, 2015; Berger et al., 2022; Cipriano et al., 2023; Durlak et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2017; Wigelsworth et al., 2019).

Universal SEL and Whole-School Approaches

Whole-school and curriculum-embedded SEL programmes demonstrate stronger and more consistent outcomes than one-off or stand-alone sessions, producing reliable gains in SE skills and academic outcomes among students aged 11-18 years (Goldberg et al., 2019; Taylor et al., 2017; Van de Sande et al., 2019). Programmes aligned with SAFE principles: sequenced, active, focused, and explicit, show enhanced effectiveness, with quality of delivery, dosage, and teacher training acting as key moderators (Clarke et al., 2015; Goldberg et al., 2019).

Targeted CBT and Self-Regulation Interventions

Targeted CBT and self-regulation interventions demonstrate robust reductions in anxiety, depression, and behavioural symptoms among students identified as high risk (Das et al., 2016; Karukivi et al., 2021). In contrast, universal resilience programmes show smaller or mixed effects on symptom reduction, though they are associated with improvements in coping and adaptive skills (Dray et al., 2017; Pandey et al., 2018). Multi-component interventions that integrate CBT within broader SEL frameworks yield the most consistent benefits across outcomes (Belfield et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2023).

Mindfulness, Yoga, and Brief Interventions

Evidence for mindfulness-and yoga-based interventions is mixed. While modest improvements in stress regulation are reported, effects are often short-lived and constrained by limited duration or poor implementation fidelity (Bidik & Sisman, 2021; Pandey et al., 2018; Šouláková et al., 2019; Troy et al., 2022). Brief and single-session interventions show promise, with emerging evidence of short-term improvement; however, evidence for sustained impact remains limited (Cohen et al., 2024).

Mental Health Literacy and Resilience Programmes

Universal mental health literacy and resilience programmes is mixed. They have been shown to improve knowledge, coping strategies, and help-seeking behaviours, though rarely lead to sustained reductions in anxiety or depressive symptoms while longer-duration and multi-component interventions outperform brief or isolated lessons (Dray et al., 2017; Grande et al., 2023; Gunawardena et al., 2023; Skeen et al., 2019; Turner & MacKay, 2015).

Moderators of Effectiveness

Intervention effectiveness is strongly moderated by fidelity of implementation, facilitator expertise, and sufficient time allocation. Programmes supported by clear manuals, training, and monitoring procedures demonstrate larger and more reliable effects (Clarke et al., 2015; Durlak et al., 2023). High-risk students derive greater benefit from targeted CBT-based approaches, whereas universal SEL programmes contribute to population-level improvements in SE competencies and conduct (Das et al., 2016; Karukivi et al., 2021). Whole-school approaches show potential, but evidence of their superiority over classroom-based or isolated interventions remains inconclusive (Wigelsworth et al., 2022). Short-duration or poorly implemented programmes show limited effectiveness, with barriers including insufficient training, lack of institutional support, and timetable constraints (Takizawa et al., 2023; Goldberg et al., 2019; Troy et al., 2022).

Stepped-Care and Brief Intervention Models

Universal preventive MH programmes demonstrate small but consistent effects on anxiety, depression, and externalising behaviours (Caldwell et al., 2019; Dray et al., 2017). Most programmes are CBT-informed (Mackenzie & Williams, 2018), with limited evaluation of alternative modalities (Knight & Samuel, 2022). CBT-based programmes outperform non-CBT approaches, while universal and indicated interventions show comparable short-term benefits, however, indicated interventions yield stronger long-term outcomes (Corrieri et al., 2014; Werner-Seidler et al., 2017) while targeted interventions are generally more effective than universal approaches for reducing depression and stress (Feiss et al., 2019). Evidence supporting multilevel stepped-care models is mixed but suggests benefit when interventions are matched to level of need (Caldwell et al., 2019).

Stepped-care approaches combining universal SE skill promotion with targeted individual or group CBT interventions demonstrate optimal outcomes (Mareya et al., 2024; NICE, 2011). Brief interventions (BIs), delivered over a small number of sessions, show emerging promise, with up to 58% of adolescents reporting improvement in indicative presentations following a single session (Cohen et al., 2024; Schleider et al., 2020; Schleider & Weisz, 2017) while longer interventions may dilute effects (Öst & Ollendick, 2017). BIs appear effective across delivery modes, including online and in-person formats, for anxiety, depression, and behavioural problems (Dobias et al., 2021; Ito-Jaeger et al., 2022; McDanal et al., 2022; Schleider et al., 2022).

Frameworks and Programme Characteristics

The CASEL framework, comprising self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making, is the most extensively researched SEL framework (Durlak et al., 2011). Universal SE programmes are typically curriculum-based and employ interactive delivery methods such as group work, role-play, and multimedia (Bidik & Sisman, 2021; Pandey et al., 2018; Wigelsworth et al., 2020; Cipriano et al., 2023). Meta-analytic evidence indicates that 73.4% of programmes are sequenced and 86.3% prioritise intrapersonal skills, with associated improvements in school climate (g = 0.81) (Cipriano et al., 2023). SAFE aligned programmes demonstrate stronger outcomes and are endorsed by multinational organisations (Belfield et al., 2015; CASEL, 2020; Cefai et al., 2018; Durlak et al., 2011; Taylor et al., 2017; UNESCO; Wigelsworth et al., 2020 Wigelsworth et al., , 2022)). Effective implementation is characterised by clear theoretical grounding, facilitator training, fidelity monitoring, feedback mechanisms, and sustainable staffing models (Abry et al., 2015; Durlak, 2016; Jones & Kahn, 2017; Low et al., 2016; Weare & Nind, 2011). Evidence also suggests that programmes are most effective within their country of origin, highlighting the importance of cultural adaptation (Cefai et al., 2018; Clarke et al., 2015; Cipriano et al., 2023; Wigelsworth et al., 2016).

Discussion

This review underscores a crossroads in adolescent MHWB research and practice, highlighting the convergence of rising MHWB needs, technological advances, and the integration of psychological and medical approaches within education, evident by the plethora of intervention and prevention models brought to light by this review process and findings (see Appendix 3). The global rise in MHWB challenges and adolescent suicide rates, alongside service gaps and a shortage of trained professionals, emphasizes the urgent need for effective, school-based interventions. Schools are uniquely positioned to provide early support and integrating social and emotional (SE) learning into curricula fosters resilience, self-awareness, coping skills, and improvements in academic, behavioural, and long-term life outcomes (Barry et al., 2017; Cipriano et al., 2023).

International frameworks from WHO, UNICEF, and UNESCO provide overarching guidance, which regional bodies such as the EU and national governments adapt to local contexts (Margaretha et al., 2023). For example, the UK prioritizes whole-school approaches and early intervention, Ireland emphasises collaboration among educators, parents, and mental health professionals, and Northern Ireland, facing higher youth MH rates, has implemented targeted strategies through its Mental Health Strategy 2021-2031. Despite these differences, all regions acknowledge the central role of schools in supporting adolescent MHWB and the need to bridge the gap between policy and practice.

Developmental Timing and Context

Adolescence represents a period of heightened neurobiological, social, and emotional change, intersecting with academic pressures, identity development, and increased exposure to stressors (Blakemore, 2019; Donaldson et al., 2023; NICE, 2022). Transitional stages, particularly the move from primary to secondary education, present both risk and opportunity, making schools a critical context for early intervention and prevention (Barry et al., 2017; Harris & Nowland, 2021; Yeager et al., 2019). Evidence that SE skills are malleable and teachable supports the inclusion of structured SE learning within secondary education, particularly for young people exposed to adversity (Evans & Kim, 2013; INEE, 2010 INEE, , 2016;; Jones & Kahn, 2017).

Universal Versus Targeted Provision

Findings consistently show that universal SEL programmes improve population-level competencies and school climate, while targeted CBT-based interventions produce stronger effects for adolescents with elevated or emerging mental health difficulties (Das et al., 2016; Karukivi et al., 2021). This distinction underscores the limitations of relying on universal provision alone to address mental disorder and highlights the value of stepped-care models that differentiate promotion, prevention, and treatment (Neil & Christensen, 2009; Knight & Samuel, 2022). Evidence suggests that combining universal SE skill development with targeted and indicated interventions within a coherent framework optimises outcomes and resource use (Mareya et al., 2024; NICE, 2011).

Universal SE programmes promote foundational competencies and positive school climate, whereas targeted and indicated interventions, particularly CBT-based, are crucial for students with elevated mental health needs (Caldwell et al., 2019; Feiss et al., 2019; Cipriano et al., 2023). Brief interventions (BIs), including single-session formats, show promise in addressing acute or high-risk needs (Schleider et al., 2020; Cohen et al., 2024). School-based interventions targeting depression or anxiety showed larger effects when delivered by mental health professionals (Zhang et al., 2023). For universal SEL, teacher-led programmes produced greater improvements in school functioning than programmes delivered by non-school personnel (Cipriano et al., 2023c), although a Japanese meta-analysis found no significant overall difference in SEL outcomes by facilitator type (Takizawa et al., 2023).

In the context of a stepped care model, SE competencies fall in the broader landscape of health promotion which has a beneficial knock-on effect on prevention (Carroll et al., 2020) highlighting interrelated but different streams of care need. These findings highlight caution and a need for delineating programs into areas of stepped care (Downes & Cefai., 2016; Rampazzo et al., 2016), defining what is being targeted at which level of need and in which way (see Figure 3). development fosters resilience and prevention at the population level, while targeted and specialist interventions provide support for students with elevated needs. Implementation, Fidelity, and Workforce Considerations

Implementation quality emerged as a critical determinant of effectiveness. Programmes delivered with clear theoretical grounding, structured manuals, adequate dosage, and fidelity monitoring consistently outperform poorly implemented or short-term initiatives (Abry et al., 2015; Clarke et al., 2015; Durlak, 2016).

Facilitator expertise is equally important, mental health professionals achieve greater effects in targeted interventions, while teachers are particularly effective in delivering universal SE programmes (Zhang et al., 2024; Cipriano et al., 2023; Skryabina et al., 2016). These findings highlight the need for co-ordinated, tiered workforce models that align educational and mental health expertise (Ho et al., 2016).

Brief Interventions and Emerging Modalities

Brief interventions (BIs) offer a promising, scalable option within stepped-care systems, demonstrating rapid improvements for some adolescents across delivery formats, including digital and in-person modes (Cohen et al., 2024; Schleider et al., 2020; Schleider & Weisz, 2017). However, evidence for sustained impact remains limited, and longer programmes may dilute effects when poorly targeted (Öst & Ollendick, 2017).

Similarly, mindfulness and yoga-based approaches show mixed results, often constrained by weak implementation and short follow-up periods (Bidik & Sisman, 2021; Troy et al., 2022). These findings suggest that innovation should prioritise precision, engagement, and integration rather than programme length.

Duty of Care and Risk of Harm

A critical finding of this review is that indiscriminate implementation of MHWB programmes may be ineffective or harmful. Large-scale studies report null effects or short-term deterioration in symptoms following universal classroom-based interventions, including CBT and DBT-informed programmes (Harvey et al., 2023; Lendrum et al., 2013; Stallard et al., 2013). These outcomes raise ethical concerns regarding the misapplication of interventions and support the Prevalence Inflation Hypothesis, whereby normal distress may be pathologized through inappropriate screening or intervention (Foulkes & Andrews, 2023). Duty of care therefore necessitates careful matching of intervention type to student need, alongside robust referral pathways for specialist support.

Gaps in the Evidence Base

Despite growing evidence for the effectiveness of school-based social-emotional (SE) and mental health programmes, several key research gaps remain. Comparative studies across different contexts, programme components, and implementation strategies are limited, particularly regarding moderators such as duration, fidelity, and quality of delivery (Durlak et al., 2023; Dowling & Barry, 2020). Further investigation is needed into participant factors, including gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, baseline skills, and adaptations for diverse subgroups (Cipriano et al., 2023b; Rowe & Trickett, 2018; Fischer et al., 2024; Littlefair et al., 2024). There is also a lack of clarity on core SE components, longitudinal effects, and the use of logic models to guide programme design (Durlak et al., 2023; Berg et al., 2017). Measurement issues persist, including psychometric validity, cultural fit, and the comparison of self-report versus observer ratings (McKown, 2019; Campbell et al., 2016; Collie et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2022), as well as the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of online versus in-person assessment tools (Assessment Work Group, 2019; Müller et al., 2020). Equity concerns remain, with underrepresentation of marginalised groups and limited understanding of cultural influences on SE development (Jagers et al., 2019), alongside uncertainties regarding sustainability, fade-out effects, and long-term programme impact (van Aar et al., 2017; Bailey et al., 2020). The quality and impact of facilitator training, including online approaches, also require further evaluation (Oliveira et al., 2021; Combs et al., 2021). Additional gaps include rigor in reporting intervention context, participants, and outcomes to support meta-analyses and generalizability (Hennessy et al., 2022), clarification of mediators and moderators affecting effectiveness (Osher et al., 2020; Durlak et al., 2023), and consistent terminology for emotional and SE concepts. Emerging areas for exploration include the interplay between neurodevelopment and SE learning (Feiss et al., 2019), how facilitator SE competencies influence school climate and student outcomes (Jennings & Greenberg, 2009), and the integration of technology, including online programmes, digital games, blended teaching, and smartphone applications, for delivery, evaluation, and training (Erstad et al., 2024; Topping et al., 2022).

Recommendations

For policymakers: Integrating SE competencies into secondary school curricula, potentially as a mandatory part of education with changes to policies or funding priorities that would improve MH provision, particularly for adolescents.

Introduction of an integrated stepped care MHWB model as a joined-up MH care strategy. Introduction of a core wellbeing curriculum programme based on a stepped care model would improve MH provision and prioritises student wellbeing alongside academic achievement.

For schools and educators: implementing effective programs for adolescents, with a focus on MHWB by adopting SE universal, selective and indicative strategies, matching need with appropriate level of care towards an integrated support system. When selecting interventions, conducting a needs assessment to determine its target population (e.g., grade, types of students) and target outcomes (e.g., intra/interpersonal competencies). Consulting multiple stakeholders (e.g., teachers, students, parents) to understand their needs. Considering capacity (e.g., resources, practitioners' skills) when selecting an intervention. Providing adequate initial and ongoing training for facilitators. Monitoring outcomes for problem solving and future innovation.

For program developers: Designing SE content that is age and developmental stage appropriate. Specific to adolescents, giving attention to real-world challenges like stress, MH crises, and post-secondary transitions with recommendations on accessibility to MH services and making programs more appealing by utilising digital platforms.

For future research: Identify areas where further studies are needed, especially on the long-term impact of SE programs regarding the effectiveness of provision, economic and outcome measures with attention to equity, inclusion, and reliability.

Ventures incorporating multiple stakeholders such as the one involving mdeducational foundation charity, Pneuma Healthcare and Ulster University, are actively involving students and educators in all stages of research and development, from planning and delivery to monitoring and evaluation. Interventions are being developed to be flexible and age-appropriate, considering students' diverse backgrounds, including gender, culture, disability, and beliefs.

Limitations

Although this umbrella review provides a broad overview of school-based MHWB interventions for adolescents, it has some limitations. The quality and scope of the included systematic reviews and metaanalyses vary, which can affect how well findings can be compared or applied more widely. Many reviews did not explore how interventions work for different groups, such as by gender, socioeconomic status, or cultural background. Most studies came from high-income countries, especially the U.S., which may limit how relevant the findings are for the UK and Ireland. Also, relying mainly on published studies may introduce bias, as studies with negative or no results are less likely to be published. Finally, while grey literature was included, newer approaches, especially digital or hybrid models may not yet be fully represented in the evidence base.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that school-based MHWB programmes can positively influence adolescents' wellbeing, behaviour, and educational engagement when they are thoughtfully designed, developmentally appropriate, and well implemented. Universal SEL approaches are effective for promoting foundational competencies, improving school climate, and supporting population-level wellbeing. However, they are insufficient on their own to meet the needs of students experiencing elevated or emerging mental health difficulties. Targeted and indicated interventions, particularly those grounded in CBT, play a critical role in addressing higher levels of need. Collectively, the evidence strongly supports integrated stepped-care models that align intervention intensity with student need, safeguard against potential harm, and ensure clear referral and escalation pathways.

These findings emerge at a pivotal moment in research, policy, and practice, as rising adolescent MHWB needs, increasing suicide rates, workforce shortages, and service gaps converge with advances in digital delivery and growing integration of psychological and medical approaches within education. Schools are uniquely positioned to provide early identification, prevention, and support, and international organisations such as the WHO, UNICEF, and UNESCO have laid important foundations by promoting MHWB within educational systems. Regional and national adaptations across Europe, including the UK, Ireland, and Northern Ireland, further highlight both shared commitment and persistent challenges in translating policy into sustainable practice.

Importantly, effectiveness is not inherent to programme content alone. Outcomes depend critically on developmental fit, implementation fidelity, facilitator expertise, and systemic integration within schools.

Evidence of mixed or adverse effects from poorly targeted universal interventions reinforces the ethical imperative for evidence-informed, context-sensitive practice and careful delineation across tiers of care. Concerns regarding prevalence inflation and the inappropriate use of clinical tools in universal settings further underscore the need for age-appropriate screening, outcome measures, and co-constructed intervention design. Future progress requires a shift from the proliferation of programmes toward the refinement of systems. Strengthening implementation science, addressing equity and cultural adaptation, investing in workforce development, and generating robust long-term and real-world multi stakeholder evidence are essential. Embedding MHWB within secondary education through coordinated, multi-tiered stepped-care frameworks, integrating universal curricula with targeted, specialist, and digitally supported interventions, offers the most promising pathway for supporting adolescent mental health at scale while balancing promotion, prevention, and care.
