Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

We examined the dimensionality of four cognitive-linguistic skills (phonological awareness, morphological awareness, orthographic awareness, and vocabulary knowledge) among Chinese-English bilingual children (N = 1295, M age = 8.61, SD = 1.46). We tested five theoretically motivated models within each language and proficiency group (emerging vs. advanced bilinguals) to examine the measurement structure of these skills. Results revealed a common three-factor solution (phonological, orthographic, and morpho-lexical dimensions) in L1 Chinese across proficiency levels. In L2 English, a phonological-morphological dimension emerged for emerging bilinguals, while a morpho-lexical dimension emerged for advanced bilinguals, with an orthographic awareness dimension for both groups. Across both languages, morphological awareness and vocabulary consistently clustered into a shared morpho-lexical dimension in advanced bilinguals, reflecting increasing integration of cognitive-linguistic processes. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of bilingual literacy development and provide a developmental framework for understanding how cognitive-linguistic skills become increasingly coordinated across languages.

The Dimensionality of Cognitive-Linguistic Skills in Chinese-English Bilingual Children: A Developmental Perspective

Bilingual individuals engage in a dynamic process of simultaneously developing cognitive-linguistic skills related to phonology, orthography, and semantics, across languages throughout their lives (Grosjean, 2010). These skills are thought to underlie bilingual literacy development, yet their dimensionality remains uncertain. While studies have investigated the impacts of cognitive-linguistic skills on bilingual literacy (e.g., Deacon et al., 2009; McBride-Chang et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2009; Yeong & Rickard Liow, 2011), limited studies have thoroughly and systematically examined the dimensionality of these skills. Additionally, the relationship among cognitive-linguistic skills may be developmentally dynamic and may vary with language proficiency (Deacon et al., 2009; Perfetti & Hart, 2002; Tomblin & Zhang, 2006; Tong et al., 2009). However, few studies, to our knowledge, have adopted a developmental perspective to understand how these skills evolve as children develop bilingual language and literacy. To address this gap, in the present study we examined the developmental dimensionality of cognitive-linguistic skills in Chinese-English bilingual children with varying proficiency levels.

Dimensionality of Cognitive-Linguistic Skills

Reading is a complex process that relies on multiple cognitive-linguistic skills operating at different aspects, such as phonological awareness, morphological awareness, orthographic awareness, and vocabulary knowledge (e.g., Landerl et al., 2022; McBride et al., 2022). Previous studies have provided considerable insights into how these cognitivelinguistic skills contribute to reading ability in both Chinese and English (e.g., McBride-Chang et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2009; Yeong & Rickard Liow, 2011). Although these studies have established the importance of each of these skills in bilingual literacy, fewer have investigated their interrelationships, namely, whether they reflect distinct dimensions or a DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS smaller number of unified underlying constructs. Indeed, the few studies that have explored this question (e.g., Keenan et al., 2008; Lonigan & Burgess, 2017; Tomblin & Zhang, 2006) have primarily focused on the structure of reading skills per se rather than on their underlying cognitive-linguistic skills. While such research has advanced our understanding of reading skills as a multidimensional construct, it is equally important to investigate the dimensionality (underlying structure and interrelations) of the foundational cognitivelinguistic constructs. A clearer understanding of these skills is crucial to inform early, detailed assessment and intervention for bilingual children at risk of language and literacy difficulties (Huo et al., 2025).

Developmental research has long suggested that the latent organization of cognitivelinguistic skills may change over development, although existing findings have been mixed.

Some studies have reported developmental reorganization of cognitive-linguistic skills (e.g., Tong & McBride-Chang, 2010), whereas others have found relatively stable latent structures despite age-related improvements in performance (e.g., Lonigan et al., 2009). For example, Tong and McBride-Chang (2010) showed that the structure of phonological, morphological, orthographic, and sub-character processing in Chinese children underwent dynamic reorganization rather than simple age-related differentiation. They identified a print-nonprint distinction in kindergarten, a fully differentiated four-factor structure in Grade 2, and a more integrated configuration by Grade 5, in which morphological and orthographic skills clustered into a broad lexical-morpho-orthographic factor alongside a distinct phonological factor. This developmental pattern suggests a progression from coarse print sensitivity to increasingly differentiated metalinguistic skills, followed by higher-order integration of meaning-related processes. In contrast, in a study with English-speaking preschool children, Lonigan et al. (2009) reported that phonological awareness, memory, and lexical access formed distinct latent factors that remained largely invariant across the preschool years.

Although children's performance on these cognitive-linguistic skills improved substantially with age, their underlying latent organization remained largely stable.

It should be noted, however, that previous studies examining the dimensionality of reading and related skills have focused on monolingual children (Lonigan et al., 2009; Lonigan & Burgess, 2017; Tomblin & Zhang, 2006). Although a few studies have examined bilingual populations (e.g., Sun et al., 2018), they have primarily focused on cross-language structural correspondence rather than developmental changes in dimensionality across different stages of bilingual proficiency. This gap leaves an important question unanswered: Whether phonological awareness, morphological awareness, orthographic awareness, and vocabulary knowledge show greater differentiation or integration at different levels of bilingual proficiency, and whether their organization differs between children's first (L1) and second (L2) languages. The present study aimed to address this question by examining how these cognitive-linguistic skills are organized across proficiency groups in both the L1 and L2 of Chinese-English bilingual children in Hong Kong.

Integrated Dimensions Across Domains

To guide our modeling approach, we drew on theoretical accounts and empirical evidence that suggest how particular cognitive-linguistic skill domains cluster or diverge over the course of development (e.g., Deacon & Kirby, 2004; McBride-Chang et al., 2008). These frameworks motivate a set of alternative factor structures (dimensions) that reflect distinct hypotheses about the (re)organization of cognitive-linguistic skills (see Figure 1). We discuss each dimension separately below.

Orthographic-Phonological Dimension

Orthographic and phonological processing skills are often theorized to function as a distinct but intertwined mechanism. Within dual-route models of reading, word identification is proposed to rely on coordinated mappings between orthographic representations and their corresponding phonological codes (e.g., Grainger & Ziegler, 2011). In English, longitudinal studies have shown reciprocal relations between phonological sensitivity and letter-sound knowledge, suggesting interdependent development of the two systems (Burgess & Lonigan, 1998; Castles & Coltheart, 2004). Previous findings further indicate that orthographic information can influence performance even in tasks primarily targeting phonological processing (Rastle & Brysbaert, 2006). Similar mechanisms have also been proposed for Chinese. Chinese is a morphosyllabic writing system in which phonological information is often embedded within orthographic structures, particularly through phonetic radicals (Perfetti et al., 2005; Tong & McBride-Chang, 2010; Zhou & Marslen-Wilson, 2000). Consistent with the Lexical Constituency Model (Perfetti et al., 2005), Chinese reading involves the integrated processing of orthographic, phonological, and semantic representations rather than independent activation of separate codes. Research has shown that orthographic and phonological information are jointly activated during Chinese character recognition, with phonetic radicals providing cues for character pronunciation (Hsuan et al., 2018; Zhou & Marslen-Wilson, 2000). Zhang and Xing (2023) further demonstrated that orthography-phonology connections in Chinese are bidirectional but asymmetric, with stronger orthography-to-phonology activation than phonology-to-orthography activation.

Phonological-Morphological Dimension

A second theoretical prediction concerns the developmental coupling between phonological and morphological awareness (see Deacon & Kirby, 2004 for a relevant discussion). Across languages, sensitivity to phonological structure provides a foundation for identifying morphemic units and boundaries in spoken words (Kuo & Anderson, 2006; Ruan et al., 2018). Developmentally, children's ability to detect phonological patterns facilitates recognition of morphemic boundaries, while increased morphological knowledge further DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS supports analysis of complex word structures (Deacon & Kirby, 2004; Kuo & Anderson, 2006). Previous studies in English have shown that phonological awareness and morphological awareness are highly interrelated and jointly contribute to vocabulary development and literacy outcomes (e.g., Carlisle, 2000; Nagy et al., 2006). Intervention studies have further shown that integrated phonological-morphological training produces larger literacy gains than targeting either skill alone (Collins et al., 2020; Goodwin et al., 2017). These findings suggest a unified dimension capturing children's sensitivity to the phonological-morphological (sound-meaning) structure of English words.

In turn, Chinese is a morphosyllabic language in which syllables and morphemes are tightly aligned, with each typically encoded by a single written character. This structural correspondence means that phonological representations are closely connected with morphemic information, allowing early phonological (syllabic) sensitivity to support the development of morphological analysis (Pan et al., 2016). However, because Chinese contains a large number of homophones (e.g., 梨/li2/ 'pear' and 离/li2/ 'to leave,' or 鞋/xie2/ 'shoe' and 邪/xie2/ 'evil'), phonological information alone is often insufficient to distinguish lexical meanings, requiring children to integrate phonological and morphological cues during word recognition. Consistent with this interaction, empirical studies have shown that phonological and morphological awareness are highly correlated in Chinese children and jointly contribute to literacy development (e.g., Tong & McBride-Chang, 2010).

Morphological-Lexical Dimension

A third theoretical account emphasizes the interdependence between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge. Morphological awareness plays an increasingly central role in reading development across languages and writing systems, making its link with vocabulary progressively stronger as semantic demands rise (Deacon et al., 2014; Kieffer & Lesaux, 2012; McBride-Chang et al., 2008; Spencer et al., 2015). Morphological analysis also provides a powerful generative mechanism for vocabulary expansion by allowing learners to infer meanings from internal word structure (Crosson et al., 2021; Levesque et al., 2017). In English, children can infer new word meanings by identifying roots, affixes, and compounding structures, thereby expanding and refining their semantic representations (Carlisle, 2000; Carlisle & Katz, 2006). Several longitudinal studies have shown that morphological awareness predicts vocabulary gains after controlling for phonological skills and decoding (e.g., Sparks & Deacon, 2015). By late elementary school, the two skills become tightly linked, and their latent correlation approaches unity (Kieffer & Lesaux, 2012), consistent with the argument that reciprocal growth makes morphological awareness and vocabulary increasingly difficult to separate (Nagy, 2007). These findings suggest that morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge draw on a shared meaning-based system (Spencer et al., 2015), providing a principled rationale for modeling them as a potentially single dimension.

In Chinese, sensitivity to morphemic boundaries and compound word structures enables children to learn new word meanings, particularly in a lexicon characterized by extensive semantic compounding (Chen et al., 2009; McBride-Chang et al., 2008; Shu et al., 2006). Homophone awareness further contributes to vocabulary development, as children must distinguish among multiple morphemes sharing the same pronunciation to accurately access meaning (Liu & McBride-Chang, 2010). Notably, studies also report consistent associations between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge in both Chineseand English-speaking learners (e.g., Kuo & Anderson, 2006), underscoring a shared developmental linkage across languages.

Developmental Shifts in the Dimensionality of Cognitive-Linguistic Skills

Another important theoretical question in bilingual literacy development concerns whether the dimensionality of cognitive-linguistic skills varies with language proficiency. The Lexical Constituency Model (Perfetti et al., 2005) proposes that skilled reading relies on coordinated orthographic, phonological, and semantic constituents that remain functionally distinguishable despite their interaction during word recognition. Consistent with this view, dimensionality studies have identified phonological awareness, morphological awareness, orthographic awareness, and vocabulary knowledge as separable constructs in early literacy development (e.g., Sun et al., 2018; Tong & McBride-Chang, 2010). These findings suggest that cognitive-linguistic skills may initially emerge as distinct and functionally separable components.

An alternative perspective emphasizes increasing integration among cognitivelinguistic representations with literacy development. According to interactive models of reading development, such as the triangle model of reading (Harm & Seidenberg, 2004), orthographic, phonological, and semantic representations are acquired through interconnected learning mechanisms and become increasingly coordinated with experience. Consistent with this perspective, Tong and McBride-Chang (2010) provided evidence that cognitive-linguistic skills that were initially distinguishable became increasingly interconnected over the course of Chinese literacy development. Similarly, the Lexical Quality Hypothesis (Perfetti & Hart, 2002) argues that skilled reading relies on tightly bound lexical representations in which orthographic, phonological, and semantic information is simultaneously activated. From this perspective, developmental changes in the dimensionality of cognitive-linguistic skills may reflect increasing coordination among initially distinct components, resulting in greater overlap among latent constructs as language and literacy proficiency increases.

The Present Study

Building on theoretical and empirical evidence regarding the interdependence of cognitive-linguistic skills, the present study investigated how the dimensionality of four cognitive-linguistic abilities (i.e., phonological awareness, morphological awareness, orthographic awareness, and vocabulary knowledge) is organized across development in Chinese-English bilingual children. The study further examined whether the latent factor structure of these skills is similar or different across bilingual proficiency levels, and whether the structural organization differs across the two languages. We systematically tested and compared a set of theoretically motivated factor structures for cognitive-linguistic skills for each language and proficiency group (Figure 1). In doing so, we sought to characterize the dimensionality of cognitive-linguistic skills in bilingual learners of L1 Chinese and L2 English. By mapping how these skill structures evolve both within and across languages, we sought to provide a developmental framework for understanding how bilingual children progressively coordinate phonological, orthographic, morphological, and lexical processing as their bilingual proficiency grows.

Based on the theory and literature reviewed above, we hypothesized that the dimensionality of cognitive-linguistic skills would differ across L1 Chinese and L2 English, given their different developmental and linguistic contexts (Cheng et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2023). We further hypothesized that morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge would become increasingly integrated with increasing bilingual proficiency, such that these skills would be more closely represented as a common dimension among advanced than emerging bilinguals (Kuo & Anderson, 2006; Sparks & Deacon, 2015).

Measures

Phonological Awareness

DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS Chinese phonological awareness was assessed using syllable and onset deletion tasks (McBride-Chang et al., 2003). The syllable deletion task consisted of 29 test items. Children were asked to say a word/non-word without saying one of its syllables (e.g., /jyun4 zi2 bat1/ 'pen' without /zi2/). A child's score was the total number correct (max = 29), with Cronbach's alpha reliability of .82. The onset deletion task consisted of 22 test items.

Children were asked to say a single-or two-syllable word without the initial phoneme of the word (e.g., say /si1/ without /s/ or /hun2 kwat1/ without /h/). A child's score was the total number correct (max = 22), with Cronbach's alpha reliability of .98. English phonological awareness was assessed using an invented spelling task (McBride-Chang, 1998), which required children to analyze spoken pseudowords and represent their sound structures in writing. Its use as an indicator of phonological awareness was supported by previously reported associations with conventional phonological awareness tasks (Ho & Fong, 2005). The task consisted of 12 single-syllable pseudowords ranging from three to six letters in length. Children listened to a recording of a native English speaker pronouncing each pseudoword and were instructed to spell the word on a response sheet.

Responses were scored using a 5-point scale (0-5), with higher scores reflecting more accurate phonological representations in spelling. A child's score was the sum of the item scores (max = 60), with Cronbach's alpha reliability of .93.

Morphological Awareness

Chinese morphological awareness was assessed using a morphological compounding task (McBride-Chang et al., 2003) and a morpho-syntax task (Chik et al., 2012). In the morphological compounding task, children were orally presented with short scenarios (e.g., "We call paper that is white in color white paper") and then were asked to come up with a compound word based on a new related example (e.g., "So what do we call paper if it is red?" ; correct answer: "red paper"). Items were ordered by grade level, and the section was DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS discontinued after three consecutive incorrect responses. One point was awarded for each correct response (max = 44). Cronbach's alpha reliability was .90. In the morpho-syntax task, children were presented with a written sentence while simultaneously hearing it read aloud.

For each item, they were asked to identify the incorrect word and replace it with a more appropriate one (e.g., 科學家經常有新的發覺 "Scientists always have new awareness", in which the incorrect word 發覺 'awareness' should be replaced with 發現 'discovery'). The task consisted of 18 test items. Each item was worth up to three points: one point for correctly identifying the error and two points for accurately replacing it (one point for syntactic appropriateness and one point for semantic appropriateness; partially correct responses received 0.5 point where applicable). A child's score was the sum of the item scores (max = 54). Cronbach's alpha reliability was .91. English morphological awareness was assessed using a morpheme compounding task (McBride-Chang et al., 2008) and a WUG test (Berko, 1958). The morpheme compounding task consisted of 11 items. Children were first presented with an example of how to construct a compound word (e.g., "a box we use to hold our lunch is called a lunchbox") and then were asked to come up with a compound word based on a new example (e.g., "What do we call a box that we use to hold dinner?"). Each correct answer was given 1 point (max = 11), with Cronbach's alpha reliability of .83. The WUG test consisted of four items. The first two items asked children to make nouns plural, and the other two asked them to form a progressive and a past tense, respectively. Two points were given for the correct answer, and 1 point was given for the partially correct answer (max = 8). Cronbach's alpha reliability was .90.

Orthographic Awareness

Chinese orthographic awareness was assessed using a lexical decision task (Ho et al., 2000). A total of 60 characters were included, with 30 low-frequency Chinese characters and 30 pseudo-characters. The pseudo-characters included two types of orthographic violations: 1) illegitimate combinations where semantic and phonetic radicals were positioned in nonstandard locations contrary to Chinese orthographic rules; 2) structurally impossible configurations formed by pairing two semantic radicals or two phonetic components.

Children were asked to distinguish between real and pseudo characters. Each correctly identified item was given 1 point (max = 60), with Cronbach's alpha reliability of .93. English orthographic awareness was assessed using a delayed copying task and a lexical decision task (Lo et al., 2018). In the delayed copying task, children were required to copy an unfamiliar word after seeing it on a laptop screen. The task consisted of 10 items with orthographically complex low-frequency words (6-11 letters; e.g., predicament, controversy). Scoring followed Lo et al.'s (2018) procedure, and scores were allocated separately for copied letters and their positions. The maximum possible score was 204, with Cronbach's alpha reliability of .93. In the lexical decision task, children were required to distinguish an English nonword from three similar stimuli, including a real word (e.g., nice), a pseudoword (e.g., nise), and a nonword (e.g., niie). The tasks consisted of 30 items, and one point was given for the correct identification (max = 30). Cronbach's alpha reliability was .89.

Vocabulary Knowledge

Vocabulary knowledge in both Chinese and English was assessed using two tasks: expressive vocabulary and vocabulary definition. The items were selected based on age of acquisition and arranged in increasing order of difficulty. In Chinese, the expressive vocabulary and vocabulary definition tasks consisted of 23 and 27 items, respectively. In the expressive vocabulary task, children were shown pictures of objects and situations and asked to name them in Cantonese. The pictures were adapted from the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test-Third Edition (PPVT-III; Dunn & Dunn, 1997), and one point was awarded for each DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS correct response. In the vocabulary definition task, children were asked to define orally presented Chinese words. Responses were scored from 0 to 2 according to the accuracy of the definitions. The items were adapted from McBride-Chang et al. (2008). Each task was discontinued after five consecutive incorrect responses. Cronbach's alpha reliability was .67 for the expressive vocabulary task and .85 for the vocabulary definition task.

The corresponding English expressive vocabulary and vocabulary definition tasks consisted of 23 and 26 items, respectively. The administration procedures, scoring criteria, and discontinuation rules were the same as those used for the Chinese tasks. Cronbach's alpha reliability was .85 for the expressive vocabulary task and .86 for the vocabulary definition task.

Procedure

Children completed the language and literacy assessment battery individually, supervised by trained research assistants with backgrounds in psychoeducational assessment who were fluent in Cantonese and English. The tasks were administered in a single session of approximately 40 minutes. Most children were tested in a quiet room at their school during school hours, while some children were tested at home for logistical reasons. Before each test, the children were given a detailed explanation of the procedure and their consent was obtained.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using the lavaan package in R (Version 4.5.2; R Core Team, 2025). First, to examine the dimensional structure of cognitive-linguistic skills across bilingual development, a series of confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) were conducted separately for emerging and advanced bilingual groups in each language. Five theory-driven models (see Figure 1) were specified: (1) a one-factor model representing a general cognitive-linguistic ability (baseline model); (2) a four-factor model representing DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS phonological awareness, morphological awareness, orthographic awareness, and vocabulary knowledge as correlated but separable cognitive-linguistic skills (Model 2); (3) three alternative three-factor models reflecting different theoretical perspectives: Model 3A (phonological, orthographic, and morpho-lexical factor), Model 3B (phonologicalmorphological factor, orthographic awareness, and vocabulary knowledge), and Model 3C (orthographic-phonological factor, morphological awareness, and vocabulary knowledge).

Model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) (Kline, 2023). Competing models were evaluated based on overall model fit, information criteria (Akaike information criterion [AIC] and Bayesian information criterion [BIC]), and theoretical interpretability. When competing models demonstrated comparable fit, the more parsimonious and statistically admissible model was preferred (Schermelleh-Engel et al., 2003), particularly when alternative models showed evidence of poor discriminant validity or inadmissible parameter estimates.

Results

Descriptive Statistics and Correlations

Descriptive statistics for the measures are presented in Table 1 separately for emerging and advanced bilingual groups. Independent-samples t tests confirmed that the advanced bilingual group performed significantly better than the emerging bilingual group across all cognitive-linguistic measures (all ps < .001), consistent with expected developmental improvements in literacy skills. Correlations among all variables for each group are presented in Tables 2 and 3. Most measures were substantially correlated, indicating shared variance among the cognitive-linguistic skills. In particular, morphological awareness measures were strongly correlated with vocabulary measures in both Chinese and English, whereas phonological awareness measures showed moderate correlations with orthographic and morphological measures.

CFA for Chinese Cognitive-Linguistic Skills

Next, confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) were conducted to evaluate the dimensionality of cognitive-linguistic skills in each language. The fit indices for all models are presented in Table 4. For the emerging bilingual group, the one-factor model provided an acceptable fit. Among the alternative multidimensional models, Model 3A, which specified separate phonological and orthographic awareness factors together with a combined morpholexical factor, demonstrated the best overall fit. It also yielded the lowest AIC (18630.52) and BIC (18729.82) values among the competing models. Although Model 3B showed acceptable fit, the correlation between phonological-morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge was extremely high (r = .997), indicating limited distinction between these constructs. Model 3C and the four-factor model yielded estimated correlations between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge that exceeded 1.00 (rs = 1.031 and 1.040, respectively), indicating that these constructs could not be empirically distinguished in either model. Post-estimation checks further showed that the latent covariance matrices were not positive definite. Therefore, neither model was considered further. Taken together, Model 3A was retained as the final model for emerging bilingual children (see Figure 2, left panel).

For the advanced bilingual group, the one-factor model, Models 3B and Model 3C provided a poorer fit (see Table 4). The four-factor model yielded the best overall fit indices, whereas Model 3A, which specified separate phonological awareness and orthographic awareness factors together with a combined morpho-lexical factor, also demonstrated good fit. However, the four-factor model produced an inadmissible solution, with the estimated latent correlation between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge exceeding one, suggesting that the two constructs were not sufficiently distinguishable in this group. In DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS contrast, Model 3A yielded a statistically plausible solution with moderate inter-factor correlations and the lowest BIC value (17282.68). Following the principle of parsimony (Schermelleh-Engel et al., 2003), Model 3A was therefore retained as the final model for the advanced bilingual group (see Figure 2, right panel).

CFA for English Cognitive-Linguistic Skills

For the emerging bilingual group, the one-factor model provided a poor fit, whereas all multidimensional models substantially improved model fit. The four-factor model showed the best overall fit indices, closely followed by Model 3B, which specified phonologicalmorphological awareness, vocabulary knowledge, and orthographic awareness. Models 3A and 3C provided comparatively poorer fit (see Table 4). In addition, Model 3C yielded a correlation greater than 1.00 between the orthographic-phonological factor and morphological awareness (r = 1.061), making the model uninterpretable. Model 3B had the lowest BIC (17536.78) among the competing models. The four-factor model also showed a very high correlation between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge (r = .948), indicating limited distinction between these constructs. Given its comparable fit, lower BIC, and greater parsimony, Model 3B was retained as the final model for the emerging bilingual group (see Figure 3, left panel).

Finally, for the advanced bilingual group, the one-factor model provided a good fit, and all multidimensional models showed further improvements in model fit. Models 3A, 3B and the four-factor model demonstrated excellent fit, whereas Model 3C showed comparatively poorer fit (see Table 4). Model 3B yielded the lowest AIC (13760.97), which was nearly identical to that of Model 3A (13761.03), whereas Model 3A yielded the lowest BIC (13865.74).

Despite its excellent fit, Model 3B showed a very high correlation between phonological-morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge (r = .963). The squared DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS correlation between these factors (r² = .927) exceeded the AVE values for both phonologicalmorphological awareness (AVE = .645) and vocabulary knowledge (AVE = .820). This indicates that the two factors shared more variance with each other than either factor captured from its own indicators, thereby failing to satisfy the Fornell-Larcker criterion for discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The four-factor model also showed a very high correlation between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge (r = .934), indicating limited distinction between these constructs. In contrast, Model 3A yielded more clearly differentiated factors, with inter-factor correlations ranging from .708 to .829, and provided a more parsimonious representation of the data. Given its excellent fit, lowest BIC, clearer factor differentiation, and greater parsimony, Model 3A was retained as the final measurement model for the advanced bilingual group (see Figure 3, right panel).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the developmental dimensionality of four key cognitive-linguistic skills (phonological awareness, morphological awareness, orthographic awareness, and vocabulary knowledge) in Chinese-English bilingual children.

By testing theoretically motivated models across two proficiency levels (emerging vs. advanced bilinguals), we aimed to determine whether these skills function as distinct dimensions or integrate into more general constructs as language proficiency develops. Our results first showed that the structural organization of these skills differed across languages:

In L1 Chinese, a common three-factor structure (phonological, orthographic, and morpholexical) emerged across proficiency groups. In contrast, the three-factor structure of L2 English differed across proficiency groups. Among emerging bilinguals, phonological and morphological awareness formed a common factor, whereas vocabulary knowledge and orthographic awareness remained distinct. In contrast, among advanced bilinguals, morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge formed a common morpho-lexical DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS factor, while phonological and orthographic awareness remained distinct. Morpho-lexical integration was evident in both Chinese proficiency groups but only in the advanced English group. Together, these findings contribute to our understanding of how the organization of cognitive-linguistic skills varies across languages and may differ over the course of bilingual development.

Developmental Reorganization of Cognitive-Linguistic Skills

The results first showed that L1 Chinese demonstrated a common three-factor structure across proficiency levels, with morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge consistently forming a shared morpho-lexical factor. This pattern is consistent with the linguistic characteristics of Chinese, in which morphemes serve as the fundamental units of both word formation and lexical meaning. Because most Chinese words are compounds composed of meaningful morphemes, vocabulary acquisition often requires sensitivity to morphological structure. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated close associations between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge in Chinese-speaking children (Liu & McBride-Chang, 2010; Lu et al., 2025; McBride-Chang et al., 2008). Together, these findings suggest that morphological and lexical knowledge may be functionally intertwined from relatively early phases of Chinese literacy development.

Compared with L2 English, which showed different factor structures across proficiency groups, L1 Chinese exhibited a more stable organizational pattern across groups.

One possible explanation is that morpho-lexical integration may emerge earlier in Chinese due to the morphosyllabic nature of the writing system. Because Chinese characters simultaneously encode morphemic and semantic information, and because compound formation is highly productive, children are exposed to systematic links between morphological structure and lexical meaning from the beginning of literacy acquisition (Kuo & Anderson, 2006; McBride-Chang et al., 2008). Consistent with this view, longitudinal DIMENSIONALITY OF BILINGUAL SKILLS studies have shown that associations between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge are already evident in kindergarten and the early primary grades in Chinese (Cheng et al., 2015; McBride-Chang et al., 2008). Thus, the stable morpho-lexical factor observed across proficiency levels may reflect the earlier integration of morphological and lexical knowledge in L1 Chinese.

In contrast, our results for L2 English revealed different latent structures of cognitivelinguistic skills across proficiency groups, a pattern consistent with possible developmental reorganization. The clustering of phonological and morphological awareness among emerging bilinguals suggests that, in the early phases of L2 English development, morphological processing may be closely tied to phonological processing. Although morphology encompasses semantic, phonological, and syntactic information (Mahony et al., 2000), less proficient bilingual learners may initially access morphological structure through phonological awareness. This interpretation is consistent with evidence showing strong associations between phonological awareness and morphological awareness among young monolingual English readers (e.g., Cunningham & Carroll, 2015; Inoue et al., 2023). At early phases of second language acquisition, limited lexical knowledge may constrain direct access to morphemic meaning, leading children to rely more heavily on phonological cues when identifying and manipulating morphological units.

Interestingly, among advanced bilinguals, morphological awareness was more closely aligned with vocabulary knowledge, forming a unified morpho-lexical factor. This difference between proficiency groups may reflect changes in the functional role of morphology during literacy acquisition. Whereas morphology appears closely tied to phonological processing in earlier phases of L2 English development, it may increasingly support lexical and semantic representations as proficiency grows. Consistent with this interpretation, morphological awareness has been associated with multiple aspects of vocabulary knowledge, including vocabulary breadth and depth, in English literacy development in both monolingual and bilingual contexts (Kieffer & Lesaux, 2012; Sparks & Deacon, 2015). Morphological knowledge enables learners to recognize meaningful constituents in words, infer the meanings of unfamiliar words, and establish connections among morphologically related words (Carlisle, 2000; Kuo & Anderson, 2006). This interpretation is also consistent with theories that conceptualize morphology as an integrative mechanism linking phonological, orthographic, and semantic representations (Kirby & Bowers, 2017; Levesque et al., 2021).

As bilingual learners accumulate lexical experience, morphology may increasingly support the organization of semantic and lexical knowledge, aligning more closely with vocabulary knowledge at more advanced levels of L2 English development.

Morphological-Lexical Dimension Across Languages

Importantly, morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge converged into a shared dimension across both languages, particularly among advanced bilinguals, suggesting that the two constructs may become increasingly interconnected at the latent construct level as language and literacy proficiency develops. The Lexical Quality Hypothesis (Perfetti & Hart, 2002) provides a principled account of this convergence. Rather than reflecting two independent skills, the observed pattern suggests that morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge increasingly draw on a shared system of high-quality lexical representations. As these representations become more precise and well specified, the connections between form and meaning may become more tightly integrated, reducing the functional separability of morphological awareness and semantic access. From a processing perspective, this shift can be understood as a transition from relatively sequential to increasingly coordinated operations. For less proficient learners, morphological decomposition and meaning retrieval may occur in a stepwise manner. With increasing proficiency, however, these processes may become more coordinated, such that morphological structure and lexical meaning are increasingly integrated during word recognition (Liang & Chen, 2014; see also Perfetti & Hart, 2002, for a discussion of the shared representational system). This increasing coordination may render the two constructs more interdependent and, at the latent level, less distinguishable. These findings suggest that morpho-lexical integration may represent a fundamental organizational principle of cognitive-linguistic development.

Beyond representational integration, this convergence can also be understood as reflecting a mutually reinforcing developmental process (Carlisle, 2000; Kuo & Anderson, 2006; Nagy et al., 2006). As learners accumulate morphological knowledge, they acquire productive tools for inferring and retaining the meanings of unfamiliar words, while expanding vocabulary in turn enhances sensitivity to shared morphemic structure.

Longitudinal evidence supports this reciprocal relation, showing that morphological awareness predicts vocabulary growth even after controlling for phonological skills, and that vocabulary knowledge, in turn, facilitates the acquisition of new morphological patterns (Sparks & Deacon, 2015). Evidence from meta-analyses further supports this developmental pattern, indicating that the association between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge strengthens with age and represents one of the most robust relations among cognitive-linguistic skills (Cheng et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2023). This increasing association is consistent with the view that the two constructs become progressively more integrated over the course of literacy development. This morpho-lexical integration pattern can be further understood in light of language-specific properties. In Chinese, the close mapping between morphemes and characters, together with the high productivity of compounding, links morphological analysis closely to lexical learning from the early stages of literacy development (McBride-Chang et al., 2008). In English, although morphological processing involves the decomposition of roots and affixes, it serves a similar function in supporting the inference and generalization of word meanings (Carlisle, 2000; Nagy et al., 2006). Despite these differences in writing systems, both languages exhibit a shared functional linkage between morphological processing and vocabulary development. Taken together, these findings support the view that morpho-lexical integration reflects a consistent and developmentally driven feature of cognitive-linguistic (re)organization across languages.

Although the present study focused on within-language dimensionality, the observed convergence between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge across L1 Chinese and L2 English may also have implications for cross-language transfer. The Linguistic Interdependence Hypothesis and the Transfer Facilitation Model (Koda & Reddy, 2008) suggest that metalinguistic skills developed in one language can support literacy development in another through shared underlying processes. This interpretation is consistent with evidence from Chinese-English bilinguals showing that Chinese morphological awareness predicts English morphological awareness beyond other cognitive-linguistic factors (Lu et al., 2025). Similarly, sensitivity to morphemic structure acquired through the compound-rich Chinese writing system may facilitate the development of comparable morphological skills in English (Luo et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2018). Given the role of morphology in vocabulary learning, such transfer may contribute indirectly to the increasingly close association between morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge observed in more proficient bilinguals. Nevertheless, the present study did not directly examine cross-language transfer. Future research should investigate whether the morpholexical dimension identified in L2 English is influenced by earlier development of morphological and lexical skills in L1 Chinese.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations are worth mentioning. First, the present study relied on crosssectional proficiency groupings, limiting conclusions about within-individual structural reorganization and cross-language transfer. Although similar morpho-lexical organization was identified across L1 Chinese and L2 English, longitudinal studies are needed to examine how these structures change over time and whether earlier L1 Chinese morpho-lexical skills predict the development of the corresponding dimension in L2 English. Second, given that radicals constitute a fundamental feature of the Chinese writing system (McBride, 2016), future models could incorporate more fine-grained subcomponents, such as semantic radical awareness, to further strengthen the construct validity of the morpho-lexical dimension in Chinese. Third, some of our tasks showed relatively lower Cronbach's alpha reliability. This may partly reflect the nature of the assessment battery, in which multiple complementary subtasks were originally designed to provide a comprehensive assessment of a broader cognitivelinguistic skill. Future research should examine the reliability and validity of these measures using alternative approaches, such as composite scores across complementary sub-tasks or higher-order latent-variable models that account for measurement error. Fourth, in the CFA analysis, model selection was based on a balance between statistical fit and parsimony, particularly in cases where competing models showed highly similar fit indices. Under such conditions, the selection of more parsimonious models may obscure subtle distinctions among latent constructs. Future studies with larger samples and increased statistical power may allow for more precise differentiation among competing structural models. Finally, differences between the Chinese and English tasks limit cross-language comparability. Thus, cross-language patterns may partly reflect measurement methods rather than underlying constructs. Future studies should use more closely matched tasks across languages. Together, addressing these limitations will help clarify the developmental, structural, and crosslinguistic mechanisms underlying morpho-lexical integration in bilingual literacy.

Conclusion

This study examined the dimensionality of key cognitive-linguistic skills across proficiency groups in Chinese-English bilingual children. By testing theoretically motivated models across two proficiency levels, we sought to determine whether these skills function as distinct dimensions or integrate into more general constructs at different levels of bilingual proficiency. The findings showed that the dimensionality of cognitive-linguistic skills partly varied across languages and proficiency groups. Whereas L1 Chinese showed a common three-factor structure across proficiency levels, the three-factor structure of L2 English differed between the two groups: phonological and morphological awareness formed a common factor among emerging bilinguals, whereas morphological awareness and vocabulary knowledge formed a morpho-lexical factor among advanced bilinguals. Together, these findings suggest that morpho-lexical integration may be an important feature of cognitive-linguistic organization whose role may differ across languages and levels of bilingual proficiency, thereby providing a developmental framework for understanding the structure of bilingual literacy.
