Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Adolescent well-being is shaped by the social and cultural environments that structure young people's opportunities, relationships, and daily lives. In multicultural societies, adolescents from different ethnocultural backgrounds share a broader context, even as their everyday environments and experiences differ. Research has nonetheless studied them separately: mainstream well-being research has focused largely on Western majority populations and relied on overall evaluations or aggregate scores, whereas acculturation research has examined the psychological and sociocultural adaptation of minoritized groups. Because majority and minoritized adolescents belong to the same super-diverse society, their well-being warrants a common research agenda. Establishing whether well-being can be examined using a comparable multidimensional framework across groups is an important first step toward identifying shared and distinct patterns, before investigating how these patterns emerge. We examined subjective well-being among 1,385 secondary school adolescents in Flanders, Belgium (956 Belgian-majority and 429 Turkish-Moroccan minoritized adolescents), assessing the comparability of its dimensional structure before comparing individual well-being indicators and psychometric networks. Factor analyses supported a broadly comparable five-domain structure across groups. Item-level comparisons revealed mixed profiles rather than a consistent advantage for either group, with more favorable evaluations of some aspects of well-being coexisting with less favorable evaluations of others. Network analyses identified differences in overall structure and aggregate connectivity, with greater global strength in the Turkish-Moroccan network, although no individual association differed significantly after correction for multiple comparisons. Prominent associations occurred both within and across well-being domains in both groups. These findings suggest that a common multidimensional framework can support comparisons across ethnocultural groups while revealing differences that global evaluations or composite scores may obscure. They provide a starting point for future research examining how adolescents' well-being profiles and their interconnections develop within their (shared) social, cultural, and institutional environments.

Bridging Well-Being and Acculturation Research

These two traditions have developed largely in parallel. Acculturation research has provided important insights into intercultural experiences and their implications for wellbeing, but has traditionally focused on migrants and ethnocultural minorities, often treating majority populations as the standard against which their adaptation is evaluated (Berry, 1997; Sam & Berry, 2010). Within this tradition, researchers have commonly distinguished between psychological adaptation, concerned with emotional well-being and distress, and sociocultural adaptation, concerned with the ability to navigate everyday social and cultural demands (Ward & Kennedy, 1999; Ward & Rana-Deuba, 1999). Mainstream well-being research, meanwhile, has developed broader approaches to understanding flourishing and quality of life, although influential parts of its evidence base have relied heavily on Western populations (Bentley et al., 2019; Henrich et al., 2010; Ruggeri et al., 2020).

These different emphases have left limited opportunities to examine the multidimensional well-being of majority and minoritized adolescents within a common framework. Given that cultural diversity is an integral part of contemporary society, understanding both shared and distinct experiences of well-being should be central to well-being research rather than confined to a separate tradition. Such an understanding can be pursued descriptively, without assumptions about the social or cultural processes through which shared and distinct patterns emerge.

Comparing Well-Being Across Ethnocultural Groups

An important starting point for such research is adolescents' own evaluations of their lives. Subjective well-being is particularly relevant because these evaluations do not simply mirror the objective conditions in which adolescents live. Transactional perspectives emphasize that the significance of environmental demands depends partly on how individuals appraise them in relation to their goals and available resources (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Consistent with this, research in culturally diverse educational settings suggests that associations between educational practices and student experiences can differ across cultural groups and classroom compositions (Abacioglu et al., 2019 (Abacioglu et al., , 2023)). Thus, adolescents who share schools, neighborhoods, or institutions may nevertheless experience and evaluate these environments differently, while those facing different circumstances may report similar levels of well-being. Subjective evaluations offer insights that cannot be inferred from objective circumstances alone.

Examining these evaluations across ethnocultural groups also requires attention to measurement comparability. Before interpreting similarities and differences, it is important to establish whether well-being can be represented by the same broad dimensions in each group. Such a shared structure provides a basis for comparison, both of how adolescents evaluate specific aspects of their lives and of how these aspects relate to one another.

Multidimensional Well-Being

Well-being encompasses more than happiness, life satisfaction, or the absence of psychological distress. Psychological, public health, capability, and quality-of-life traditions recognize psychological functioning, physical health, everyday activities, social relationships, meaning, and environmental resources as relevant components of well-being (Huppert & So, 2013; Ruggeri et al., 2020; Ryan & Deci, 2001; Ryff & Keyes, 1995). Although a global or aggregate score may summarize general functioning, it can obscure variation across these components. A multidimensional framework makes that variation visible without assuming that all indicators reflect a single undifferentiated experience.

Evidence from adolescent populations illustrates why these distinctions matter: Across 36 countries, psychosomatic complaints and schoolwork pressure increased between 2002 and 2018, whereas average life satisfaction remained comparatively stable (Cosma et al., 2020). Although these population-level trends do not establish how different aspects of well-being relate within individuals, they demonstrate that indicators commonly associated with adolescent well-being need not follow the same patterns. A multidimensional approach can thus show which aspects of life are evaluated more or less favorably and whether different experiences coexist within a broader profile of well-being.

A multidimensional perspective is particularly relevant to comparisons across ethnocultural groups. European studies have documented heterogeneous findings among adolescents with migration backgrounds, including disadvantages, similarities, and advantages relative to majority peers across different adjustment and well-being outcomes (Delaruelle et al., 2021; Dimitrova et al., 2016; Mood et al., 2016). Rather than reading such variation as inconsistent group differences, a multidimensional perspective allows that groups may differ in some aspects of well-being but not others. Comparing individual indicators within a common dimensional framework can reveal where relative strengths and difficulties are concentrated.

Comparability also makes it possible to examine a second source of heterogeneity: how these indicators relate to one another. Two groups may report similar levels on several well-being indicators while differing in their patterns of association. Conversely, differences in individual indicators need not imply differences in their broader relational organization. Understanding well-being therefore requires attention not only to its constituent aspects but also to the patterns of association among them.

A Network Perspective on Adolescent Well-Being

A multidimensional measurement framework identifies distinguishable aspects of well-being, but it does not necessarily reveal how specific experiences are associated after accounting for other indicators. Factor analysis is valuable for identifying coherent dimensions and evaluating their comparability across groups, whereas psychometric network analysis offers a complementary perspective on associations among individual indicators (Bringmann et al., 2022; Epskamp et al., 2018). In a Gaussian graphical model (GGM), indicators are represented as nodes, and connections represent associations after accounting for the other indicators in the network (Epskamp et al., 2018; Kossakowski et al., 2016). This approach allows us to examine associations within and across well-being domains and compare their overall patterns between groups.

A network perspective may be particularly informative during adolescence, a developmental period characterized by increasing autonomy, changing peer relationships, heightened sensitivity to social evaluation, and ongoing identity development (Blakemore & Mills, 2014; Crone & Dahl, 2012). As adolescents navigate these changes, experiences across different areas of life may be closely related. For example, peer relationships are associated with adolescents' self-perceptions, emotional well-being, and adjustment (Brown & Larson, 2009; Rubin et al., 2015). Examining such associations may provide a more detailed understanding of adolescent well-being than considering broader dimensions alone.

Research in developmental psychopathology illustrates the relevance of examining such associations. McElroy et al. (2018) reported differences in depression and anxiety symptom networks between childhood and adolescence, suggesting that patterns of association may vary across developmental groups. Although symptom networks differ conceptually from multidimensional well-being networks, this research demonstrates the value of examining relational structures rather than assuming they remain consistent across populations. Network studies have also revealed connections across constructs that are often examined separately: Campbell and Osborn (2021) identified family support as an important bridge between psychological well-being and depression and anxiety symptoms among Kenyan adolescents.

Beyond symptom-focused research, network approaches have been applied to positive adolescent functioning, including Engagement, Perseverance, Optimism, Connected-ness, and Happiness (EPOCH) well-being traits among Chinese adolescents (Zeng et al., 2019), subjective well-being among Chinese high school students (Wang et al., 2023), and mental well-being across India, Israel, and the United Kingdom (Shukla et al., 2022). Longitudinal research has further examined changes in the network organization of life satisfaction, affect, and psychological well-being over time (Blasco-Belled et al., 2024). However, much of this research has concentrated on psychological symptoms, positive psychology traits, affect, and life satisfaction. Less attention has been given to associations involving broader aspects of adolescent well-being, such as physical health, social relationships, and environmental resources. Moreover, despite growing cross-national research, evidence on differences in well-being network structures between ethnocultural groups within the same national and institutional contexts remains limited.

The Present Study

The present study examines multidimensional subjective well-being among Belgian-majority and Turkish-Moroccan minoritized adolescents, focusing on their reported experiences across different aspects of life and how these experiences are interconnected. Our approach is informed by the Adolescent Well-Being Framework (Ross et al., 2020), developed through an international consultation process involving United Nations agencies and organizations working with young people. The framework recognizes five broad domains encompassing health and nutrition; connectedness, positive values, and opportunities to contribute to family and community life; safety and a supportive environment; learning, competence, education, skills, and employability; and agency and resilience. These domains provide a developmental basis for examining well-being beyond affective states or life satisfaction. We use the framework as a conceptual reference rather than a fixed measurement model, allowing the dimensional structure of adolescents' subjective evaluations to be examined empirically.

Belgium provides a relevant setting for examining adolescent well-being across ethnocultural groups growing up within a shared educational and institutional context, although their experiences and opportunities within these environments may differ. Turkish-and Moroccan-origin communities have longstanding histories of postwar labor migration and have experienced socioeconomic and educational inequalities and discrimination (Baysu et al., 2021 (Baysu et al., , 2024;; Gharaei et al., 2024; Phalet et al., 2007; Timmerman et al., 2003). Against this background, comparing the well-being of majority and minoritized adolescents can help identify commonalities and differences that may warrant further investigation into the social, cultural, and institutional circumstances in which they develop.

Turkish-and Moroccan-origin adolescents were combined in the current study for analysis to support sufficiently precise network estimation. Although the two communities share certain historical and structural circumstances, this grouping does not imply cultural equivalence or homogeneity within either community. Moreover, ethnocultural background represents only one aspect of adolescents' social position, alongside factors such as socioeconomic circumstances and gender (Kern et al., 2020). The findings should therefore be interpreted as comparisons between two analytically defined groups rather than as evidence of uniform experiences within them.

Against this background, we address three research questions. First, do Belgian-majority and Turkish-Moroccan minoritized adolescents share a comparable multidimensional structure of subjective well-being? Second, how do their evaluations of specific aspects of well-being differ, and do these differences reveal distinct profiles rather than a consistent advantage or disadvantage for either group? Third, how are different aspects of well-being related to one another within each group, and do these patterns of association differ between groups when examined using psychometric network analysis?

Method

Participants and Procedure Data were drawn from the first wave of PsychAcc, an ongoing longitudinal research project investigating psychological acculturation and well-being among secondary school students in Flanders, Belgium. The broader project examines these processes among adolescents from Belgianmajority and ethnoculturally minoritized backgrounds. Participating schools were strategically selected to represent variation in educational track, denominational affiliation, location (urban/rural), socioeconomic composition, linguistic diversity, and eligibility for government support. Schools with high proportions of Turkish-and Moroccan-origin students were intentionally oversampled to ensure adequate representation of these populations.

Within participating schools (N = 25), staff identified classes matching target populations, although all students in selected classes were eligible regardless of background. Data were collected from students in the third and fifth years of secondary education between October 2025 and January 2026. Trained researchers administered a Dutch-language survey on tablets or school computers during two consecutive class periods. Participation was voluntary; students could omit questions or withdraw at any time. Parental consent was obtained prior to participation, and students received school-determined incentives. The study was approved by KU Leuven's Social and Societal Ethics Committee (Reference G-2025-9101-R²[MAR]).

The full Wave 1 dataset comprised 2,155 respondents. For the present study, we selected adolescents from Belgian-majority and Turkish-Moroccan minoritized backgrounds based on self-reported information about their own cultural identification and their family's origins. Adolescents were included in the Turkish-Moroccan group if they reported Turkish or Moroccan identification or origin for themselves or at least one family member across the generations assessed, including parents and grandparents. This yielded an initial sample of 1,388 participants (957 Belgian-majority and 431 Turkish-Moroccan). Following response-quality screening, three participants were excluded (one Belgian-majority and two Turkish-Moroccan) because they met multiple indicators of potentially unreliable responding. The final analytic sample therefore comprised 1,385 adolescents, including 956 Belgian-majority and 429 Turkish-Moroccan adolescents. Within the Turkish-Moroccan group, 272 adolescents were of Moroccan origin, 155 were of Turkish origin,and 2 reported mixed Turkish-Moroccan origins. Regarding migration generation, 13.3% were first-generation adolescents (born outside Belgium), 74.8% were second-generation (born in Belgium with at least one parent born abroad), and 11.0% were thirdgeneration or later (born in Belgium to parents also born in Belgium). Migration generation could not be determined for the remaining 0.9%.

Participants had a mean age of 15.46 years (SD = 1.19), with Belgian-majority adolescents being slightly younger (M = 15.33, SD = 1.14) than Turkish-Moroccan adolescents (M = 15.75, SD = 1.24). Regarding gender, 711 participants identified as male, 636 as female, and 16 as another gender; 11 preferred not to disclose their gender, and gender information was missing for another 11 participants. Participants were enrolled predominantly in the third (n = 820) or fifth (n = 562) year of secondary education, with schoolyear information unavailable for three participants. Parental education and employment indicated socioeconomic disparities: tertiary education was reported for 84.3% of Belgianmajority versus 35.8% of Turkish-Moroccan adolescents; maternal employment rates were 90.6% versus 52.2%, and paternal employment rates were 91.9% versus 82.8%, respectively.

Measures

Subjective Well-Being

We assessed subjective well-being using a study-specific adolescent adaptation of the World Health Organization Quality of Life-Brief Version (WHOQOL-BREF; WHO-QOL Group, 1998), translated and back-translated for Dutch administration. The original WHOQOL-BREF comprises 26 items covering four domains: physical health, psychological faunctioning, social relationships, and environment, alongside two global items assessing overall quality of life and general health. Its multidimensional coverage broadly corresponds to key areas of the Adolescent Well-Being Framework proposed by Ross et al. (2020), allowing us to examine different aspects of adolescents' functioning and circumstances from their own perspectives.

To focus on specific aspects of subjective well-being, we excluded the two global assessment items (Q1 and Q2). Three further adaptations were made to improve the instrument's developmental relevance. First, the original mobil-ity item (Q15) was omitted because mobility limitations were expected to show limited relevance and variability in this community-based adolescent sample. Second, the information-access item (Q13) was replaced with satisfaction with one's ability to provide for or support others, drawing on the broader WHOQOL framework, which originally considered activities as a provider or supporter within social relationships. This adaptation was intended to capture a form of social functioning considered more relevant to adolescents than access to information in a highly digitally connected context. Third, the sexual-activity item (Q21) was replaced with satisfaction with family support, complementing the existing item assessing support from friends.

The resulting instrument comprised 23 items rated on 5point response scales corresponding to the content of each question (e.g., intensity, capacity, satisfaction, or frequency). Negatively oriented items assessing pain interference (Q3), dependence on medical treatment (Q4), and negative affect (Q26) were reverse-scored so that higher scores consistently indicated more favorable functioning or well-being.

The original WHOQOL-BREF domain structure provided the starting point for assessing the instrument's multidimensional coverage. However, rather than assuming that this structure would apply unchanged to adolescents from both ethnocultural groups, we examined the organization of the adapted indicators empirically. Following exploratory factor analyses (EFAs) and item refinement, 19 indicators were retained across five modeled domains: psychological functioning (enjoyment of life, meaning, body image, selfsatisfaction, and negative affect); everyday functioning (concentration, energy, sleep, daily activities, and work capacity); social relationships (personal relationships, family support, and friend support); environmental conditions and resources (physical environment, financial resources, living space, and health-service access); and a narrower, twoitem health-related functioning cluster (pain interference and dependence on medical treatment) (see the Appendix for the full list of retained items). Thus, the five-factor model included four broader multi-item domains and one more limited health-related cluster. This empirically derived organization differed from the original WHOQOL-BREF structure,particularly in distinguishing everyday functioning from psychological and health-related experiences. The factor-analytic procedures informing this organization and its comparability across groups are described below (see also Supplementary Materials).

Internal consistency was evaluated for the five empirically derived domains separately in each group. Cronbach's alpha coefficients (Belgian-majority/Turkish-Moroccan, respectively) were .84/.80 for psychological functioning, .77/.81 for everyday functioning, .74/.75 for social relationships, and .61/.71 for environmental conditions and resources. For the two-item health-related functioning domain, Spearman-Brown coefficients were .45/.42. Given its limited item coverage and modest reliability, the healthrelated domain was interpreted cautiously.

Analytic Strategy

Data Screening

Analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.5.2). Values outside the expected 1-5 response range were recoded as missing,and negatively oriented indicators were reversescored prior to analysis. Response quality was assessed across the 23 administered well-being indicators using three criteria: low within-person response variability (SD less than 0.30), long-string responding (identical responses across at least 80% of the items in the analysis sequence), and multivariate outlyingness (Mahalanobis distance exceeding the .999 quantile of the chi-square distribution with 23 degrees of freedom). Participants were excluded when at least two criteria were met. Person-level missingness exceeding 20% was also flagged for inspection but did not independently warrant exclusion. Three participants were excluded, yielding a final analytic sample of 1,385 adolescents.

Across the 19 retained indicators, 117 of 26,315 possible item responses were missing (0.44%), with itemlevel missingness ranging from 0% to 2.38%. Approximately 2% of participants had missing responses on Q10, Q11, and Q12. Given the low overall missingness, no separate missingness-mechanism model was used to determine exclusions or imputation. Missing data were handled according to the requirements of each analytical procedure. The EFAs used complete cases within each group to estimate polychoric correlation matrices, whereas confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) used full-information maximum likelihood (FIML). Primary network models were estimated using pairwise-complete Spearman correlations to retain available observations for each item pair. The Network Comparison Test (NCT) required complete responses across all 19 indicators and was therefore conducted on 1,341 participants (918 Belgian-majority and 423 Turkish-Moroccan). Although pairwise-complete estimation minimizes information loss, it does not eliminate potential bias if missingness is systematically related to unobserved responses.

Dimensional Structure and Measurement Comparability

We first examined whether a common dimensional organization of well-being indicators could be supported across the Belgian-majority and Turkish-Moroccan groups. Given the ordinal response format of the items, exploratory analyses were based on group-specific polychoric correlation matrices. Parallel analyses were conducted separately for each group to inform factor retention and suggested a fivefactor solution in both groups. We subsequently conducted EFAs separately by group using all 23 administered indicators, principal axis factoring, and oblimin rotation to allow correlations among factors. Factor loadings with absolute values of .30 or greater were considered salient for interpretation.

The factor-loading patterns were then compared across groups to identify a common, interpretable set of indicators. Item refinement was guided by cross-group con-sistency and theoretical interpretability. Four indicators were excluded: perceived safety (Q8) did not reach the salient-loading threshold of .30 in the Turkish-Moroccan group; satisfaction with the ability to support others (Q13) and transportation (Q25) showed cross-loadings on multiple factors in this group; and leisure opportunities (Q14) loaded on different factors across groups. Negative affect (Q26) also showed divergent loading patterns, clustering with psychological indicators in the Belgian-majority group but with health-related indicators in the Turkish-Moroccan group. However, Q26 was retained within psychological functioning because of its theoretical relevance to this domain. These decisions were exploratory and aimed to establish a common set of indicators for subsequent item-level analyses (see Supplementary Tables S4-S5).

Based on these analyses, 19 indicators were retained across five domains: psychological functioning, everyday functioning, social relationships, environmental conditions and resources, and health-related functioning. The healthrelated domain comprised only two indicators, pain interference (Q3) and dependence on medical treatment (Q4), which consistently clustered together across groups. Although this domain had more limited measurement coverage than the others, it was retained to preserve the representation of physical health experiences in subsequent analyses.

We subsequently evaluated this proposed organization using multigroup CFA in lavaan (Rosseel, 2012). The four broader domains and the two-item health-related cluster were specified as five correlated factors. The CFA models were estimated using maximum likelihood, treating the 5-category response scales as approximately continuous, which allowed FIML estimation for incomplete responses. Model fit was assessed using the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

To examine whether the proposed dimensional organization was broadly comparable across groups, we evaluated configural invariance, assessing whether the same pattern of factor loadings could be supported, followed by metric invariance, constraining corresponding factor loadings to equality across groups. Comparability was evaluated using overall model fit and changes in approximate fit indices between increasingly constrained models. Scalar invariance was not evaluated because the study did not aim to compare latent factor means. Instead, subsequent descriptive comparisons and network analyses focused on observed item-level responses and their conditional associations. The measurement analyses were therefore intended to assess broad dimensional comparability rather than equivalence of latent well-being levels or response-category functioning.

Descriptive Comparisons

We calculated means and standard deviations for each of the 19 retained well-being indicators separately for the Belgian-majority and Turkish-Moroccan groups. Group differences in observed item scores were examined using two-tailed independent-samples t tests, and Cohen's d, calculated using the pooled standard deviation, was used to quantify standardized mean differences. Positive d values indicate higher scores in the Belgian-majority group, whereas negative values indicate higher scores in the Turkish-Moroccan group. To account for multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted across the 19 item-level tests using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. These analyses were intended to describe differences in reported itemlevel experiences.

Network Estimation and Visualization

We estimated item-level networks separately for the two groups using the same 19 nodes. Given the ordinal and skewed distributions of the indicators, networks were based on Spearman rank-order correlations calculated using pairwise-complete observations. Networks were estimated using the bootnet package with estimateNetwork(default = "ggmModSelect", corMethod = "spearman", missing = "pairwise"). The ggmModSelect procedure uses stepwise model selection to minimize the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) and estimates a sparse GGM with unregularized partial correlations (Epskamp et al., 2018; Isvoranu & Epskamp, 2023). Each edge represents the conditional association between two indicators after accounting for the other indicators in the network. Because model selection sets some edge estimates to zero, retained and omitted edges should not be interpreted as definitive evidence for the presence or absence of associations in the population. Supplementary sensitivity analyses indicated that the main network patterns were broadly consistent across the specifications examined (see Supplementary Materials). All other estimation arguments used package defaults.

For visual comparison, node positions were held constant across groups using averageLayout(), and the same edge scale was applied to both networks. In qgraph, cut was set to 0 and maximum to the largest absolute edge weight observed across the two networks. Other visual settings affected presentation rather than estimation. Nodes were grouped according to the five empirically supported domains. Between-group comparisons were evaluated using permutation tests, whereas bootstrap analyses were used to assess the accuracy and stability of network estimates (Epskamp et al., 2012).

Network Comparison

We compared the Belgian-majority and Turkish-Moroccan networks using the NCT (van Borkulo et al., 2023). Because the permutation-based NCT involves repeated estimation of networks on identically structured datasets, the analysis was conducted on participants with complete data across the 19 retained indicators. This resulted in an NCT sample of 1,341 adolescents (918 Belgianmajority and 423 Turkish-Moroccan). Given the very low overall item-level missingness (0.44%), this complete-case restriction resulted in limited loss of information. Groups were treated as independent, with 1,000 permutations, all edges tested, and Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied to edge-level comparisons. The network-structure invariance test evaluated whether the overall configuration of edge weights differed across groups, the global-strength test compared the sum of absolute edge weights, and edge-level tests examined specific pairwise differences. A random seed of 1234 was used for the permutation procedure.

Strength, Bridge Strength, and Network Accuracy

We used strength and bridge strength to describe how individual indicators were positioned in the estimated networks. Strength centrality, estimated using 'qgraph', is the sum of the absolute edge weights connecting a node to all other nodes and reflects its overall connectivity. Expected influence is a related measure that sums edge weights while retaining their positive or negative signs, thereby distinguishing connections in opposite directions. Because the estimated networks contained almost exclusively positive edges, expected influence provided little additional information, and strength was retained as the primary node-level index. To examine connections across different aspects of well-being, we defined five communities corresponding to the empirically supported well-being domains, with each community comprising the indicators belonging to that domain. Bridge strength, estimated using 'networktools', is the sum of the absolute edge weights connecting a node to indicators in other communities (Jones et al., 2021). It therefore describes the extent to which an indicator is connected to aspects of well-being outside its own domain, rather than only to indicators within the same domain. Strength and bridge-strength indices were standardized within each network and interpreted as relative descriptive rankings within their corresponding networks.

Edge-weight accuracy and strength-centrality stability were evaluated in bootnet with 1,000 bootstrap iterations. Nonparametric bootstrapping was used to assess edgeweight accuracy and to conduct within-network edge-and strength-difference tests. We report 95% nonparametric bootstrap intervals as indices of estimation precision; these intervals were not treated as conventional null-hypothesis tests of whether an edge differs from zero. Case-dropping bootstrapping was used to evaluate the stability of strength centrality. Correlation stability (CS) coefficients above .25 were treated as minimally acceptable and values above .50 as preferable (Epskamp et al., 2018). The bootstrap procedures used a random seed of 1234. These uncertainty analyses were used to qualify within-network interpretation and did not replace the NCT for between-group inference. The analyses were exploratory and not preregistered; accordingly, the findings are interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Results

Dimensional Structure and Measurement Comparability

Parallel analysis initially suggested five factors. Indicators Q8, Q13, Q14, and Q25 showed weak or inconsistent loadings across groups and were excluded to improve comparability. The refined 19-indicator solution contained four multi-item domains that were broadly consistent in both groups: psychological functioning (Q5, Q6, Q11, Q19, Q26), everyday functioning (Q7, Q10, Q16, Q17, Q18), social relationships (Q20, Q21, Q22), and environmental conditions and resources (Q9, Q12, Q23, Q24), alongside a consistent two-item health-related functioning cluster (Q3, Q4). Although Q26 showed some loading variation across groups, it was retained in the psychological domain because of its substantive relevance.

In multigroup CFAs, the five-domain configural model showed acceptable fit, χ 2 ( 284) = 1699, CFI = .918, TLI = .902, RMSEA = .060, and SRMR = .040. Most factor loadings were strong and consistent across groups, particularly in the psychological, everyday functioning, and social domains. Environmental loadings were somewhat weaker, and one loading in the health-related functioning domain was relatively low, but the overall pattern was similar across groups. Metric invariance was supported: constraining factor loadings to equality produced small changes in approximate fit indices, ∆CFI = -.005, ∆RMSEA less than .001, and ∆SRMR = .007. Although the chi-square difference test was statistically significant (p less than .001), given the large analytic sample (N = 1,385) and the known sensitivity of chi-square difference tests to sample size, our invariance conclusion was based primarily on changes in approximate fit indices (Chen, 2007). The factor results supported using the same broad domain labels in the two groups and reduced the concern that subsequent network comparisons simply reflected different item clustering.

Domain Profiles

Table 1 presents the indicator-level descriptive statistics and group comparisons across the 19 retained indicators. Means on all indicators were above the scale midpoint in both groups. Friend support, physical environment, living space, and health-service access were among the highestrated indicators, whereas sleep, concentration, and work capacity were among the lowest-rated indicators in both groups. The group differences therefore occurred within an overall favorable profile rather than separating one generally high-well-being group from one generally low-well-being group.

Turkish-Moroccan adolescents reported significantly more favorable scores on meaning in life, body image, selfsatisfaction, concentration, and personal relationships, as well as less negative affect and less dependence on medical treatment (reverse-scored). Belgian-majority adolescents reported significantly more favorable scores on financial re-sources, daily activities, living space, and health-service access. All 11 of these differences remained statistically significant after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for multiple comparisons. The group difference in work capacity, although significant before correction (p = .033), did not remain significant after adjustment (p BH = .052). Effect sizes were generally small, with the largest differences observed for body image (d = -0.41), self-satisfaction (d = -0.40), and financial resources (d = 0.34).

Network Structure and Overall Connectivity

The estimated networks are visualized in Figure 1. Of the 171 possible pairwise connections, 48 (28.1%) were retained in the Belgian-majority network and 45 (26.3%) in the Turkish-Moroccan network. The mean absolute weight of retained edges was .159 and .184, respectively. Thus, although the Belgian-majority network contained slightly more retained connections, those in the Turkish-Moroccan network were stronger on average. These descriptive characteristics provide an overview of the estimated networks but do not, on their own, establish meaningful differences in connectivity.

The NCT indicated significant differences in both overall network structure (M = .251, p = .002) and global strength (S = .690, p = .024). Global strength, defined as the sum of absolute edge weights, was higher in the Turkish-Moroccan network (8.233) than in the Belgian-majority network (7.543). This indicates greater overall connectivity among well-being indicators in the estimated Turkish-Moroccan network, despite its slightly smaller number of retained edges. The significant structure test further indicates that the overall configuration of associations differed between groups.

However, no individual edge difference remained significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. Therefore, although the networks differed in their overall structure and connectivity, the analyses did not identify specific associations that differed reliably between groups. Apparent differences in individual connections were consequently interpreted descriptively rather than as statistically established group differences.

Relationships Within and Across Well-Being Domains

Because none of the corrected edge-level tests showed significant group differences, we describe the strongest relations within each network without treating individual connections as group-specific. The strongest estimated conditional associations were predominantly within domains. In the Belgian-majority network, the largest within-domain edges linked body image with self-satisfaction (.504, 95% nonparametric bootstrap interval [.434, .546]), enjoyment of life with meaning in life (.396, [.332, .452]), personal relationships with friend support (.357, [.278, .407]), living space with health-service access (.342, [.253, .388]), and concentration with work capacity (.330, [.253, .378]). In the Turkish-Moroccan network, the largest within-domain edges linked enjoyment of life with meaning in life (.412, [.325, .492]),daily activities with work capacity (.358, [.254, .436]), living space with health-service access (.348, [.245, .452]),body image with self-satisfaction (.296, [.200, .410]), and personal relationships with friend support (.280, [.182, .393]). The association between pain interference and dependence on medical treatment also appeared in both networks. These estimates showed that several of the comparatively strongest conditional associations occurred between indicators within the same well-being domain.

Cross-domain associations were generally smaller than within-domain associations but included several comparatively strong connections. In the Belgian-majority network, the largest cross-domain edge linked friend support with living space (.227, 95% nonparametric bootstrap interval [.142, .280]), followed by energy with physical environment (.162, [.100, .214]) and negative affect with energy (.157, [.085, .218]). In the Turkish-Moroccan network, the largest crossdomain edge linked negative affect with pain interference (.272, [.157, .367]), followed by friend support with living space (.210, [.111, .310]). Several other cross-domain estimates were less precise, with bootstrap distributions extending across zero. Because bootstrap intervals characterize edge-weight accuracy rather than serve as conventional significance tests, inclusion or exclusion of zero was not used to determine whether an edge was retained. These individual cross-domain edges should be distinguished from bridge strength: edge weights represent specific conditional associations between pairs of nodes, whereas bridge strength summarizes each node's aggregate connectivity with nodes outside its own domain.

Network Accuracy and Stability

The 95% nonparametric bootstrap intervals were narrowest for the strongest edges and generally wider for weaker associations, indicating greater precision for the highest-magnitude relations than for small edge-rank differences. The intervals were interpreted as measures of edgeweight accuracy rather than as conventional significance tests. Case-dropping bootstrap analyses indicated adequate stability of strength centrality in both groups. The CS coefficient was .75 in the Belgian-majority network and .52 in the Turkish-Moroccan network, with both values exceeding the .50 guideline commonly used to indicate good stabil-ity. Strength rankings were therefore considered sufficiently stable for descriptive interpretation.

Shared Dimensions, Different Profiles of Subjective Well-Being

The identification of broadly comparable dimensions provides an important starting point for examining well-being across the two groups. Factor analyses supported five domains encompassing psychological functioning, everyday functioning, social relationships, environmental conditions and resources, and health-related functioning. These empirically identified domains show substantial conceptual alignment with the multidimensional perspective of the Adolescent Well-Being Framework (Ross et al., 2020), while also largely reflecting the operationalization of well-being in the WHOQOL-BREF. These correspondences support the relevance of the identified dimensions for capturing different aspects of adolescents' well-being. Evidence for configural and metric comparability further suggests that these dimensions can provide a common basis for examining well-being across ethnocultural groups, without implying that adolescents necessarily evaluate their experiences similarly.

Within this broadly comparable structure, the two groups showed different profiles of relative strengths and difficulties. Turkish-Moroccan adolescents generally reported more favorable evaluations of several psychological and relational aspects of well-being, particularly body image, self-satisfaction, meaning in life, and personal relationships. They also reported more favorable outcomes on concentration, negative affect, and dependence on medical treatment. In contrast, Belgian-majority adolescents reported more favorable evaluations of environmental and material aspects of well-being, including financial resources, living space, and access to health services, as well as satisfaction with daily activities. Although most differences were small, their contrasting directions suggest that more favorable experiences in one area of life do not necessarily coincide with more favorable experiences in others.

The relatively favorable environmental evaluations among Belgian-majority adolescents are consistent with research documenting socioeconomic and educational inequalities affecting Turkish-and Moroccan-origin communities in Belgium (Baysu et al., 2021 (Baysu et al., , 2024;; Phalet et al., 2007; Timmerman et al., 2003). These broader inequalities provide relevant context for interpreting differences in perceived financial resources, living space, and access to health services. However, these indicators capture adolescents' subjective evaluations rather than objective socioeconomic or institutional conditions, and the analyses cannot establish whether structural inequalities account for the observed differences. The more favorable psychological and relational evaluations among Turkish-Moroccan adolescents further suggest that subjective well-being does not necessarily correspond uniformly to socioeconomic advantage.

These findings also offer a useful perspective on the heterogeneous literature concerning well-being among adolescents with migration backgrounds. Previous research has documented circumstances in which immigrant-origin adolescents report comparable or more favorable outcomes than majority peers despite exposure to socioeconomic disadvantage or discrimination, often discussed in relation to the immigrant paradox (Dimitrova et al., 2016; Geel & Vedder, 2010; Mood et al., 2016). Our findings suggest that the apparent presence or absence of such an advantage may depend partly on how well-being is conceptualized and operationalized. Studies focusing primarily on psychological adjustment may capture a different pattern from those examining material resources, everyday functioning, or social relationships. Consequently, seemingly inconsistent findings across studies may not necessarily be contradictory but may instead reflect differences in the aspects of well-being being assessed. This underscores the value of multidimensional approaches that preserve these distinctions rather than assuming that global evaluations or aggregate scores adequately represent the full range of adolescents' well-being experiences.

The Interconnected Organization of Well-Being

Beyond differences in individual indicators, the network analyses showed how aspects of well-being were associated with one another. Several prominent associations occurred within the same well-being domains. Enjoyment of life and meaning in life were closely connected, reflecting the relationship between hedonic and eudaimonic aspects of well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2001). Self-satisfaction was strongly associated with body image, a finding that may be particularly relevant during adolescence, when physical changes and sensitivity to social evaluation can shape selfperceptions (Wichstrøm & von Soest, 2016). Within the social domain, personal relationships were closely associated with friend support, whereas associations between living space and health-service access, and between pain interference and dependence on medical treatment, reflected connections within environmental and health-related experiences. These patterns suggest that the broader dimensions identified through factor analysis are also recognizable in the conditional associations among individual indicators, consistent with previous network research on quality of life and adolescent well-being (Blasco-Belled et al., 2024; Kos-sakowski et al., 2016).

The networks also revealed associations across domains, highlighting connections between aspects of well-being that are often examined separately. In both groups, perceived friend support was associated with satisfaction with living space, suggesting that adolescents' evaluations of their social relationships and living environments are interconnected. This finding raises broader questions about how residential environments shape opportunities for social interaction and friendship formation. In particular, residential segregation may influence whom adolescents encounter and develop relationships with, while the resulting social networks may, in turn, shape opportunities for intergroup contact and integration. These processes were not directly measured in the present study, but they illustrate why the relationship between social and environmental experiences warrants further investigation.

Other cross-domain associations were more prominent in one estimated network than the other. Among Belgianmajority adolescents, energy was associated with evaluations of the physical environment and negative affect, whereas negative affect and pain interference showed a prominent association in the Turkish-Moroccan network. These patterns illustrate connections between physical experiences, emotional well-being, and environmental evaluations, consistent with research documenting relationships between physical and psychological aspects of adolescent functioning (Harte et al., 2024). However, although the networks differed significantly in overall structure, no individual association differed significantly after correction for multiple comparisons. These patterns should therefore be understood as descriptive features of the estimated networks rather than established differences in specific associations between groups.

At the global level, the Turkish-Moroccan network showed stronger overall connectivity. This suggests that different aspects of well-being were, on average, more closely associated among Turkish-Moroccan adolescents. Because global strength summarizes the network as a whole, however, it does not indicate which specific associations contribute to this difference. The stronger connectivity may reflect more closely intertwined experiences across areas of life, but differences in response patterns or in the precision of network estimation cannot be ruled out, and the present data do not allow these possibilities to be distinguished.

At the level of individual indicators, strength centrality analyses identified self-satisfaction, enjoyment of life, and satisfaction with daily activities as relatively strongly connected indicators in both networks. One possible explanation is that these indicators capture broad evaluations of adolescents' experiences that are closely related to several aspects of their lives. Self-satisfaction, for example, may reflect evaluations shaped by emotional experiences, social relationships, and everyday functioning, while enjoyment of life may draw on satisfaction across multiple domains. Their relatively high connectivity may therefore partly re-flect the breadth of experiences represented by these indicators, as well as conceptual overlap among measures. This interpretation is consistent with previous network research highlighting associations among life satisfaction, affect, and psychological functioning (Blasco-Belled et al., 2024).

Bridge-strength analyses provided a complementary perspective by identifying indicators with relatively strong connections across well-being domains. Among Belgianmajority adolescents, energy showed the highest bridge strength, with friend support also exhibiting relatively strong cross-domain connectivity. Energy may connect experiences of physical functioning with participation in everyday activities and psychological well-being, while supportive friendships may be associated with emotional experiences, self-evaluations, and opportunities for social engagement (Brown & Larson, 2009; Rubin et al., 2015). Previous network research has similarly highlighted social support as a connection between psychological well-being and psychopathology among adolescents (Campbell & Osborn, 2021). These interpretations suggest why energy and friend support might be associated with experiences extending beyond their respective domains, although their relevance is not necessarily limited to Belgian-majority adolescents.

Among Turkish-Moroccan adolescents, personal relationships showed the highest bridge strength. The prominence of personal relationships may be considered in light of research linking peer experiences, intergroup contact, and discrimination to belonging and identity among Turkish-and Moroccan-origin adolescents in Belgium (Kende, Baysu, Phalet, et al., 2021; Kende, Baysu, Van Laar, et al., 2021). Research on school diversity climates has further highlighted the relevance of discrimination experiences for academic adjustment in these populations (Baysu et al., 2024). These findings suggest that relational experiences may be associated with multiple aspects of adolescents' lives, particularly in social environments where experiences of acceptance and exclusion are consequential. Living space, in turn, showed relatively high overall strength centrality in this network, which may reflect the relevance of perceived material circumstances to several aspects of everyday well-being.

Whereas these interpretations emphasize adolescents' social circumstances, cultural-psychological perspectives offer a complementary interpretation by emphasizing the meanings adolescents attach to their experiences. Research on culturally situated psychological functioning suggests that relationships, achievements, obligations, and everyday experiences may carry different significance across social and cultural contexts (Coşkan et al., 2016; De Leersnyder et al., 2013; Yılmaz et al., 2024a Yılmaz et al., , 2024b)). Cultural orientations emphasizing family relationships and interdependence, documented in research involving Turkish and Moroccan migrant families, may further shape how relational experiences are understood and evaluated (Güngör et al., 2012; Phalet & Schönpflug, 2001). Importantly, relational indicators were prominent in both networks, through friend support among Belgian-majority adolescents and personal relationships among Turkish-Moroccan adolescents. Their prominence suggests a shared relevance of social connections, whereas their particular associations may reflect differences in social circumstances, cultural expectations, or the meanings attached to relationships. Taken together, these perspectives suggest that the observed network patterns may reflect both adolescents' social circumstances and the meanings they attach to their experiences. Their respective contributions remain to be investigated, and differences in the relative prominence of individual indicators should be interpreted descriptively rather than as statistically established differences in centrality between groups.

Implications for Research, Measurement, Policy, and Practice

The findings have implications for how adolescent wellbeing is assessed and monitored. Overall well-being scores remain useful for summarizing population-level patterns and tracking changes over time, but they should be complemented by domain-and indicator-level assessments. In practice, this means reporting not only whether well-being differs between populations, but also which aspects of wellbeing account for these differences. Such an approach could help researchers and institutions identify specific needs, monitor changes that may be overlooked by overall measures, and make more informed decisions about where additional investigation or support is warranted.

The findings also suggest opportunities for developing and evaluating interventions that address multiple aspects of well-being. Network approaches can complement conventional assessments by identifying associations across domains and their indicators, and generating hypotheses about which experiences may be relevant to consider together. Rather than interpreting highly connected indicators as intervention targets, researchers could use these associations to formulate questions about potential pathways of change and test them through longitudinal or experimental designs. This would help establish whether interventions addressing particular experiences have implications for other aspects of well-being, and under what circumstances.

A further implication concerns coordination across the institutions and services involved in adolescents' lives. Psychological, educational, healthcare, and social services often address different aspects of well-being through separate professional responsibilities. Although these approaches serve important purposes, their separation may make it more difficult to recognize or respond to needs spanning multiple areas of life. Multidimensional assessments could provide a shared basis for communication among professionals, helping them identify where complementary forms of support may be appropriate. For example, difficulties identified through psychological assessments might warrant consideration alongside adolescents' school experiences, social relationships, or material circumstances, rather than being addressed exclusively at the individual level. Whether more coordinated approaches improve outcomes remains an important question for future evaluation.

Finally, the findings have implications for evaluating the equity of policies and interventions. Improvements in average well-being do not necessarily indicate that all adolescents benefit equally, particularly when favorable outcomes in some domains coexist with difficulties in others. Evaluations could therefore examine changes across specific wellbeing dimensions and population groups, alongside differences in access to and experiences of support. Involving adolescents from different backgrounds in identifying priorities, interpreting findings, and evaluating proposed solutions could further help ensure that interventions respond to their experiences rather than assumptions about their needs.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the networks were estimated from cross-sectional data and represent conditional associations among adolescents' reported experiences. Although these analyses provide insight into the organization of well-being indicators at the group level, they cannot establish the direction of associations, how these relationships develop over time, or whether changes in one aspect of well-being influence others (Fisher et al., 2018). Moreover, associations estimated across individuals do not necessarily reflect the processes operating within individuals over time.

Second, although the study compared adolescents from different ethnocultural backgrounds, it did not directly assess many of the social, cultural, and institutional conditions that may contribute to the observed patterns. Ethnocultural group membership does not capture the diversity of adolescents' lived experiences and should not be treated as an explanatory mechanism. Consequently, the analyses cannot determine whether differences in well-being profiles or network organization are associated with socioeconomic circumstances, discrimination (e.g., Benner et al., 2018), school environments, cultural orientations, or family and community resources. Nor can they establish whether the same experiences carry similar meanings across groups.

Third, Turkish-and Moroccan-origin adolescents were combined to support sufficiently precise network estimation. Although this decision increased the available sample size, it may have obscured meaningful variation between and within these populations, whose social circumstances and experiences are not necessarily equivalent. The unequal group sizes may also have affected the precision and stability of network estimates. Larger and more balanced samples would allow these populations to be examined separately and support more differentiated analyses of within-group heterogeneity.

Fourth, the measurement findings should be interpreted in light of the analyses conducted. Configural and metric invariance were examined, providing support for a broadly comparable dimensional structure and factor loadings across groups. Because scalar invariance was not tested, the study does not draw conclusions about latent factor mean differences; group comparisons instead focus on individual indicators. The health-related domain also comprised only two indicators, potentially limiting the breadth and stability of this dimension. Further validation of the adapted WHOQOL-BREF, including its reliability, factor structure, and measurement properties across adolescent populations, is therefore warranted.

Finally, the item-level comparisons involved multiple statistical tests. Although the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to control the false discovery rate, statistically significant differences were generally small. Their substantive importance should therefore be evaluated in relation to effect sizes and the overall pattern of findings. The exploratory nature of the analyses, particularly the network comparisons, also calls for replication in independent samples before firm conclusions can be drawn about the generalizability of the observed profiles and network characteristics.

Addressing these limitations, future studies could combine repeated assessments of well-being with direct measures of socioeconomic resources, discrimination, school climate, social networks, and cultural orientations. Longitudinal designs that distinguish within-person change from between-person differences would help clarify how associations among well-being indicators evolve and whether contextual experiences precede changes in well-being. Together, these approaches could help distinguish competing explanations for the observed profiles and network patterns.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that broadly comparable dimensions of adolescent subjective well-being can coexist with heterogeneous profiles and differences in network organization across ethnocultural groups. By integrating dimensional, item-level, and network perspectives, it offers a more differentiated understanding of well-being than summary measures alone can provide. The findings also highlight the importance of examining adolescents' experiences within the social, cultural, and institutional contexts they share, without assuming that ethnocultural background itself explains the observed differences. This approach provides a foundation for future research investigating how multidimensional well-being develops and how its different aspects are interconnected in culturally diverse societies.
