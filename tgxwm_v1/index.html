Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

The addiction field is unusually rich in durable, contested certainties: that confrontation breaks through denial, that medication treatment merely substitutes one drug for another, that a single treatment philosophy fits all, or, conversely, that any criticism of a favored model reflects stigma rather than evidence. This conceptual article introduces two linked constructs to explain how such certainties survive contrary evidence. Occam's Disposable Razor (ODR) names a bounded micro-level event: the moment at which a simpler, coherent, evidence-consistent explanation is available and is nonetheless discarded because accepting it threatens identity, belonging, authority, safety, or an inherited narrative, with parsimony, noncontradiction, and falsifiability abandoned together. Ratified Reality Drift (RRD) names the macro-level process by which the protected interpretation is repeated, institutionalized, and inherited as taken-for-granted reality within families, recovery communities, treatment organizations, and policy systems. We distinguish the constructs from motivated reasoning, cognitive dissonance, belief perseverance, groupthink, and the normalization of deviance; specify a nine-stage coding frame with explicit criteria for an ODR attribution; and apply the framework symmetrically to patients, families, mutual-aid communities, clinicians, and institutions, including both critics and defenders of the brain disease model and of medications for opioid use disorder. We separate defensive discarding from legitimate skepticism and from protected private meaning, address the risk that the construct could itself be weaponized, and derive eight falsifiable propositions with associated research designs. The framework has not yet been tested empirically; its contribution is a symmetrical, testable research program for a familiar clinical intuition.

Discarding the Razor, Ratifying the Drift: Identity-Protective Reasoning and the Institutional Entrenchment of Contested Certainties in Addiction Treatment and Policy

Everyone who works in or around addiction has watched the moment. A family finally hears that the simplest account of a son's missing money, weight loss, and shuttered friendships is a return to heroin use, and reaches instead for an elaborate story about a demanding job. A counselor with three decades of abstinence-based practice reads a synthesis of trial evidence on opioid agonist treatment and concludes, without engaging the findings, that the studies must reflect industry influence. A researcher committed to a neurobiological account of addiction encounters population data on natural recovery (e.g., Kelly et al., 2017) and files them under exceptions. In each case a simpler, coherent, testable explanation was momentarily on the table, and in each case, it was set aside, not for want of intelligence or evidence, but because accepting it would have cost something: an identity, a membership, an authority, a livelihood, or a story a family has told itself for years.

The behavioral sciences possess ample vocabulary for the general tendency at work here.

Motivated reasoning describes the influence of directional goals on cognition (Kunda, 1990); identity-protective cognition describes reasoning organized around the defense of group membership (Kahan, 2013); and research on belief perseverance documents how conclusions can outlive their evidential support (Ross et al., 1975). What literature lacks, we contend, is a bounded unit of analysis for the specific inflection point at which a person or institution, confronted with a simpler and evidence-consistent explanation, discards it in favor of a costlier protected narrative. It also lacks a connected account of how that single act of discarding, repeated and rewarded, hardens into the settled common sense of a family, a fellowship, a profession, or a policy regime. This article proposes two linked constructs, first formulated in prepublished papers by the second author (K. Kesling, 2026a Kesling, , 2026b) ) and developed here for the addiction field. The first, Occam's Disposable Razor (ODR), names the micro-level event: the identifiable moment when the razor of parsimony is not merely misapplied but thrown away, together with the companion disciplines of noncontradiction and falsifiability. The second, Ratified Reality Drift (RRD), names the macro-level career of the interpretation thus protected: its repetition, its institutional ratification, and its eventual inheritance by newcomers as unremarkable truth. The addiction field is an apt proving ground for both constructs, for three reasons. Its clinical encounters routinely stage exactly the confrontation the ODR construct describes. Its recovery pathways are, on the field's own best current theorizing, processes of social identity transition (Best et al., 2016), which places identity protection at the causal center rather than the periphery. And its institutions (treatment programs, mutual-aid fellowships, professional bodies, and drug-policy regimes) have repeatedly sustained practice orthodoxies at odds with accumulated evidence, sometimes at documented cost to life (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [NASEM], 2019; Wakeman & Rich, 2018).

Our aim is deliberately symmetrical. The framework is not an instrument for diagnosing error in patients, in 12-step members, in critics of medication, or in any other convenient outgroup. Clinicians, researchers, harm-reduction advocates, and the authors of conceptual articles are equally eligible subjects of it. The article proceeds as follows. We first situate the constructs in the motivated-reasoning, philosophy-of-science, and correction literatures, taking care to calibrate claims to the current state of evidence. We then define ODR, present a ninestage observable process model with explicit criteria for an ODR attribution, and develop RRD through social-constructionist and epistemic-injustice scholarship. We next set the constructs against their closest neighbors and state what they add. Three applications in the addiction field follow: clinical encounter, recovery identity, and institutional paradigms. We then erect safeguards against misuse of the construct, derive eight falsifiable propositions, and consider limitations and rival explanations.

Conceptual Foundations

Motivated Reasoning and the Defense of Identity Kunda's (1990) canonical statement established that directional goals shape which beliefs people arrive at, but through ordinary cognitive machinery: biased memory search, selective recruitment of standards, and asymmetric scrutiny of congenial and uncongenial evidence.

People do not believe whatever they wish; they believe what they can construct a justification for, and motivation governs the construction. Epley and Gilovich (2016) summarize the mechanics as a matter of differential standards: people ask whether they can believe a welcome conclusion but whether they must believe an unwelcome one. Kahan (2013) reported that such reasoning is often organized around the protection of group identity and, strikingly, that greater cognitive sophistication can amplify rather than dampen it, because able reasoners are better at constructing the required justifications, a finding that has since been contested (Tappin et al., 2021; see Proposition 4). Kim et al. (2020) argue that rational belief maintenance and nonrational discounting can produce indistinguishable behavior, so that the two must be told apart by process evidence, a caution our staged model takes seriously. Gershman (2019) supplies the formal bridge between this psychology and our first construct. He shows that robustness to disconfirmation need not reflect motivated reasoning at all: within a Bayesian framework, a reasoner who can invoke ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses may rationally protect a central belief, because the auxiliaries absorb the blame for anomalous data and so function as what Lakatos (1970) called a protective belt. Lakatos's own criterion for when such protection has gone wrong is historical: a research program degenerates when its auxiliaries stop anticipating novel facts and merely accommodate known ones. Neither account identifies the moment at which protection is switched on for reasons other than evidence. What our framework adds is a specification of that moment, when a simpler rival explanation becomes locally available and identity-relevant, and of what is discarded in it. Gershman's result also sets a standard the framework must meet. Because protection by auxiliary hypotheses can be rational, an ODR attribution requires evidence that the auxiliaries were recruited in response to threat rather than evidence, a requirement the coding rule below makes explicit.

One important complication must be registered at the outset. Flores (2025) argues that identity-protective reasoning is not uniformly an epistemic vice: at the collective level it can support a division of cognitive labor in which groups test divergent beliefs, and for members of marginalized groups it can correct for the distortions that arise from taking ideologically skewed evidence at face value. This defense matters for the addiction field, where the evidential environment surrounding people who use drugs has long been shaped by stigma and moralization (Room, 2005), the conditions under which Fricker's (2007) analysis of credibility deficits applies with most force. A person who uses drugs and discounts a clinician's catastrophizing prognosis, or a community that distrusts institutions that have long pathologized it, may be reasoning protectively and well.

Our construct is therefore not defined by the mere presence of identity motivation. It is defined, as the following sections specify, by the conjunction of identity motivation with the discarding of a simpler explanation that is evidence-consistent, and with the abandonment of the disciplines that would allow the protected alternative to be tested.

Three Guardrails: Parsimony, Noncontradiction, Falsifiability

The framework braids three normative traditions that psychological accounts of belief defense usually leave implicit. The first is parsimony. Sober (2015) shows that appeals to simplicity are legitimate when they proxy for likelihood or predictive accuracy and illegitimate when they merely express taste, and a recent multi-author appraisal argues that parsimony is a weaker proxy for good models than often assumed and can mislead in complex domains (Dubova et al., 2025). We embrace both cautions. The construct does not assert that the simpler explanation is always true; addiction is a paradigm case of multifactorial causation (West & Brown, 2013). It asserts something narrower: that when a simpler explanation is coherent, consistent with the available evidence, and locally adequate, its rejection specifically because of what accepting it would cost is an epistemically distinctive event. Parsimony here functions as a tripwire for motivated addition, not as a metaphysical thesis that reality is simple.

Which explanation counts as simpler is not self-evident, and the holder of a protected narrative may experience it as the simple one. To a counselor committed to abstinence, the claim that medication is just substitution feels like plain truth. The framework therefore uses a relative criterion that can be assessed against the record rather than against anyone's intuition. Following Sober's (2015) treatment of parsimony as a proxy for likelihood, an explanation is simpler in the relevant sense when, given the evidence available at the moment of decision, it accommodates that evidence with fewer ad hoc auxiliary assumptions, meaning assumptions that lack independent support and are introduced only to neutralize a specific piece of evidence. On this criterion the substitution claim is the more complex account, because sustaining it against mortality evidence (NASEM, 2019) requires auxiliaries (that the trials measured the wrong outcomes, that their funders biased them, that this patient differs from every trial participant) that are invoked for that purpose and supported by nothing else. The judgment is made on the record and can be contested on the record.

The second guardrail is noncontradiction. The protected narratives at issue characteristically require their holders to affirm incompatible propositions across contexts: that a program is evidence-based and that inconvenient evidence does not apply to it; that a loved one is fine and that nothing may be asked about how they are. The third is falsifiability in Popper's (1963) methodological sense: not a demand that every belief be testable, but a discipline of specifying what would count against one's claim. The distinctive signature of the event we describe is that the three guardrails are dropped together. The simpler account is refused (parsimony), the incompatibilities of the protected account are tolerated (noncontradiction), and the protected account is rendered untestable by design (falsifiability). Any one lapse is unremarkable; the conjunction, under identity threat, is the phenomenon.

What Correction Research Does and Does Not License

A framework of this kind must be calibrated to the correction literature rather than to its folklore. The claim that factual correction routinely backfires, strengthening the misperception it targets, has not survived systematic scrutiny. Wood and Porter (2019) found factual adherence rather than backfire across a large set of contentious items; Swire-Thompson et al. (2020) traced many apparent backfire findings to measurement and design artifacts; and Nyhan (2021) argues that the durability of misperceptions owes more to the fading of corrective effects and to the elite cues and group identities that sustain false beliefs than to corrections boomeranging.

Accordingly, our framework does not predict that evidence typically makes things worse. It predicts something more specific and more testable: that correction will be least effective, and auxiliary-hypothesis generation most florid, precisely where the corrected belief is load-bearing for identity or institutional position. This is an interaction claim, not a universal backfire claim. Anglin (2019) offers a calibrating anchor: participants who evaluated belief-inconsistent evidence with evident bias nonetheless shifted their beliefs in its direction. Resistance is not the default response to evidence; it is the exception the framework must explain.

Occam's Disposable Razor: The Micro-Moment

We define Occam's Disposable Razor as follows: a bounded cognitive-social event in which an agent (individual or corporate), confronted with a simpler explanation that is coherent, consistent with the evidence available to the agent, and locally adequate to the phenomenon, rejects that explanation primarily because accepting it would threaten identity, belonging, authority, safety, or an inherited narrative, and in doing so suspends parsimony, noncontradiction, and falsifiability together. Three features of the definition deserve emphasis.

First, availability: the simpler explanation must actually be on the table (presented, articulated, or demonstrably known), which distinguishes ODR from mere ignorance. Second, primacy of threat: the rejection must be driven by appraised cost rather than by evidential objection; where a genuine evidential objection is offered and defended, the event is ordinary scientific or clinical disagreement, not ODR. Third, conjunction: all three guardrails are dropped. This is what separates the construct from its neighbors. Motivated reasoning names a pervasive tendency; identity-protective cognition names a motive; belief perseverance names an outcome. ODR names a moment, the instant a working tool is abandoned precisely when it would work, and a moment can be observed, coded, timed, and experimentally provoked in ways a tendency cannot.

The stages are sequential in the ideal type, but need not all be present in every episode; they are offered as a coding frame, each stage paired with the class of observable indicator that would evidence it in addiction-relevant settings and with its role in an ODR coding. The model's value is operational. It converts a fuzzy clinical intuition ("they are in denial"; "the committee would not hear it") into a shared vocabulary that allows two observers to locate an episode, disagree about a stage, and resolve the disagreement against a transcript. Because not every stage must be present, the frame needs an explicit rule for when an episode counts as ODR. An episode is coded as ODR only when five stages are documented on the record. Stage 1 establishes availability: the simpler account was presented, articulated, or demonstrably known. Stages 3 and 4 establish two guardrail lapses: a contradiction is tolerated rather than resolved, and no condition is named under which the protected account would be given up. Stage 5 establishes threat, and it must be shown by markers on the record (explicit reference to role, funding, membership, family standing, or loyalty as the stakes) rather than inferred from the rejection itself, which would make the attribution circular. Stage 6 establishes the parsimony lapse: auxiliary hypotheses are introduced in place of the adequate simpler account, without a defended evidential objection. Stage 2 (momentary recognition) and Stage 7 (closure) strengthen coding but are not required, because both are often visible only in their behavioral residue. Stages 8 and 9 are not part of the ODR event at all; they mark its entry into RRD.

Two clarifications forestall predictable objections. The model does not claim that people consciously traverse the stages. Nor does it presuppose that the discarded simpler explanation is correct. It presupposes only that the explanation was evidentially adequate at the moment of rejection, which is an assessable property of the record, not of the future.

Ratified Reality Drift: The Macro-Process

A single discarded razor is a psychological event. Ratified Reality Drift begins when the protected interpretation leaves the individual and enters circulation. We define RRD as the process by which an interpretation originally preserved through one or more ODR events is repeated, socially rewarded, institutionally adopted, and eventually inherited by people who never faced the original choice, for whom it arrives not as a defended position but as unremarkable reality.

The sociological machinery here is classical. Berger and Luckmann (1966) describe how habitualized actions become reciprocally typified and institutionalized, how institutions acquire legitimating theories, and how successive generations encounter the result as objective fact.

Ocasio (2023) extends the account across levels, tracing how micro-institutions emerge within organizations and are nested in higher-level institutional orders and emphasizing the committed networks of role practitioners that sustain them. What RRD adds to this general machinery is a claim about input and selection: that a systematically important class of the interpretations that get institutionalized consists of those forged in identity defense, because such interpretations arrive already bundled with loyalty tests, insider vocabularies, and penalties for dissent, precisely the features that accelerate ratification. An interpretation that survived because questioning it felt like betrayal will be transmitted with the betrayal-feeling attached.

Fricker (2007) supplies the second mechanism: the distribution of credibility. Ratification is not only repetition; it is the differential assignment of authority to tell the story. In addiction contexts, credibility economies are unusually explicit. Lived-experience status can function as a credential that licenses some claims and disqualifies others; professional titles do the same in the opposite direction; and people currently using drugs are routinely subject to the credibility deficit Fricker calls testimonial injustice, their own accounts of their situation discounted in advance.

Stigma toward people who use drugs is pervasive (Room, 2005), and health professionals frequently regard patients with substance use disorders as manipulative or poorly motivated (van Boekel et al., 2013). RRD therefore predicts that protected interpretations will drift fastest where credibility is most unequally distributed, because the people best placed to voice the simpler explanation are the people least likely to be believed.

The drift terminus is worth marking. At full ratification, the interpretation no longer needs defending because it is no longer experienced as an interpretation. Program manuals state it; induction reproduces it; funding categories presuppose it; and a newcomer who articulates the original simpler explanation is not refuted but corrected, the way one corrects a spelling error. It is at this terminus that the framework connects to documented harm. Where an interpretation hostile to effective treatment has been ratified, for instance the construal of opioid agonist medication as "replacing one addiction with another," people die of the drift's consequences without any individual, at the end, discarding anything at all (NASEM, 2019; Wakeman & Rich, 2018). Festinger's (1957) cognitive dissonance describes the pressure registered at Stage 3 of the model. ODR concerns one particular resolution of that pressure, in which the contradiction is tolerated and auxiliaries are added rather than either belief being revised, and it requires that this resolution be driven by identity threat. Lakatos (1970) described the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses and the difference between progressive and degenerating research programs. His criterion is retrospective and applies to programs over years, whereas ODR identifies a single, datable episode in which auxiliaries are recruited for identity rather than evidential reasons. Vaughan's (1996) normalization of deviance is the nearest analog to RRD. In her account of the Challenger launch decision, anomalies that should have triggered concern were incrementally redefined as acceptable risk until the redefinition was simply how work was done. RRD shares that terminus but differs in its input and its signature. Its input is an interpretation forged in identity defense rather than an incrementally accepted technical anomaly, and it predicts that such interpretations are transmitted with loyalty markers and penalties for dissent attached, producing the defended-then-inherited archival trajectory of Proposition 8.

The residual contribution, then, consists of three claims that none of the neighbors makes jointly: that the discarding of an available, adequate explanation under identity threat is a bounded and codable event; that the event is marked by the joint suspension of three normative disciplines; and that interpretations preserved in this way enter a distinctive institutional career.

Each claim is exposed to test in the propositions below. If they fail, the constructs should be absorbed into their parents.

Three Sites of Drift in the Addiction Field

The Clinical Encounter: "Denial" Reconsidered

The concept of denial has an ambivalent history in addiction treatment: indispensable as a description, disastrous as a warrant for confrontation. The ODR construct recasts denial as a locatable event rather than a character trait. On this reading, the patient who deflects the simplest account of their laboratory results is not exhibiting a defect of personality; they are, at an observable moment, appraising the simpler account as a threat to identity and belonging and paying a real epistemic price to keep a narrative that still organizes their life. This reframing is consistent with the empirical trajectory of the field. The confrontational style once justified by trait-denial performs poorly. In a controlled comparison of therapist styles, the more the therapist confronted, the more the client drank a year later (Miller et al., 1993), and a review of four decades of outcome research found no evidence base for confrontational therapies and several signals of harm (White & Miller, 2007). Motivational interviewing, by contrast, is built on the premise that direct assault on the protected narrative entrenches it and that change talk must be elicited rather than imposed, and it has a substantial evidence base (Lundahl et al., 2010; Miller & Rollnick, 2013). In our vocabulary, motivational interviewing works in part because it lowers the identity price of the simpler explanation before asking anyone to accept it: it addresses Stage 5 of the model rather than colliding with Stage 6.

The construct applies with equal force across the desk. Clinicians hold identity-laden narratives about their own efficacy, their program's philosophy, and their profession's authority, and the clinical literature on stigma shows that health professionals' regard for patients with substance use disorders is frequently low, with consequences for care quality (van Boekel et al., 2013). A clinician who attributes every treatment failure to patient noncompliance has rendered their own practice unfalsifiable. The model codes that blanket attribution as Stage 4, and where the simpler account was on the record (Stage 1), a contradiction is tolerated (Stage 3), the stakes are marked (Stage 5), and auxiliaries take the simpler account's place (Stage 6), the episode is no less an ODR event for occurring on the credentialed side of the encounter. Even the field's language operates as ratification machinery. Experimental evidence indicates that describing a person as a "substance abuser" rather than as "having a substance use disorder" shifts trained professionals toward punitive judgments (Kelly & Westerhoff, 2010), a demonstration in miniature of how a ratified vocabulary carries a protected interpretation into the judgments of people who never chose it.

Recovery Identity and the Mutual-Aid Commons

The social identity model of recovery holds that durable recovery is substantially a process of identity transition, accomplished through migration between social networks and the meaningful activities they sustain (Best et al., 2016; Dingle et al., 2015; Frings & Albery, 2015).

This body of work gives the present framework its most delicate application, because it entails that identity protection is not incidental to recovery but constitutive of it. A recovery identity is precisely a narrative that must be defended for the person to stay well, and the fellowship that reinforces it is doing therapeutic work of demonstrable value (Kelly et al., 2020).

The framework therefore does not pathologize recovery identity. It predicts, rather, a specific vulnerability at the community level: interpretations that have become identityconstitutive for a community recovery will be defended by ODR dynamics even where they conflict with evidence bearing on other people's recovery. The clearest contemporary case is the construal, still audible in parts of the mutual-aid and treatment world, of agonist medications as incompatible with "real" recovery. Under that construal, people using methadone or buprenorphine may be discouraged from full participation or from their medication itself, against strong evidence that these medications reduce mortality (NASEM, 2019). In one cohort of patients beginning buprenorphine treatment, most attended Narcotics Anonymous meetings, only about a third of those attending disclosed their medication, and roughly a quarter of those who disclosed reported being encouraged to reduce or stop it, even though meeting attendance was itself associated with better treatment retention (Monico et al., 2015). The model's stages are readily instantiated: the simpler explanation ("this person is in recovery and takes medication") is available; its acceptance threatens a boundary that has organized members' own transformation; auxiliary hypotheses proliferate; the claim is insulated from disconfirmation; and the interpretation is transmitted to newcomers as settled fact. Symmetry cuts here too. A harmreduction community for which opposition to abstinence frameworks has become identityconstitutive can ratify its own protected interpretations, discounting evidence of benefit from 12step participation (Kelly et al., 2020) with equal fluency. Different shelter; same razor in the bin.

Institutions and Paradigms: The Brain Disease Model and Medication Stigma

At the institutional scale, the framework engages the field's longest-running paradigm dispute. The brain disease model of addiction was advanced in part as an anti-stigma and accessto-care strategy (McLellan et al., 2000; Volkow et al., 2016); its critics argue that its empirical promises are only partially fulfilled and its social benefits overstated (Hall et al., 2015; Heather, 2017), while social researchers have documented how addiction concepts themselves are made and remade by institutional practice (Fraser et al., 2014). The present framework takes no side in that dispute, and this abstention is methodological rather than diplomatic: the framework's subject matter is not which model is true but how each camp handles the moment when the other camp's simpler explanation of a given finding becomes available. Kuhn (1962) taught that paradigm allegiance is never merely evidential; the present framework adds a codable account of the moments at which allegiance is purchased at the price of the guardrails.

A case worked shows how the coding applies to the defenders of a model, not only to its critics. Population survey data indicate that roughly half of U.S. adults who report resolving a significant alcohol or drug problem did so without treatment, mutual help, or other recovery support (Kelly et al., 2017). The simpler account available on that record is that many such problems resolve without following a chronic, relapsing course, so that the chronic-illness description fits treated populations more closely than the population as a whole. A defender for whom the model secures funding, clinical legitimacy, and professional standing may instead reply that the people who resolved their problems were never truly addicted, that their selfreports are unreliable, or that their remission will prove temporary. Each auxiliary may be true in particular cases. The ODR question is whether any of them is offered with independent support or a specified test, and whether its proponent would accept the same reasoning if it were applied to trial data favoring their own position. Where the auxiliaries arrive without either, and the stakes are visible on the record, the episode meets the coding rule. The same rule applies without modification to a critic for whom opposition to the model has become an identity and who meets neuroscientific or treatment-response evidence with auxiliaries of the same kind.

Drug policy supplies the starkest institutional cases because the drift is documented against a body count. The interpretation of agonist treatment as moral substitution rather than medicine was forged in identity-laden commitments (of drug-free treatment systems, of criminaljustice institutions, of communities organized around abstinence) and then ratified into regulation, insurance design, and correctional practice, such that access restrictions persisted long after the mortality evidence was in (NASEM, 2019; Wakeman & Rich, 2018). By the terminus of the drift, officials enforcing the restrictions were not defending anything; they were administering reality as they had inherited it. RRD names exactly this conversion of a defended interpretation into an administrative fact and directs empirical attention to the archival record of the conversion: the hearings, guidance documents, and training materials through which the protected interpretation shed its defenders and became furniture.

Drift can also be reversed, and reversals are where the framework is most exposed to test.

In the United States, office-based prescribing of buprenorphine for opioid use disorder required a separate federal waiver, under a framework created in 2000, until Congress eliminated the requirement in December 2022 (Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 , 2022 , div. FF, § 1262).

The waiver's history, from its creation through successive revisions of its patient limits to its repeal, is exactly the kind of documentary record Proposition 8 requires. The framework predicts that justification density in that record should have risen again during the contest over repeal, as an interpretation that had become furniture was forced once more to defend itself. Institutional inertia predicts no such rise.

Safeguards: Symmetry, the Butter-Knife Problem, and Refusal Versus Discard

A construct of this kind invites predictable misuse. Deployed as a rhetorical weapon, it would allow any disputant to code any resistance as "discarding the razor," converting a diagnostic instrument into an unfalsifiable insult: a butter knife waved as though it still had an edge. Three fences are therefore built into the construct itself. First, the availability and adequacy conditions are evidential, not rhetorical. An ODR attribution requires showing, from the record, that a simpler explanation was present and evidentially adequate at the moment of rejection, that the threat was marked on the record, and that the rejection was not accompanied by a defended evidential objection. Where those showings cannot be made, the attribution fails. Second, the construct is self-applying by design. Any research program built on it must include the reflexive case, the investigators' own handling of evidence unfriendly to the construct, among its objects; a literature that only ever finds ODR in its opponents would itself instantiate Stage 8 of Table 1.

Third, the framework distinguishes defensive discarding from legitimate skepticism. Skepticism proposes a test; discarding forecloses one. The critic who says "your trial population does not resemble my caseload, and here is the comparison that would settle it" has kept every guardrail; the critic who says "trials cannot capture what we do here" has dropped all three.

A final distinction protects something the framework has no business pathologizing. The construct's own metaphor marks it: declining to shave is not the same as throwing the razor away. A person may decline to apply parsimony to a conviction that claims no authority beyond their own life; what the framework names begins only when the tool is discarded, and others are then asked to treat the result as settled. We therefore distinguish protected private meaning from protected public claims. A bereaved parent's conviction that their child's final relapse was not the whole truth of them, a person in recovery's spiritual account of their own transformation, a faith commitment that no evidence could unseat these are unfalsifiable by design and unaffected by the framework, provided they claim authority only over the holder's own meaning. The failure mode the framework names begins at the crossing, when a private conviction demands public immunity and the unfalsifiable narrative claims jurisdiction over another person's treatment options, a program's admission rules, or a policy's design. Flores's (2025) defense of identity-protective reasoning marks the same border from the other side. Protective reasoning that shields a marginalized standpoint against distorted evidence is doing epistemic work; protective reasoning that shields an institution's convenience against undistorted evidence is doing damage.

The construct's target is the second, and its conditions are written to keep it there.

A Research Agenda: Eight Falsifiable Propositions

The framework earns its keep only if it can be wrong. The following propositions are stated so that null or contrary results would count against it.

Proposition 1. Experimentally induced identity threat (for example, a threat manipulation versus a neutral control, administered to treatment staff before they evaluate evidence unfavorable to their program model) will increase rejection of an evidentially adequate simpler explanation, and a prior self-affirmation manipulation will attenuate that increase, a pattern testable in a 2 × 2 threat-by-affirmation design.

Proposition 2. Within coded transcripts of clinical case conferences and mutual-aid business meetings, auxiliary-hypothesis density (Stage 6) will rise discontinuously after threatappraisal markers (Stage 5), rather than accumulating linearly with disagreement per se.

Proposition 3. The three guardrail lapses will cluster. Because their conjunction is part of the definition of ODR, this proposition is tested by coding the lapses independently of, and blind to, the threat and defense codes: among episodes that show threat markers and auxiliary proliferation, refusal of an adequate simpler account will co-occur with contradiction tolerance and falsifiability avoidance at rates above those observed in episodes of ordinary disagreement matched for topic and intensity. Proposition 5. Public commitment will moderately drift: individuals and organizations that have publicly staked their identity to an interpretation (position statements, branded treatment philosophies) will show slower belief revision in response to the same evidence than uncommitted counterparts, controlling for prior belief strength.

Proposition 6. Consistent with Nyhan (2021) and Wood and Porter (2019), corrections will not generally backfire, but correction effectiveness will be inversely related to the identity load of the corrected belief. The interaction, not the main effect, is the framework's prediction.

Proposition 7. Credibility inequality will predict ratification speed: in comparative organizational data, protected interpretations will stabilize into policy fastest in settings where the people positioned to voice the simpler explanation (patients, peer workers, line staff) carry the largest testimonial deficits.

Proposition 8. Archivally, ratified interpretations will show the predicted life course: identity-defensive origins (contested, heavily justified), a ratification phase (adoption into manuals, training, and regulation), and an inheritance phase in which justification density falls toward zero while compliance persists. This is the documentary signature of an interpretation that no longer needs defending because it has become reality. Where a ratified interpretation is later challenged, justification density should rise again.

Suitable designs are within reach of existing methods: vignette and affirmation experiments with professional samples; conversation-analytic and content-analytic coding of recorded case conferences against the Table 1 frame and its coding rule, with inter-rater reliability as the first hurdle; longitudinal comparison of organizations before and after evidence shocks such as the publication of major syntheses; and archival process-tracing of regulatory regimes for agonist treatment, for which the documentary record is unusually complete, including the U.S. buprenorphine waiver from its creation to its repeal.

Limitations and Rival Explanations

Four limitations deserve candor. First, construct redundancy is the objection the framework must answer most directly: is ODR anything more than motivated reasoning with a memorable name? Table 2 concedes the overlap with each neighbor. Our answer is that the construct's content lies in three restrictions the parent literature does not impose (the availability of a locally adequate simpler explanation, the conjunction of the three guardrail lapses, and the boundedness of the event) and in the linkage to a macro-process, RRD, that motivated-reasoning research does not attempt. These restrictions are empirical hostages. If guardrail lapses coded blind to threat do not cluster (Proposition 3), or if auxiliary density tracks disagreement rather than threat (Proposition 2), the construct collapses back into its parents and should be retired.

Second, measurement is genuinely hard: Stages 2 and 7 are inferential, deciding what counts as a Stage 5 threat marker requires coder judgment, and the coding frame will live or die by demonstrated reliability on real transcripts. Third, the framework's normative components ("evidentially adequate," "primarily because of threat") import judgment, and judgment imports the coder's own commitments; the reflexivity requirement and the record-based criteria are disciplines, not solutions. Fourth, the scholarship on which the framework draws is largely Anglophone and high-income in provenance, whereas both recovery pathways and credibility economies are culturally variable. The drift mechanics may differ where treatment systems, families, and fellowships are configured differently, and the framework should travel as a hypothesis, not as an export.

Two rival explanations merit standing acknowledgment. Simple institutional inertia, in which interpretations persist because change is costly rather than because anyone is defending them, will explain some of what RRD claims; the discriminating evidence is the archival life course of Proposition 8, since inertia predicts flat justification density while RRD predicts the defended-then-furniture trajectory. Rational-updating accounts, on which apparent resistance reflects reasonable priors or credible auxiliaries (Gershman, 2019; Kim et al., 2020), will explain some individual episodes; the discriminating evidence is the threat-linked pattern predicted by Propositions 1, 2, and 6. Where the rivals win, the framework should yield.

Conclusion

The addiction field does not suffer from a shortage of evidence; it suffers, recurrently, from a surplus of protected certainty. This article has argued that protection has a locatable anatomy. At the micro level, Occam's Disposable Razor names the bounded moment at which a simpler, adequate explanation is discarded because it costs too much to accept, and at which parsimony, noncontradiction, and falsifiability are dropped together. At the macro level, Ratified Reality Drift names the career by which the interpretation thus protected is rewarded, institutionalized, and inherited until it is no longer recognizable as an interpretation at all. The constructs are symmetrical by design, fenced against rhetorical misuse, respectful of the identity work that recovery genuinely requires and of the private meanings no evidence should be asked to arbitrate, and cashed out in eight propositions that could show them to be wrong. If the framework survives that testing, it offers the field a shared vocabulary for its most expensive habit: throwing away the tool at the moment it works and then teaching the next generation that nothing was thrown.
