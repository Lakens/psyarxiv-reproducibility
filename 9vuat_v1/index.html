What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Artificial intelligence is rapidly becoming an active member of organizational teams. Yet successful human-AI symbiosis depends on more than intelligent algorithms, trust calibration, or effective task allocation. As AI becomes a social interaction partner, organizations must also establish shared moral norms governing how artificial teammates are treated. Surprisingly, little is known about whether employees apply the same ethical standards to AI coworkers as they do to human colleagues or why these judgments differ.

To address this question, we conducted two preregistered online vignette experiments (N = 546) in which participants observed coworkers engaging in identical inappropriate workplace gossip directed either at a human colleague or an AI teammate. Building on Bandura's theory of moral disengagement, we tested whether cognitive moral disengagement explains differences in observers' moral evaluations and willingness to intervene.

Across both studies, participants consistently judged gossip about an AI teammate as more morally acceptable and reported significantly lower willingness to intervene than when the same behavior targeted a human colleague. AI also increased endorsement of all eight mechanisms of moral disengagement. Parallel mediation analyses showed that moral disengagement fully accounted for the effects of target identity on both outcome variables, indicating that AI changes the cognitive processes through which observers evaluate unethical workplace behavior. Although the specific mechanisms contributing unique indirect effects varied somewhat across studies, dehumanization and distortion of consequences emerged as the most robust mediators. An exploratory analysis further demonstrated that these effects generalized across male and female perpetrators.

These findings extend research on human-AI symbiosis by identifying shared moral norms as a previously overlooked prerequisite for successful collaboration. They suggest that organizations should focus not only on designing trustworthy AI systems but also on fostering ethical workplace cultures in which inappropriate behavior is evaluated according to the behavior itself rather than whether its target is human or artificial.

1. Introduction

Imagine overhearing two coworkers ridiculing another member of their team. They mock poor performance, question competence, and make derogatory remarks behind the person's back. Most observers would probably consider such behavior inappropriate and expect someone to intervene. But would the same moral judgment apply if the target were not a human colleague, but an AI teammate?

This omission is particularly striking because organizations are fundamentally social systems. Their functioning depends not only on formal rules but also on informal norms that define which behaviors are considered acceptable and which are condemned. These norms emerge through countless everyday interactions in which employees continuously evaluate the behavior of others. Observers play a particularly important role in this process because they determine whether inappropriate behavior is tolerated, challenged, or sanctioned. Their reactions influence whether isolated incidents remain exceptions or gradually become accepted features of organizational culture. Understanding observer reactions is therefore essential for understanding how ethical climates develop within organizations.

Recent reviews likewise argue that social interaction and team processes remain among the least understood aspects of human-AI teamwork despite their central importance for successful collaboration (Braun et al., 2023; Iftikhar et al., 2023). While previous research has primarily investigated how humans interact directly with AI, comparatively little attention has been devoted to situations in which employees merely observe how AI is treated by others. This represents an important gap because observer judgments influence the emergence of organizational norms regardless of whether AI itself is directly involved in the interaction.

One particularly common, yet surprisingly understudied, form of negative workplace interaction is inappropriate workplace gossip. Gossip is a ubiquitous feature of organizational life (for current reviews see, for example, Khalid et al., 2025 , Sun et al., 2020 , or Wax et al., 2022) and fulfills several important social functions, including information exchange, relationship maintenance, and informal norm enforcement. However, gossip also possesses a darker side. When employees communicate unfairly or maliciously about absent coworkers, gossip becomes a subtle but powerful form of workplace mistreatment that can damage reputations, undermine trust, reinforce social exclusion, and contribute to toxic organizational climates. Importantly, gossip rarely affects only the perpetrators and their target. Coworkers who witness such interactions continuously evaluate whether the observed behavior is morally acceptable and whether intervention is warranted. Through these evaluations, observers contribute to defining the social norms that govern future interactions within teams.

The organizational relevance of these processes is well established. Workplace bullying has been identified as one of the most severe interpersonal stressors in organizational settings and is associated with substantial psychological, organizational, and economic consequences. Across Europe, approximately 3% of employees experience severe workplace bullying, around 10% report occasional bullying, and an additional 10-20% regularly encounter negative interpersonal behaviors that, although not fulfilling the strict definition of bullying, nevertheless represent considerable workplace stressors (Zapf et al., 2020). Comparable prevalence estimates have been reported in Germany (Lange et al., 2019). Such experiences are associated with burnout, psychological distress, absenteeism, reduced organizational commitment, lower productivity, and increased turnover intentions (Raja et al., 2018), generating considerable costs for organizations worldwide (O'Rourke & Antioch, 2016).

However, almost all existing research implicitly assumes that the target of workplace mistreatment is another human employee. As AI increasingly becomes embedded within organizational teams, this assumption can no longer be taken for granted. Employees may not only collaborate with AI teammates but also witness coworkers mocking AI-generated work, ridiculing AI after mistakes, or engaging in inappropriate gossip about artificial teammates. Whether observers evaluate such situations according to the same moral standards as identical behavior directed toward human coworkers remains largely unknown.

At first glance, different evaluations may appear justified. Unlike humans, current AI systems are generally not perceived as capable of experiencing humiliation, emotional suffering, or psychological pain. Consequently, inappropriate behavior directed toward AI may appear comparatively harmless because no sentient individual seems to be affected. Yet this reasoning overlooks a broader organizational issue. Ethical norms are shaped not only by who experiences harm but also by which behaviors become socially accepted. If employees repeatedly tolerate disrespectful communication toward AI teammates because they perceive no meaningful victim, organizations may gradually normalize hostile interaction patterns, reduced accountability, and diminished respect within hybrid teams. Thus, understanding observer reactions to inappropriate workplace behavior directed toward AI is not merely a question about attitudes toward technology. It is fundamentally a question about how moral norms emerge in organizations where humans and AI increasingly work side by side.

The crucial question, however, is why observers should evaluate identical workplace behavior differently depending solely on whether the target is a human colleague or an AI teammate. We argue that the theory of moral disengagement provides a compelling explanation.

According to Bandura's social cognitive theory, individuals generally strive to behave in accordance with their internal moral standards (Bandura, 1991). Moral self-regulatory mechanisms normally discourage unethical behavior by eliciting self-sanctions such as guilt, shame, or self-condemnation whenever one's actions-or one's approval of others' actionsconflict with these standards. These self-regulatory processes enable individuals to maintain ethical conduct without requiring continuous external control.

Importantly, however, these mechanisms are not automatically activated. Bandura and colleagues (1996) argue that individuals can temporarily disengage their moral selfregulation, allowing them to tolerate or justify behavior that would otherwise violate their moral principles. Rather than changing the behavior itself, moral disengagement changes how the behavior is interpreted. Harmful actions can be cognitively reconstructed in ways that make them appear less unethical and, consequently, more acceptable (Bandura, 1999 (Bandura, , 2011)). Bandura (2011) describes several mechanisms through which this process occurs. Individuals may portray harmful behavior as serving a socially desirable purpose (moral justification), use sanitized language that downplays its severity (euphemistic labeling), compare it with more severe misconduct (advantageous comparison), shift responsibility to authorities or situational circumstances (displacement of responsibility), distribute responsibility across multiple actors (diffusion of responsibility), minimize the harmful consequences of the behavior (distortion of consequences), reduce moral concern for the victim (dehumanization), or attribute responsibility for the harm to the victim itself (attribution of blame). Collectively, these mechanisms weaken moral self-sanctions and increase the perceived acceptability of unethical behavior.

We argue that AI teammates may facilitate precisely these cognitive processes. Importantly, our argument is not that employees consciously consider mistreatment of AI to be acceptable. Instead, we propose that characteristics commonly attributed to AI make moral disengagement more likely. Current AI systems are generally perceived as lacking subjective experiences such as emotional suffering, humiliation, or psychological pain. As a result, observers may find it easier to conclude that inappropriate gossip directed toward an AI teammate causes little or no genuine harm. Consequently, they may perceive fewer moral reasons to condemn the behavior or to intervene. This argument extends beyond the simple observation that AI cannot be emotionally hurt. Moral disengagement does not require behavior to become objectively less harmful; it merely requires people to perceive it as less morally relevant. In other words, identical behavior can be evaluated differently because observers cognitively reconstruct the situation in different ways. AI may facilitate exactly this reconstruction. Observers may minimize the consequences of inappropriate gossip because they assume that AI cannot suffer (distortion of consequences), perceive AI as less deserving of moral protection because it is regarded as a technological artifact rather than a social being (dehumanization), or experience less personal responsibility to intervene because no genuine victim appears to exist (diffusion of responsibility). Thus, characteristics of AI may indirectly weaken moral self-regulation by making several mechanisms of moral disengagement easier to activate. This perspective is particularly relevant for understanding human-AI symbiosis. Symbiotic collaboration requires more than efficient task allocation or appropriate reliance on AI recommendations; it also requires shared moral norms regarding how artificial teammates should be treated. Organizations continuously develop these norms through everyday interactions. Every instance of tolerated disrespect communicates which behaviors are considered acceptable within the team. Consequently, whether observers condemn or ignore inappropriate behavior directed toward AI teammates may shape not only interactions with AI itself but also the broader ethical climate of hybrid teams. Understanding observer reactions is therefore essential for understanding how successful human-AI symbiosis develops over time.

Several streams of prior research provide indirect support for this reasoning. Research in human-robot interaction suggests that people do not apply identical moral standards to human and artificial targets. For example, participants showed fewer concerns about administering electric shocks to a robot than would be expected based on Milgram's original obedience experiments (Bartneck & Hu, 2008). Similarly, observers evaluate identical abusive behavior as less problematic when it is directed toward a robot rather than a human, although robot abuse is still generally considered morally wrong (Sanoubari et al., 2021). Importantly, these moral judgments appear to depend on the extent to which artificial agents are perceived as possessing a mind. Lower mind attribution has been linked to greater aggression toward robots (Keijsers et al., 2021), whereas increasing mind attribution reduces the perceived acceptability of robot mistreatment (Keijsers et al., 2022), suggesting that moral concern for artificial entities increases when they are believed to possess capacities such as thinking or feeling.

Related work further suggests that observers systematically apply different standards of responsibility to human and artificial actors. Humans are generally held more accountable for failures than robotic agents (Lei & Rau, 2021 , 2023), while employees become more willing to accept discriminatory AI-supported recommendations when higher levels of moral disengagement are activated (Ebrahimi & Matt, 2023). More broadly, moral disengagement has repeatedly been linked to unethical organizational behavior, reduced accountability, and dysfunctional team processes (Moore et al., 2012).

Taken together, these findings suggest that people do not evaluate human and artificial entities according to identical moral standards. However, they provide only limited insight into why these differences emerge in everyday workplace interactions and whether moral disengagement represents the underlying psychological mechanism.

Accordingly, we propose that moral disengagement explains why observers may judge identical workplace gossip differently depending solely on whether the target is a human colleague or an AI teammate. Specifically, we argue that inappropriate gossip directed toward AI facilitates greater moral disengagement, which subsequently increases the perceived moral acceptability of the observed behavior and reduces observers' willingness to intervene. By focusing on observers rather than perpetrators or victims, the present research shifts attention toward the social processes through which ethical norms are established and maintained in increasingly hybrid human-AI workplaces.

Taken together, the existing literature suggests that people often apply different moral standards to human and artificial entities. At the same time, it leaves a fundamental question unanswered. As AI increasingly becomes embedded in organizational teams, how do employees develop moral norms toward artificial teammates?

Addressing this question is essential for advancing research on human-AI symbiosis. Successful symbiosis is typically understood as the effective integration of complementary human and artificial capabilities. However, sustainable collaboration depends not only on whether humans trust AI, rely on its recommendations, or coordinate tasks efficiently. It also depends on whether AI is incorporated into the social and moral fabric of organizational life. Once AI becomes a teammate rather than merely a tool, employees inevitably develop beliefs about how AI should be treated, which behaviors toward AI are acceptable, and when inappropriate conduct should be challenged. These judgments shape the informal norms that govern everyday interactions within hybrid teams and therefore represent an essential, yet largely overlooked, component of successful human-AI collaboration.

The present research addresses this issue by examining observer reactions to inappropriate workplace gossip directed toward either a human colleague or an AI teammate. We propose that moral disengagement represents the psychological mechanism through which the identity of the target influences ethical evaluations and behavioral responses. Rather than investigating how employees interact directly with AI, we examine how they evaluate the behavior of others toward AI. This observer perspective provides a novel lens for understanding how ethical norms emerge in hybrid work environments.

Accordingly, this research makes three contributions.

First, it broadens research on human-AI symbiosis by shifting attention from functional collaboration to social norm formation. Existing research has predominantly examined how humans collaborate with AI to accomplish organizational tasks. We complement this perspective by investigating how employees construct moral norms once AI becomes a social member of the team. In doing so, we extend the literature beyond cooperation, trust, and technology acceptance toward the interpersonal dynamics that shape everyday organizational life.

Second, we introduce moral disengagement as a theoretical framework for explaining why identical workplace behavior may be evaluated differently depending solely on whether its target is human or artificial. Previous studies have repeatedly documented differences in moral evaluations of humans and artificial agents, yet the psychological mechanisms underlying these differences have remained largely unexplained. By integrating Bandura's theory with research on hybrid teams, we offer a theoretically grounded explanation for how characteristics commonly attributed to AI influence observer judgments.

Third, we demonstrate that observer reactions constitute an important mechanism through which ethical climates develop in hybrid workplaces. Whether employees tolerate or challenge inappropriate behavior toward AI teammates does not merely reflect attitudes toward technology; it contributes to establishing broader organizational norms regarding accountability, respect, and acceptable workplace conduct. Our findings therefore highlight that successful human-AI symbiosis requires not only technological integration but also the development of shared moral standards governing interactions with artificial teammates.

To test these assumptions, we conducted two online vignette experiments in which participants observed coworkers engaging in inappropriate workplace gossip about either a human colleague or an AI teammate. After reading the scenarios, participants evaluated the observed behavior with respect to moral disengagement, perceived moral acceptability, and their willingness to intervene. Conducting two independent studies allowed us not only to replicate the findings across different samples but also to explore whether the gender of the perpetrators influences these evaluations. Whereas Study 1 presented male perpetrators, Study 2 employed identical scenarios featuring female perpetrators.

If AI teammates facilitate moral disengagement, observers should be more likely to cognitively justify or minimize inappropriate workplace gossip when the target is artificial rather than human. Consequently, higher levels of moral disengagement are expected across its underlying mechanisms.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Open Science and Ethics

Both studies were preregistered prior to data collection on the Open Science Framework (OSF), see Kroner & Arlinghaus, 2024 (Study 1) and Arlinghaus & Maier, 2024 (Study 2). Study 2 was designed as a preregistered direct replication of Study 1 using identical materials, procedures, and hypotheses. The only planned difference between both studies concerned the gender of the perpetrators depicted in the experimental stimulus. Ethical approval for both studies was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Bielefeld University before data collection (Study 1: 2023-233, Study 2: 2024-319). After acceptance of this manuscript, study data and materials will be made publicly available.

2.2. Design and Participants

We conducted two preregistered online vignette experiments using a between-subject design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two experimental conditions in which they observed coworkers engaging in inappropriate workplace gossip about either a human colleague or an AI teammate (see Figure 1) and filled out a questionnaire afterwards. Study 2 served as a direct replication of Study 1. The experimental materials, procedure, and questionnaire remained identical across both studies, except that the comic depicted two male coworkers in Study 1 and two female coworkers in Study 2.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009). Assuming a small-to-medium effect size (f² = .075), a significance level of α = .05, statistical power of .90, and nine predictors in the mediation analyses, a minimum sample size of 273 participants was required. To compensate for expected exclusions due to preregistered quality criteria, both preregistrations planned to recruit approximately 314 participants per study.

Study 1 primarily recruited participants through the university's participant pool (SONA), supplemented by volunteers from the authors' personal networks. No compensation was given in Study 1 but students could get partial course credit. Study 2 recruited participants through the German crowdsourcing platform Clickworker. Participants in the Clickworker sample received €1.03 for completing the study.

After accounting for all exclusions (e.g., due to premature withdrawal from the study), we ended up with roughly the number of participants we had planned for. Study 1 sample (N = 275) consists of 182 women and 93 men aged between 18 and 75 (M = 27.09, SD = 9.14). Study 2 sample (N = 271) comprises 107 women and 162 men aged between 18 and 77 (M = 43.92, SD = 12.20).

2.3. Materials and Procedure

After providing informed consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of the two experimental conditions. They were instructed to imagine themselves as observers of a workplace interaction and subsequently viewed a short comic depicting two coworkers gossiping about a third team member.

The experimental manipulation solely concerned the identity of the target of the gossip. Depending on the assigned condition, the coworkers made identical derogatory comments either about a human colleague or about an AI teammate. Importantly, all participants observed the same inappropriate workplace behavior. Thus, the study did not compare appropriate versus inappropriate communication but instead manipulated whether identical bullying-related comments were directed toward a human or an artificial coworker.

To ensure ecological validity, the comic materials were pretested before the main studies. A larger pool of potential dialogue statements was evaluated regarding the extent to which the comments were perceived as rejecting, hurtful, and representative of workplace bullying. Based on these ratings, the final dialogue was selected because it was consistently perceived as highly inappropriate while remaining realistic for workplace interactions.

The visual appearance of the comics was held constant across conditions. In Study 1, the comic depicted two male coworkers, whereas Study 2 used an otherwise identical version showing two female coworkers. Apart from the manipulated target (human vs. AI) and the gender of the gossiping coworkers across studies, all textual and visual elements remained unchanged.

After reading the comic, participants completed the same questionnaire assessing moral disengagement, moral acceptability, willingness to intervene, and additional exploratory questions. Finally, participants answered demographic questions, completed two attention checks, and had the opportunity to provide optional qualitative feedback. The Experimental Stimulus Note. 2.4. Measures

Moral disengagement was assessed using adapted versions of the eight mechanisms proposed by Moore et al. (2012): moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison, displacement of responsibility, diffusion of responsibility, distortion of consequences, dehumanization, and attribution of blame. Each mechanism was measured with three items using seven-point Likert scales from1 = Strongly Disagree to7 = Strongly Agree. Example item: "Compared to other illegal things people do, gossiping about the new coworker/AI isn't worth worrying about." (advantages comparison) Perceived moral acceptability of the observed behavior was measured with four adapted items based on Bartneck and Keijsers (2020). Participants indicated the extent to which they considered the observed workplace gossip morally acceptable using seven-point Likert scales from1 = not at all to7 = Very much. Example item: "How violent would you rate the behaviour?" Willingness to intervene was assessed with four adapted items based on Sanoubari et al. (2021). These items measured participants' willingness to confront the perpetrators or otherwise intervene after observing the gossip scenario on a seven-point Likert scale from1 = Strongly Disagree to7 = Strongly Agree. Example item: "I think someone should have stopped my coworkers right away." Unless stated otherwise, higher values indicated stronger endorsement of the respective construct. All questionnaire items were presented in German.

2.5. Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics 30. To test the preregistered hypotheses, parallel mediation analyses were performed using PROCESS Model 4 (Hayes, 2022). Experimental condition (human vs. AI) served as the independent variable, the eight mechanisms of moral disengagement were entered simultaneously as parallel mediators, and either moral acceptability or willingness to intervene served as the dependent variable. Indirect effects were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap samples, and statistical inference was based on 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals. Because heteroscedasticity could not be ruled out, PROCESS estimated heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC3) standard errors throughout all regression models.

To address the exploratory research question regarding perpetrator gender, data from both studies were combined and analyzed using a 2 (target: human vs. AI) × 2 (perpetrator gender: male vs. female) multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), with moral acceptability and willingness to intervene as dependent variables.

All analyses followed the preregistered exclusion criteria (Arlinghaus & Maier, 2024 , Kroner & Arlinghaus, 2024). Participants were excluded if they did not complete the study, were younger than 18 years, failed one or both attention checks, or exhibited a relative speed index greater than 2.0 (Leiner, 2019). In addition, preregistered sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating the primary analyses after excluding extreme outliers to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

3. Results

>Results

3.1. Study 1: Male Perpetrators

Two preregistered parallel mediation analyses (PROCESS Model 4; Hayes, 2022) were conducted to examine whether the relationship between target identity (human colleague vs. AI teammate) and the two outcome variables was explained by the eight mechanisms of moral disengagement.

Effects on Moral Disengagement (H1).

As expected, the experimental manipulation significantly affected all eight moral disengagement mechanisms. Participants who observed coworkers gossiping about an AI teammate reported higher levels of moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison, displacement of responsibility, diffusion of responsibility, distortion of consequences, dehumanization, and attribution of blame than participants who observed identical gossip about a human colleague (all ps < .001; Table 1). Thus, Hypothesis 1 was fully supported.

Moral Acceptability (H2 and H3).

Consistent with Hypothesis 2, inappropriate gossip about an AI teammate was perceived as significantly more morally acceptable than identical gossip about a human colleague, B = 1.92, SE = 0.16, t(273) = 11.81, p < .001. However, after accounting for the eight moral disengagement mechanisms, this relationship was no longer statistically significant, whereas the total indirect effect remained significant (Table 2), indicating that differences in moral acceptability were explained by moral disengagement.

Examination of the individual indirect effects showed a remarkably selective pattern. Although the manipulation increased all eight mechanisms, only distortion of consequences and dehumanization consistently explained why gossip about AI was perceived as more morally acceptable. Attribution of blame additionally emerged as a small but significant suppressor effect. None of the remaining five mechanisms uniquely contributed to the mediation model. Accordingly, Hypothesis 3 received partial support.

Willingness to Intervene (H4 and H5).

Participants were also significantly less willing to intervene when the gossip targeted an AI teammate rather than a human colleague, supporting Hypothesis 4. Again, this relationship became non-significant after including the eight mediators, while the total indirect effect remained significant (Table 3).

As for moral acceptability, the indirect effect was primarily carried by distortion of consequences and dehumanization. No other mechanism uniquely explained observers' reduced willingness to intervene. Thus, Hypothesis 5 also received partial support.

4. Discussion

Artificial intelligence is increasingly moving beyond the role of a technological tool to become an active member of organizational teams. As this transformation continues, understanding successful human-AI collaboration requires more than examining how humans cooperate with artificial teammates. It also requires understanding the moral norms that govern these interactions and how people respond when AI becomes the target of inappropriate workplace behavior. The present research addressed this question by investigating whether observers evaluate identical workplace gossip differently depending on whether it targets a human colleague or an AI teammate and by examining the psychological mechanisms underlying these judgments.

Across two preregistered experiments, we found consistent support for all preregistered hypotheses. Participants considered identical workplace gossip substantially more morally acceptable when it targeted an AI teammate rather than a human coworker and were significantly less willing to intervene on behalf of the target. As predicted, AI also increased endorsement of all eight mechanisms of moral disengagement. Most importantly, moral disengagement fully explained the relationship between target identity and both outcome variables, suggesting that observers did not simply apply different moral standards to AI and humans. Rather, they cognitively reconstructed the same behavior in ways that made it appear less problematic when the target was artificial.

Beyond replicating the overall pattern across two independent samples, the present findings provide three broader contributions. First, they identify moral disengagement as a psychological mechanism explaining why unethical workplace behavior is judged more leniently when directed toward AI. Second, they extend moral disengagement theory to the perspective of third-party observers, demonstrating that these cognitive mechanisms shape not only how individuals justify their own actions but also how they evaluate the behavior of others. Third, they contribute to the emerging literature on human-AI symbiosis by suggesting that successful collaboration depends not only on technological capabilities but also on the moral norms that regulate interactions with artificial teammates.

An additional strength of the present research is the robustness of the observed effects across both studies. Although gender stereotypes frequently shape evaluations of interpersonal workplace behavior, the combined analyses revealed no evidence that perpetrator gender influenced either moral acceptability or willingness to intervene. Instead, participants' judgments were driven consistently by whether the target of the gossip was human or artificial. This finding strengthens confidence in the generalizability of the reported effects and suggests that the target's ontological status constitutes a substantially stronger determinant of observers' moral evaluations than the gender of the individuals engaging in the behavior.

4.1 AI Changes Moral Judgment Through Moral Disengagement

The present findings advance existing research on moral evaluations of AI by identifying a psychological mechanism through which observers come to judge identical workplace behavior differently depending on whether it targets a human colleague or an AI teammate. Previous research has repeatedly demonstrated that people evaluate abusive behavior toward robots and AI more leniently than equivalent behavior directed at humans (Bartneck & Keijsers, 2020; Sanoubari et al., 2021). However, this literature has largely established that such differences exist, while providing only limited insight into why observers apply different moral standards. The present research addresses this gap by demonstrating that moral disengagement constitutes a key cognitive process through which the ontological status of the target shapes ethical judgments.

According to Bandura's social cognitive theory (1991, 1999, 2016), moral disengagement does not alter individuals' underlying moral values. Instead, it allows people to cognitively reconstruct harmful conduct in ways that reduce anticipated self-sanctions and make unethical behavior appear more acceptable.

Our findings suggest that the mere knowledge that the target is an AI teammate facilitates this reconstructive process. Participants did not simply become more tolerant of workplace gossip toward AI; rather, they systematically endorsed every mechanism that enables individuals to reinterpret unethical conduct, ranging from moral justification to dehumanization and distortion of consequences. This pattern indicates that AI may fundamentally alter the cognitive processes through which observers evaluate workplace misconduct. Importantly, the present findings extend moral disengagement theory beyond its traditional focus on actors who justify their own unethical behavior. Most previous applications of the theory have examined how perpetrators disengage from their moral standards to explain or excuse their own actions. In contrast, our findings demonstrate that similar cognitive processes also operate among third-party observers. Observers appear to morally reconstruct the behavior of others, thereby becoming more accepting of unethical conduct and less willing to intervene when the target is artificial rather than human. This extension is theoretically meaningful because bystanders play a critical role in establishing and maintaining organizational norms. If observers consistently interpret mistreatment of AI as less problematic, they may implicitly legitimize behaviors that would otherwise violate shared standards of workplace conduct. More broadly, these findings extend emerging work on human-AI symbiosis.

Much of the existing literature has focused on factors such as trust, acceptance, transparency, explainability, or decision quality as prerequisites for successful collaboration between humans and AI. While these factors undoubtedly remain essential, our findings suggest that successful human-AI teamwork also depends on the moral norms that regulate everyday social interactions. The challenge is therefore not only to design AI systems that people are willing to trust and collaborate with, but also to foster organizational environments in which inappropriate treatment is evaluated according to the behavior itself rather than the ontological status of its target.

4.2 Not All Moral Disengagement Mechanisms Are Equally Relevant

While the present findings consistently support moral disengagement as the psychological mechanism underlying more lenient evaluations of gossip directed toward AI teammates, they also suggest that not all mechanisms contribute equally to this process. Although the AI manipulation significantly increased endorsement of all eight mechanisms across both studies, only a subset uniquely explained differences in moral acceptability and willingness to intervene when the mechanisms were considered simultaneously. This distinction is theoretically informative because it suggests that AI does not simply increase moral disengagement in a global sense. Instead, certain cognitive mechanisms appear to be particularly relevant when observers evaluate unethical behavior directed toward artificial rather than human team members.

Most notably, dehumanization emerged as one of the most robust mechanisms across analyses. At first glance, this finding may appear paradoxical because AI is, by definition, not human. However, dehumanization within Bandura's framework is not restricted to denying biological humanity. Rather, it refers more broadly to denying a target full moral standing and reducing the empathy and moral concern typically afforded to others. From this perspective, our findings suggest that observers implicitly assign AI teammates a lower degree of moral patiency than human coworkers. Consequently, unethical behavior directed toward AI may appear less objectionable because the target is perceived as less capable of being genuinely wronged. This interpretation aligns closely with previous research demonstrating that artificial agents are attributed fewer mental capacities, emotional experiences, and moral rights than humans (Gray et al., 2007; Weisman et al., 2017).

A similarly consistent pattern emerged for distortion of consequences, particularly with regard to observers' willingness to intervene. This mechanism reflects the tendency to minimize or disregard the harm caused by one's actions. Existing work has repeatedly shown that people consider harm toward artificial agents less morally problematic when these agents are perceived as incapable of suffering (Darling, 2016; Lima et al., 2020). Our findings extend this literature by suggesting that such beliefs do not merely influence moral evaluations directly but also operate through a broader process of moral disengagement. If observers assume that AI teammates cannot experience humiliation, emotional pain, or social exclusion in meaningful ways, workplace gossip naturally appears less harmful and therefore less worthy of intervention. Importantly, this does not necessarily imply that observers become less sensitive to unethical behavior itself. Rather, they appear to perceive the consequences for the target as less severe.

The remaining mechanisms showed a more heterogeneous pattern. For example, advantageous comparison and diffusion of responsibility emerged as unique mediators only in Study 2, whereas attribution of blame contributed to explaining moral acceptability but not willingness to intervene. Rather than interpreting these differences as theoretically meaningful, we caution against overemphasizing individual indirect effects. The eight mechanisms of moral disengagement are conceptually related and empirically correlated, meaning that each unique indirect effect represents the variance remaining after statistically controlling for the other seven mechanisms. Under such conditions, relatively small fluctuations across independent samples can influence whether individual indirect effects reach conventional significance thresholds. Because the overall mediation pattern replicated remarkably closely across both studies and the combined analyses revealed no moderating influence of perpetrator gender, we consider the replicated total indirect effects to provide the stronger theoretical evidence. Thus, the present findings primarily support the conclusion that AI facilitates moral disengagement as a broader cognitive process while suggesting that dehumanization and distortion of consequences constitute its most reliable components in the context of human-AI teamwork.

4.3 Human-AI Symbiosis Requires Shared Moral Norms

The present findings also contribute to a growing shift in how successful human-AI collaboration is conceptualized. Much of the existing literature has focused on technological and cognitive prerequisites of effective teamwork with AI, including trust, transparency, explainability, perceived competence, and decision quality. These factors are undoubtedly essential for enabling productive collaboration between humans and intelligent systems. However, they primarily address whether people are willing and able to work with AI. Comparatively little attention has been devoted to the moral norms that govern everyday interactions once AI becomes an accepted member of a team.

The present research suggests that these social norms constitute an equally important component of successful human-AI symbiosis. Across both studies, participants consistently judged identical workplace behavior differently solely because the target was an AI teammate rather than a human coworker. Importantly, this difference did not arise because the behavior itself changed, but because observers cognitively reconstructed the same behavior in ways that made it appear less harmful and more morally acceptable. In other words, the introduction of AI into a team may alter not only how work is performed but also how workplace norms are interpreted and enforced.

This observation has broader implications for organizational functioning.

Organizations rely heavily on informal social norms to regulate interpersonal behavior, particularly in situations where formal policies provide limited guidance. Bystanders play a central role in maintaining these norms by expressing disapproval, confronting inappropriate conduct, or supporting affected coworkers. The present findings suggest that these corrective processes may become weaker when the target of unethical behavior is artificial rather than human. Although current AI systems may not themselves experience emotional harm, observers' increased tolerance of everyday norm violations may gradually contribute to organizational cultures in which disrespectful behavior becomes more readily accepted. Thus, the consequences of AI-directed mistreatment may extend beyond the artificial target itself and influence how ethical standards are collectively maintained within teams.

More broadly, our findings suggest that successful human-AI symbiosis should not be understood solely as a technological challenge but also as a social and moral one. As AI increasingly assumes collaborative roles in organizational settings, designing systems that are trustworthy and capable will be necessary but not sufficient. Equally important will be fostering organizational cultures in which expectations for respectful conduct are guided by the nature of the behavior rather than by the ontological status of its target.

4.4 Practical Implications

The present findings have practical implications for organizations that increasingly integrate AI into collaborative work environments. To date, discussions of responsible AI in organizations have focused predominantly on technological issues such as fairness, transparency, privacy, explainability, and accountability. While these aspects are indispensable, they primarily address the design and deployment of AI systems. Our findings suggest that equal attention should be devoted to the social norms governing how employees interact with artificial teammates.

One implication is that organizations should not assume that respectful treatment of AI will emerge naturally as AI becomes more integrated into everyday work. Across both studies, observers considered identical workplace gossip more acceptable and were less willing to intervene when the target was an AI teammate rather than a human coworker.

If such reactions become common in organizations, they may gradually normalize disrespectful interactions with artificial teammatesnot because employees consciously endorse unethical behavior, but because these behaviors are perceived as causing less harm and therefore evoke weaker moral concern. Importantly, the significance of this finding does not depend on whether current AI systems are capable of experiencing emotions or suffering. Rather, it concerns the social consequences of employees' behavior. Organizational cultures are shaped through repeated observations of what is tolerated, challenged, or ignored. Bystanders play a central role in this process by reinforcing or discouraging behavioral norms. Consequently, consistently overlooking inappropriate behavior toward AI may unintentionally weaken broader norms of civility and respectful communication within teams, even if the immediate target is unable to experience harm.

Organizations may therefore benefit from explicitly incorporating expectations regarding respectful interactions with AI into leadership practices, onboarding processes, and organizational codes of conduct. Such measures should not be understood as protecting AI for its own sake. Instead, they help establish consistent interpersonal standards that promote professionalism, psychological safety, and respectful communication across increasingly hybrid teams composed of both human and artificial members.

4.4 Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. At the same time, these limitations point toward promising directions for future research on the moral dynamics of human-AI collaboration.

First, both studies relied on vignette-based scenarios. This approach allowed us to isolate the causal effect of target identity while holding the gossip behavior itself constant, thereby maximizing internal validity. However, participants evaluated hypothetical situations rather than responding to actual workplace interactions.

Although vignette experiments are well established for studying moral judgment, future research should examine whether the observed effects generalize to more immersive contexts, such as laboratory interactions, virtual reality, or field studies in organizations where AI teammates are part of everyday work.

Second, the present research focused on workplace gossip as a common and organizationally relevant form of interpersonal mistreatment. Gossip represents only one manifestation of unethical workplace behavior, however. Future studies should investigate whether the observed pattern extends to other forms of misconduct, including social exclusion, verbal abuse, incivility, cyberbullying, or more severe violations of organizational norms. Establishing the generalizability of moral disengagement across different forms of AI-directed mistreatment represents an important next step.

Third, our studies examined evaluations from the perspective of third-party observers. This perspective was chosen because observers play a crucial role in reinforcing or discouraging organizational norms. Nevertheless, unethical workplace behavior also involves perpetrators and targets whose perceptions and motivations may differ substantially. Future research should therefore investigate how moral disengagement operates from the perspective of employees engaging in misconduct toward AI, as well as how such behavior influences subsequent teamwork, trust, and collaboration.

Finally, the present findings should be interpreted in the context of contemporary AI systems. Rapid advances in artificial intelligence are continuously changing how people perceive and interact with artificial teammates. As AI becomes increasingly autonomous, socially interactive, and physically embodied, perceptions of agency, experience, and moral standing may likewise evolve. Future research should therefore examine whether the mechanisms identified here remain stable across different forms of AI or whether increasing anthropomorphism, embodiment, and mind attribution reduce observers' tendency to morally disengage when artificial teammates become targets of unethical workplace behavior.

Taken together, these limitations highlight that the present research should be viewed as an initial step toward understanding the moral foundations of human-AI collaboration. Rather than providing definitive answers, our findings identify moral disengagement as a promising theoretical framework for explaining how people navigate the ethical challenges that accompany increasingly social forms of artificial intelligence.

4.5 Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is transforming organizations not only by changing how work is performed, but also by changing the social contexts in which moral judgments are made. Across two preregistered experiments, we found that observers judged identical workplace gossip more leniently and were less willing to intervene when the target was an AI teammate rather than a human colleague. Moral disengagement consistently explained these differences, indicating that the presence of AI can lower the cognitive barriers that normally discourage everyday workplace misconduct.

These findings extend current perspectives on human-AI symbiosis by suggesting that successful collaboration depends not only on intelligent technologies, but also on shared moral norms. As AI increasingly becomes a social actor within organizational teams, ensuring that ethical standards apply consistently across human and artificial coworkers may prove just as important as developing AI systems that are trustworthy, transparent, and technically capable.
