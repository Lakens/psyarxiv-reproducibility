What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Externalizing is a major spectrum of the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP). Comprehensive models of late-adolescent and adult Externalizing emphasize behavioral disinhibition, but no comprehensive models exist for youth. We developed an elaborated model of youth Externalizing psychopathology that includes an overarching dimension and specific symptom factors. In large diverse population-based and clinicreferred samples, we used confirmatory factor analyses of ADHD, ODD, and CD symptoms and supplemental indices to adjudicate among models of youth Externalizing. Based on model fit, reliability, and discriminant validity, increasingly elaborated models comprising an Externalizing dimension plus seven fine-grained symptom factors outperformed simpler models containing omnibus ADHD, ODD, and CD symptom factors. Within-disorder specific symptom factors showed distinct correlation patterns with cross-disorder symptom factors. Results replicated across clinic and non-referred samples, with Externalizing most strongly reflecting defiant behavior. This elaborated model is foundational for future investigations of measurement invariance, causes, correlates, and outcomes of youth Externalizing.

Introduction

Externalizing psychopathology describes symptoms of DSM (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) disorders including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), and conduct disorder (CD) in children and adolescents (Bezdjian et al., 2011; Lahey et al., 2004; Lahey et al., 2008), substance use disorders in older adolescents and adults (Krueger et al., 2002), and antisocial personality disorder in adults (ASPD; Krueger, Markon, Patrick, Benning, & Kramer, 2007). Like other psychological constructs, externalizing disorders are best characterized by a dimensional, as opposed to categorical, model (Kotov et al., 2017 (Kotov et al., , 2021;; Krueger et al., 2018 Krueger et al., , 2021a)), which posits that they exist along a continuum (Vrieze, McGue, Miller, Hicks, & Iacono, 2013). These disorders are also highly comorbid, suggesting that their covariation may be explained by an underlying dimension, termed Externalizing (Achenbach, 1966; Lahey et al., 2008), that confers risk for each disorder.

Many studies have found that a broad externalizing factor captures much of the covariation among symptoms of these disorders in youth (Achenbach 1966) and provides a useful target for studies of their development, etiology, and treatment. Despite the utility of this broad factor, many studies have also demonstrated meaningful disorder-specific variation (Krueger et al., 2002) or, in some cases, heterogeneity within disorders (Lahey et al., 2004). For example, studies have demonstrated that inattentive and hyperactive/impulsive symptoms of ADHD represent distinct, but related, constructs (Beiser, Dion, & Gotowiec, 2000; Lahey & Loeber, 1997; Molina, Smith, & Pelham, 2001). This is reflected in the DSM-5 as ADHD has three subtypes, one primarily characterized by inattention, one primarily characterized by hyperactivity/impulsivity, and one combined presentation subtype. Notably, some studies support further distinguishing hyperactive from impulsive symptoms (Olino, Dougherty, Bufferd, Carlson, & Klein, 2014), but this is less common. ODD has also been decomposed into dimensions of irritability / negative affect and defiant behavior, which differ in their correlations with other forms of psychopathology and etiology (Burke et al., 2014; Stringaris & Goodman 2009; Burke et al., 2014; Burke, Hipwell, & Loeber, 2010; Herzhoff & Tackett, 2016; Rowe, Costello, Angold, Copeland, & Maughan, 2010; Waldman et al., 2021). Whereas irritability / negative affect is more strongly associated, both phenotypically and genetically, with anxiety and mood disorders, defiant behavior is more strongly associated with ADHD, CD, and subsequent substance use problems (Rowe et al., 2010; Waldman et al., 2021). Finally, CD can be parsed into aggression and rule-breaking dimensions, shown in studies using DSM symptoms of CD (Frick et al., 1991; Frick et al., 1993; Lahey et al., 2000; Maughan, Rowe, Messer, Goodman, & Meltzer, 2004; Tackett, Krueger, Sawyer, & Graetz, 2003) and dimensional measures of conduct problems (e.g. ASEBA; Achenbach & Rescorla, 2001) (Burt, 2009 (Burt, , 2012)).

These two approaches, modeling covariation among multiple disorders as broad factors (i.e., lumping) and modeling dimensions within disorders (i.e., splitting), are typically used separately, such that studies do not include both a broad factor and fine-grained symptom dimensions. Nevertheless, the importance of both levels of the psychopathology hierarchy is emphasized in data and theory driven models of psychopathology (e.g., HiTOP; Kotov et al., 2017) and in recent exploratory investigations of psychopathology broadly (Forbes et al., 2024).

The balance between these two approaches is key. Grouping disorders with different causes, developmental courses, and responses to treatment may mask our ability to accurately operationalize psychopathology constructs and determine how they should be treated (Lahey, 2004). On the other hand, there is value to researching and treating multiple, seemingly distinct disorders that share a common etiology (Barlow, Farchione, Bullis, et al., 2017; Barlow, Farchione, Sauer-Zavala, et al., 2017; Karlsson Linnér, Mallard, Barr, Sanchez-Roige, Madole, et al., 2021).

Only one study to our knowledge has tested a hierarchical model with multiple DSM externalizing disorders in youth. Bezdjian and colleagues (2011) used exploratory hierarchical factor analyses to examine the covariation of symptoms of ADHD, ODD, and CD. Their results indicated substantial covariation among symptoms of these disorders, reflected in the broad overarching externalizing dimension, and subdimensions within symptom clusters, which are reflected in lower-order factors. At the highest levels of the hierarchy, they found support for a broad externalizing dimension that encompassed all symptoms and, at the lowest levels of the hierarchy, evidence that ADHD symptoms cluster into three separable factors of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity and that CD symptoms cluster into symptoms reflecting rulebreaking and aggression. Notably, at the lowest levels they also observed several instances of cross-disorder loadings in which items also loaded onto factors other than the disorder they represent. For example, some CD symptoms loaded on the ODD dimension and some hyperactivity items loaded on the CD dimension.

The Present Study

Herein we propose an elaborated model of externalizing psychopathology in youth. By elaborated, we mean a model that encompasses both a general Externalizing dimension as well as several fine-grained specific symptom factors. This model can best capture both the commonality among and uniqueness of different aspects of externalizing psychopathology. To test this model, we analyze symptom-level data, given that symptoms are more granular than diagnoses or symptom dimensions, thus provide more information which should better allow meaningful distinctions among factors to emerge (Levin-Aspenson, 2023; Waldman and Poore, 2023). Despite the desirability of these two properties of model elaboration and granularity, few studies have embodied them. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, few representative recent studies of Externalizing psychopathology have included symptom-level data that encompass the disorders ADHD, ODD, and CD. We explore the utility of several approaches for adjudicating among alternative models of externalizing psychopathology in youth using confirmatory factor analyses and relevant supplemental indices. Specifically, we compared 1) models with only a broad Externalizing dimension or only specific symptom factors to models that include both simultaneously and 2) models with disorder-level symptom factors (i.e., ADHD, ODD, and CD) to models with more fine-grained symptom factors that represent meaningful heterogeneity within disorders (e.g., inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity separately within ADHD). To make these comparisons and to help adjudicate among alternative models we used a combination of conventional fit indices and alternative indices. The latter reflect important model characteristics such as differences across models in: 1. the percent of variance explained in individual symptoms by the factors in the model; and 2. the magnitude of Externalizing and specific factor loadings, their precision (standard errors, or SEs), and their variability (standard deviations, or SDs). We conducted these analyses separately in two large samples of youth, a population representative sample of twins and their siblings and a clinic-referred sample, which allowed us to examine the replicability of our findings on the structure of externalizing psychopathology as well as its components (e.g., factor loadings' magnitudes and their precision and variability). We hypothesized that more elaborated models (i.e., those with a broad Externalizing dimension as well as fine-grained symptom factors) would better represent externalizing psychopathology in youth than simpler models. This should aid in identifying the most comprehensive and valid operationalization of youth externalizing psychopathology useful for subsequent studies of its etiology, course, correlates, and treatment.

Confirmatory Factor Analyses.

We used three metrics to evaluate the performance of each model: 1) traditional goodness-of-fit indices, 2) the variance explained in each symptom by their respective factors, and 3) information about the standardized factor loadings, including their magnitude (i.e., means), precision (i.e., standard errors [SEs]), and variability (i.e., standard deviations [SDs], and contrasted these across models. We used these supplemental indices as they can aid in adjudicating among competing models that show very similar model fit, or when fit indices may be biased for certain models (e.g., due to overfitting or to the model's fit propensity (Bonifay & Cai, 2017; Preacher, 2006).

First, for goodness-of-fit indices, which indicate the discrepancy between the variance/covariance matrix implied by the model and the variance/covariance matrix in the observed data, we report the c 2 statistic and its associated degrees of freedom, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and its 95% confidence interval, and weighted root mean square residual (WRMR). Each individual fit index has its own strengths and limitations, and no consensus has been reached regarding the superiority of one over the other (Loehlin & Beaujean, 2017). We thus used a combination of CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and WRMR to evaluate the adequacy of model fit and help adjudicate among alternative models, using accepted conventions for excellent fit (i.e., CFI and TLI values ≥ .95 and RMSEA and SRMR values < .05) ( Cole, 1987; DiStefano et al., 2018; Hu & Bentler, 1999). We examined factor loadings for the specific symptom factors on the higher-order Externalizing dimension to elucidate the nature of that dimension and assessed replication across the twin-sibling and clinic samples by examining cross-sample similarities and differences in factor loadings (using Tucker's congruence coefficient; Tucker, 1951).

Second, following recent papers highlighting the utility of these supplemental indices (Waldman et al., 2023; Waldman & Poore, 2023), we report the percent of variance (R² ) explained in the symptoms overall and separately by the Externalizing dimension and specific symptom factors, and the mean, standard errors, and standard deviations of the factor loadings for the Externalizing dimension and specific symptom factors across models in both samples.

We used ANOVAs with targeted a priori orthogonal contrasts to test differences in these alternative indices across bifactor models that include the Externalizing dimension and specific symptom factors. Specifically, these contrasts test differences in models that include: 1. the 7 fine-grained specific symptom factors versus the 3 ADHD, ODD, and CD symptom factors, and 2. orthogonal versus correlated specific factors. These contrasts are intended to test our hypotheses about the importance of increasing model elaboration for the validity of alternative models of youth Externalizing. Effect sizes for these indices are presented alongside all statistical hypothesis tests.

Thirteen item-level CFAs with increasing complexity were specified to evaluate and contrast alternative models of the underlying structure of youth externalizing psychopathology.

Externalizing was parameterized using both a bifactor and a higher-order factor framework.

Bifactor models included a general Externalizing dimension onto which all symptoms load, and specific symptom factors that were orthogonal to the externalizing dimension, onto which only some symptoms load. Thus, the Externalizing dimension captures the covariance among all symptoms, whereas the specific factors capture covariance that is unique to that construct (e.g. inattention). The higher-order model differs by having all specific symptom factors load on the Externalizing dimension. We also tested modified versions of these models in which residual correlations were included among all or some of the specific factors, as described below.

Models were considered untenable if they had factor loadings that were out of bounds (i.e., > | 1|), not in the predicted direction, large standard errors, or negative residual variances.

Methodologically, we demonstrated the value of recently proposed (Waldman et al., 2023) simple descriptive statistical indices as adjuncts to using model fit for adjudicating among alternative structural models of psychopathology. The majority of applied researchers still rely exclusively on conventional fit indices for adjudicating among rival structural models of psychopathology despite their shortcomings. Herein we demonstrate the value of simple alternative indices that resolved important differences among competing models even when they had similar fit indices. Second, we demonstrated that the more elaborated the model, the more variance in the symptom indicators is attributable to the fine-grained specific symptom factors while the variance attributable to the general Externalizing dimension stays quite constant. This means that the specific factors are better targets for investigating causes, correlates, and outcomes in more elaborated models than in simpler models of youth externalizing. Third, we demonstrated a high degree of replication of our findings across multiple distinct criteria (i.e., model fit; the magnitude, precision, and variability of standardized factor loadings; discriminant validity) between non-referred and clinically-referred samples.

There also are several noteworthy specific findings from this study. First, more elaborated models of Externalizing fit best, but other less complex models fit well and thus could be considered as reasonable contenders. Adjudicating among these models relied on alternative indices in addition to conventional model fit indices, as discussed below. Second, the strongest indicator of youth Externalizing appears to be Defiant Behavior based on loadings of the 7 finegrained symptom factors on the higher-order Externalizing factor. Third, there was strong replication between the clinic and twin-sibling samples, given the high correlation of symptoms' factor loadings on the Externalizing dimension and the specific symptom factors. This held for all bifactor models of Externalizing except the Externalizing + correlated ADHD, ODD, and CD factors model, which despite showing good model fit had many problematic model features, likely due to overfitting. Externalizing factor loadings were higher in the clinic than the twinsibling sample, despite similar levels of specific factor loadings across the two samples. This may reflect the greater level of cohesiveness of indicators of overall Externalizing in clinical relative to non-clinical populations.

Fourth, several alternative indices favored more versus less elaborated models of youth Externalizing, despite somewhat similar fit. Increased model elaboration was associated with a linear increase in the variance explained in symptoms by the specific factors -but not by the Externalizing dimension -and a greater balance in the variance explained by the specific factors relative to the Externalizing dimension. While the magnitude of Externalizing dimension loadings did not differ across models, specific factor loadings were higher in more elaborated models, and both sets of factor loadings were more homogenous and more precisely estimated in more elaborated models.

Fifth, there were important differences in the specific symptom factors within DSM disorders in their correlations with the fine-grained specific symptom factors within the other disorders, suggesting unique profiles of comorbidity for each specific symptom factor. Relative to Inattention, Impulsivity and Hyperactivity showed higher correlations with Irritability (in the clinic sample), Defiant Behavior, and Aggression. Relative to Irritability, Defiant Behavior showed higher correlations with Impulsivity, Hyperactivity, and Aggression in the twin sample and with rule Breaking in both samples. Finally, relative to Rule Breaking, Aggression showed higher correlations with Impulsivity, Hyperactivity (in the clinic sample), Irritability, and Defiant Behavior, whereas Rule Breaking showed a higher correlation with Inattention (in the twin sample). Pending further replication, these differences in comorbidity could have both research and clinical implications.

Advantages of an Elaborated Model of Youth Externalizing

Models of Externalizing in the extant literature -whether at the phenotypic (Krueger et al., 2021a; Lahey et al., 2004 Lahey et al., , 2008) ) or etiological level (Karlsson Linnér, Mallard, Barr, Sanchez-Roige, Dick, et al., 2021; Lahey et al., 2011; Waldman et al., 2016) -have exclusively or primarily focused on the broad Externalizing dimension at the expense of the narrower specific or lower-order factors (cf. (Barr et al., 2022)). Herein, we showed how this focus may be myopic.

In this study we integrated DSM symptoms and their fine-grained factors into an elaborated model of youth Externalizing psychopathology. An elaborated model of youth Externalizing as specified herein has a number of advantages. First, it includes and integrates disparate specific fine-grained symptom factors that have appeared largely in isolation in the extant child psychopathology literature. While researchers have delineated specific symptom factors within ADHD (Lahey et al., 1994; Nikolas & Burt, 2010), ODD (Burke et al., 2014; Rowe et al., 2010; Stringaris & Goodman, 2009; Waldman et al., 2021), and CD (Burt, 2009 (Burt, , 2012;; Lahey et al., 1998; Nock et al., 2006; Tackett et al., 2003 Tackett et al., , 2005)), as well as across these disorders (Burt et al., 2001; Dick et al., 2005; Lahey et al., 2004 Lahey et al., , 2008 Lahey et al., , 2011;; Olino et al., 2014 Olino et al., , 2018;; Waldman et al., 2001 Waldman et al., , 2016;; Watts, Poore, & Waldman, 2019), these factors have not previously been integrated into a comprehensive model that also includes an overarching Externalizing dimension. Hierarchical dimensional models of psychopathology typically have focused on higher-order dimensions, such as Externalizing, while neglecting lower-order specific factors.

Our elaborated model rectifies this by encompassing both an overarching Externalizing dimension and 7 fine-grained specific symptom factors without sacrificing one for the other. The advantages of this more elaborated model were demonstrated by better fit, increased explained variance in individual symptoms, more balanced contributions of the Externalizing dimension and specific factors to symptoms' explained variance, better measurement precision and lower variability in the loadings of symptoms on their factors, the replication of these results across both non-referred and clinically-referred samples, and differentiated patterns of comorbidity among the specific factors. Relatedly, it was only in the more elaborated models that an appreciable amount of variance in the individual symptoms was accounted for by the finegrained specific factors, which is important for them to be useful as predictors of important criteria and as outcomes of relevant risk factors. Such nuanced findings would be missed in models that include only omnibus ADHD, ODD, and CD symptom dimensions (Perlstein et al., 2024; Vize et al., 2023).

This elaborated model also facilitated the finding that youth Externalizing most strongly reflects defiant behavior, rather than behavioral disinhibition, in contrast to findings on Externalizing in late adolescence / adulthood (Karlsson Linnér, Mallard, Barr, Sanchez-Roige, Madole, et al., 2021; Krueger et al., 2002 Krueger et al., , 2005 Krueger et al., , 2007;; Young et al., 2000). This insight would be missed in less elaborated models that combine Defiant Behavior and Irritability symptoms in an omnibus ODD symptom dimension. It is our contention that more elaborated models will be particularly useful for elucidating the etiology (Waldman & Poore, 2023) and outcomes of youth

Externalizing. In addition to the importance of model elaboration, we contend that more granular indicators of psychopathology -individual symptoms in the present case -allow for more reliable and valid characterizations of both higher-and lower-order psychopathology factors relative to diagnoses or symptom dimensions (Forbes et al., 2023; Levin-Aspenson, 2023).

Indeed, it is difficult to imagine that our findings supporting the importance of increased model elaboration would have emerged without the use of symptom-level indicators, given the greater information they provide.

Fit Indices, Alternative Indices, and Model Adjudication

Across both samples, the bifactor and higher-order models containing an Externalizing dimension plus 7 fine-grained specific symptom factors with within-disorder correlated residuals fit best, equivalent to the fit of a model with 7 correlated symptom factors. Less elaborated models of Externalizing with 3 specific symptom factors corresponding to ADHD, ODD, and CD fit a bit worse, whereas models with an Externalizing dimension only or 3 or 7 orthogonal symptom factors alone fit poorly. These results suggest that the best use of fit indices may not be to declare that one model is victorious over all others, but rather to exclude certain models as untenable and include others as viable contenders (Waldman et al., 2023). Rather than being in opposition to each other, some of the alternative contender models may have different uses. As an example, bifactor and higher-order models often are very difficult to discriminate based on model fit (Markon, 2019; Mulaik & Quartetti, 1997). Our findings herein suggest that the higherorder model was particularly useful in showing that Externalizing most strongly reflected the Defiant Behavior symptom domain, whereas the bifactor model was particularly useful for investigating the measurement properties of the Externalizing dimension and specific symptom factors. We suspect that the bifactor model also will be more useful in external validity analyses of associations of the Externalizing dimension and 7 fine-grained specific symptom factors with relevant causes, correlates, and outcomes.

Given that conventional fit indices only take one so far in model adjudication, herein we showed the incremental importance of using supplemental indices of model reliability and validity, rather than relying only on model fit as is the norm in studies of the structure of psychopathology. Specifically, we showed that one could meaningfully differentiate among alternative bifactor models of externalizing based on: 1. the balance of variance explained in individual symptoms by the specific symptom factors versus the Externalizing dimension, 2. the magnitude, variability, and (to a lesser extent) precision of loadings on the specific symptom factors and Externalizing dimension, 3. replication of results across non-referred and clinicreferred samples, and 4. differences in the comorbidity patterns of specific symptom factors within DSM disorders. These findings highlight the importance of using alternative indices to augment conventional fit indices in adjudicating among alternative models of psychopathology (Waldman et al., 2023).

Limitations and Future Directions

Our study had several limitations that should be redressed in future research. First, we had to exclude several conduct disorder symptoms due to low endorsement rates, which may not have been the case if we had a much larger sample. While omission of these symptoms may not have affected the structure of Externalizing, it may have limited our ability to precisely measure and effectively cover Externalizing at the higher extreme. Second, our data were limited to parent report. Future research should examine the structure, reliability, replicability, measurement, and external validity of Externalizing using data from multiple sources, including teachers and the youth themselves (once they reach late childhood). One caveat regarding this recommendation is that children have been shown not to be optimal informants for externalizing psychopathology, particularly ADHD and ODD symptoms (Jensen et al., 1999; Loeber et al., 1990 Loeber et al., , 1991)). Third, we tested alternative models of Externalizing across a wide age range.

Future studies using larger samples should examine developmental similarities and differences in this elaborated model and measurement and structural invariance of the Externalizing dimension and the fine-grained specific symptom factors across age.

There are also several profitable future directions that follow from the research we present herein. First, our findings should be replicated in both large non-referred population-based and clinic-referred samples. This should be achievable given the availability of large samples containing data on youth externalizing symptoms assessed using different measures. Such research will help in evaluating the replicability and validity of our best-fitting models, as well as the importance of a model that includes both an Externalizing dimension and more fine-grained symptom factors. Second, related to the point above, our elaborated model of youth externalizing should be extended to examine measurement and structural invariance in other samples that include participants of different racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic backgrounds.

Longitudinal studies also should be conducted to examine phenotypic and etiologic continuities and changes in the Externalizing dimension and the fine-grained symptom factors across development.

Third, our results suggested unique profiles of comorbidity for each specific symptom factor.

Pending further replication, these differences in comorbidity could have both important research and clinical implications. For example, these comorbidity profiles may be useful in guiding the search for genetic and environmental risk factors that are unique to each symptom factor or common across subsets of those factors. Similarly, these profiles could be used to find preventive and therapeutic interventions specifically tailored to particular symptom factors or sets thereof which may help lay the groundwork for more personalized interventions. Future research should focus on extending the discriminant validity findings we presented herein by extensively examining the associations of both the Externalizing dimension and the fine-grained specific symptom factors with external validators across various domains. These may include constructs in the domains of relevant behavioral, personality, and neurocognitive correlates (e.g., Poore & Waldman, 2025), as well as causes such as genetic and environmental risk factors (e.g., Hicks et al., 2004; Waldman & Poore, 2023), correlates (e.g., neurocognitive underpinnings), and outcomes (e.g., various aspects of adult adjustment). This can help find differences between specific factors that were difficult to distinguish in their comorbidity patterns in the current study (i.e., impulsivity and hyperactivity). These studies also can help flesh out the overarching construct of Externalizing in youth to address whether its central construct is Defiant Behavior, behavioral disinhibition, or some combination of other traits.

Fourth, our model of youth Externalizing should be broadened to include measures outside of DSM symptoms such has been done in the literature on Externalizing in late adolescence and adulthood, both phenotypically (Krueger et al., 2002 (Krueger et al., , 2005 (Krueger et al., , 2007 (Krueger et al., , 2021b) ) and etiologically (Karlsson Linnér, Mallard, Barr, Sanchez-Roige, Dick, et al., 2021; Young et al., 2000). Although some attempts along these lines have been made for youth Externalizing (Mann et al., 2017), more research of this sort is needed.

The present findings also have methodological implications for future research on psychopathology beyond the realm of youth Externalizing.
