Answers were coded as 0=no, and 1=yes. Items will be summed, resulting in a score from 0 to 11 with higher scores indicating higher levels of material hardship.

Neighborhood collective efficacy (NCE) is a combined measure of social control (5 items - someone would intervene if: children were skipping school and hanging out on the street, children were spray-painting buildings, children were showing disrespect to an adult, a fight broke out in front of the house of building, the fire station closest to the neighborhood was threatened) and levels of cohesion and trust (4 items - neighbors are willing to help, it is a close-knit neighborhood, people generally get along with each other, people share the same values) in the neighborhood. Each item could be answered from 1=very likely to 4=very unlikely. We will sum the items to yield a total neighborhood collective efficacy score that could range from 9 to 38, with higher scores indicating lower neighborhood collective efficacy. Both mother and father completed the scale. In our study, we will use data reported by the mother, and in cases where data was missing, we used the father’s reported scores if these were available (a total of 195 mothers’ missing reports on this scale were filled with the father’s scores).

Child emotional and physical maltreatment was measured with three subscales from the Conflict Tactics Scale - Parent Child Version (CTS-PC) (Straus et al., 1998), informing about the frequency with which the primary caregiver used psychological aggression, physical assault, and neglect against the child in the past year. The primary caregiver was asked a series of questions about their behavior as well as the behavior of the other parent, for example “How often in the past year did you slap the child on the hand, arm, or leg?” with frequency response options ranging from “it never happened” to “more than 20 times.”

Each of the subscales consists of five items. In line with recommendations from the scale developer (Straus et al., 1998) we will assign the midpoint of the ranges in the following manner: responses of “never” and “not in the past year” will be coded as 0; “once” will be coded as 1; “twice” will be coded as 2; “3-5 times” will be coded as 4; “6-10 times” will be coded as 8; “11-20 times” will be coded as 15; and “20 or more times” will be coded as 25. The five questions for each subscale will then be added together to create an overall count of the number of times the form of abuse had occurred in the past year.

Scores of material hardship, neighborhood collective efficacy, and child emotional and physical maltreatment will be standardized based on our study sample.

Receptive vocabulary and verbal ability were measured with the third edition of the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT-III) (Dunn & Dunn, 1997). During the PPVT, the interviewer would read a word and ask the child to identify the picture that corresponds to that word among a set of four pictures on an easel or activity book. Child’s scores were computed based on the information recorded by the interviewer in Section B of the In-Home Activity Workbook (Bendheim-Thoman Center for Research and Wellbeing & Columbia Population Research Center, 2018). PPVT has been used as a measure of cognitive competence in early childhood and of school readiness (Razza et al., 2010). In our study, we will use PPVT raw scores and standardize them based on our study sample.

Outcome of childhood loneliness: educational achievement

Educational achievement will be assessed with three different outcomes reported by the young adult at the age of 22: out-of-school suspension, high school grades, and educational attainment.

Out-of-school suspension refers to whether the adolescent was temporarily excluded from school as a disciplinary action from 4th grade to 12th grade. That is after the age of 9, which is after the time of exposure in our study. This variable will be coded as 0 if the participant didn’t receive an out-of-school suspension or if this happened before 4th grade (n=34), and as 1 if the participant received an out-of-school suspension from 4th grade to 12th grade.

High school grades are assessed with the question “What best represents your grades in high school?”. Originally, scores range from 1 (mostly D’s or lower) to 8 (mostly A’s). This variable will be dichotomized into half B’s/half Cs or lower (=1), and mostly B’s or higher (=0).

Educational attainment is defined as the highest level of education completed by the age of 22, being 1=less than high school, 2=high school or equivalent, 3=some college or technical education, and 4=completed college or graduate school.

statistical_tests To answer our first research question (Are early-life socio-environmental indicators and verbal abilities associated with childhood loneliness?), we will use data collected at birth, and at years 3, 5, and 9. We will fit several sets of ordinal proportional odds logistic regression models to explore if the socio-environmental indicators (material hardship, neighborhood collective efficacy, child’s emotional and physical maltreatment) and verbal ability scores at age 5 are associated with childhood loneliness at ages 5 and 9 (persistent, transient, or never) while adjusting for relevant confounders (measured at birth and year 3). For this, we will first fit three analogous models, including each one of the socio-environmental indicators as the predictor. Second, we will include the three socio-environmental indicators as predictors in the same regression model. Third, we will fit a model with verbal ability as the predictor. Fourth, we will run three analogous models, including one of three socio-environmental indicators, the verbal ability indicator, and an interaction term between each of the socio-environmental indicators and verbal ability. All models will include demographic variables and the mother’s and child’s characteristics at the child’s age of 3 as confounders. See Table 1 for a summary of all the planned regression models. The proportional odds assumption will be evaluated with the Brant-Wald test and by running stratified binomial regression models for “persistent or transient” versus “never” lonely, and “persistent” versus “transient or never” lonely to compare the differences in the predictor’s estimates between the two models. The analyses will be conducted across imputed datasets, and estimates will be pooled using Rubin’s rules.

To answer the second research question (Are children who report persistent loneliness during childhood at a higher risk of later poorer educational achievement?), we will use data collected at birth and years 3, 5, 9, and 22. We will use propensity score weighting for covariate adjustment (step 1, design phase) before the estimation of the marginal effects of interest (step 2, analysis phase). As suggested in Nguyen & Stuart (2024), we will conduct multiple imputation as an early step (step 0) in our analysis. Then, we will estimate the propensity score weights in the imputed datasets for each of the exposure regimes (“within” method).

Since we have a multilevel exposure, to achieve covariate balance among the three loneliness categories (persistent, transient, and never lonely), we will use Generalized Propensity Scores (GPSs) (Imbens, 2000; Imai & van Dyk, 2004). GPSs extend the traditional binary propensity score framework to multi-category exposures, allowing us to estimate the conditional probability of assignment to each childhood loneliness group given the observed covariates. These estimated probabilities can then be used to construct weights that generate a weighted pseudo-population in which the distribution of covariates is balanced across the loneliness categories.

Our generalized propensity score models will include as covariates: demographic variables, mother’s and child’s characteristics at the child’s age of 3, and socioenvironmental conditions and child’s verbal ability at the child’s age of 5. These variables are expected to be either related to educational attainment or both educational attainment and childhood loneliness (Bergstra et al., 2019; Brookhart et al., 2006). This decision is based on previous subject matter theory, so it will be independent of results arising from research question 1. Weight estimation will be conducted using different estimation methods whose performance will be compared in terms of achieved covariate balance (e.g., covariate balancing propensity scores, generalized boosted models). The objective will be to obtain absolute standardized mean differences of < 0.10 for all covariates in the weighted sample for each of the pairwise comparisons between exposure groups (i.e., persistent vs. never, persistent vs. transient, and transient vs never). We will consider stabilizing or truncating the weights in the case of extreme weights.

Finally, we will use weighted g-computation to estimate pairwise average treatment effects (ATE) (step 2) (Arel-Bundock et al., 2024; Greifer, 2025). The ATE of exposure a0 relative to exposure a1 refers to the comparison of mean outcomes had the entire population been observed under one exposure a0, versus had the entire population been observed under another exposure, a1 (McCaffrey et al., 2013). We will estimate three marginal ATEs per outcome (i.e., out-of-school suspension, high school average grades, and educational attainment at age 22), corresponding to the following pairwise contrasts: reporting persistent loneliness (a=2) during childhood compared to not reporting it (a=0), reporting persistent loneliness (a=2) compared to reporting transient loneliness (a=1), and reporting transient loneliness (a=1) compared to not reporting it (a=0). Estimation of confidence intervals will be done by bootstrapping.

R packages mice (for multiple imputation), MatchThem (for estimation of the weights in the imputed datasets), weightIt (for propensity score and weight estimation), cobalt (for covariate balance assessment), and marginaleffects (for treatment effect estimation) will be used to conduct the analysis.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

We examine (1) whether indicators of early-life adversity are associated with children's loneliness complaints and their recurrence at ages 5 and 9; and (2) whether children's loneliness complaints are associated with poorer educational achievement measured at age 22 after adjusting for early-life adversity indicators. We used data from the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study (US, original sample n=4,898; study sample n=2,456). Children's loneliness complaints were reported by the principal caregiver. We applied (1) binomial logistic regression and (2) weighted g-computation.

In our sample, 6.8% of children had recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness complaints. Indicators of socioenvironmental early-life adversity were associated with higher odds of loneliness complaints. Moreover, higher material hardship and lower neighborhood collective efficacy were associated with higher odds of complaints recurring at ages 5 and 9. We observed no association between caregiverreported loneliness in childhood and later educational achievement. We note limitations in our measurement of childhood loneliness.

Introduction

Loneliness is a distressing experience that arises perceiving that one's interpersonal needs and expectations are not satisfied by one's interpersonal relationships (Cunningham et al., 2025). Previous studies show that at age 5 children can provide valid definitions of loneliness, identify interpersonal contexts and associated aversive feelings (i.e., sadness, aloneness), as well as suggest ways of coping (i.e., make contact with others) (Cassidy & Asher, 1992; Qualter et al., 2015). As children grow older, definitions of loneliness become more abstract, and the psychological component (i.e., feeling part of a peer group) gains relevance (Verity et al., 2021).

Loneliness during the school years has been associated with more negative attitudes toward school, lower academic performance, and greater school drop-out intentions. However, most existing evidence is cross-sectional (Eccles et al., 2023; Frostad et al., 2015; Jefferson et al., 2023), limiting conclusions about the directionality of these associations. One exception is a longitudinal study observing that those who reported loneliness at age 12, and recurrently at ages 12 and 18, were more likely to finish school with lower qualifications (Matthews et al., 2023).

The association between early-life loneliness and educational achievement is possibly explained to some extent by shared socioenvironmental risk factors. For instance, family economic hardship may hinder a child's social participation (Hjalmarsson & Mood, 2015) and worsen the home environment by increasing parental stress (Schneider et al., 2017). Neighborhood quality and safety may affect youths' mental health and trust in others (Donnelly et al., 2016), influencing the way they build their relationships. Child maltreatment has been associated with higher loneliness since early in life, with a recent review and meta-analysis emphasizing that this association is especially visible in the case of emotional abuse or emotional neglect (De Heer et al., 2024). All three socioenvironmental factorsfamily material hardship, neighborhood quality, and child maltreatment -have been previously associated with poorer educational achievement (Brooks-Gunn & Duncan, 1997; Nieuwenhuis & Hooimeijer, 2016; Zhang et al., 2025).

Child's early-life cognitive ability is another potential confounder in the association between childhood loneliness and educational achievement. Verbal ability is a strong predictor of school readiness and later educational success (Pace et al., 2017), and a previous prospective study observed that higher vocabulary in childhood predicted lower loneliness in adolescence (Fuhrmann et al., 2022). Some evidence also suggests early-life language ability contribute to emotional and behavioral development in childhood (Clegg et al., 2015). Notably, children from lower socioeconomic status families tend to show lower language skills than their peers by school entry (Pace et al., 2017).

Loneliness becomes problematic when it persists over time, contributing to further social withdrawal and disconnection (Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010; Vanhalst et al., 2015). Recurrent loneliness may even be more critical at a young age, given that loneliness shows a relatively high rank-order stability over the life span (Buecker et al., 2021; Mund et al., 2020), and that childhood and adolescence are sensitive periods for the development of social and cognitive skills and formation of social connections (Blakemore & Mills, 2014; Orben et al., 2020). However, most of the previous studies exploring early-life adversity, loneliness, and educational achievement have been focused on adolescence and young adulthood.

Therefore, our study aims are twofold. First, we explore whether three early-life socioenvironmental indicators and child's verbal ability are associated with child's caregiver-reported loneliness complaints, categorized as complaints recurring at both ages 5 and 9 (hereafter "recurrent"), transient (reported at only one of these ages), or not reported. Second, we investigate whether caregiverreported loneliness complaints are associated with a higher risk of poorer educational achievement measured in young adulthood, adjusting for early-life socioenvironmental conditions and child's verbal ability as potential confounders.

We hypothesize that children whose caregivers report recurrent complaints of loneliness will more often come from poorer socioenvironmental contexts and have lower verbal ability. Moreover, we expect these children to be at a higher risk of poorer educational achievement measured in young adulthood, even after considering initial disparities in early-life risk factors.

Method

Sample

We leveraged data from the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study (FFCWS), a longitudinal birth-cohort study from 20 large urban U.S. cities. These cities were selected to represent all U.S.

cities with a population of 200,000 or more. Baseline data were collected at the hospital when the participating children were born, beginning in 1998. Additional data were collected by telephone interviews and in-home assessments when the children reached ages 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, and 22. The original sample included 4,898 children. By design, FFCWS oversampled the children born to unmarried mothers (n = 3,710) compared to children born to married parents (n =1,187) (Reichman et al., 2001). Parents provided written informed consent prior to participation. Follow-up and additional modules (e.g., home-visits) included additional consent forms.

For the current study, we used data from 2,466 children who had complete data on the loneliness measures at ages 5 and 9. Of these 2,466, 10 participants were excluded due to attending an ungraded high school. Therefore, our final sample consists of 2,456 participants.

Measures

Loneliness was measured at ages 5 and 9 with the item "Child complains of loneliness" from the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL/4-18) (Achenbach, 1992). The item was answered by the child's mother or principal caregiver, who was asked to indicate whether the statement was not true (=0), sometimes or somewhat true (=1), or very true or often true of their child (=2). Because only 5 caregivers reported the item as "very true or often true" at both time points, we dichotomized the scores, considering values of 1 and 2 as the caregiver reporting the child's loneliness complaints at that time point (=1), versus not reporting loneliness complaints (=0), to ensure enough participants in each exposure subgroup. Based on scores from both waves, we created three groups: "recurrent" (n=168) if the caregiver reported loneliness complaints at both ages 5 and 9, "transient" (n=603) if the caregiver reported loneliness complaints at one time point, and "not reported" if the caregiver did not report loneliness complaints at either age (n=1,685).

Measures of early-life adversity at age 5:

Material hardship was a measure of to what extent basic needs are covered in the household and the capability to meet expenses. It consisted of eleven items answered by the child's mother that explored if in the last 12 months there have been problems due to the unpayment of the rent or mortgage, unpayment of gas, oil or electricity bills, covering doctor visits or hospital stay expenses, if children or adults in the household had been on the need of receiving meals for free due to lack of money, or if there has been need to borrow money from friends or family to help pay bills. Answers were coded as0=no, and1=yes. We summed the items, resulting in a score from 0 to 11 with higher scores indicating higher levels of material hardship.

Neighborhood collective efficacy (NCE) was a combined measure of social control (5 itemssomeone would intervene if: children were skipping school and hanging out on the street, children were spray-painting buildings, children were showing disrespect to an adult, a fight broke out in front of the house of building, the fire station closest to the neighborhood was threatened) and levels of cohesion and trust in the neighborhood (4 items -neighbors are willing to help, it is a close-knit neighborhood, people generally do not get along with each other, people do not share the same values). Each item could be answered from1=very likely to4=very unlikely. Two items were reversed-coded. We summed the items to yield a total neighborhood collective efficacy score that could range from 9 to 36, with higher scores indicating lower neighborhood collective efficacy. Both mother and father completed the scale. In our study, we used data reported by the mother, and in cases where data was missing, we used the father's reported scores if these were available (a total of 210 mothers' missing reports on this scale were filled with the father's scores).

Child emotional and physical maltreatment was measured with three subscales from the Conflict Tactics Scale -Parent Child Version (CTS-PC) (Straus et al., 1998) informing about the frequency with which the primary caregiver used psychological aggression, physical assault, and neglect against the child in the past year. The primary caregiver was asked a series of questions about their behavior as well as the behavior of the other parent, for example, "How often in the past year did you slap the child on the hand, arm, or leg?" with frequency response options ranging from "it never happened" to "more than 20 times".

Each of the subscales consisted of five items. In line with recommendations from the scale developer (Straus et al., 1998) we assigned the midpoint of the ranges in the following manner: responses of "never" and "not in the past year" are coded as 0; "once" as 1; "twice" as 2; "3-5 times" as 4; "6-10 times" as 8; "11-20 times" as 15; and "20 or more times" as 25. The five questions for each subscale were then added together to create an overall count of the number of times the form of abuse had occurred in the past year.

Final scores of material hardship, neighborhood collective efficacy, and child emotional and physical maltreatment were standardized (z-scores) within our study sample.

Receptive vocabulary and verbal ability were measured with the third edition of the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT-III) (Dunn & Dunn, 1997). During the PPVT, the interviewer would read a word and ask the child to identify the picture that corresponds to that word among a set of four pictures on an easel or activity book. Child's scores were computed based on the information recorded by the interviewer in Section B of the In-Home Activity Workbook (Bendheim-Thoman Center for Research and Wellbeing & Columbia Population Research Center, 2018). PPVT has been used as a measure of cognitive competence in early childhood and of school readiness (Razza et al., 2010). In our study, we used the PPVT raw scores and standardized (z-scores) them within our study sample.

Educational achievement measured at age 22:

Out-of-school suspension referred to whether the participant was temporarily excluded from school as a disciplinary action between the 4 th and the 12 th grade, that is, after the time of exposure in our study (i.e., after age 9). This variable was coded as 0 if the participant didn't receive an out-of-school suspension or if this happened before 4 th grade (n=34), and as 1 if the participant received an out-ofschool suspension between the 4 th and the 12 th grade.

High school grades were assessed with the question "What best represents your grades in high school?". Originally, the scores ranged from 1 (mostly D's or lower) to 8 (mostly A's). This variable was dichotomized into half B's/half Cs or lower (=1), and mostly B's or higher (=0).

Educational attainment was defined as the highest level of education completed by the age of 22, being1=less than high school,2=high school or equivalent,3=some college or technical education, and4=completed college or graduate school. We dichotomized the scores into high school/equivalent or lower (=1), and some college/technical education or higher (=0).

Potential confounders

All models were adjusted for child's sex (boy/girl), child's race/ethnicity self-reported at age 22 or at age 15 if the age-22 self-report was missing (White non-Hispanic, Black/African American non-Hispanic, Hispanic/Latino, other non-Hispanic, multi-racial non-Hispanic), mother's age in years, mother's ethnicity (White non-Hispanic, Black non-Hispanic, Hispanic, other), mother's place of birth (outside the US: yes/no), child's low weight at birth (yes/no <2,500g), and parents' relationship status (married, cohabiting not married, not cohabiting) measured at the time of birth. Moreover, motherreported child's general health status (fair/poor health, good/very good/excellent), and child's physical disability (yes/no) (e.g., blindness, respiratory problems, speech problems, Down syndrome), measured at the child's age of 3. Additionally, we included adjustments for several measures regarding mother's education, cognitive abilities and health state measured at child's age of 3: educational attainment (Less than HS, HS or equivalent, Some college, tech; College or graduate), cognitive ability (measured with the Similarities subtest of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale -Revised, WAIS-R), self-reported general health status (1=fair/poor health,0=good/very good/excellent), and fulfilment of clinical criteria for four mental health conditions: major depressive episode (conservative criteria), generalized anxiety disorder, alcohol abuse, and drug abuse. These were measured with the Composite International Diagnostic Interview -Short Form (CIDI-SF), a standardized instrument for assessment of mental disorders intended for use in epidemiological, cross-cultural, and other research studies (Kessler et al., 1998). Statistical analysis

First, we handled missing values in our final sample using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), including all study variables and four auxiliary variables (i.e., NCE at age 3, non-violent discipline CTS's subscale at age 5, child's PPVT at age 9, and educational attainment reported by the primary caregiver at age 22), obtaining 20 imputed datasets.

To answer our first research question we used data collected at birth, and at ages 3, 5, and 9 to fit several sets of binomial logistic regression models to explore associations of early-life adversity indicators with the likelihood of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints ("recurrent or transient"

versus "not reported") and with the likelihood of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints recurring at ages 5 and 9 ("recurrent" versus "transient or not reported"). For each comparison, we first ran three analogous models, including one of the socioenvironmental indicators (i.e., material hardship, neighborhood collective efficacy, and child maltreatment) as the predictor (Models 1-3). Second, we included the three socioenvironmental indicators as predictors in the same regression model (Model 4). Third, we fit a model with verbal ability as the predictor (Model 5). Fourth, we ran three analogous models, including one of three socioenvironmental indicators, the verbal ability indicator, and an interaction term between each of the socioenvironmental indicators and verbal ability (Models 6-8).

We had no prior hypotheses on the direction or magnitude of the interaction. All models were adjusted for all the demographic variables and the mother's and child's characteristics at the child's age of 3.

To examine associations between caregiver-reported loneliness complaints and educational achievement measured in young adulthood (research question 2), we used data collected at birth and at ages 3, 5, 9, and 22. There are several potential sources of confounding, and we aimed to adjust for all measured confounders. To achieve covariate balance among the three loneliness complaints groups, we used Generalized Propensity Scores (GPSs) (Imbens, 2000; Imai & van Dyk, 2004), which extend the traditional binary propensity score framework to multi-category exposures, allowing us to estimate the probability of assignment to each group given the observed covariates. Propensity scores are a method for covariate adjustment that is particularly useful when adjusting for a high number of covariates, as they summarize the high-dimensional covariate information into a single score representing the probability of the exposure given the observed covariates. In our case, the propensity scores are used to weight individuals according to the probability of receiving the exposure given the observed covariates. This weighting creates a pseudo-population in which the distribution of confounders is independent of the exposure, aiming to resemble the balance expected under a randomized experiment. An important advantage of propensity score weighting is that covariate balance can be directly assessed after weighting, typically by plotting the standardized mean differences across exposure groups. This allows comparison of alternative model specifications and the selection of the specification that achieves the best balance with the data at hand. In addition, modern implementations allow the use of flexible, data-adaptive methods (e.g., machine learning algorithms) to estimate the propensity score. These methods can capture nonlinearities and interactions without requiring the analyst to prespecify them, increasing the likelihood of correct model specification and improving confounding control (Greifer, 2025).

We used three different adjustment sets to compare the relative importance of key covariates. First, minimally adjusted, including child's sex, child's race/ethnicity, mother's age, and parental relationship status at birth. Second, fully adjusted, including an array of covariates expected to be either related to young adulthood educational achievement or both educational achievement and childhood loneliness based on previous subject matter theory (Bergstra et al., 2019). Namely, we included the following covariates: child's sex, child's race/ethnicity, mother's age in years, mother's race/ethnicity, mother's place of birth, child's low weight at birth, and parents' relationship status at the time of child's birth; child's poor general health status, child's physical disability, mother's education attainment, mother's cognitive ability, mother's poor general health status, and mother's fulfilment of clinical criteria of major depressive episode, generalized anxiety disorder, alcohol abuse, and drug abuse when the child was aged 3; and material hardship, neighborhood collective efficacy, child maltreatment and child's verbal ability when the child was aged 5 (for a graphical representation see Figure 1). Third, we conducted fully adjusted models that additionally included child temperament as a sensitivity analysis. In the presence of additional sources of confounding not accounted for in our analysis, the results may be biased.

We compared the performance of different estimating methods and model specifications in terms of resulting absolute standardized mean differences between exposure groups and average effective sample sizes across imputations. A satisfactory covariate balance is defined by an absolute standardized mean difference of < 0.10. The final models used generalized boosted models for propensity score weights estimation (McCaffrey et al., 2013).

Finally, we used weighted g-computation to estimate pairwise average treatment effects (ATE) (Greifer, 2025; Snowden et al., 2011). The ATE of exposure a0 relative to exposure a1 refers to the comparison of mean outcomes had the entire population been observed under one exposure, a0, versus had the entire population been observed under another exposure, a1 (McCaffrey et al., 2013).

G-computation is commonly used in settings with time-varying exposure and time-varying confounders affected by prior exposure, where standard stratification-based regression adjustment yields biased estimates. In a simpler setting like ours, where all variables are measured at baseline, gcomputation is still useful because it targets the marginal exposure-outcome association (populationaverage) rather than the conditional association (covariate-specific). We focus on marginal associations because they correspond to contrasts between the expected potential outcomes under exposure a1 and a0 in the entire population, whereas conditional associations describe the association within covariate strata, that is, within individuals with a fixed set of covariate values (Greifer, 2025).

Conditional effects can be harder to interpret, especially when the model includes non-linear terms or interactions. Furthermore, the g-computation framework can be combined with flexible or nonparametric estimators (e.g., machine-learning methods) for the outcome or propensity score models, which reduces reliance on strict parametric assumptions and increases the likelihood of correct model specification (Snowden et al., 2011).

We will estimate three marginal ATEs per outcome (i.e. high school grades, out-of-school suspension, and educational attainment measured at age 22), corresponding to the following pairwise contrasts: recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness complaints (a2) compared to no reported loneliness complaints (a0), recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness complaints (a2) compared to transient caregiver-reported loneliness complaints (a1), and transient caregiver-reported loneliness complaints (a1) compared to no reported loneliness complaints (a0). We used robust standard errors that treat the weights as fixed to estimate the confidence intervals (Greifer, 2025). Propensity score weights and ATEs were estimated in each imputed dataset separately, and estimates were combined afterward (i.e., "within" method) (Nguyen & Stuart, 2024).

Sensitivity analysis

Based on previous studies indicating sex/gender differences in loneliness during childhood and adolescence (Maes et al., 2019), we first ran sex-stratified sensitivity analyses to explore potential differential associations across females and males. Second, we included a measure of child's emotionality and shyness at age 1 as a covariate to explore the potential confounding role of child's early life temperament. Third, we ran the additional outcome models using an alternative cut-off point for high school grades (mostly B's vs. lower; half B's, half A's vs. lower). Fourth, we ran a complete case analysis (n=1,264) to compare with our main analysis applying multiple imputation. Fifth, we ran an additional analysis conducting the multiple imputation in the original study sample (n=4,898).

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the sample across time points are displayed in Table 1. In our sample, 24.6% of caregivers reported their child complained of loneliness at one time point, and 6.8% reported loneliness complaints at both ages 5 and 9. We observed differences by sex and at the intersections with race/ethnicity. The prevalence of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints recurring at ages 5 and 9 was 5% among boys and 8.8% among girls. Among girls, those of Black (non-Hispanic) ethnicity, multi-racial (non-Hispanic), or belonging to ethnic groups classified as "other" had the highest prevalence, ranging from 10.6% to 10.9%. Among boys, those in the "other" ethnic group exhibited the highest prevalence (8.2%). Prevalence of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints across boys and girls by race/ethnicity is presented in SM Table s1.

Mothers of children included in our sample were more often single, of Black (non-Hispanic) ethnicity, and born in the US compared to those of excluded participants (n=2,442). See complete attrition analysis in SM Table s2.

Early-life socioenvironmental factors and child's verbal ability as predictors of caregiverreported loneliness complaints (recurrent, transient, or not reported at ages 5 and 9) (RQ1) All three early-life socioenvironmental indicators at age 5 were associated with higher odds of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints in middle childhood. The odds of loneliness complaints (either recurrent or transient at ages 5 and 9) were 19% greater per 1 SD increase in child maltreatment scores (OR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.09-1.30), 17% greater per 1 SD increase in material hardship (OR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.07-1.28) and 16% greater per 1 SD decrease in neighborhood collective efficacy (OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.06-1.27).

Additionally, material hardship (OR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.21-1.60) and lower neighborhood collective efficacy (OR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.02-1.40) were associated with higher odds of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints recurring at ages of 5 and 9. In the case of child maltreatment, the odds of recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness complaints compared to transient/no reported loneliness were 16% greater (OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 0.99-1.36), but the confidence interval included the null value. The magnitude of these estimates remained similar when including all socioenvironmental predictors in the same model. All results for the binomial logistic regression models can be found in Table 2.

There was a significant interaction between material hardship and child's verbal ability when both were included as predictors of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints recurrence in the model (OR: 1.158, 95% CI: 1.003-1.337).

We conducted further exploratory analysis to explore the direction and magnitude of the interaction, observing that the association between material hardship and recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness complaints was greater among children with higher verbal ability (OR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.03-1.51 at -1 SD; OR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.24-1.63 at the mean; OR: 1.62, 95% CI: 1.33-1.97 at +1 SD) (see the interaction analysis at SM Figure s1).

Associations between caregiver-reported loneliness complaints and educational achievement measured in young adulthood (RQ2)

The minimally adjusted outcome models reached good covariate balance between exposure groups after weighting (SM Figure s2). We observed no evidence of an association between caregiverreported loneliness complaints and any of the educational achievement indicators measured in young adulthood in the weighted outcome models (results can be found in SM Table s3).

The fully adjusted models reached a good covariate balance between the no reported (0) and the transient (1) caregiver-reported loneliness complaint groups. The balance between the recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness complaints at ages 5 and 9 (2) group and the transient (1) and no reported loneliness complaints (0) groups was overall good, with absolute standardized mean differences (SMD) of < 0.10 for all covariates except for child's sex (max SMD: 0.117), mother's age (max. -0.138), and child maltreatment scores (max. -0.112). Plots illustrating the covariate balance achieved after weighting can be found in SM Figure s3.

We observed no evidence of an association between caregiver-reported loneliness complaints and any of the educational achievement indicators measured in young adulthood in the sample. Results for all pairwise comparisons across exposure groups can be found in Table 3.

Sensitivity analysis

In the sex-stratified analysis, the associations between socioenvironmental early-life adversity indicators and caregiver-reported loneliness complaints (either recurrent or transient at ages 5 and 9) were evident in girls (material hardship OR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.11-1.43; low neighborhood collective efficacy OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.03-1.34; child maltreatment OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.16-1.51), but in boys, only for low neighborhood collective efficacy (material hardship OR: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.95-1.23; low neighborhood collective efficacy OR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.01-1.30; child maltreatment OR: 1.10, 95% CI: 0.97-1.25). Material hardship was associated with greater odds of recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness complaints at ages 5 and 9 among both girls (OR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.18-1.69) and boys (OR: 1.35, 95 % CI: 1.07-1.71).

Moreover, verbal ability moderated the association of material hardship with recurrence of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints solely among boys, so that the association was greater at higher levels of verbal ability (OR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.73-1.45 at -1 SD; OR: 1.43, 95% CI: 1.13-1.82 at the mean; OR: 1.99, 95% CI: 1.42-2.79 at +1 SD). Results for the sex-stratified binomial models can be found in SM Tables s4-s5 and Figure s4.

Due to the small proportion of boys in the caregiver-reported recurrent loneliness group, the generalized propensity scores did not reach good covariate balance after propensity score weighting among boys (Figure s5). When exploring the association between caregiver-reported loneliness complaints and educational achievement measured in young adulthood across boys and girls, marginal ATE pairwise comparisons were compatible with a null effect as in the main analysis (Table s6).

Our main results were consistent with additional sensitivity analyses. The results did not change after including a measure of child's early-life temperament as a covariate, as all associations remained compatible with a null effect (see SM, Tables s7-s8 and Figure s6). Alternative cut-off points for low high school grades did not materially change the conclusions, with one exception: the transient vs. not reported loneliness complaints comparison became statistically significant (RR 0.69, 95% CI 0.50-0.97) when lower grades were defined as an average of half A's and half B's or lower. However, this finding emerged only under one of the alternative definitions, so it should be interpreted with caution (see SM Tables s9 and s10). Complete case analysis and analysis after conducting multiple imputation in the original sample (then restricting the analysis to the selected study sample) yielded very similar estimates to the main analysis conducted after multiple imputation of missing values in the final sample. See SM Tables s11-s12, and Figure s7 (complete case analysis) and SM Tables s13-s14 and Figure s8 (multiple imputation in the original sample).

Discussion

In our study, we used data from the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study spanning 22 years since birth to answer two connected research questions. We first observed that material hardship, low neighborhood collective efficacy, and child maltreatment were associated with higher odds of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints. Moreover, material hardship and low neighborhood collective efficacy were also associated with the recurrence of these complaints at ages 5 and 9.

However, we did not observe an association between caregiver-reported loneliness complaints and educational achievement measured in young adulthood. This may be partly due to limitations in how loneliness was measured, particularly its reliance on a single caregiver-reported item.

Our results are in line with previous studies highlighting the relevance of early-life environment for youth's social connection and well-being (Donnelly et al., 2016; Hjalmarsson & Mood, 2015; Schneider et al., 2017). In our study, material hardship, neighborhood conditions, and child maltreatment appeared as independent predictors of later caregiver-reported loneliness complaints. Moreover, we observed that material hardship and low neighborhood collective efficacy were associated with higher odds of recurrent loneliness complaints at ages 5 and 9. In sex-stratified analysis, we observed that these associations had larger effect sizes among girls. While child's verbal ability was not associated with caregiver-reported loneliness complaints, it moderated the association of material hardship with loneliness complaints: the association was greater among children with higher levels of verbal ability. Due to the exploratory nature of these models, results should be interpreted with caution. A plausible post hoc explanation is that children with higher verbal ability are more capable of engaging in complex relational comparisons and communicating their experiences of loneliness. In sex-stratified analysis, the moderating role of child's verbal ability was observed among boys only.

Regarding our second research question, we did not find evidence of an association between caregiver-reported loneliness complaints and educational achievement measured in young adulthood, in contrast to the associations previously reported in adolescent samples (Eccles et al., 2023; Frostad et al., 2015; Jefferson et al., 2023; Jefferson et al., 2023; Matthews et al., 2023). This was not merely a result of a strict covariate adjustment since there was no evidence for an association in the minimally adjusted outcome models either. Our study differs from previous ones in other aspects that might have been key. First, while our exposure was measured at ages 5 and 9, previous studies explored loneliness later in life, from age 11 onwards, and mostly during adolescence. Children tend to conceptualize loneliness in terms that are closer to objective social isolation (Cassidy & Asher, 1992; Verity et al., 2021). The association with educational achievement may only emerge later in life, when loneliness becomes more prevalent, peer acceptance gains more relevance, sensitivity for social threats increases, and the educational context becomes more demanding (Qualter et al., 2015 (Qualter et al., , 2025)). Second, we used self-reported indicators of educational achievement measured in young adulthood while several previous studies used indicators of aptitude and well-being at school measured at a younger age. For instance, Eccles et al. (2023) reported an association of higher levels of loneliness with poorer self-reported achievement perception, higher feelings of school dissatisfaction, and greater feelings of school pressure, and Frostad et al. (2015) explored the association of loneliness with the intention to leave school at age 16. Recurrent loneliness may make the individual more prone to cognitive biases, lower self-esteem, and hypersensitivity to social threats (Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010). Therefore, loneliness could have stronger associations with measures that refer to educational perceptions, motivations, and attitudes than with measures that refer to the actual educational performance. Nevertheless, negative perceptions of one's educational capabilities may represent a risk factor for poorer academic trajectories, through pathways such as reduced classroom engagement due to perceived exclusion, avoidance of collaborative learning, and heightened stress responses in peer-evaluative settings. Third, several previous studies have used measures that assess school loneliness specifically (Frostad et al., 2015; Jefferson et al., 2023), while in our study child's complaints of loneliness were reported by their mothers. Future studies could benefit from exploring associations with different dimensions of loneliness and types of interpersonal relationships (i.e., school, family, or peer-related loneliness) (Cunningham et al., 2025). Finally, all previous studies used European samples (i.e., UK, Denmark, Norway) compared to ours from the United States, and cultural differences might also underlie the different results.

Strengths and limitations

This pre-registered study leverages data from an ethnically diverse sample of children born in the US, followed since birth to young adulthood. The availability of several health and social measures permitted us to adjust our analysis for several known confounders of our associations of interest. The sensitivity analyses showed the robustness of the conclusions drawn from our main analysis.

Our study has several limitations. First, although single-item loneliness measures are considered reliable (Mund et al., 2023), the only measure of childhood loneliness available in the FFCWS was reported by the child's mother. This may have introduced reporting bias, as caregiver-reported loneliness complaints may not fully reflect the subjective experience of loneliness felt by the children themselves. In addition, although single-item loneliness measures are considered reliable (Mund et al., 2023), loneliness comprises social, emotional, and cognitive aspects (Cunningham et al., 2025) and our operationalization obscured potential associations with later educational outcomes driven by any one specific facet. Additionally, caregivers are not independent observers of the family relational context, and relying on mother's self-report might have introduced social desirability bias. Also, it is possible that our loneliness measure mainly captured family-related loneliness, which might not necessarily translate into other social contexts. Previous studies suggest that relationship problems carry over to other contexts, and have observed associations between parent-and peer-related loneliness in adolescents (Geukens et al., 2022). However, other studies have found peer-and familyrelated loneliness to be associated with different outcomes (e.g., different psychopathology symptoms in Lasgaard et al., (2011)). Similarly, peer-related and family-related loneliness might have different implications for later educational attainment, and in that case, our study may have failed to capture such implications. Second, our categorization of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints as recurrent, transient, or not reported was based on just two measurement points, four years apart (at ages 5 and 9). This may not adequately capture the real-life developmental trajectories of loneliness during these years, as recurrence at ages 5 and 9 does not imply persistence but may instead reflect intermittent distress. Furthermore, to delimit our exposure groups, we classified children as "lonely" at each time point if their caregiver reported complaints of loneliness "sometimes" or "often". Although this dichotomization made our statistical approach feasible, we lost information on the intensity or frequency of the reported complaints. Altogether these compromise construct validity and the interpretation of group comparisons. Third, despite the efforts to adjust for all potential sources of confounding, we cannot completely rule out the possibility of unmeasured or residual confounding biasing our results. Thus, our results should be interpreted without making causal attributions. This is common in observational studies due to the absence of randomization. For instance, father's health status, educational attainment, or cognitive abilities could be potential unmeasured confounders of the associations examined in this study. Fourth, the long interval of 11 years between caregiver-reported loneliness complaints and educational achievement measures may have failed to capture shorter-term consequences of loneliness that are not long-lasting and may have increased the risk of unmeasured confounding. Fifth, the generalized propensity scores in the sex-stratified analysis did not reach good covariate balance due to few boys in the recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness group. Sixth, we have estimated standard errors treating weights as fixed (i.e., ignoring uncertainty in their estimation) (Greifer, 2025) as the combination of bootstrapping and generalized boosted models was not computationally feasible. Seventh, these results from a solely US-based sample might not be generalizable to other populations. Additionally, although our findings from the complete case analysis were similar to those obtained after multiple imputation, there is a risk of selection bias in our analytic sample that multiple imputation cannot resolve. The attrition analysis suggested systematic differences between the original FFCWS sample and selected study participants. It is plausible that children experiencing more severe adversities or recurrent loneliness complaints were also the most difficult to retain, which may have resulted in an attenuation of the associations observed in our sample.

Conclusion

Growing up in a family facing material hardships or experiencing child maltreatment in early life was associated with a higher risk of caregiver-reported loneliness complaints at ages 5 and 9. Moreover, material hardship was associated with recurrent caregiver-reported loneliness complaints at both time points. This is in line with previous studies emphasizing the relevance of early-life conditions for loneliness. However, caregiver-reported loneliness complaints did not independently predict educational achievement measured in young adulthood. Future studies in other populations that use child-reported assessments of loneliness, incorporate a multidimensional operationalization of loneliness, include measures of loneliness spanning from childhood to adolescence, and combine different indicators of educational success and school wellbeing (e.g., perceptions, motivations, school performance) are needed to further clarify these associations.
