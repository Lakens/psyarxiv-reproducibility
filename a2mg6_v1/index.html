Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Anhedonia is a core symptom of depression and is associated with greater illness severity and poorer outcomes in young people. The Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) is a validated 14-item multidimensional measure, but no empirically derived screening threshold exists for the original English-language scale.

To derive a provisional ASA screening threshold in older adolescents and young adults.

In 1199 participants aged 16-25 years recruited in the UK, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses evaluated the ASA against three reference criteria: depressive symptom caseness (Mood and Feelings Questionnaire [MFQ] ≥27), self-reported clinician diagnosis of depression (n = 709) and anhedonia caseness on the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS; n = 433). Optimal thresholds were identified using the Youden index, and performance was compared across age groups and gender and in a 5-month follow-up subsample (n = 332).

The ASA had excellent discrimination against MFQ caseness (AUC = 0.89, 95% CI 0.87-0.91; optimal threshold 16.5, sensitivity 86%, specificity 77%). Optimal thresholds were 15.5 against both clinician diagnosis (AUC = 0.74, 95% CI 0.70-0.79; sensitivity 88%, specificity 47%) and SHAPS caseness (AUC = 0.83, 95% CI 0.79-0.87). Discrimination did not differ by age group or gender. At follow-up, discrimination was outstanding (AUC = 0.94, 95% CI 0.91-0.96), although the optimal threshold was higher (19.5).

Thresholds converged at 15.5-16.5 across reference criteria, supporting a provisional screening threshold of ≥17 for identifying clinically relevant anhedonia in young people.

Replication against structured clinical assessment is needed before this threshold is used diagnostically.

Introduction

Depression is common among young people: a recent meta-analysis of over 500,000 participants across 29 countries found that approximately one in five children and adolescents report depression or clinically significant depressive symptoms (Lu et al., 2024).

Early identification and intervention in this age group are therefore critical (Thapar et al., 2012 (Thapar et al., , 2022)).

Anhedonia, a diminished ability to experience interest or pleasure in typically rewarding activities, is a core symptom of major depressive disorder (MDD) in both DSM-5 and ICD-11 (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013; World Health Organization [WHO], 2022). It is highly prevalent among young people with depression, affecting approximately 50-78% (Malhi & Mann, 2018), and is associated with greater illness severity and poorer outcomes (Gabbay et al., 2015). Current treatments have limited effectiveness in targeting anhedonia specifically (Cao et al., 2019; Cuijpers et al., 2014), underscoring the need for accurate, developmentally appropriate assessment.

The Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) is a 14-item self-report measure grounded in qualitative research on young people's lived experiences of anhedonia (Watson et al., 2020 (Watson et al., , 2021)). Items are rated for frequency over the past two weeks on a four-point scale (0 = never to3 = always) and load on three factors: (1) Enjoyment, excitement, and emotional flattening; (2) Enthusiasm, connection, and purpose; and (3) Effort, motivation, and drive.

The ASA showed excellent internal consistency in adolescents aged 11-18 years (α = .93-.94; Watson et al., 2021), and our companion study has extended evidence for its reliability, three-factor structure, and convergent, concurrent and predictive validity to 16-25year-olds (α = .92; test-retest ICC = .69; Demir-Kassem et al., 2026).

Despite this psychometric support, no empirically derived screening threshold exists for the original English-language ASA. Two recent studies of a Chinese translation have begun to address this. Ye et al. (2025) identified a threshold of 20.5 for adolescents aged 13-18 years with prominent anhedonia, defined using latent profile analysis, and Kuan et al. (2026) derived thresholds distinguishing adolescents with clinician-diagnosed MDD (21.5) and subthreshold depression (15.5) from healthy controls. However, both studies used a translated version of the scale in predominantly early-to mid-adolescent samples. Without a threshold for the original scale, ASA scores are difficult to interpret in research and clinical settings, limiting its utility relative to measures such as the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS), for which cut-offs have been established in clinical and non-clinical populations (Ho et al., 2024).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis is widely used to derive cut-off scores by balancing sensitivity and specificity. A challenge for anhedonia measures, however, is that there is no gold-standard assessment of clinically significant anhedonia. Consistent with previous cut-off studies of anhedonia measures (Ho et al., 2024; Kuan et al., 2026), we therefore used depressive symptom caseness on the Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (MFQ; Wood et al., 1995) as the primary reference criterion, with self-reported clinician diagnosis of depression and anhedonia caseness on the SHAPS (Snaith et al., 1995) as secondary criteria. Throughout, we use 'clinically relevant anhedonia' to refer to levels of anhedonia associated with clinically significant depressive symptoms, as indexed by these criteria.

The present study aimed to derive a provisional ASA screening threshold for clinically relevant anhedonia in young people aged 16-25 years in the UK. ROC analyses evaluated the ASA against each reference criterion, with optimal thresholds identified using the Youden index and overall discrimination assessed using the area under the curve (AUC). We also examined whether discriminatory performance differed by age group and gender, and whether it was maintained in a follow-up subsample assessed approximately five months later.

2.4. Questionnaires

>Questionnaires

Depression

The Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (MFQ)

The MFQ is a self-report questionnaire commonly used to screen for depressive symptoms in children and adolescents (Jarbin et al., 2020). It consists of 33 items rated on a three-point scale (0 = not true,1 = sometimes,2 = true), with higher scores indicating higher levels of depression and is one-dimensional. Scores range from 0 to 66. Previous research has reported excellent internal consistency for MFQ scores (Cronbach's alpha .91 to .93), together with evidence of validity and reasonable diagnostic accuracy, with a suggested clinical depression cut-off score of 27 (Thabrew et al., 2018).

Anhedonia The Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS)

The SHAPS is a self-report tool designed to assess hedonic capacity, or the ability to experience pleasure (Snaith et al., 1995). It comprises 14 items, each rated on a four-point scale, covering various aspects of pleasurable experiences in four domains: interest/pastimes, social interaction, sensory experience, and food/drink. Previous research has reported good internal consistency for SHAPS scores (Cronbach's α = .86). Originally, it was scored using a dichotomous system with a total score ranging from 0 to 14. However, we used a four-point Likert scale (items scored 1-4; total scores 14-56, with higher scores indicating greater anhedonia) to better distinguish levels of severity for each item (Franken et al., 2007). A recent study identified a cut-off of 23.5 in a non-clinical student sample (Ho et al., 2024); this corresponds to SHAPS scores ≥24 using the same 1-4 scoring scheme.

The Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA)

The ASA was created in collaboration with adolescents experiencing symptoms of depression and anhedonia (Watson et al., 2021). Informed by qualitative interviews, the ASA is a multidimensional self-report questionnaire designed to measure anhedonia in adolescents aged 11-18 years. It covers various components of the anhedonia experience relevant to this age group. The scale consists of 14 items rated on a four-point scale (0 = never,1 = sometimes,2 = often,3 = always), with higher scores indicating higher levels of anhedonia. Total scores range from 0 to 42. The ASA comprises three subscales:  ASA S1-Enjoyment, Excitement, and Emotional Flattening: It has 7 items in the scale and can range from 0 to 21.  ASA S2-Enthusiasm, Connection, and Purpose: has 3 positively worded items, with scores ranging from 0 to 9. Items were reverse scored for analysis so that, consistent with the other ASA subscales and the total score, higher scores indicate greater anhedonia.

 ASA S3-Effort, Motivation, and Drive: It has 4 items in the scale and can range from 0 to 12 (see Supplement for scale details).

The scale encompasses both consummatory (enjoyment in the moment) and anticipatory (looking forward to future pleasures) aspects of anhedonia, as well as motivational deficits.

This multidimensional approach provides a more comprehensive assessment of anhedonia in adolescents compared to the SHAPS. Data were analysed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Participant demographics were summarized using descriptive statistics, and associations between questionnaire measures were examined using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients.

The discriminatory performance of the Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) and its subscales was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. This analysis was conducted to assess the ability of the ASA to discriminate between participants classified as positive and negative according to each external reference criterion.

The ROC curve plots the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1specificity) for various threshold settings. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify the overall ability of the scale to discriminate between positive and negative cases. In general, an AUC of 0.5 suggests no discrimination between participants classified as positive and negative according to the reference criterion, 0.7 to 0.8 is considered acceptable, 0.8 to 0.9 is considered excellent, and more than 0.9 is considered outstanding (Hosmer et al., 2013).

For each scale and subscale, the optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden index (Youden, 1950), which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity. Sensitivity, specificity, and the Youden index were reported for these optimal cut-off values. Because ASA total and subscale scores take integer values, ROC thresholds ending in .5 were interpreted operationally as the next integer score (e.g., a threshold of 16.5 corresponds to a screening score of ≥17). AUC 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the nonparametric DeLong method; sensitivity and specificity 95% confidence intervals used the Wilson method. Optimal thresholds were selected in the same data used to estimate their apparent accuracy, so the threshold is provisional. The reporting of screening performance was informed by STARD 2015 (Bossuyt et al., 2015).

The primary reference criterion was the Mood and Feelings Questionnaire cut-off; secondary criteria were self-reported clinician diagnosis of depression and the SHAPS cut-off. These criteria were used as convergent external benchmarks rather than as definitive diagnostic gold standards for current anhedonia.

 Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (MFQ): The MFQ is commonly used to screen for depressive symptoms in children and adolescents. Participants scoring 27 or higher are presumed to have high levels of depression (Thabrew et al., 2018). The MFQ cut-off score distinguishes individuals likely experiencing significant depressive symptoms from those who are not, providing an external criterion for comparison with the ASA. Scores of 27 or higher are coded as 1, while scores lower than 27 are coded as 0.

 Self-reported Clinician Diagnosis of Depression: In a sub-sample, participants were asked whether they had a clinical diagnosis of depression from a health practitioner.

Those who answered "yes" were coded as 1, and those who answered "no" were coded as 0; participants who were unsure whether they had received a diagnosis (n = 20) were excluded. This variable was used as a clinically relevant external reference criterion for depression.  Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS): SHAPS is a commonly used scale for measuring anhedonia. A cut-off score on the SHAPS identifies individuals with significant anhedonia, making it a relevant anhedonia-specific benchmark for validating the ASA's ability to detect anhedonia. A recent study (Ho et al., 2024) in a non-clinical university student sample found the cut-off score for SHAPS to be 23.5 for non-clinical groups. Scores higher than 23.5 are coded as 1, while scores of 23.5 or lower are coded as 0.

To further investigate potential differences in discriminatory performance across demographic groups, independent-group area differences under the ROC curve were analysed. This included comparisons of AUCs between age groups (16-19 and 20-25) and genders (male and female, excluding the other-gender category). The significance of these differences was tested using z-tests for the AUC differences, with asymptotic 95% confidence intervals calculated under the nonparametric assumption. A p-value less than .05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

4. Discussion

The present study provides empirically derived provisional screening thresholds for the original English-language Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) in a large sample of older adolescents and young adults aged 16-25 years. Across three external reference criteria at baseline, optimal ASA thresholds were broadly similar, ranging from 15.5 to 16.5. The MFQbased analysis yielded an optimal threshold of 16.5, whereas both SHAPS-defined anhedonia caseness and self-reported clinician diagnosis of depression yielded thresholds of 15.5. This convergence suggests that ASA scores around 16-17 may represent a useful provisional screening range. For practical screening purposes, a score of ≥17 is therefore proposed as the primary candidate threshold. Together, these findings address a key gap identified in the Introduction: the absence of an empirically derived screening threshold has, until now, limited the ASA's use for identifying individuals with clinically relevant anhedonia symptoms in research and clinical settings.

Cut-off performance across reference standards

The strongest evidence for the ASA's discriminatory accuracy came from the MFQ-based analysis, which showed excellent AUC in the full sample and was outstanding in a follow-up sub-sample assessed approximately five months later (AUC = .94), with a similar sensitivity/specificity balance (86%/86% versus 86%/77%). This consistency across assessments provides further support for the discriminatory performance of the ASA, although the optimal cut-off at follow-up was somewhat higher (19.5 compared with 16.5 at baseline), suggesting some variation in the precise threshold over time.

Discriminatory power was lower when self-reported clinician diagnosis was used as the reference standard (AUC = .74) than for the SHAPS (.83) and MFQ (.89) criteria. Several factors plausibly explain this pattern. Clinical diagnosis was based on participant self-report of a prior diagnosis from a health practitioner rather than a structured diagnostic interview conducted as part of the study, introducing measurement noise (recall accuracy, heterogeneity in diagnostic practice, and variation in time since diagnosis or treatment) that would tend to attenuate observed discrimination. The MFQ, by contrast, is a continuous, validated dimensional measure completed by all participants at the same assessment, providing a cleaner and more proximal benchmark. The comparison with SHAPS-defined caseness should also be interpreted in light of the conceptual scope of the two measures: as discussed in our companion validation paper (Demir-Kassem et al., 2026), the SHAPS assesses a narrower construct, hedonic capacity, whereas the ASA was developed to capture a broader multidimensional experience of anhedonia encompassing motivation, effort, connection and purpose alongside enjoyment. Given this, the excellent discriminatory power observed against SHAPS-defined caseness (AUC = .83) should be interpreted as an estimate of the ASA's discrimination against this specific self-report benchmark. On balance, and consistent with the convergence across reference criteria, the MFQ-based cut-off of 16.5 supports the proposed screening threshold of ≥17, with the diagnosis-and SHAPSbased cut-offs (both 15.5) offered as converging, secondary benchmarks. The sensitivity of 88% and relatively low specificity of 47% against reported diagnosis indicate that many participants without an affirmative history of diagnosis nevertheless screened positive; this may reflect elevated symptoms without a reported diagnosis rather than definitive falsepositive screening.

Subscale-level performance

A consistent pattern emerged across all three reference standards and the follow-up sample: the total ASA score performed as well as, or better than, any individual subscale, and the Enthusiasm, Connection, and Purpose subscale (ASA-S2) consistently showed the weakest discriminatory power (AUCs ranging from .63 to .78), while Enjoyment, Excitement, and Emotional Flattening (ASA-S1) and Effort, Motivation, and Drive (ASA-S3) performed comparably to, and at times matched, the total score. This pattern converges with findings from our companion psychometric validation study (Demir-Kassem et al., 2026), in which the same subscale (ASA-S2) showed the lowest internal consistency and the weakest convergent validity of the three ASA dimensions. Taken together, the two studies suggest that the enthusiasm, connection, and purpose items, despite tapping a conceptually important aspect of anhedonia identified in the qualitative work underpinning the ASA, may be somewhat less precise as a discriminator of clinically relevant anhedonia symptoms than the other two dimensions. This reinforces the recommendation that the total ASA score, rather than individual subscale scores, be used when applying these cut-offs for screening, while the subscale-level findings may still hold value for characterising the specific profile of an individual's anhedonic experience.

Demographic differences and generalisability of the cut-off

Reassuringly, independent-group comparisons indicated no significant differences in AUC by age group (16-19 versus 20-25 years) or gender for any of the three reference standards, supporting similar discriminatory performance across this broader age range and across genders. Some variation in the optimal cut-off value itself was nonetheless observed between subgroups, particularly for the smaller clinical-diagnosis sub-sample, where the cutoff for the youngest age band diverged more markedly from the other subgroups (age 16-19: ≥23, with only 33 reference-positive cases). Given the smaller cell sizes involved once the sample was stratified by both diagnosis status and demographic group, this subgroupspecific estimate should be interpreted cautiously and would benefit from replication in a larger, independently diagnosed clinical sample.

Proportion above the screening threshold and screening utility

Applying the recommended MFQ-based cut-off, approximately 60% of the overall sample scored above the proposed screening threshold , with broadly similar rates across age and gender subgroups. This is a notably high base rate relative to general population estimates, and likely reflects the characteristics of the recruitment strategy (a convenience sample recruited partly through advertisements referencing mood and wellbeing, and skewed towards female participants) rather than the true population prevalence of clinically significant anhedonia. Reassuringly, the clinical relevance of the proposed screening threshold is supported by the substantially higher proportion scoring above the threshold among participants reporting a clinical diagnosis of depression (84.1%) compared with those without (50.9%), indicating that the proposed threshold is meaningfully associated with selfreported clinical diagnosis even though the absolute proportions figures in this sample should not be generalised as population estimates. At ≥17, positive and negative predictive values against MFQ caseness were 84.2% and 79.3%, respectively, and depend on the high reference-positive proportion in this sample. These findings are broadly consistent with cut-off determination work on other anhedonia measures in adolescent samples, such as the SHAPS (Ho et al., 2024).

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The sample was large and provided substantial power for ROC analysis, cut-offs were derived across three conceptually distinct external reference criteria, and discriminatory performance was examined in a follow-up sub-sample collected approximately five months after baseline. The examination of AUC differences by age and gender also allowed a direct test of whether a single cut-off is appropriate across this broader age range.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, an important limitation concerns the nature of the external reference criteria. The MFQ assesses depressive symptom severity rather than anhedonia specifically and includes symptoms overlapping with anhedonia, the SHAPS is itself a self-report measure of hedonic capacity, and clinical diagnosis was based on participant self-report of receiving a clinical diagnosis rather than directly from a clinician using a structured diagnostic interview, which was not feasible within the large online survey design. Accordingly, none of these criteria constitutes a gold-standard assessment of current clinically significant anhedonia, and the thresholds identified here should therefore be regarded as provisional screening thresholds. The use of participant-reported clinical diagnosis may have introduced noise into that reference criterion and may partly explain its comparatively lower AUC; future work should replicate the cut-off analysis in samples with independently verified clinical diagnoses and structured assessments of current anhedonia.

Because the MFQ includes anhedonia-related items, some content overlap with the ASA may inflate the MFQ-based AUC.

Second, the sample was a convenience sample recruited via schools, a university research panel, and online advertisement, was predominantly female, and showed a high overall rate of depressive symptoms; the specific cut-off values, and particularly the prevalence estimates reported here, may not generalise directly to more representative or more diverse clinical populations. Third, the clinical-diagnosis and SHAPS sub-samples were considerably smaller than the full sample, and the subgroup-level cut-off estimates derived from them (particularly for the youngest age band) should be treated as preliminary. Finally, although the follow-up sample provided valuable evidence regarding the discriminatory performance of the ASA over time, the follow-up ROC analysis used ASA and MFQ scores collected at the same time point, so it addresses concurrent rather than predictive accuracy. Future research should examine whether ASA scores above the proposed cut-off predict clinically meaningful outcomes over time, such as treatment response or the subsequent course of depression.

Conclusions

This study provides the first empirically derived provisional screening thresholds for the original English-language Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents, addressing a key barrier to its use in research and clinical practice. A cut-off of approximately 16.5 on the total ASA score, benchmarked against symptom-based caseness, showed excellent discriminatory performance, with similarly outstanding discriminatory performance observed at follow-up and discriminatory performance did not differ significantly by age or gender within the 16-25year range. Converging cut-offs derived from self-reported clinician diagnosis and an established anhedonia measure lend further support to a screening threshold in this range.

Combined with evidence from our companion validation study that the ASA's psychometric properties extend reliably into young adulthood, these findings support the use of the ASA, with a provisional screening threshold of ≥17, to identify clinically relevant anhedonia in young people aged 16-25 years who may warrant further assessment.
