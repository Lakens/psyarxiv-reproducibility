Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Introduction

Lived experience is increasingly recognised alongside clinical, professional and academic expertise, challenging assumptions about whose perspectives are credible in shaping practice, research and policy (Fricker, 2007; Greenhalgh et al., 2016; Sonuga-Barke et al., 2024). Within autism research, autistic researchers and community members have called for meaningful involvement in determining research priorities, shaping methodological approaches and interpreting findings, highlighting the importance of research that reflects the experiences, needs and priorities of autistic people themselves (Fletcher-Watson et al., 2019). These developments also reflect wider commitments to children's rights, disability justice, and epistemic justice, as well as growing expectations from research funders that people and communities should influence the research that affects them. Children and young people (CYP) are therefore increasingly understood not simply as recipients of services but as knowledge holders and experts in their own lives, capable of contributing to research design, interpretation and translation (Fletcher-Watson et al., 2025; Lundy & McEvoy, 2012; Sonuga-Barke et al., 2024).

Co-production with CYP has demonstrated what becomes possible when participation extends beyond consultation. Youth advisory groups, creative participatory methods, co-produced self-report measures and sustained partnerships can enable young people to influence priorities, methods and interpretation, particularly when researchers invest in accessible communication, long-term relationships and shared decision-making (Kakoulidou et al., 2024; Lukito et al., 2025; Pavlopoulou, 2020). These developments are important advances. The argument developed here is not that participatory research has failed, but that its next methodological challenge is to examine more closely how participation is experienced and how those experiences affect the evidence that research is able to produce. This paper addresses that challenge by developing the Experience-Sensitive Method of Inquiry (ESMI) for participatory and translational research with CYP, with relevance to CYP with SEND. The argument proceeds through three linked limitations in current knowledge production (epistemic, participatory and experiential) before setting out eight experience-sensitive dimensions, participatory reflexivity, and the Agency, Belonging and Collaboration (ABC) heuristic through which ESMI can be enacted in practice.

This dual focus is central to ESMI: research should be more responsive and less burdensome for CYP with Special Educational Needs and Disabilities (SEND) while also generating evidence that better reflects their experiences. These are linked methodological aims rather than a trade-off between participant wellbeing and scientific quality; whether specific adaptations improve either outcome requires evaluation.

The paper advances a transdisciplinary proposition: improving research encounters is not separate from scientific rigour, because participants' experiences of methods can affect engagement, measurement, missingness and the interpretation of findings. Across trials, cohorts, Delphi studies, experimental and qualitative inquiry, ESMI invites researchers to examine these relationships from research inception through dissemination, while respecting design-specific requirements for validity, standardisation and comparability.

Epistemic limitation

Scientific methods do not merely record experience; they determine what becomes available as evidence. Recent evidence has highlighted that across a range of domains and SEND types (Cheng et al., 2025; Palikara et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Despite the growing recognition of lived experience, it remains unevenly embedded within the core production of scientific evidence. CYP may help identify priorities, advise on materials, refine interventions or support dissemination, yet they are less consistently involved in defining constructs, shaping measures, interpreting findings and determining what conclusions should be drawn (Fletcher-Watson et al., 2025; Pavlopoulou et al., 2026; Sonuga-Barke et al., 2024).

In this sense, lived experience may inform research without becoming constitutive of the evidence itself.

Across health, education, employment and implementation research, largescale longitudinal studies, intervention trials and national cohort studies continue to rely heavily on standardized psychometric instruments and researcher-defined outcome measures. These approaches are indispensable for estimating prevalence, evaluating interventions, and identifying population trends (Connelly & Platt, 2014; Fraser et al., 2013; Lombardo et al., 2026; Palikara et al., 2026; Williamson et al., 2012). However, the use of predetermined constructs raises questions about whose knowledge and priorities define what is measured (Greenhalgh et al., 2016; Fricker, 2007). Such approaches can also count the population without capturing how they understand their own circumstances (see Lombardo et al., 2026, for This matters particularly when CYP communicate, process or make meaning of information in ways that differ from the assumptions embedded in conventional measures. Experiences such as belonging, safety, sensory wellbeing, identity, fatigue, friendship, participation and everyday flourishing are reduced to predefined categories -often without capturing the multidimensionality of these phenomena -or at worst remain absent altogether. We are not arguing against standardised methods, rather, we believe that standardised measures coexist with an important epistemic limitation: they may identify whether CYP meet researcher-defined constructs without fully capturing what matters most from CYP's own perspectives (Fletcher-Watson et al., 2025; Lukito et al., 2025; Pellicano et al., 2014).

The consequences are not merely conceptual, but translational too. The responsiveness of evidence-informed policy and practice is necessarily shaped by the nature and quality of the evidence on which they are based. If children's experiences are underrepresented, represented mainly through proxy accounts, or elicited through methods that privilege neurotypical ways of communicating and knowing, implementation may reproduce the inequalities it aims to address and reduce (Fricker, 2007; Oliver, 1992). Methods of inquiry must therefore be sufficiently sensitive to lived experience for experiential knowledge to shape what counts as evidence from the outset.

Research involving CYP with SEND makes these challenges particularly visible. SEND is an important policy framework for educational entitlement and support, but it is not a homogeneous category; it encompasses highly diverse cognitive, communicative, sensory, physical, emotional and learning experiences (Department for Education & Department of Health, 2015; Education Committee, 2025). Treating CYP with SEND as a single, homogeneous population risks overlooking the considerable diversity of their experiences, needs, and forms of communication. It may also reinforce deficit-based assumptions that equate disability or difference with reduced competence, participation, or ability to contribute (Botha, 2021; Oliver, 1992). These assumptions can shape whose perspectives are recognised as legitimate sources of evidence. Where communication differs from neurotypical expectations, researchers may rely disproportionately on parents, teachers or professionals to interpret or represent CYP experiences despite evidence of discrepancies between first person and proxy reported accounts across neurodevelopmental populations (Alamolhoda et al., 2021; Berástegui et al., 2021 , Sheldrick et al., 2012; Skaletski et al., 2025). While proxy perspectives can provide valuable contextual information, they cannot substitute for first-person accounts when the focus of inquiry is lived experience. Consequently, methodological approaches that fail to accommodate diverse forms of communication and participation risk reproducing epistemic exclusion by privileging accounts that align with established research conventions over the first-person experiences of CYP.

Participatory limitation

Inviting CYP into research does not in itself address epistemic exclusion when the conceptual and methodological architecture of inquiry remains predominantly researcher defined. Participatory and co-produced research has sought to address this problem by moving from research on CYP towards research with them (Lundy & McEvoy, 2012; Sonuga-Barke et al., 2024; Fletcher-Watson et al., 2025). Evidence from autism and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) research demonstrates that meaningful partnership can influence research priorities, methods, measures and interpretation of findings (Kakoulidou et al., 2024; Lukito et al., 2025). Approaches that allow CYP to challenge norm-referenced assumptions reposition them as contributors to knowledge production rather than merely subjects of inquiry.

However, this participatory shift has been less visible across other areas of SEND research such as visual impairment, Down syndrome, and other developmental, sensory and learning differences (Cheng et al., 2025; Palikara et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). As a result, opportunities for CYP from these groups to shape research questions, methods, interpretation, and dissemination remain comparatively limited, and their experiential knowledge is less consistently represented within the evidence base.

Recent developments such as AASPIRE and RE-STAR demonstrate important advancements beyond procedural involvement towards more collaborative forms of research partnerships (Kakoulidou et al., 2024; Lukito et al., 2025 Lukito et al., , 2026;; Nicolaidis et al., 2019; Pavlopoulou et al., 2026; Sonuga-Barke et al., 2024). ESMI builds on these developments by addressing a related but less examined methodological question: Published accounts commonly describe who participated, the methods used and the influence young people had on research outputs but provide less detail about how research encounters were made psychologically safe, cognitively accessible, sensitive to sensory needs and responsive over time (Banks et al., 2013; Grace et al., 2024; Kakoulidou et al., 2024). The negotiations, relationship building and iterative adaptations that make participation possible can therefore disappear from the scientific account. This matters because the conditions of participation are not simply procedural features surrounding knowledge production. They can shape what participants are able or willing to express and what researchers are consequently able to recognise and interpret as evidence. Galvin and Todres's (2013) distinction between narrative breadth and 'lifeworld depth' draws attention to how engagement with concrete and situated experience can make visible embodied, relational and contextual meanings that may be less accessible through more abstract forms of representation.

Taking this seriously requires reflexivity not only about researcher position, but about how interactions between participants, researchers and methods shape the knowledge that becomes available.

The quality of participatory research therefore depends not only on who participates but on the conditions under which participation occurs. Research encounters need to enable CYP to exercise agency while recognising and supporting vulnerability, so that they can participate as themselves rather than having to adapt to normative research expectations. This makes embodiment central rather than incidental. Sensory demands, fatigue, movement, emotional regulation, communication and bodily comfort may all shape what can be expressed, noticed and interpreted. A method can therefore be formally accessible while still constraining what becomes available as evidence. This distinction raises a methodological question: if methods shape what becomes visible, knowable and accepted as scientific evidence, what characteristics must methods possess if experiential knowledge is to meaningfully inform that evidence? Apparent reluctance, limited verbal response or disengagement may reflect limitations in research design rather than limitations in participants themselves. Equally, silence, hesitation, movement, changes in engagement or a preference not to respond may have experiential significance that is difficult to recognise when participation is understood primarily through spoken or written contribution.

The methodological implication is that researchers cannot assume that providing an opportunity to participate gives transparent access to lived experience.

The experience-sensitive method of inquiry (ESMI)

The Experience-Sensitive Method of Inquiry (ESMI) is a methodological approach through which lived experience can become constitutive of trustworthy scientific evidence without losing the relational, embodied and contextual qualities that give that experience meaning. Rather than treating participation as a procedural requirement or ethical aspiration, ESMI positions participation as an epistemological process through which knowledge is produced. It begins from the premise that the quality of scientific evidence is inseparable from the quality of the research encounters through which that evidence emerges. ESMI complements existing participatory, inclusive and accessibility-oriented approaches rather than replacing them. While participatory methodologies have made important contributions to involving CYP in research, involvement alone does not necessarily ensure that experiential knowledge meaningfully influences evidence production. CYP may be included in studies whose research questions conceptual frameworks, outcome measures and interpretive assumptions remain largely researcher defined. ESMI builds on these approaches by making the responsiveness of research decisions to participants' experiential knowledge an ongoing focus of methodological reflexivity. This challenge is particularly relevant in research with CYP with SEND.

Conventional research methods frequently privilege neurotypical assumptions about communication, emotional expression, attention, memory, social interaction and decision-making (Botha, 2021; Fletcher-Watson et al., 2025; Lebenhagen, 2020).

Children may therefore be invited to participate while simultaneously being asked to communicate in ways that do not reflect how they naturally express themselves, to discuss experiences that do not feel psychologically safe to disclose, or to respond to concepts that fail to capture the realities of their everyday lives (Grace et al., 2024; Pavlopoulou, 2020). Under these circumstances, limited verbal responses, apparent disengagement or withdrawal may be interpreted as indicators of reduced capacity or motivation when they instead reflect a mismatch between the participant and the research method (Milton, 2012; Oliver, 1992). ESMI therefore shifts attention from participant deficits towards methodological responsiveness, encouraging researchers to ask whether difficulties in participation reflect limitations in participants or limitations in the methods used to engage them (Bottomley et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2025; Grace et al., 2024; Oliver, 1992).

Importantly, ESMI is not only a methodology for supporting participants; it is also a methodology for sensitising researchers. While reflexivity has long been recognised within qualitative inquiry (Finlay, 2002), ESMI positions it as a continuous methodological process rather than a post hoc exercise. Researchers' disciplinary traditions, theoretical commitments, language, methodological choices and interpretive frameworks shape what becomes visible as evidence and whose knowledge is recognised as legitimate (Finlay, 2002; Fricker, 2007). Participatory reflexivity therefore requires researchers to examine continually whether participants experience themselves as heard, understood, respected and able to influence the inquiry. Knowledge is not simply extracted from participants; it emerges through relationships that can reshape participants' understandings, researchers' assumptions and the knowledge ultimately produced (Banks et al., 2013; Galvin & Todres, 2013).

The intellectual foundations of ESMI draw upon phenomenological scholarship on humanising practice. Galvin and Todres (2013) argue that humanising encounters recognise people as embodied, relational and meaning-making beings rather than reducing them to diagnoses, symptoms or measurable variables. Central to this perspective is the recognition that each person inhabits an irreducibly subjective lifeworld and is therefore the primary authority on their own lived experience.

Humanising practice therefore requires attention not only to observable outcomes but also to how people understand and make sense of their experiences, recognising that these meanings cannot be fully inferred through external observation, diagnostic categories or proxy interpretation. If only participants have direct access to the meanings of their own lived experiences, then research methods must create the conditions in which those meanings can inform the production and interpretation of scientific knowledge, rather than being displaced by external classifications, assumptions or proxy interpretations. ESMI also positions reflexivity as a continuous methodological practice rather than a post hoc exercise. Reflexivity has long been recognised as important within qualitative inquiry (Finlay, 2002), yet ESMI extends this principle across participatory and translational research more broadly. Researchers' disciplinary traditions, theoretical commitments, language, methodological choices and interpretive frameworks influence what becomes visible as evidence and whose knowledge is recognised as legitimate (Finlay, 2002; Fricker, 2007). Participatory reflexivity therefore requires ongoing critical attention to how research relationships, environments and methodological decisions shape participation and knowledge production. Knowledge is not simply extracted from participants; it emerges through interactions that have the capacity to influence both researchers and participants, while shaping the evidence ultimately produced (Banks et al., 2013; Galvin & Todres, 2013).

Agency, Belonging and Collaboration

Agency, Belonging and Collaboration (ABC) provides a practical reflexive heuristic for translating experience-sensitive dimensions into methodological actions. They do not represent separate stages of participation, nor do they simplify the eight dimensions into a smaller framework. Rather, they function as interconnected principles that support researchers in examining how experiential knowledge becomes expressed, recognised, and influential throughout the research process.

The relationship between the eight dimensions and ABC remains dynamic, nonhierarchical and mutually reinforcing. Each principle draws on several experience-sensitive dimensions, and each dimension may inform more than one principle. The methodological focus is on how these relationships shape what can become known.

Agency: Do young people have genuine voice, choice and influence here?

Within ESMI, Agency concerns whether children and young people can influence the questions, constructs, methods and interpretations through which knowledge is produced. Supported particularly by the dimensions of agency, insiderness, uniqueness and sensemaking, it requires more than offering choices within predetermined procedures. Participants' experiential knowledge must be capable of challenging researchers' assumptions and redirecting inquiry.

Agency therefore encourages researchers to examine whose priorities define the research agenda, whose interpretations are privileged and whether participants have meaningful opportunities to influence decisions throughout the research process.

Belonging: Can young people participate as themselves?

Belonging concerns the relational, sensory, embodied and environmental conditions that enable children and young people to participate as themselves. The dimensions of togetherness, sense of place, embodiment and personal journey draw attention to how histories of exclusion, expectations of conformity, fatigue or sensory demands and power relationships, whether participants feel welcomed, understood, and able to contribute meaningfully. These conditions are methodologically consequential because they affect what participants can express and what researchers may mistakenly interpret as disengagement, resistance, lack of capacity or deficit. Belonging therefore encourages researchers to attend to the conditions through which participation becomes possible and meaningful.

Applying ESMI Across Research Designs

ESMI is not confined to qualitative or explicitly participatory methodologies. As a methodological orientation, experience-sensitivity can inform quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods research without prescribing a particular design or replacing its established standards. Instead, it asks how methodological decisions influence what participants can express, how participation is experienced and what becomes recognised as evidence. The form and degree of involvement will vary by design, and proposed gains in evidence quality require empirical evaluation.

In randomised controlled trials, Agency may inform the selection of outcomes that young people regard as meaningful, alongside the feasibility and acceptability of study procedures. Belonging may inform accessible recruitment, consent and assessment encounters. Collaboration may support interpretation of perceived benefits, burdens and implementation implications. These contributions could strengthen outcome relevance and the interpretation of intervention effects but should be evaluated rather than assumed to improve measurement validity.

Adaptations should be considered alongside requirements for experimental control, signal quality, standardisation and comparability. Researchers should document which elements can be adapted, which must remain consistent, why those boundaries exist and whether proposed adaptations affect measurement integrity. Experience-sensitivity does not justify relaxing scientific rigour; it creates opportunities to examine how the conditions of testing may themselves shape engagement and the evidence produced. Young people should be invited to explain how proposed procedures and constraints are experienced, identify burdens or unintended consequences, and suggest alternatives. Where an adaptation cannot be adopted, researchers should communicate the reasons without implying that the young person's experience is less valid, document the decision, and revisit it when new information emerges. This process should not transfer responsibility for methodological trade-offs onto young people or promise influence where decisions are already fixed. Instead, it makes the boundaries of influence transparent and treats disagreement as an opportunity for reciprocal learning. Any adaptations adopted should still be evaluated for their implications for data quality, standardisation and comparability; these are proposed practices requiring empirical evaluation, not guarantees of improved outcomes.

Evaluating Experience-Sensitive Research

Across these designs, ESMI invites examination of two linked questions: did the study create a more respectful, accessible and meaningful research experience for this population, and did those conditions improve what could be measured, interpreted or translated? Neither benefit should be assumed solely from the presence of participatory procedures. Their interdependence matters. Agency without belonging may leave participants unable to exercise meaningful choice; belonging without agency may create welcoming encounters without redistributing epistemic influence; and collaboration requires researchers to remain responsive to both. The relevant question is not simply whether participation occurred, but what it enabled researchers and participants to know.

In SEND research, difficulties with questionnaires, repeated assessments or experimental tasks should prompt examination of possible methodological mismatch, rather than automatic attribution to participant limitations. Such reflexivity must be balanced with the requirements of measurement validity, standardisation and comparability. ESMI therefore connects the quality of research encounters to the relevance and trustworthiness of the evidence produced, while recognising that the forms and degree of participation may vary according to the research question and design.

Future evaluation should therefore consider both participant-defined indicators of research experience (including agency, accessibility, sensory burden and whether contributions were consequential) and design-appropriate indicators of evidence quality (such as construct relevance, response patterns, measurement integrity, interpretability and uptake).

The relationship between these outcomes is an empirical question, not an automatic consequence of participation. The applications outlined above illustrate the potential of ESMI across research designs, rather than providing empirical evidence of its effectiveness.

Conclusion

Meaningful inclusion in research requires attention not only to who participates, but to how participation is experienced and how lived experience shapes knowledge production. ESMI offers a transdisciplinary methodological orientation, grounded in Agency, Belonging and Collaboration, that places these considerations at the heart of research design and interpretation. Further research is needed to evaluate its potential to improve both participants' experiences and the relevance and trustworthiness of the evidence produced. Experiencesensitivity and scientific rigour should be understood as complementary, rather than competing, commitments.

In longitudinal cohort studies, Agency may inform the selection of constructs and repeated measures that matter to participants. Belonging may support accessible follow-up as needs and circumstances change and Collaboration may help interpret missingness, attrition and developmental patterns without presuming participant deficit. Experiencesensitive practice may also help researchers examine response consistency, retention and the meaning of change over time, while preserving the comparability required for longitudinal inference. In Delphi studies, Agency may inform panel composition and the framing of consensus questions; Belonging may require accessible rounds and meaningful routes for dissent; and Collaboration may make visible differences between lived experience and professional judgements rather than treating numerical consensus as the only relevant result. Researchers should report how accessibility and deliberation shaped the process without assuming that inclusion guarantees consensus or validity. In qualitative studies, Agency may support participant-led topics and communication choices; Belonging may shape relationally safe and sensory-considerate encounters; and Collaboration may enable participants to question emerging themes and influence how findings are communicated. An experience-sensitive conversation about experimental adaptations should be honest, collaborative and accessible, taking account of each CYP's developmental experiences, communication preferences and individual circumstances.
