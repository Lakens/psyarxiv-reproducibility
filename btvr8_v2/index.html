Files that do not belong in the repository

We left out 3 files that software creates by itself, not the authors: operating system (3). They hold nothing a reader can use, but they clutter the repository, and software can mistake them for real files (for example a ._ copy of a .csv for data).

Remove these: Operating system files: macOS adds a __MACOSX folder and a ._ copy of every file it puts in a zip, and .DS_Store files to folders; Windows adds Thumbs.db and desktop.ini. On a Mac, create zips from the command line with zip -r -X, or remove them afterwards with zip -d archive.zip "__MACOSX/*" "*.DS_Store". RStudio session files (.Rproj.user/) hold open tabs and unsaved edits; R history (.Rhistory) lists the commands typed in a session and can hold file paths and passwords; Jupyter checkpoints (.ipynb_checkpoints/) are automatic copies of notebooks; compiled Python files (__pycache__/, .pyc) are recreated when the code runs; open-document lock files (~$...docx, .~lock...#) are left while a document is open; editor backups (file~, .bak, .swp) and temporary files (.tmp, .temp) are copies a program made by itself.

Keep only on purpose: version control folders (.git, .svn, .hg) hold the project’s history, which can include earlier versions of files and data that was later removed; editor settings (.idea, .vscode) can hold paths on your own computer. A .gitignore based on GitHub’s templates (https://github.com/github/gitignore) keeps them out of a repository.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The present study investigated the spatial working memory (WM) ability and its developmental change, utilizing large-scale cross-sectional and longitudinal data from an adaptive learning environment. Approximately 16% of Dutch primary school children participated in a spatial serial recall task between 2019 and 2025, providing dense, continuous measurements of the same children in their regular educational environment.

Cross-sectional analyses demonstrated the increase of working memory capacity between the ages of six and twelve on average by one item. However, the spatial complexity of the lists affected this capacity increase. Moreover, longitudinal analyses of accuracy demonstrated WM ability maturation over time, yet there was no impact of practicing the task. Furthermore, significant individual differences were observed among children. Future studies should focus on a better assessment of spatial complexity and the underlying sources for individual differences in WM development.

Introduction

Working memory (WM) is a mental system that holds information available for processing (Logie & Cowan, 2015). Therefore, WM is a key component of higher-level cognitive functioning. Depending on the type of information being processed, WM can be divided into different domains (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974; Shah & Miyake, 1996). One of these is spatial WM, which involves maintaining and manipulating information temporarily about the location of objects and their spatial relations. It is generally assumed that WM abilities develop over the course of childhood (Cowan, 2023; Waterman, Atkinson, & Allen, 2026). Throughout this development, there is a large body of evidence supporting the relationship between WM ability and other cognitive abilities such as math, spatial reasoning, and academic performance (Allen, Giofrè, Higgins, & Adams, 2021; Allen, Higgins, & Adams, 2019; Alloway & Alloway, 2010). The purpose of the current study is to assess how spatial WM ability develops. To do so, we employ a large scale dataset from a real-life learning tool using cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses.

Measurement of WM ability

One important aspect of WM ability is WM capacity, which represents the number of items that can be accessed in WM at any given time (Cowan, 2001). WM capacity is limited and increases over childhood, even when accounting for increase in knowledge, the ability to allocate attention, and strategy (Cowan, 2023). This finding may indicate that in addition to refining cognitive abilities as a whole, children may experience maturation of WM capacity (Cowan, 2016).

Measuring WM capacity, however, is not simple. First, there is no consensus on what the best measure of WM capacity is (Reynolds, Niileksela, Gignac, & Sevillano, 2022).

Typical measures rely on the set size at which a child answers a list correctly (Cowan, 2016; Finch, 2019). Reynolds et al. (2022) compared different scoring measures with varying levels of strictness and concluded that they measured verbal WM development equally well. Second, the design of the WM task affects measures of WM capacity. For example, in visuo-spatial WM tasks, the spatiotemporal structure of a list can facilitate the recall of more items, hence inflating WM capacity measures. Consecutive items that are in the same row, column, or diagonal of a grid are more likely to be recalled correctly than if they do not share any spatial characteristics (Bor, Duncan, Wiseman, & Owen, 2003; De Lillo, Kirby, & Poole, 2016; Magen & Emmanouil, 2019). There are recent studies that incorporate spatial chunking and stimulus complexity in adults (De Sardenberg Schmid & Hardiess, 2024; Huang, Ke, Li, Liu, & Ming, 2025); however, the operationalization of spatial chunking is often not clearly specified. Furthermore, in children, a small number of studies have examined the effects of spatial complexity in spatial serial recall tasks (Nutley, Söderqvist, Bryde, Humphreys, & Klingberg, 2009; Zhang et al., 2022). However, it was not clear how spatial chunking was defined in these studies. In sum, measuring WM capacity is not straightforward. When measuring WM capacity in spatial WM tasks, it is necessary to define an appropriate quantification for the spatial complexity of the items within a list.

Cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses of WM ability

Within the area of WM development, recent studies have utilized large-scale data but much of the empirical work is cross-sectional (e.g., Waterman et al., 2026). That is, WM ability is assessed for children of different ages at the same time. The advantage of this approach is its efficiency. There are, however, a number of disadvantages. First, it is difficult to assess the development of WM performance when average scores for all age groups are calculated. These averages may obscure developmental patterns. Figure 1A shows a hypothetical example. In this example, WM develops in stages represented by the staircases: With each step in the development, WM capacity increases by one item. These steps may occur at different times for each child. The points represent WM capacity measures for different children who experience one of the two possible developmental patterns. If WM performance is averaged across individuals, stages disappear and development looks linear. Another issue with this averaging is that individual differences in development, such as between the two underlying developmental patterns, cannot be captured. There are few examples in the literature that employ longitudinal designs to study WM ability (Ahmed, Ellis, Ward, Chaku, & Davis-Kean, 2022; Lehmann, 2015), mainly focusing on verbal tasks, rather than spatial tasks. The studies showed increases and sometimes nonlinear growth in WM ability. The key advantage of these longitudinal designs is that they separate within-person change from between-person differences. However, due to their high cost and logistical demands, longitudinal studies rarely include intensive measurement, and the limited number of observations per occasion makes it difficult to distinguish true WM capacity from day-to-day fluctuations in performance (Figure 1B).

In summary, the typical implementation of cross-sectional and longitudinal designs in the literature has disadvantages: cross-sectional work averages over individuals and longitudinal work samples them too infrequently. However, to capture true changes in individual WM development, a large number of observations and repeated measurements occasions within children are required. Our study combines these features by leveraging large-scale cross-sectional and longitudinal data with repeated observations within children.

Current Study

The current study has two aims. First, we aim to investigate different WM capacity measures. Second, we aim to assess the developmental change of WM capacity and performance. To achieve these aims we employ large-scale cross-sectional and longitudinal data from an adaptive spatial recall task. Adaptive learning environments provide a unique and ecologically valid opportunity to investigate cognitive development in large and diverse samples of children. In a previous study, we utilized data from this learning environment to replicate several benchmark findings of adults in children for serial recall, including set-size effects (Ertekin et al., 2025). This study primarily focused on within-list performance, adaptive ability measures, list difficulty, and error patterns, while the current study focuses on development of capacity and performance.

Our study contributes to the field of WM development in several ways. First, our dataset is exceptionally large: it includes about 16% of the primary school students in the Netherlands between the 2019 and 2024 school years (Dienst Uitvoering Onderwijs, 2026).

Most critically, the dataset offers dense, continuous measurement of the same children over extended periods, yielding around 24 million of observations for 350,000 children. This measurement density allows us to characterize individual developmental trajectories with far greater resolution than traditional designs. Second, because the adaptive game calibrates list difficulty to each child's current ability level, it avoids the typical measurement problems of fixed difficulty assessments: If the same items are used for children with very different abilities -either across or within age groups -it is common to observe floor and ceiling effects, or even the disengagement of children who experience the task as too difficult or too easy (Gonthier, Aubry, & Bourdin, 2018). Third, by measuring spatial rather than verbal WM in longitudinal data, we broaden the empirical and methodological basis for WM development.

We approach WM development from two complementary perspectives: cross-sectional for a simple representation of the population-level structure, and longitudinal for a deeper understanding of individual-level dynamics. First, in the cross-sectional analyses, we evaluate different WM capacity measures in the spatial task and assess relations between school year (grade 1-6) and WM capacity measures in the adaptive system. We particularly focus on the effect of spatial configuration on this relationship as the spatial structure of a list can support recall and can therefore inflate WM capacity estimates.

Second, we use longitudinal accuracy data to characterize developmental trajectories within individual children, including maturation and practice of the spatial task. In this type of intense longitudinal data, any improvement in WM performance could be due to natural WM development (i.e., maturation) or to better understanding the setting and rules of the current task. Therefore, these two forms of performance improvement have to be distinguished. Our dataset enables us to do this by assessing maturation effects in children with sparse playing behavior over a long period of time and by assessing practice effects in children with intense playing behavior. Note that because we only use one task, simple practice effects cannot be distinguished from transfer effects (Von Bastian & Oberauer, 2014). Together, the cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses extend previous findings (Ahmed et al., 2022; Reynolds et al., 2022) from the verbal domain to the spatial domain using a more fine grained design.

Method Mole Game and the Adaptive Learning Environment

Data were collected from the online adaptive learning platform Prowise Learn (https://www.oefenweb.nl), which focuses on K-12 education. Children practice in mathematics and language skills across 57 different learning games. The learning platform is used in Dutch schools for practice, therefore, the Mole game was not built for specific research questions. The data from the platform nevertheless provide a means for studying WM development in students' regular practice environment by analyzing millions of responses from thousands of users.

Figure 2 depicts a trial in the "Mole game" ("Mollenspel" in Dutch), a spatial forward serial recall task, used in the present study. When playing the game, children see a list, that is a sequence of moles (i.e., items) appearing in a grid, varying in 3 × 3, 3 × 4, 3 × 5, 4 × 3, 4 × 4, 4 × 5, 5 × 3, 5 × 4, or 5 × 5 configurations. The number of moles in a list (set size) varies between 1 and 9. See Supplement B for the distribution of list characteristics including, set size, grid configurations, and spatial structure.

In the Mole game, lists are presented as sessions of 10 trials where each trial may contain different numbers of moles. An empty grid is presented throughout a trial and each mole pops up (625 ms), stays still (1000 ms), and backs down (625 ms) in a square of the grid (see Figure 2). There is no time between the appearances of two consecutive moles, and the same square can be used by more than one mole in a list, and even in immediate succession. Presented lists are selected by the adaptive system to match the ability of the children. The children's task is to recall and then indicate the moles' positions by pressing the corresponding squares in the same order. After indicating the response for the entire trial, which turns squares to blue with the corresponding serial position numbers, they click the "OK" button to submit the response. If the response is correct, the answer becomes green. If the response for the trial is incorrect, the response becomes red, and the correct serial positions of the moles in corresponding squares appear. If children correctly submit all the moles in the trial within a 20-s time frame, they earn coins that can be used to acquire trophies. As children progress through the session, the coins they collect are seen in blue on the top right of the screen. The number of coins they earn or lose is related to the accuracy, time it takes for them to respond and the game difficulty they choose. The adaptivity of the platform relies on the on-the-fly Elo-based estimation algorithm originating from chess (Elo, 1978), where each player has a unique rating reflecting their ability. Differences between expected (based on the ratings) and observed outcomes are used to update the players' ratings. In Prowise Learn, students do not compete against each other like in chess, but they compete against the lists: The accuracy and speed of a student's response to a list affect both the student's ability rating and the list's difficulty rating. If a student correctly and swiftly remembers a list, their ability rating increases; if a student answers incorrectly or too slowly, their ability rating decreases. In contrast to traditional testing methodologies, this dual analytic capability enables the system to not only evaluate a student's ability but also to determine the list difficulty based on playing behavior. This feature is particularly powerful because it enables researchers to study how the characteristics of the lists contribute to the students' performance. This self-organizing system does not rely on pilot testing or reference group data, ensuring that adding more lists and participants does not affect the test validity and reliability (Brinkhuis et al., 2018; Klinkenberg, Straatemeier, & van der Maas, 2011). Additionally, to keep students motivated, they can choose the global difficulty level of the lists they are presented with.

The easy level ensures that they are able to answer about 90% of the trials correctly, the medium level keeps accuracy around 75%, and the hard level keeps the accuracy around 60% (Jansen et al., 2013). This difficulty level can be selected globally for all games by the student and modified at any time (for more details, see Klinkenberg et al., 2011; Maris & van der Maas, 2012).

Participants

Prowise Learn is presented as a gamified practice environment for schools and families. Children access the platform via schools or family subscription. Many of the math games are used as practice in class or homework. The Mole game is one of the games under the Creative Games category of Math Garden (Rekentuin in Dutch) on the platform. Participation in research is voluntary and those who choose not to participate are excluded from the study. Participants play the games both at school and at home, deciding for themselves or under the instruction of a teacher when to play, how long to play, and which learning activities to complete. Data collection was approved by the ethics review board of the University of Amsterdam (Approval No. FMG-12276). Data were collected from 357,931 primary school children for the Mole game, from Grade 1 (age around 6-7) to Grade 6 (age around 12), resulting in a total of 23,760,350 trials between 2019 and 2025, which corresponds to about 16% of the children between grades 1 to 6 (Dienst Uitvoering Onderwijs, 2026). Dutch primary education begins at age 4 in Group 1. We therefore included children in dataset starting from Group 3 (around age 6-7), which roughly corresponds to Grade 1 in many other school systems. Gender (coded as female/male) was available for only about half of the data and therefore not used to characterize the full sample. No information on other demographics or socioeconomic characteristics was provided to the researchers.

Data Selection and Analyses

To assess the data cross-sectionally and longitudinally, analyses were conducted at different levels of the data. Because we did not design the game ourselves and the data were not collected in a controlled setting, we filtered them before analysis. Figure 3 Data included accuracy of each trial, child ability after every trial, the list difficulty, and list characteristics, i.e., the location of each mole. For cross-sectional analyses, we computed set size, which constitutes an important component for list difficulty (Ertekin et al., 2025). We first analyzed the set size of lists as a proxy of WM capacity. Next, we explored the role of spatial structure in lists and its potential influence on WM capacity estimates (see Figure 4 for the operationalization of spatial structure). In the game, lists mostly have some level of spatial structure (see Supplement C). Children may encounter both structured and unstructured lists of the same set size across different sessions. For example, a child may complete a structured set-size-4 list early in their interaction with the game and later encounter an unstructured set-size-4 list. If only set size is considered, such differences in structure may obscure meaningful interpretation of WM capacity. Therefore, next, the effect of spatial structure on WM capacity, and its development was analyzed.

For analyses, we used a combination of Bayes factor tests of independence, Bayesian parameter estimation and Bayesian model comparison with Bayes factors.

For longitudinal analyses, we derived temporal variables to investigate the effects of maturation and practice over years. Figure 5 depicts how these variables are coded for the first two weeks of an exemplary child. The starting age represents the age a child starts playing the game. We expect better WM performance for older children due to maturation.

In addition, the days since start represents the number of days since a child's first game-play in the data. As we control for practice over days with the between-day gap variable, days since start represents the maturation and we expect to find a positive effect. The within-day trial index represents the sequential trial number within a day. If practice improved children's accuracy, children should become more accurate in later trials as they progress through a day. Therefore, we would expect a positive effect of this variable. Alternatively, the adaptive algorithm may account for this practice. Because children's ability estimation is updated after every trial, a correct answer increases the difficulty of the list in the next trial. Any gains from practice would then be offset by harder lists, which would lead to no effect of practice. An alternative explanation is fatigue, if no practice effects are found. However, the children can stop the game at any time and resume it whenever they want. Therefore, the effect of fatigue will most likely be minor. Finally, the between-day gap represents the number of days since the last game-play.

In our data, game-play frequency varies between children. For example, some children play every other day while others play every other month. Therefore, smaller between-day gap means more frequent play and practice over days. If practice improved children's accuracy, we would expect a negative effect of this variable. In contrast, bigger gaps between game-play correspond to more time for natural maturation in WM. If maturation is more important than practicing the game, we may find positive effects.

Overall, in this adaptive system, if developmental change reflects only maturation, accuracy should increase with starting age (between-child) and days since start (within-child). If developmental change reflects only practice, accuracy should increase with within-day trial index, and decrease with between-day gap. Finally, a mixture of maturation and practice effects would be reflected by positive effects in starting age, days since start and within-day trial index, and depending on which side is stronger we would expect a positive or a negative between-day gap effect.

In this study, we hypothesized that between-child maturation (starting age) and within-child maturation (days since start) would show positive associations with trial-level accuracy. We further hypothesized that practice-related predictors would show that practice would be less influential than maturation. Because the algorithm expects increasing performance after correct answers and practice effects may not be strong enough to support this expectation, we predicted that the probability of a correct response should decrease with increasing within-day trial index. However, between-day gap would show a positive effect on accuracy, consistent with maturation rather than practice benefits. Accuracy on each trial was regressed on game difficulty and temporal variables as fixed effects, and child random intercepts and random slopes for days since start, with these terms specified as uncorrelated, using a Bayesian logistic mixed-effects model with the mean-field algorithm. Results

Cross-Sectional Analyses

For the cross-sectional analyses of the data, we first analyzed the set size distribution in the adaptive data. Then, we identified a measure of WM capacity. Finally, we modeled the development of children's WM capacity.

The Last Set Size Distribution in the Adaptive System.

To obtain independent observations per child, we selected each child's latest correct trial in the dataset and determined the set size. Figure 6 depicts the proportion of these last correctly answered trials by grade level (i.e., 1 to 6), the last set size (i.e., 2 to 7) and game setting (i.e., Easy, Medium, and Hard). The figure shows that the set size of the most frequently played lists (i.e., the last set size) increases through grade levels. Then, Bayes factor tests of independence between grade level and the last set size were conducted separately for each difficulty setting, using the BayesFactor package with the default concentration parameter (a = 1) (Gunel & Dickey, 1974). This test assumes independent observations, which is violated when a child contributes multiple set sizes. We therefore used the latest trial for each child rather than all trials as specified in the preregistration. This change did not alter the results and conclusions.

Bayes factor tests of independence compares two models. Under the null model, the proportion of observations for each last set size is the same in every grade, and, under the alternative model, the proportion of observations for each last set size varies across grades.

Evidence against independence was decisive in all conditions (log 10 BF easy = 668, log 10 BF medium = 936, log 10 BF hard = 1749). These results suggest that the set size of the lists children engage with tracks developmental differences and may therefore be used as a measure of WM capacity.

Set Size as WM Capacity Measure.

To identify an appropriate measure of WM capacity within the adaptive system, we computed Bayes factors (BF) for models predicting children's latest ability rating from different summaries of set size (mean, median, maximum, and 90th percentile), focusing on the hard difficulty setting. BFs were computed using the generalTestBF function from the BayesFactor package (v0.9.12.4.6) with default priors, comparing each predictor model against an intercept-only model (Morey & Rouder, 2026). Mean set size had the largest BF, therefore, it was selected as the primary WM capacity measure for subsequent analyses (see Supplement D).

WM Capacity Development.

For each child we computed the WM capacity measure (mean set size of correctly answered trials) for each structure count (see Figure 4 for structure counts) to account for the effect of spatial configurations on WM capacity estimates. Figure 7 plots the across-child means of these values per grade and structure count, together with the overall grade means (black dashed line). There is a clear development in WM capacity when the lists are unstructured (i.e., when structure count equals 0). However, when the lists are highly structured, some children as early as Grade 1 achieve a WM capacity measure of five. We then fitted a Bayesian mixed-effects regression predicting the WM capacity measure (i.e., mean set size) on correct trials from grade, structure count, and their interaction, with a random intercept per child. Grade was modeled as a monotonic effect (Bürkner & Charpentier, 2020), so that its coefficient represents the expected average difference between two adjacent grades and the associated simplex parameters describe how that change is distributed over successive grade steps. We placed a uniform Dirichlet prior on the simplex (the brms default) and informative priors on the remaining coefficients the latest. The interaction was negative but small (b = -0.03), indicating that the larger the structure count, the smaller the developmental change (see Supplement D).

Between-child differences accounted for a large share of variance.

Longitudinal Analyses

To examine how children's working memory (WM) performance develops over time within the adaptive learning system, trial-level accuracy was modeled using a Bayesian mixed-effects logistic regression. We compared three models' predictive performance to see if inclusion of maturation and practice predictors improved the null model (see Supplement E). In the final model, accuracy on each trial was regressed on game difficulty, maturation (starting age, days since start), and practice (within-day trial index, between-day gap). The model included by-child random intercepts and uncorrelated by-child random slopes for days since start, as well as random intercepts for lists. We verified the stability of parameter estimates across four runs (see Supplement E for the estimation plot and weakly informative priors). Fixed effects of the model showed a clear between-child maturation effect: children who started older were more accurate (starting age: posterior mean = 0.162, SE = 0.001, 95% CI = [0.159, 0.164]). There was also within-child maturation over time (days since start: mean = 0.082, SE = 0.003, 95% CI = [0.076, 0.087]). Within a day, accuracy declined across successive trials (within-day trial index: mean = -0.071, SE = 0.002, 95% CI = [-0.075, -0.066]), showing no practice effect in the adaptive system. The gap between game-play also supported the absence of a practice effect in the system (between-day gap: mean = 0.015, SE = 0.001, 95% CI = [0.012, 0.017]), indicating that longer gaps led to improved accuracy. For game difficulty, the effects were either zero or very small across four different seeds.

Random effects revealed substantial variability at both the child and list levels.

Children differed considerably in their baseline ability (SD = 0.471, 95% CI [0.466, 0.475] on the log-odds scale), and also differed in their rate of improvement over game duration (SD = 0.152 [0.151, 0.153]). Lists also varied in difficulty (SD = 0.244 [0.240, 0.249]). To visualize the key developmental patterns in the model, Figure 8 shows the predicted accuracy as a function of variables for each difficulty level. Fitted values were obtained with the fitted() function in brms, from the population-level coefficients only and evaluated over a grid spanning the 1st to 99th observed percentile of each predictor while holding the remaining predictors at their means. For the plot, grid values were back-transformed to the original units.

Discussion and Conclusion

The present work aimed to assess the development of children's working memory ability, by providing a fine-grained analysis of large-scale cross-sectional and longitudinal data. What sets our study apart is using large-scale real-life data from an adaptive learning environment with a spatial serial forward recall task. Our data are dense and big, equivalent to about 16% of the currently enrolled primary school students in the Netherlands. Combining the advantages of cross-sectional and longitudinal data analyses, repeated measurements of children over several years allowed us to characterize individual WM development, while distinguishing developmental changes from performance fluctuations. We conducted a number of cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, assessing our hypotheses that (1.) working memory develops considerably between ages six and twelve, (2.) that the spatial structure affects measurement of WM capacity, and (3.) that there is evidence for maturation of working memory ability within children, and (4.) that practice effects are less pronounced than maturation effects. In addition, we found considerable individual differences across children, even within the same age group. The results provided evidence in favor of all of these hypotheses.

With real-life large-scale data, fitting cognitive models of WM capacity is difficult, and tracking accuracy is not straightforward in an adaptive environment. In the cross-sectional analyses, we therefore asked whether the set size of a list can serve as a proxy for WM capacity. As Elo-based ability ratings are already established as a psychometric tool for representing WM ability of children (Ertekin et al., 2025; Ven, Maas, Straatemeier, & Jansen, 2013), we used them as a benchmark to select our capacity measure as the mean set size of correctly answered lists among other summary indices. Our results, however, showed that recall performance depends not only on set size of the list but also on the spatial configuration of the list. Relying on set size alone without considering the effect of spatial complexity therefore inflates estimated capacity and, more consequentially, obscures its development. For highly structured lists, WM capacity estimates were inflated in the youngest children and the developmental differences largely disappeared, whereas unstructured lists showed a clear developmental increase. When considering the spatial complexity, WM capacity increased gradually across grades replicating studies in the literature, but with diminishing growth toward later grades (ages around 12-13). These findings imply that WM capacity development cannot be measured independently of the spatial configuration of lists.

We quantified spatial configuration as spatial structure, and we do not claim it includes all spatial information that affects recall. The existing literature on quantifying spatial configuration or chunking is inconsistent and relies in part on subjective measures (e.g., De Sardenberg Schmid & Hardiess, 2024; Huang et al., 2025). In addition, the candidate spatial variables of a list are strongly intercorrelated, which makes their separate contributions hard to isolate. It is also not feasible in practice to construct an item bank of a comparable size to ours in which the items of a list stand in no spatial relation to one another. Therefore, future studies need theoretically motivated work that extends the measurement of spatial WM capacity beyond the number of items recalled to the spatial configuration of these items.

In the longitudinal analyses, we showed that WM performance increases with maturation across primary school by extending studies on verbal WM development (Ahmed et al., 2022; Lehmann, 2015) to spatial WM development. Moreover, there was no practice effect in our system. One key result was the extent of individual differences: children of the same age differ substantially, both in the level and in the rate of development. Figure 9 provides examples of children who played the game for more than four years. Some children developed quickly and then stagnated, while others showed more continuous improvement in performance. Because our data are intensive and span long periods, individual differences are unlikely to only reflect day-to-day fluctuations in performance. These results suggest that WM development is not a uniform maturation process, and that children may differ in the timing and pace of change. In addition, the different trajectories and rates suggest that children may reach similar levels through different developmental curves.

What accounts for these differences in development is beyond the scope of the current study. They may reflect the strategies children use and when they start to use them, especially while encoding spatial complexity of lists, or other child-level factors such as socioeconomic status that we did not analyze. Furthermore, in our longitudinal analyses, the game difficulty settings had no detectable effect on accuracy. This contrasts with practice-based games in the system, such as math games for the practice of addition, where difficulty settings do affect performance. One explanation might be that WM ability is resistant to practice, similar to other stable cognitive abilities (Breit, Scherrer, Tucker-Drob, & Preckel, 2024), while math abilities are more pliable.

Overall, we showed maturation of WM ability in primary school in an adaptive system utilizing cross-sectional analyses, replicating established results, and longitudinal analyses, extending from a verbal task to a spatial task. These two sets of analyses allowed us to address methodological difficulties and substantive gaps in the WM development literature. Taken together, they show that with a careful data selection, adaptive learning environments generate data of a scale that laboratory designs rarely achieve, and provide a real-world, externally valid setting to study cognitive development.
