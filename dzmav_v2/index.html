Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). Abstract

Psychology's difficulties with replication and generalizability suggest that, however valuable, statistical and incentive reforms have not sufficed to stabilize its core constructs. It is precisely a residual implicit dualism, I argue, that contributes to this instability: the acceptance of loosely defined constructs, sustained by metaphor and detached from any concrete organized system capable of doing causal work. I propose the Materialist Model of Psychological Science (MMPS), a framework resting on four axioms (physical realization, multi-level constraint, evolutionary plausibility, and mechanistic falsifiability) and operationalized through an eight-criterion ontological audit. Its central commitment is a strict material-anchoring criterion: a construct counts as materially anchored only if it names a property, state, or process of a concrete organized system specifiable at some scale of organization, whether molecular, neural, physiological, organism-in-environment, or a concrete social configuration. The scale of concrete organization is unrestricted; the type of entity is not. Cultural, social, and contextual factors may therefore anchor a construct in their own right, provided they are cashed out as a specifiable set of persons in interaction whose composition, structure, and mechanism are stated (e.g., a measured distribution of enforcement behaviors); they may equally enter as concrete inputs modulating a mechanism at another scale. What the framework excludes, rather, is a reified abstractum credited with causal power of its own -norms, values, or 'culture' as free-standing causes. A two-step test (person-removal, then locus of causal power) screens any social, cultural, or contextual factor offered as an anchor. I illustrate the audit's operation through three worked examples (ego depletion, implicit bias, and fear conditioning), chosen to span the range from ontologically thin to mechanistically dense constructs; these examples show how the criteria discriminate and served to develop the audit; they are not an independent test of it. An out-of-sample, blinded test of whether the audit predicts replication is described separately, in a Stage 1 Registered Report protocol. The paper outlines complementary reforms, namely mechanismfocused preregistration, invariance profiling in multilab work, perturbation toolkits, and open construct registries, that redirect theory building toward cumulative, vertically integrated knowledge, while preserving exploratory diversity through graded evidential expectations instead of tolerating ontologically inflated constructs. The MMPS thus adds explicit mechanistic, evolutionary, and perturbational constraints to classical construct validity, turning diffuse calls for 'better theory' into concrete, auditable criteria for accepting, revising, or suspending psychological constructs.

Introduction

Large-scale replication initiatives have revealed that a large proportion of psychological findings demonstrate reproducibility at lower effect sizes. Furthermore, these findings are often rendered invalid when subjected to rigorous, preregistered, multi-lab protocols that employ higher powers of analysis (Open Science Collaboration, 2015). For example, two preregistered multi-laboratory tests of ego depletion failed to recover the canonical performance decrement (Hagger et al., 2016; Dang et al., 2021), meta-analytic reassessments reported small or null overall effects after bias corrections (Carter et al., 2015), and subsequent stricter inclusion criteria further attenuated estimates (Dang, 2018; Vadillo, Gold, & Osman, 2018). These outcomes have challenged the durability of the "limited resource" metaphor that for years organized explanations of self-control failure. Behavioral priming shows an analogous pattern: a multi-laboratory registered replication of "professor" priming found no effect (O'Donnell et al., 2018). However, proponents of the concept have noted that when depletion tasks are lengthened or made more arduous, minor effects re-emerge. Furthermore, they have observed that different task variants yield disparate outcomes (Dang, 2018). They have also noted that refinements, such as transitioning from a "run out of energy" model to a "motivated conservation" model, serve to reconcile previous data. Recent reviews have asserted that rigorously executed studies now tend to confirm ego-depletion effects under the appropriate conditions (Baumeister et al., 2024). Instead of a mere nullification, the replication efforts have led theorists to refine definitions of what "depletion" signifies and to identify which specific tasks genuinely tax self-control, as opposed to abandoning it.

A parallel example comes from implicit social cognition. Meta-analysis of the Implicit Association Test (IAT) found modest predictive validity (Greenwald, Poehlman, Uhlmann, & Banaji, 2009). Specifically, while the IAT frequently correlates weakly with self-report measures (positive incremental prediction), its association with actual behavior is modest.

Nevertheless, this debate has not disproven the concept of implicit bias. Critics have noted that IAT variants exhibit significant variability in terms of reliability and outcomes. They contend that the absence of converging evidence from multiple methods renders a solitary implicit measure challenging to interpret. This finding aligns with the established principles of construct validation theory. According to Campbell & Fiske (1959) and Cronbach & Meehl (1955), psychological constructs must be supported by diverse evidence, that is, convergent and discriminant validity, as well as a nomological network. The IAT and related measures have been demonstrated to index "broad implicit attitudes." However, these attitudes have yet to be definitively attributed to specific neural or cognitive mechanisms. When observed behaviors do not align with predictions, researchers can propose various post-hoc explanations (e.g., task differences, contextual effects) rather than refuting the construct: this process has the effect of rendering our constructs somewhat flexible and contested (Greenwald et al., 2020).

Conventional construct validation frameworks, such as the multitrait-multimethod matrix (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) and nomological network articulation (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955), require embedding psychological constructs in theoretical and empirical relations across independent measurements. However, many contemporary theoretical labels, such as "resources," "undifferentiated executive control," and "broad implicit attitudes," remain inadequately specified with respect to underlying mechanisms. This allows for post-hoc explanatory flexibility when findings fail to replicate. This suggests that the replication shortfall is partly symptomatic of an implicit permissiveness about ontological commitments.

That is, constructs are deemed explanatory before they achieve sufficient mechanistic individuation.

Mechanistic Testing and Falsifiability

I would therefore attribute the field's difficulty to a residual implicit dualism, that is, a practical separation between higher-level vocabularies and materially individuated mechanisms. By contrast, domains in which constructs have been progressively anchored to multi-level mechanisms display greater stability and translational reach: fear conditioning and extinction, with coordinated rodent-human circuit mapping (Milad & Quirk, 2012), and frontoparietal multiple-demand (MD) networks underlying flexible cognitive control (Duncan, 2010; Fedorenko, Duncan, & Kanwisher, 2013), offer exemplars of evolutionary plausibility, anatomical specificity, and perturbation testability. Such exemplars narrow the space of interpretation, thereby limiting the ad hoc rescue hypotheses that so often accompany fragile behavioral effects. A companion paper develops the diagnostic side of this claim, namely how a discipline can be materialist in name while its constructs remain unanchored in practice (Zerhouni, under review).

Mechanistic standards in contemporary neuroscience underscore why such integration matters: explanation strengthens when component interactions are specified such that targeted perturbations (lesion, stimulation, pharmacological modulation) produce predicted behavioral and physiological changes (de Haan & Karnath, 2018; Jannati et al., 2022). A fundamental tenet of Craver's mechanistic approach (Craver, 2007 (Craver, , 2009) ) asserts that an explanation is more robust when it delineates specific components and their interactions. This delineation enables the prediction of alterations that result from interventions on these components. The approach is put into practice by using lesions, brain stimulation or pharmacological manipulation to evaluate theoretical concepts. For instance, if a specific brain region is theorized to be indispensable for a particular cognitive function, then its impairment or disruption should result in a corresponding decline in performance, thereby serving as a "lesion-symptom" test. Noninvasive brain stimulation, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), is frequently characterized as inducing a transient "virtual lesion." This is achieved by temporarily disrupting cortical activity, thus eliciting an observable causal effect on behavior (Pascual-Leone, Walsh, & Rothwell, 2000).

Constructs that do not specify any manipulable mechanism, however, cannot be tested in this manner. For instance, the assertion that "people ran out of willpower" is challenging to refute, as "willpower" lacks a specific locus that can be stimulated or damaged. One could argue that the task was not sufficiently draining without compromising the overarching concept. This renders the construct unfalsifiable in practice, encouraging a cumulative culture of metaphor and rhetoric rather than precise prediction. Consequently, the absence of a definitive account of the biological conveyance of a construct rules out perturbation tests. None of this means that higher-level concepts are useless: they serve as heuristic starting points. The problem arises when they are treated as explanations instead of placeholders for mechanisms yet to be specified.

Higher Resolution and the Risk of Fragmentation

One may object that new methods, such as high-resolution imaging or individualized brain mapping, will eventually unveil further details of these vague constructs without requiring such ontological discipline. One should nonetheless remain cautious, for history shows that increasingly detailed measurement can just as easily obscure our ideas if the central concept is not clearly defined. For instance, delineating numerous subregions within the prefrontal cortex will prove beneficial only insofar as each is associated with a distinct function; absent this, the alternative risks engendering a convoluted network of intercorrelations devoid of any unifying explanatory principle. Absent an explicit commitment to the delineation between psychological labels and their biological correlates, one risks accumulating "orphan" findings incapable of cohering into a genuine theoretical framework. In other words, rapid progress in methodological resolution, be it refined lesion-behavior mapping or individualized stimulation targeting, risks amplifying fragmentation if the ontological status of the discipline lags behind, since greater granularity may simply decorate under-specified entities with additional correlational detail. A further consideration is that vagueness is not always a passive defect merely awaiting better methods as Frankenhuis, Panchanathan and Smaldino (2023) argue that ambiguity in social-science constructs can be strategically advantageous to theorists, an imprecise construct being harder to falsify and easier to reconcile with discordant results. If some of the imprecision the audit targets is sustained by incentive rather than by ignorance, then methodological refinement alone cannot remove it, and an explicit ontological criterion, one that renders the absence of a specifiable realizer visible and scoreable, constitutes the more direct remedy. This tension is the reason for the Materialist Model of Psychological Science (MMPS). The MMPS clearly connects ideas in the mind to mechanisms at many levels. The model's core idea is that all psychological claims should be grounded in physical systems and meet specific criteria. The MMPS articulates the following axioms: physical realization, multi-level constraint, evolutionary plausibility, and mechanistic falsifiability. It is operationalized through an eight-criterion ontological audit: material anchoring, multi-method convergence, mechanistic specificity, evolutionary plausibility, contextual invariance, measurement traceability, prospective predictive performance, and integrative compatibility. We demonstrate this approach using an "ontological audit" for constructs. This audit is a checklist of eight criteria that robust psychological constructs should meet. These criteria encourage an integrative strategy by tying high-level concepts to lower-level biological mechanisms.

The Materialist Model of Psychological Science (MMPS): Axioms and Rationale

The MMPS is built on four axioms that specify what it takes for a psychological construct to earn its place. Each axiom imposes a constraint that, individually, is uncontroversial, but taken together, they rule out a surprisingly large class of constructs that the field currently treats as established.

The following four axioms are proposed as a means to transform a general materialist stance into an operational research program. Physical Realization posits that a legitimate psychological construct is defined by a reproducible pattern of causal organization instantiated in a specifiable concrete organized system, that is, a system characterizable through its composition, environment, structure, and mechanism. The scale is unrestricted: a neural circuit, a molecular or physiological pathway, an organism acting in an environment, or a concrete social configuration (a specifiable set of persons in interaction) all qualify. What the axiom excludes is not a level of organization but a type of entity, namely an abstract object credited with causal power of its own, such as a reified norm, value, meaning, or identity-narrative. De-neuralizing a construct is therefore permitted; de-materializing it is not. This extends the classic construct validity requirements that measures be embedded in theoryconstrained nomological networks (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Campbell & Fiske, 1959) and aligns with mechanistic norms in contemporary explanation (Craver, 2007). It rests on causal closure: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause (Kim, 1998). If that is true, then any psychological phenomenon that produces a measurable effect, that is, a behavior, a physiological change, a neural activation, must itself be physical. Non-physical constructs, by definition, cannot enter the causal chain. This is the core commitment that separates the MMPS from frameworks tolerating explanatory vocabularies disconnected from any concrete organized system. Multi-Level Constraint posits the reciprocal relationship between, on the one hand, behavioral regularities, computational descriptions, and phenomenological or social observations, and, on the other, neural, physiological, genetic, and evolutionary information.

This axiom rejects insulated functional vocabularies sustained only by predictive convenience or metaphor (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Craver, 2007; Duncan, 2010). Evolutionary Plausibility posits that constructs should either demonstrate an adaptive or phylogenetic rationale or provide explicit justification for their culturally emergent status, grounded in evolved enabling capacities (e.g., the coordinated rodent-human mapping in fear extinction circuitry, Milad & Quirk, 2012). Mechanistic Falsifiability stipulates that constructs must yield perturbation-sensitive predictions (e.g., lesion, stimulation, pharmacological, genetic, developmental, ecological) whose failure would motivate revision or elimination rather than ad hoc contextual rescue (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; de Haan & Karnath, 2018; Jannati et al., 2022). Although convergence across multiple independent assay families (behavioral performance, physiological indices, neuroimaging signatures, computational model fits) is implicit in the multi-level constraint axiom, I propose Convergent Multi-Method Anchoring as an auxiliary principle, one meant to recall the reduction of single-method artifacts and the historical centrality of discriminant and convergent evidence to construct legitimation (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). Multi-method integration narrows mechanistic hypothesis space by demanding that distinct operationalizations load on a common latent structure while resisting overfitting to any single paradigm; it is precisely the failures of ego depletion and the variability of implicit attitude measures that illustrate the risks of premature theoretical commitment absent such triangulation (Greenwald et al., 2009; Hagger et al., 2016; Carter et al., 2015).

These axioms aim to integrate psychological explanations into materially individuated mechanisms and distinguish them from metaphorical labels associated with replication fragility (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; Hagger et al., 2016; Carter et al., 2015) and are detailed below.

Physical realization

Physical Realization requires that every construct map onto a reproducible pattern of causal organization in some specifiable concrete organized system, a neural circuit, a physiological pathway, a molecular cascade (Poldrack, 2010), an organism acting in a measured environment, or a concrete social configuration whose composition, structure and mechanism can be stated. This does not mean that every study must include neuroimaging, nor that the realizer must be biological. It means, rather, that the construct, as defined, must be compatible with a physical implementation in some concrete system, and that this implementation must in principle be testable. A construct unable even hypothetically to specify the kind of concrete system that realizes it is, under this axiom, not yet a construct but merely a label. In practice, physical realization means seeking reproducible signatures of the realizing system: brain activity patterns or physiological reactions where the anchor is biological, and measured distributions of behavior across a specified set of interacting persons where the anchor is a social configuration. It is precisely unintended dualism that this approach counters, that is, the crediting of an abstract object with causal power of its own, and not the use of non-neural levels of description as such. De-neuralizing an explanation is permitted; de-materializing it is not.

Multi-Level Constraint

The Multi-Level Constraint axiom emphasizes the reciprocal constraints between different levels of analysis. Psychological theories should connect phenomena observed at behavioral, cognitive, and social levels with data from neural, genetic, and evolutionary levels. For instance, patterns of behavior or subjective reports should inform, and be informed by our knowledge of brain circuits, gene regulation, and adaptive history. This approach discourages functional or computational descriptions that exist in isolation and use only predictive convenience or metaphor without biological or evolutionary grounding. In line with the work of Cronbach & Meehl (1955), Campbell & Fiske (1959), Craver (2007), and Duncan (2010), this approach emphasizes integrated evidence across levels. Specifically, if a theory posits a certain mental process, researchers should test it using multiple methods, such as behavioral experiments, physiological measurements, neuroimaging, and computational modeling. If a construct is real, converging methods should converge on it. If they don't, either the methods are flawed or the construct is not what we think it is. This axiom forces that question rather than letting it remain implicit.

Evolutionary Plausibility

Psychological constructs should earn credibility when they can be tied to an evolutionary rationale (Tooby & Cosmides, 1992). Ideally, a construct is an adaptation or has a clear phylogenetic history (Tinbergen, 1963). For example, many emotional and perceptual systems can be understood as evolved solutions to survival challenges (Buss, 2015 , Barrett & Kurzban, 2006). If a phenomenon seems culturally specific, this principle suggests providing an account of how it builds on more general evolved capacities (Henrich, 2015). A classic example is fear extinction: studies have found similar brain mechanisms in rodents and humans (Milad & Quirk, 2012), which supports the idea that this learning process has a shared evolutionary basis (Tovote, Fadok, & Lüthi, 2015). Conversely, a highly abstract social phenomenon may be explained by demonstrating how it emerges from fundamental cognitive functions shaped by natural selection (Haselton & Nettle, 2006). The bar here is not a just-so story, it is a minimal requirement: can you offer a plausible account of why this capacity exists in a species shaped by selection? If you cannot, that does not kill the construct, but it should make you wonder whether you are studying a real process or a measurement artifact. Hence, incorporating evolutionary reasoning clarifies why certain cognitive or behavioral patterns exist and how they vary across species or populations. Keeping evolutionary plausibility may also help translate from basic research to clinical application (Fullana et al., 2020). Even constructs in psychopathology can be read in this way, as evolved responses or trade-offs: anxiety and low mood, for example, can be understood as defensive responses shaped by natural selection (Nesse, 2019).

Mechanistic Falsifiability

Mechanistic falsifiability emphasizes that constructs should lead to clear and testable predictions about system perturbations (Boes et al., 2015). In practice, this means that a theory should specify how the system will behave when the underlying mechanism is disrupted or modified (Boes et al., 2015 , Casey et al., 2005). For instance, if we assert that a specific brain circuit is responsible for a cognitive function, interventions such as lesions, brain stimulation, pharmacological agents, genetic modifications, or developmental changes should alter that function as predicted (Craver, 2007 , de Haan & Karnath, 2018). If an experiment fails to produce the expected result, the construct should be revised or rejected rather than being given an ad hoc explanation. In other words, it allows for falsifiability by forcing ideas to be precise enough to be proven wrong. For instance, if a cognitive theory predicts that disrupting region X impairs memory in a particular manner and this prediction fails, then the theory should be reconsidered (e.g., Tonegawa et al., 2015; Robbins & Arnsten, 2009). Mechanistic falsifiability does not imply immediate abandonment at the first setback; however, it does demand that theories have clear operational criteria for testing and potential disproof (Popper, 1959, see also Boes et al., 2015 , Joutsa et al., 2022 for lesion-network mapping).

Convergent Multi-Method Anchoring as an Auxiliary Principle

Convergent multi-method anchoring highlights the importance of using diverse methods to validate constructs. While the Multi-Level Constraint axiom implies the use of multiple lines of evidence, this principle explicitly calls for triangulation to minimize method-specific artifacts. Historically, convergent and discriminant validation (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Cronbach & Meehl, 1955) have been central to the legitimization of constructs. In contemporary practice, a putative memory construct should demonstrate aligned patterns across behavioral performance, brain imaging signatures, and computational model parameters. These patterns should be evident not only within a single task or laboratory pipeline, but also across different tasks and pipelines (Flake & Fried, 2020; Chester & Lasko, 2021; Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002). Neglecting this principle has come at a cost: ego depletion research and implicit bias studies often relied heavily on one paradigm, and effects weakened or conflicted when alternative tasks or analytic approaches were employed (Carter, Kofler, Forster, & McCullough, 2015; Greenwald, Poehlman, Uhlmann, & Banaji, 2009; Hagger et al., 2016). Cross-validation strengthens a materialist program by ensuring that conclusions are not artifacts of a single instrument, scoring rule, or context (Hedge, Powell, & Sumner, 2018).

Ontological Audit: Criteria and Scoring Framework

To operationalize the axioms, I introduce an eight-criterion Ontological Audit evaluating candidate constructs on: (1) Material Anchoring: the construct names a property, state, or process of a concrete organized system specifiable at some scale of organizationmolecular, neural, physiological, organism-in-environment, or a concrete social configuration (Poldrack, 2010). The scale of concrete organization is unrestricted; it is the type of entity that is not. Cultural, social and contextual factors may anchor a construct in their own right when cashed as a concrete configuration of persons in interaction whose composition, structure and mechanism are stated, and they may equally enter as concrete measurable inputs modulating a mechanism at another scale. What is excluded, precisely, is a reified abstractum credited with causal power of its own. The strict two-step clause (person-removal, then locus of causal power; see the next subsection) applies to any cultural, social or contextual factor offered as anchor or invoked in the anchoring story, and not to biological anchors, which are assessed directly on whether their composition, structure and mechanism are stated; (2) Multi-Method Convergence: independent operationalizations converging on a common latent pattern (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Greenwald et al., 2009); (3) Mechanistic Specificity: articulated internal causal steps mapping inputs to transformations and outputs (Craver, 2007 , Piccinini & Craver, 2011); (4) Evolutionary Plausibility: adaptive/phylogenetic grounding or a justified culturally emergent lineage grounded in evolved enabling capacities, that is, a rationale for why the capacity exists, not a substitute for the concrete-system anchoring required at C1 (Milad & Quirk, 2012); (5) Contextual Invariance Testing: systematic probing of boundary conditions across laboratories, populations, and tasks, motivated by large-scale replication shortfalls (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; Hagger et al., 2016; Carter et al., 2015); (6) Measurement Traceability: a transparent mapping from raw indicators to construct scores that minimizes construct-indicator ambiguity (Flake & Fried, 2020; Chester & Lasko, 2021; Hedge, Powell, & Sumner, 2018); (7) Prospective Predictive Performance: successful a priori or preregistered out-of-sample predictions, including perturbation outcomes; and (8)

Integrative Compatibility: coherence with established neural network findings and the absence of unaddressed conflict with solid biological evidence (Poldrack & Yarkoni, 2016 , Duncan, 2010; Kaplan & Craver, 2011). All material needed to conduct an audit is available in Appendices A to I of this paper. A summary of the appendices is given in Supplementary Material.

The Strict Material-Anchoring Clause (C1)

Material anchoring (C1) is the audit's central ontological commitment, and it is materialist about the kind of entity a construct must name rather than about the level at which it must be named: a construct is materially anchored if, and only if, it names a property, state, or process of a concrete organized system, that is, a system characterizable through its composition, environment, structure, and mechanism, specifiable at some scale of organization. That scale may be a molecular cascade, a neural circuit state (e.g., amygdala-vmPFC plasticity), a physiological pathway (e.g., an HPA-axis cortisol response), an organism-in-environment process (a person performing a task under measured demands), or a concrete social configuration (a specifiable set of persons in interaction, such as a measured distribution of norm-enforcement behaviors). The scale of concrete organization is unrestricted; the type of entity is not. It is a materialist commitment without being a reductive one: the explanatory currency is the concrete organized system at whatever scale carries the causal work, and what higher-level vocabularies must do to earn their keep is name such a system rather than float free of any. De-neuralizing an explanation is permitted; de-materializing it is not.

Cultural, social, and contextual factors are indispensable to psychology, and within this framework they may enter in either of two admissible roles: as an anchor in their own right, when the factor is cashed out as a concrete configuration of persons in interaction whose composition, structure, and mechanism are stated and in which the causal power is lodged, or as a concrete measurable input that modulates a mechanism specified at another scale. What is inadmissible is a third possibility, namely a reified abstractum credited with causal power of its own, standing over and above the persons who instantiate it. A two-step test enforces this distinction. Its scope is confined to the social case: a molecular cascade or a neural circuit would trivially survive the removal of persons, and this tells us nothing about its concreteness; step 1 is a test for reified social abstracta, not a general discriminator of concrete from abstract. A biological anchor, by contrast, is assessed directly, on whether its composition, structure, and mechanism are stated. Step 1 (person-removal) asks: if every concrete person and their physical interactions were removed, would the putative input still do causal work? If so, it is an abstract entity, a norm or value reified with independent causal power, and cannot enter the mechanism. Step 2 (locus of causal power) applies when the input does not survive person-removal, and asks where the causal power is lodged, in an abstract object standing over and above the concrete configuration, or in the concrete configuration of persons itself.

A norm reified as a free-standing prescription and a norm cashed as a measured distribution of sanctioning behaviors both die on person-removal; it is only step 2 that separates them, admitting the latter as a concrete configuration, anchor or input depending on the explanatory role the construct assigns it, and rejecting the former. The framework is thus materialist about the type of entity that may carry causal work rather than about the level at which it does so, a position that amounts neither to constructivism, since no social substance is reified, nor to a denial that culture matters, since concrete social configurations both carry causal work of their own and demonstrably shift mechanisms at other scales.

Mapping Common Failures

Drawing strong psychological conclusions from regional activation alone ('reverse inference') is statistically unreliable unless it is constrained by formal cognitive ontologies.

Overinterpreting such signals can lead to ontological inflation, which is the proliferation of labels that are not based on mechanistic specificity or predictive constraint (Poldrack & Yarkoni, 2016). Vague localization, which is the attribution of a phenomenon to an imprecise or overly broad neural region, blocks falsifiable tests (Poldrack, 2010). Single-method dependence is the reliance on one task or assay, which can result in method artifacts (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Flake & Fried, 2020). Black-box functionalism describes inputoutput regularities without specifying internal causal steps (Craver, 2007). Adaptive storytelling offers post hoc evolutionary narratives without comparative or phylogenetic evidence (Gould & Lewontin, 1979; Tinbergen, 1963). Context-limited fragility refers to effects that vanish or invert across laboratories, populations, or task variants (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; Yarkoni, 2022). Indicator proliferation involves multiplying loosely related measures under one label, which obscures what is actually being measured (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Flake & Fried, 2020). Retrospective rationalization involves explaining unexpected results after the fact instead of revising the construct (HARKing; Kerr, 1998).

Ontological inflation is the unchecked creation of new labels that duplicate or conflict with established mechanisms ("concept creep"; Haslam, 2016; Poldrack & Yarkoni, 2016). Each criterion of the audit isolates a distinct failure mode observed in contested constructs: vague localization, single-method dependence, black-box functionalism, adaptive storytelling, context-limited fragility, indicator proliferation, retrospective rationalization, and ontological inflation.

Scoring Protocol.

Each criterion is scored 0 (absent), 1 (partial), or 2 (well evidenced), producing a total index from 0 to 16. Constructs that reach a score of 14 points or above and have no zeros on the three core mechanistic criteria (i.e., Material Anchoring, Mechanistic Specificity, and Prospective Predictive Performance) are classified as Accept. Scores between 8 and 13, or a profile with exactly one core mechanistic criterion at 0, are labeled Improvable and require targeted remediation (for example, adding a perturbation study to raise C7 or a cross-method study to raise C2). Scores below or equal to 7, or any profile with two core mechanistic criteria at 0, are placed in Suspend status until substantive mechanistic evidence is produced. These rules are applied in order of severity: Suspend first, then Improvable, then Accept, and a construct receives the first class whose condition it meets. A total of 7 with one core criterion at 0 is therefore suspended, and a total of 13 is Improvable. These thresholds extend the cumulative-validation and convergent-discriminant logic articulated by Cronbach and Meehl (1955) and Campbell and Fiske (1959) into a replication-sensitive era shaped by large-scale, preregistered tests and meta-analytic corrections (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; Hagger et al., 2016; Carter, Kofler, Forster, & McCullough, 2015). The workflow proceeds through dossier assembly (tasks, measures, proposed mechanisms), criterion scoring with concise evidential notes, aggregate classification, and a remediation plan (e.g., a preregistered TMS or lesion-network study to boost predictive performance), followed by registration and periodic updating in an open construct registry to limit rediscovery cycles and selective citation (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; de Haan & Karnath, 2018; Jannati, Oberman, Rotenberg, & Pascual-Leone, 2022). Full scorecards with criterion-by-criterion justifications appear in Appendix H.

Dynamic Updating and Governance.

Scores may be "partial" because constructs are expected to shift between categories as new mechanistic evidence emerges. The system is intentionally non-dogmatic; it favors iterative refinement over the premature discarding of ideas that show a plausible path toward mechanistic specification. Multidisciplinary audit panels comprising experts in theory, neuroscience, evolutionary biology, and quantitative methods reduce the risk that any one field will dominate the evaluation. Publicly archived justifications for each criterion limit the kind of ad hoc contextual "patching" typical of fragile paradigms. By tying acceptance to mechanistic density and perturbation testability, the audit operationalizes the axioms and offers a concrete alternative to the recurring boom-and-bust cycle of metaphor-driven constructs revealed by replication work (see Appendix G).

Case Studies: Applying the Ontological Audit Negative Construct I: Ego Depletion (Limited Self-Control Resource)

Ego depletion was originally framed as a domain-general "limited resource" whose temporary expenditure impairs subsequent self-control, but large preregistered multilab replications failed to reproduce the canonical decrement (e.g., Hagger et al., 2016 , Vadillo et al., 2018), and meta-analytic re-analyses applying bias corrections substantially attenuated or nullified pooled effects, indicating weak contextual invariance (Criterion C5) and poor prospective predictive performance (C7); moreover, proposed physiological substrates (e.g., blood glucose) were either unsubstantiated or later abandoned, leaving material anchoring (C1) underspecified and the mechanistic pathway ("resource depletion") metaphorical rather than causally specific (C3).

Positive Mechanistic Exemplar: Fear Conditioning and Extinction Circuitry

Fear conditioning/extinction constitutes a mechanistically dense construct: convergent rodent-human research maps acquisition and extinction to interactions among amygdala nuclei, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and related thalamo-cortical pathways, satisfying material anchoring (C1, Maren, Phan, & Liberzon, 2013 , Tovote et al., 2015) and mechanistic specificity (C3, Quirk & Mueller, 2008 , Do-Monte, Quiñones-Laracuente, & Quirk, 2015) through delineated synaptic plasticity, inhibitory microcircuitry, and contextual modulation processes; multi-method convergence (C2, Fullana et al., 2016 , Lissek et al., 2005) spans behavior, autonomic indices, fMRI, electrophysiology, and computational learning models that quantify prediction error dynamics. Evolutionary plausibility (C4; Milad & Quirk, 2012; Maren, 2001; LeDoux, 2014) is strong given conserved defensive learning architectures across mammals, while contextual invariance (C5, Myers & Davis, 2007 , Dunsmoor & Kroes, 2019 , Wang, Olsson, Lipp, & Ney, 2024) is supported by cross-species replication of acquisition and extinction profiles and by translational paradigms (e.g., reinstatement, renewal) that generalize boundary conditions; measurement traceability (C6, Lonsdorf et al., 2017) benefits from standardized protocols (CS-US pairings, physiological readouts) and hierarchical modeling of associative strength, and prospective predictive performance (C7, Motzkin et al., 2015 , Likhtik et al., 2014) is demonstrated by perturbation studies (lesions, TMS, pharmacological modulation of NMDA or GABAergic systems) producing predicted alterations in acquisition/extinction curves.

Integrative compatibility (C8, Etkin, Büchel, & Gross, 2015) is high: findings align with broader network models of affect regulation and with executive control contributions (i.e., multiple-demand/frontoparietal systems modulating fear expression, Ochsner & Gross, 2005), while lesion and stimulation evidence reinforces necessity/sufficiency in line with mechanistic falsifiability (A4); the construct's evolutionary grounding and perturbation responsiveness also furnish clear translational pathways (e.g., exposure therapy optimization, biomarker development), illustrating how cumulative mechanistic density constrains theoretical interpretation space and limits ad hoc rescue strategies (see Appendix H).

Comparative Synthesis

Comparing audit profiles demonstrates discriminative capacity: ego depletion falls below elimination thresholds due to simultaneous deficits in anchoring, specificity, invariance, and prediction; implicit bias occupies a revisable middle zone where targeted mechanistic diversification and perturbation studies could elevate scores; fear conditioning/extinction illustrates the upper bound where multi-level constraint and perturbation falsifiability converge to produce cumulative, predictive traction, which is consistent with the hypothesis, not tested here, that replication reliability tracks mechanistic density rather than procedural rigor alone.

Mapping Audit Criteria onto the Stimulus → Mechanism → Representation → Behavior Schematic

A mechanistic account of a psychological construct can be schematized as a constrained sequence: ( i

Stimulus Layer: Input Definition and Controlled Manipulation

High-quality constructs presuppose precise specification of stimulus classes (or internal/experimental manipulations) that trigger mechanistic processing; contextual invariance (C5) evaluates whether the mapping from standardized stimuli to downstream representations generalizes across laboratories, demographic groups, and ecological variations (multi-lab failures in ego depletion demonstrate a breakdown at this entrance point).

Prospective predictive performance (C7) extends this by requiring a priori forecasts about how alterations of stimulus parameters (e.g., unconditioned stimulus intensity, timing, contingency shifts) will modulate both intermediate representations (e.g., associative strength curves in conditioning) and behavioral outputs. Without rigorous specification of stimulus control and predicted transformations, failures at later links can be obscured by uncontrolled input heterogeneity, enabling after-the-fact moderator proliferation.

Mechanism Layer: Componential and Network Organization

Material anchoring (C1) requires tying the construct to specified components of a concrete organized system. For the neural constructs discussed here, circuit nodes (e.g., basolateral amygdala or ventromedial prefrontal cortex), neurotransmitter systems, or physiological pathways, rather than to generic locational tags ("prefrontal resources"). Mechanistic specificity (C3) then demands articulation of the organized interactions (e.g., feedforward sensory relays, Hebbian plasticity at synapses, inhibitory intercalated cells mediating extinction) that transform inputs into representational states. Evolutionary plausibility (C4) constrains candidate mechanistic organizations by invoking phylogenetically conserved architectures (defensive learning circuits) or adaptive rationales, excluding ad hoc assemblies invoked solely to salvage predictions. Integrative compatibility (C8) ensures proposed component configurations do not contradict convergent lesion, stimulation, or network analyses (e.g., multiple-demand frontoparietal systems modulating control), fostering cumulative refinement rather than proliferation of parallel, incompatible mechanisms.

Representation Layer: Intermediate Internal States

Multi-method convergence (C2) evaluates whether independent measurement families (behavioral performance metrics, physiological indices, imaging activation patterns, computational model parameters) converge on the same latent representational constructs (e.g., prediction error signals, extinction memory traces) rather than fragmenting into paradigm-specific surrogates. Measurement traceability (C6) interrogates the one-to-few mapping between these latent constructs and the operational indicators, requiring explicit modeling (e.g., hierarchical Bayesian learning curves) that renders indicator variance interpretable in mechanistic terms. Failures here, such as overreliance on a single latency metric in implicit bias, produce representational ambiguity. This ambiguity leads to unstable mechanistic inferences and inflated flexibility in theory adjustment. These issues undermine falsifiability despite apparent behavioral regularities. Representations may be regarded as population-code configurations whose informative content is exposed by the representational dissimilarity framework and lawful alterations across processing phases. This frames "representation" as a physically instantiated encoding state, which is testable with multivariate analyses and model-based predictions, and which supports C1/C3/C6 (Kriegeskorte & Diedrichsen, 2019).

Behavior Layer: Observable Outputs and Perturbation Signatures

Contextual invariance (C5) at the behavior tier requires that predicted output patterns (e.g., extinction curve acceleration following strengthened vmPFC inhibition) replicate under controlled variations, so that lawful boundary conditions can be distinguished from post hoc moderators. Prospective predictive performance (C7) obliges preregistered forward predictions, not only about behavioral metrics (reaction times, error rates, physiological responses) but about how experimental or causal perturbations (lesions, TMS pulses, pharmacological manipulations) will shift those metrics in quantitatively specified ways.

Integrative compatibility (C8) cross-validates that observed behavioral disruptions map onto known necessity or sufficiency relations established via perturbation methods, which reinforces the integrity of the mechanistic chain. Where behavioral effects disappear under rigorous replication (ego depletion) or fluctuate with context and task parameters (implicit bias), low scores at C5 and C7 signal that the Stimulus/Behavior mapping lacks the counterfactual stability expected of mature constructs. It is precisely this target that Duncan's (2010) domain-general multiple-demand architecture illustrates: a control network specified well enough that perturbation predictions (C7) can be stated in advance and checked for coherence with an established network (C8).

Vertical Integration and Replication Reliability

Viewing the audit vertically, robust replication emerges when constraints accumulate across all four links: narrowly specified stimuli feed into biologically individuated mechanisms; these generate quantitatively modeled representations captured consistently across methods; and the resulting behaviors manifest predicted patterns resilient to contextual variation and perturbation tests. Fear conditioning/extinction exemplifies this integrated profile: conserved stimuli (CS-US pairings) produce circuit-level plasticity and inhibitory modulation, captured by convergent imaging, psychophysiological, and computational indices, culminating in behavioral acquisition/extinction curves that replicate across species and shift predictably under pharmacological or stimulation perturbations. By contrast, ego depletion lacks mechanistic individuation and predictive perturbation mapping, causing upstream indeterminacy that allows downstream behavioral failures to be rationalized via flexible moderator narratives; implicit bias occupies an intermediate position where diversification of measurement families and explicit perturbation hypotheses could strengthen vertical ties. The audit thus furnishes a diagnostic of why replication reliability might track mechanistic density, a relation that remains to be tested: insufficiencies in any segment loosen inferential coupling, expanding the space for post hoc accommodation and reducing the probability that preregistered, cross-context tests will converge.

Boundaries and productive pluralism

The ontological audit is proposed as a regulative ideal rather than a gate that excludes exploratory or qualitative research at inception. Early-stage constructs may originate in phenomenological observation, ethnographic description, or sociocultural analysis, provided investigators articulate a prospective pathway toward material anchoring, that is, toward naming the concrete organized system (at whatever scale, biological or social) of which the construct is a property, and the perturbation signature or cross-context invariance that would corroborate it (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Duncan, 2010). The pluralism the framework protects is, therefore, pluralism over the scale of concrete organization at which a construct anchors, over the cultural and contextual inputs it may incorporate, and over phase-graded evidential expectations: it is not a tolerance of constructs that remain abstract in principle.

First-person or cultural evidence can seed hypotheses about internal encoding states, about concrete social configurations that carry causal work themselves, or about the concrete cultural inputs that modulate a mechanism at another scale; these hypotheses are later adjudicated with the audit's material, evolutionary, and predictive criteria. What the framework resists is the indefinite circulation of metaphorical constructs once repeated attempts fail to elevate core benchmarks such as material anchoring (C1) or perturbationsensitive prediction (C7). The audit thus channels methodological diversity toward cumulative mechanistic density, while preserving room for interpretive richness at the discovery phase.

Conceptual and Methodological Challenges Reductionism and Loss of Higher-Level Explanatory Richness

A materialist framework that evaluates constructs based on neurobiological and evolutionary criteria may seem to undermine the independence of higher-level psychological phenomena, such as social norms, cultural cognition, and symbolic reasoning, by reducing them to lowerlevel neural descriptions. However, the Materialist Model of Psychological Science (MMPS) explicitly rejects such reductionism in favor of multi-level constraint. According to this perspective, higher-level regularities maintain their explanatory power when they are in a reciprocal relationship of constraint with lower-level mechanisms (Mitchell, 2009; Wimsatt, 2007). A substantial body of literature on explanatory pluralism demonstrates that levels of description are most scientifically productive when linked rather than insulated (Bechtel & Abrahamsen, 2005; Potochnik, 2017). Accordingly, the MMPS allows for abstraction, provided that it is mechanistically sound, that is, capable of generating predictions that can be tested against biological implementations. For instance, computational models of extinction learning can specify how altering contingency schedules should modulate ventromedial prefrontal cortex-amygdala dynamics. This furnishes bridge principles between levels (Gershman, 2017). This is formalized in the Integrative Compatibility Criterion (C8), which requires coherence with established biological evidence without insisting that every theoretical statement be translated into neurophysiology. Thus, C8 merely demands that these answers not contradict each other (Marr, 1982), while allowing for projection to lower levels when appropriate methods become available (Kaplan & Craver, 2011). In practice, then, a social-cognitive theory may remain primarily cultural or algorithmic so long as it avoids making claims that violate known neural constraints and articulates testable predictions that could be mapped downward.

At its core, the MMPS promotes constraint-based pluralism rather than eliminativism; here, however, "pluralism" designates pluralism over the scale of concrete organization at which a construct anchors (biological or social) and over the cultural and contextual inputs it may incorporate, rather than any tolerance of abstracta. Cognitive and cultural-level explanations persist as accounts of concrete organized systems, whether they anchor a construct directly or supply inputs that modulate a mechanism at another scale, and not as free-standing abstracta granted a compatibility pass. It is precisely this anchoring, together with evolutionary scaffolding, that prevents ad hoc rescue and secures accountability to empirical evidence (Anderson, 2014). In this reciprocal arrangement, cultural and computational insights guide investigation at other scales while, in turn, mechanistic findings refine higher-level theories.

Re-Introduction of Dualism via "Representations"

A common objection is that maintaining a "representation" box within the stimulus → mechanism → representation → behavior schematic reintroduces a quasi-mental layer and, consequently, a dualist ontology. In the MMPS, however, "representation" is used solely as a level-of-description label for physically instantiated, mechanistically individuated encoding patterns of neural or physiological activity that are defined by (a) the information they convey about the environment (Poldrack, 2010) and (b) their role in organized transformations (Kriegeskorte & Diedrichsen, 2019; Shea, 2018). Ontologically, nothing beyond the mechanism is added; the term earns its place by providing explanatory compression and coordinating convergent, multi-method evidence (criterion C2 ; Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002) while supporting measurement traceability (C6).

Anti-dualist safeguards are built directly into the audit. Material anchoring (C1; Kriegeskorte & Diedrichsen, 2019) prohibits "content" talk not tied to a candidate concrete substrate. Mechanistic specificity (C3; Piccinini & Craver, 2011) requires an explicit map of how a representational state is generated, transformed, and consumed, mirroring the "encodingrecoding-decoding" logic of contemporary mechanistic models. Prospective predictive performance (C7; de Haan & Karnath, 2018; Jannati, Oberman, Rotenberg, & Pascual-Leone, 2022) then demands that perturbing the physical substrate (e.g., via lesion, stimulation, or pharmacology) modulate downstream behavior as predicted by the representational account.

When these conditions are not met, the purported "representation" is considered unsupported.

Within the MMPS, representations do exist only as causally testable physical states.

Representations do not constitute an additional ontological layer, but instead function as a pragmatic vocabulary for describing organized information flow in brains. This is fully compatible with mechanistic and evolutionary realism (Anderson, 2014; Clark, 2023).

Charge of Over-Stringency may stifle innovation

These eight audit criteria, especially the requirement for perturbation-sensitive falsifiability, may be applied too early in a construct's life cycle. This could penalize exploratory theorizing, slow innovation, and privilege well-established domains (e.g., fear learning) over emerging areas. This is because the apparatus and tools necessary for biological or neurological investigations are not widespread, nor is the expertise to use them. (Scheel, Tiokhin, Isager, & Lakens, 2021). For this reason, the audit is phase-sensitive. During the discovery stage, provisional scores of 0-1 on most criteria are acceptable if investigators articulate a trajectory toward mechanistic specification (e.g., identifying candidate circuits or preregistering a future perturbation study). Constructs showing partial but incomplete support are routed to the Improvable category rather than being discarded so that resources can be allocated toward diversifying measurement families or designing causal tests (Nosek, Spies, & Motyl, 2012). This preserves exploratory flexibility while installing checkpoints that prevent the indefinite proliferation of loosely constrained tasks. In contrast, other programs that proceed without any ontological barriers have repeatedly invested substantial resources in effects that later failed boundary-condition or prospective prediction tests, as demonstrated by large-scale replication efforts in social cognition and self-control (Hagger et al., 2016; Open Science Collaboration, 2015).

Cultural and Social Phenomena: Risk of Biological Reduction

Many psychological constructs are shaped by culture: language-mediated norms, institutional roles, and other socially transmitted practices. One may be tempted to conclude that the audit must relax its material-anchoring demand for such constructs. It need not, since that demand was never one of biological reduction. Under the strict material-anchoring clause, a social factor may anchor a construct in its own right: what the clause requires is that it be a concrete, specifiable configuration, that is, a specifiable set of persons in physical interaction with a measurable distribution of behavior, whose composition, structure and mechanism can be stated, rather than a reified abstractum credited with causal power over and above the persons who instantiate it (step 2 of the clause). Such a configuration may either carry the causal work itself, in which case it is the anchor, or modulate a mechanism specified at another scale, in which case it is a concrete input; the clause is indifferent between these two roles and discriminates only against the abstractum. The Evolutionary Plausibility criterion (C4) supplies lineage, an account of the evolved enabling capacities (e.g., symbolic manipulation and coalition tracking) on which the culturally emergent phenomenon builds (Boyd & Richerson, 2005), but a C4 lineage does not by itself discharge the concrete-system anchoring required at C1. Operationally, a socio-cognitive construct satisfies the audit when what it invokes is cashed as a concrete configuration, either one that carries the causal work itself, or one whose effect on a mechanism at another scale is specified. Tightness-looseness theory (Gelfand et al., 2011), for instance, can be read as naming a measured distribution of norm-enforcement and sanctioning behaviours across a specifiable population, that is, a concrete, measurable input whose effect on a neural mechanism can be specified: cross-cultural EEG recordings show that the neural signature of norm-violation detection differs between Chinese and American participants (Mu, Kitayama, Han, & Gelfand, 2015). The configuration passes step 1 (it dies if the persons and their enforcement interactions are removed) and step 2 (its causal work is carried by the concrete distribution of behaviours, not by 'the norm' as a free-standing prescription). Under the concrete-system criterion, the measured enforcement distribution is itself an admissible anchor, and the neural finding is then a multi-level constraint on it (C8) rather than the anchor of last resort, though a construct may of course anchor at the neural scale instead, and this one can be read either way. Similarly, extinction learning depends on a concrete, measured protocol parameter that laboratories set by convention rather than by theory: when unconditioned-stimulus intensity is manipulated between groups, a highintensity US yields greater resistance to extinction and stronger 24-hour fear recovery than a low-intensity US (Ouyang & Dunsmoor, 2024). Here, a concrete, measured parameter of laboratory practice (the shock-intensity setting) maps onto a mechanistic parameter (reinforcer strength) that modulates amygdala-vmPFC plasticity; in this instance the anchor is biological, and the concrete laboratory practice is the input. In both cases, altering a specified concrete configuration yields a predictable shift in a mechanism, while C4 separately asks what evolved capacities the practice builds on.

Treating a cultural factor as a concrete input to a biological mechanism amounts neither to biological eliminativism nor to social constructivism. It is not eliminativism: culture-level descriptions retain their explanatory usefulness as specifications of which concrete inputs shift which mechanisms, and in which direction. Nor is it constructivism: no social substance is reified here, for the causal work is done by concrete configurations of persons and by the biological mechanisms they modulate, never by 'culture' as an abstract cause acting over-andabove them. What the strict clause forbids is precisely cultural reification, that is, treating 'culture', 'values', or 'identity narratives' as free-standing causes. When researchers can specify the concrete configuration and show how manipulating it (e.g., prestige cues or language formalities) alters encoding states or behavioral outputs, references to 'culture' cease functioning as unfalsifiable buffers and become testable hypotheses (Chiao & Immordino-Yang, 2013). Culturally informed constructs thereby retain their explanatory richness while remaining accountable, as every other construct must be, to the material anchoring the strict clause demands.

Apparent Double Standard: Successful Mechanistic Domains vs. Fragile Social-Cognitive Effects

Selecting fear conditioning/extinction and frontoparietal control as "mature" case studies may seem to bias the evaluation in favor of subfields that have benefited from decades of animal model infrastructure and well-established perturbation toolkits. This makes domains such as complex social interaction or moral cognition appear comparatively deficient. However, the purpose of the audit is diagnostic, not endorsing. It identifies areas requiring infrastructural investment, such as standardized multi-method protocols, explicit mechanistic hypotheses, and perturbation leverage points, to bring them up to the same evidential standard (Chambers, 2019). Mechanistic exemplars (e.g., fear circuitry and multiple-demand/frontoparietal control) demonstrate attainability; they show that cumulative constraint and predictive traction are feasible endpoints, not accidents of history (Milad & Quirk, 2012; Duncan, 2010).

The criteria are content-agnostic. Social-cognitive constructs can earn material anchoring (C1) at the network level. For example, the causal involvement of the right temporoparietal junction (rTPJ) in belief attribution has been demonstrated using disruptive transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) (Young, Camprodon, Hauser, Pascual-Leone, & Saxe, 2010). The TPJ's role in theory of mind has also been repeatedly localized and functionally characterized (Saxe & Kanwisher, 2003). Contextual invariance (C5) can be achieved through coordinated, cross-cultural, preregistered replications of core paradigms. This approach is already being employed by distributed consortia, such as the Psychological Science Accelerator and the Many Labs initiatives (Klein et al., 2018; Moshontz et al., 2018). This framework offers a roadmap, not an endorsement, of a single research tradition. The audit tool aims to foster progress toward mechanistic density, perturbation-sensitive prediction, and cross-context generality.

Perturbation Emphasis: Practical and Ethical Constraints

Perturbation-based tests, such as lesions, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), or pharmacological challenges, can be impractical or unethical in many human domains, including developmental science, clinical psychiatry, and high-level social reasoning. To avoid disadvantaging these fields, the mechanistic falsifiability of the MMPS is defined as requiring counterfactual leverage rather than necessarily invasive intervention. This leverage can be obtained through natural experiments (e.g., developmental trajectories, quasiexperimental designs, and Mendelian randomization using genetic polymorphisms), computational "lesioning" of validated models, ecological manipulations of inputs and contexts, and noninvasive neuromodulation (e.g., TMS/tDCS), when feasible (Casey, Tottenham, Liston, & Durston, 2005; Davey Smith & Hemani, 2014; Jannati, Oberman, Rotenberg, & Pascual-Leone, 2022; Pearl, 2009; Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002). The audit permits "virtual perturbations" when direct manipulation is unethical, provided that the predictions are a priori, discriminative, and model-linked (e.g., a preregistered forecast that a developmental or genetic "instrument" will shift a specific latent parameter and downstream behavior in a specified direction). In such cases, ethical criteria are more stringent: when direct intervention is constrained, stronger demands are placed on multi-method convergence (C2), predictive modeling (C7), and transparent counterfactual reasoning to attain comparable mechanistic density (Boes et al., 2015; de Haan & Karnath, 2018; Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven, & Mellor, 2018). This preserves fairness across subfields while maintaining the core falsifiability standard: constructs must make risky, testable predictions about how targeted changes to the system (real or virtual) would alter observable outcomes.

Risk of Metric Gaming and Formalism Without Substance

Formal checklists and numerical thresholds can incentivize procedural compliance without promoting substantive epistemic progress. For instance, a single fMRI experiment may be conducted to "check the box" for Material Anchoring (C1), or a minimally preregistered, weakly specified forecast may be employed to satisfy Prospective Predictive Performance (C7). Meta-scientific critiques demonstrate that laboratories occasionally preregister hypotheses while leaving essential analytical decisions unresolved or adding neuroimaging results that lack theoretical integration (Nosek, Spies, & Motyl, 2012; Scheel, Tiokhin, Isager, & Lakens, 2021). Earlier waves of statistical reform revealed similar issues with mandatory power analyses or checklist reporting when incentive structures remained unchanged (Ioannidis, 2019).

The audit is explicitly designed to increase the difficulty of merely checking boxes by making its eight criteria interdependent. Elevating one dimension in isolation, for example adding a localization result to satisfy C1, carries little weight if the construct still lacks mechanistic specificity (C3) or prospectively falsifiable perturbation predictions (C7). Improving C1 requires articulating a C3 mechanism and generating new C7 predictions, creating a structural incentive for mechanistic densification. This cross-criterion logic reveals internal inconsistencies; for example, a localization claim unsupported by a causal pathway or forward prediction yields a mixed evidential profile that invites critical scrutiny rather than automatic acceptance (Craver, 2007). Furthermore, transparency and external review increase the cost of performative compliance.

Public archiving of the evidence for each criterion, combined with independent panel review, mirrors initiatives such as Registered Reports and emerging Quality of Evidence frameworks, which have been shown to curb superficial adherence (Chambers & Tzavella, 2022).

Philosophical Skepticism: Are Mechanisms Always the Right Explanatory Currency?

Some argue that complex psychological phenomena are better understood through dynamical systems, ecological coupling, or network-level emergent properties that resist decomposition into the traditional "mechanism + representation" model (Chemero, 2009; Kelso, 1995; Sporns, 2010). The MMPS is format-neutral regarding mechanistic descriptions. A mechanism may be a coupled dynamical system, a control-theoretic feedback loop, or an emergent network motif, provided that it is materially instantiated, yields discriminative and perturbation-sensitive predictions, and can be empirically constrained by multi-method evidence (Bechtel & Abrahamsen, 2005; Kaplan & Craver, 2011). According to this perspective, emergentist or dynamical models pass the test when they explain how changes in parameters (e.g., connectivity weights, gains, or resource availability) affect internal encoding states and observable behavior in ways that can be tested in the future (Bassett & Sporns, 2017; Friston, 2010; Wilson & Collins, 2019). The framework excludes explanatory opacity, which is invoking emergence without articulating transformation pathways or delivering predictive leverage. Thus, the MMPS extends, rather than excludes, sophisticated dynamical/ecological accounts so long as they meet the same standards of material anchoring, mechanistic specificity, multi-method convergence, and prospective prediction as more classical mechanistic proposals.

Methodological and Institutional Implications

The MMPS reframes methodology as a program of mechanistically constrained tests rather than a series of single-paradigm demonstrations with substantial p-values. Preregistration is thus reframed as a commitment device in which researchers declare ex ante which internal transformations are expected to link a controlled stimulus to changes in encoding states and behavior and which audit criteria each hypothesis is meant to advance (e.g., C3 mechanistic specificity, C7 prospective prediction; Open Science Collaboration, 2015; Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). Registered Reports operationalize this logic by requiring authors to map each hypothesis to a target mechanism, a perturbation or boundary-condition test, and an a priori success or failure criterion. This ensures that null findings on core criteria, especially C7, are published and counted (Chambers & Tzavella, 2022; Hagger et al., 2016). Meanwhile, computational modeling serves as a primary bridge from C3 to C7. Executable models instantiate proposed transformations, generate latent variables that can be identified in neural or physiological data, and produce quantitative perturbation forecasts. Once preregistered, these forecasts provide rigorous opportunities for falsification (Craver, 2007; Palminteri, Wyart, & Koechlin, 2017; Wilson & Collins, 2019).

Since many replication failures are contextual, large, distributed collaborations should transition from issuing simple "did it replicate?" verdicts to performing invariance profiling. This involves systematically crossing laboratories, cultures, and procedural variants to determine whether divergences indicate construct fragility (downgrading C5) or parameterizable boundary conditions that refine the mechanism (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Klein et al., 2018; Moshontz et al., 2018). This requires open data, code, and materials so that auditors can trace how raw signals become construct scores and perform multiverse and specification-curve analyses to reveal hidden analytic degrees of freedom (C6). High volatility across specifications indicates weak traceability and should prompt remediation before allocating more resources (Carter, Kofler, Forster, & McCullough, 2015; Flake & Fried, 2020). When direct perturbation is feasible, lesion-behavior mapping, noninvasive stimulation, pharmacological challenges, and natural experiments provide the counterfactual leverage that the falsifiability axiom requires (de Haan & Karnath, 2018; Jannati, Oberman, Rotenberg, & Pascual-Leone, 2022). When direct perturbation is not feasible, "virtual perturbations" (e.g., model lesioning, Mendelian randomization, and developmental instruments) can satisfy C7, provided that the predictions are preregistered, discriminative, and mechanistically interpretable.

Further narrowing the hypothesis space and raising prior plausibility, embedding constructs evolutionarily and comparatively (C4) reveals that phylogenetically conserved circuits (e.g., in fear extinction) and cross-species homologies function as constraint multipliers rather than optional embellishments (Tinbergen, 1963; Milad & Quirk, 2012; MacLean et al., 2014). To enforce these standards, independent ontological audit panels convened by journals, societies, or consortia can issue public scorecards and register them in an open construct registry.

Publishing claims that exceed a construct's audited phase would require explicit justification, reducing the incentive to overgeneralize tenuous effects (Fanelli, Costas, & Larivière, 2015; Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven, & Mellor, 2018). Training should mirror this architecture.

Graduate curricula organized around the four axioms (physical realization, multi-level constraint, evolutionary plausibility, and mechanistic falsifiability) would instill audit logic early on and prevent the proliferation of poorly constrained constructs downstream. Finally, adaptive governance, including annual meta-audits of the registry, dashboards that track criterion migration, and targeted infrastructure investments when fields chronically stall on C7, for example keeps the ecosystem visible, accountable, and improvable (Open Science Collaboration, 2015).

A complementary line of work addresses this same structural problem from the side of infrastructure rather than content. Recent proposals for FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) theory argue that psychological theories are typically articulated in prose, with limited formal specification of scope, dependencies, and revision rules; consequently, mixed findings accumulate without any framework for deciding when revision, qualification, or reconstruction is warranted (Heo & Jeon, 2026; van Lissa et al., 2026). The remedy proposed there is to treat a theory not as a static textual claim, but as a versioned digital object that can be explicitly specified, archived, and iteratively refined, with revisions and boundary specifications documented and traceable across versions rather than made implicitly in response to isolated findings. The MMPS and the FAIR programme are best understood as addressing orthogonal halves of the same problem, and they compose naturally.

FAIR theory supplies the infrastructural question, that is, how a theory's commitments are represented, versioned, and linked to evidence, but it is deliberately agnostic about content: it does not adjudicate whether a given construct has earned its explanatory place. The ontological audit supplies precisely that missing evaluative content, namely substantive criteria (material anchoring, mechanistic specificity, perturbation-sensitive prediction) that determine whether a construct is credible in the first place. Conversely, the audit has a representational gap that FAIR fills: criterion scores, their evidential justifications, and the migrations of a construct between Accept, Improvable, and Suspend are precisely the kind of This is, indeed, the same discipline that the audit's phase-based governance imposes through its remediation and sunset rules.

Conclusion

The MMPS makes a simple bet: that the constructs surviving mechanistic scrutiny will also be the ones that replicate. The three worked examples presented here (ego depletion, implicit bias, fear conditioning) illustrate how the audit discriminates among them; however, since they were used in developing the criteria, they cannot themselves confirm the bet. Whether it holds is an empirical question, one that must be tested out of sample and blind, in a separate preregistered study.

It offers a constraint-based pluralism, materialist as to the type of entity that may carry causal work (a concrete organized system, never a reified abstractum), yet pluralist as to the scale at which a construct anchors and as to the cultural and contextual inputs it may incorporate.

Computational, cultural, and social-cognitive explanations are valid provided the construct meets three conditions. First, it anchors in a concrete organized system at some scale (molecular, neural, physiological, organism-in-environment, or a concrete social configuration). Second, it admits any cultural or social factor only as a concrete configuration that survives the two-step test, that is, one whose causal work is carried by the specifiable set of persons in interaction rather than by an abstractum standing over and above them, whether that configuration serves as the construct's anchor or as an input modulating a mechanism at another scale. Third, it yields perturbation-sensitive predictions. Coherence with established evidence at adjacent scales, along with downward projection when methods permit, is retained as an integrative demand (C8). Following this same logic, our treatment of representation holds that representations are retained strictly as causally testable, physically instantiated encoding states, and not as a quasi-mental layer. It is precisely by compressing explanations across methods, by supporting traceable measurement, and by surviving perturbation-sensitive tests, that representations earn their keep.

Early-stage constructs can (and should) receive provisional scores as long as they demonstrate a trajectory toward mechanistic density. Registered reports, preregistration, computational modeling, multi-lab projects, open data and code pipelines, and perturbation toolkits (real or ethically acceptable "virtual" perturbations) are coordinated instruments that can be used to upgrade specific criteria (e.g., from C3 to C7). Independent audit panels and open construct registries provide governance and accountability, reducing rediscovery cycles, selective citation, and concept creep. Training reforms, with curricula organized around the four axioms and the audit, aim to inoculate the next generation of researchers against the failure modes that the framework targets: vague localization, single-method dependence, black-box functionalism, adaptive storytelling, context-limited fragility, indicator proliferation, retrospective rationalization, and ontological inflation. Meta-audits and dashboards reveal the health of the construct ecosystem and guide infrastructure investment toward chronic bottlenecks.

Two broad outcomes follow from adopting this framework. The first is epistemic consolidation: constructs migrate, sometimes slowly, sometimes abruptly, toward mechanistic maturity, accumulating predictive traction and reducing the interpretive slack that currently enables ad hoc rescue. The second future is epistemic triage. Some constructs will stall or fail not because they were unworthy of exploration but because the community possesses a principled way to decide when to revise, pause, or pivot. Both outcomes, consolidation and triage, are signs of a healthier science.

Psychology's recent credibility crisis revealed that transparency alone is insufficient. We must also recalibrate our ontological standards: what counts as a construct, how it earns its keep, and when it should be retired. While not claiming to be the last word, the MMPS and its Ontological Audit provide a workable, albeit temporary, template with explicit axioms, concrete criteria, phase-sensitive thresholds, and institutional mechanisms that reward theories for becoming denser, riskier, and more tightly integrated with the rest of the life sciences. If this framework is widely adopted and adaptively governed, I hope it can help transform the field's current pluralism, which is a patchwork of metaphors, into cumulative mechanistic science.

Glossary of Recurrent Failure Modes

How to use: When planning or reviewing, scan this list to identify potential failure modes and link each one to its relevant criterion. For instance, 'vague localisation' corresponds to C1/C3, 'indicator proliferation' to C6, and 'retrospective rationalisation' to C7. Add domain-specific variants during audits and cite the glossary in Registered Reports so that reviewers are aware of the risks that have been mitigated.

 Vague localization: attributing a construct to imprecise or overly broad neural real estate, blocking falsifiable tests.

 Single-method dependence: relying on one paradigm or assay, risking method-specific artifacts.

 Black-box functionalism: describing input to output regularities without internal causal steps.

 Adaptive storytelling: post hoc evolutionary narratives lacking comparative or phylogenetic support.

 Context-limited fragility: effects that vanish or invert across labs, populations, or task variants.

 Indicator proliferation: multiplying loosely related measures under one label, obscuring what is measured.

 Retrospective rationalization (HARKing): explaining unexpected results after the fact instead of revising claims.

 Ontological inflation: unchecked creation of new labels that duplicate/conflict with established mechanisms.

Material anchoring (C1) and mechanistic specificity (C3) principally inhabit the Mechanism tier, demanding explicit identification of component sets (circuits, pathways, computational operators) and their organized causal relations; evolutionary plausibility (C4) spans Mechanism and Representation by requiring phylogenetically or adaptively interpretable design constraints that narrow admissible mechanistic configurations; multi-method convergence (C2) and measurement traceability (C6) bridge Representation to Behavior, ensuring that multiple independent assay families converge on the same intermediate state taxonomy and that operational indicators map transparently onto those states; contextual invariance (C5) and prospective predictive performance (C7) stress the reliability of the Stimulus → Behavior mapping under prespecified boundary manipulations and forward-looking perturbations; integrative compatibility (C8) enforces vertical coherence across the entire chain, disallowing mechanistic proposals that contradict established neural network or perturbation evidence.
