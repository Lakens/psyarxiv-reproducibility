To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 2 imprecise p values out of 127 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 39 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Cooperation depends not only on how much people trust and reciprocate, but on how it is maintained and repaired after rupture. We analysed a 10-round Trust Game in participants with borderline personality disorder (BPD), an affective-symptom sample (AD), and community controls (N = 1,300 participants contributing 1,310 games; BPD n = 468, AD n = 556, controls n = 286 games), using mixed-effects and hidden Markov models. Average behaviour was broadly similar across groups, although AD trustees returned more than controls. As investors, BPD participants adjusted less to feedback than controls. Among trustees, AD differed most clearly from BPD: AD trustees followed cooperative response patterns more often, resumed cooperation more readily after exploitative returns, and had higher model-implied long-run cooperation at low and moderate partner investments. The occupancy and repair differences persisted when hidden states were estimated without diagnostic information, but not when BPD was restricted to interview-confirmed cases. AD-control differences in cooperative patterns did not persist among participants tested online. BPD-control differences in maintaining and restoring cooperation were smaller and depended on investment and hidden-state specification. Clinical differences were more evident in interaction dynamics than in average behaviour.

Introduction

Interpersonal dysfunction is a hallmark of many psychiatric conditions, yet characterising the dynamics through which cooperation unfolds and breaks down in these disorders remains methodologically challenging (Kupferberg et al., 2016; Skodol et al., 2002). Cooperation is not a unitary decision but a dynamic interpersonal process in which trust is extended, reciprocated, sometimes violated, and at times repaired (Fehr & Fischbacher, 2003; King-Casas et al., 2008). These dynamics plausibly depend, in part, on mentalizing, broadly understood as interpreting a partner's behaviour in terms of underlying intentions and using this information to guide one's own responses (Fonagy et al., 2002; Frith & Frith, 2012). Disruptions in mentalizing have been proposed as one pathway through which social learning and relationships deteriorate, particularly in borderline pathology (Fonagy & Luyten, 2009). Identifying where and how cooperation fails in clinical populations therefore requires methods capable of capturing these dynamics rather than merely their endpoints.

The multi-round Trust Game (MRT) offers such a framework (Berg et al., 1995; King-Casas et al., 2005). In each round, an investor decides how much of an endowment to send to a trustee; the transferred amount is multiplied, and the trustee then decides how much to return. Investor transfers index interpersonal trust, whereas trustee returns index reciprocity (King-Casas et al., 2005). The repeated structure allows cooperation to emerge through generous exchanges, to be disrupted by defections, and potentially to be repaired through strategic signals such as coaxing, that is, fair returns following low investment in order to encourage the partner back into cooperation (King-Casas et al., 2008). The MRT therefore captures not only how much individuals cooperate, but also how cooperation evolves over the course of an interaction (King-Casas et al., 2005).

However, Trust Game studies that reduce behaviour to average investment or average return across rounds collapse these dynamics into a single summary index, and clinical studies have often relied on small samples (n < 50). Such approaches obscure the processes of greatest theoretical and clinical interest. Two individuals may show identical mean reciprocity while relying on markedly different strategies: one may sustain stable cooperation throughout, whereas another may alternate between generous and exploitative responses. If clinical groups differ not in how much they cooperate, but in how they cooperate -whether they maintain cooperation, how they respond to rupture, and whether they repair it -then aggregate averages are unlikely to detect these distinctions. Computational psychiatry seeks to move beyond such summary measures by fitting generative models that formalise the latent processes shaping observable behaviour (Huys et al., 2016; Montague et al., 2012).

Hidden Markov models (HMMs) provide a natural framework for this aim (Visser & Speekenbrink, 2022). HMMs represent behaviour as transitions between latent states whose number and characteristics are inferred from the data, with transition probabilities that may depend on task variables such as partner investment. In the MRT, the recovered states can be interpreted post hoc, for example, as exploitative, cautious, or cooperative modes of responding, on the basis of their emission distributions (Guennouni, Dupret, et al., 2026). Applied to cooperation data, the transition structure of an HMM can be interpreted in terms of clinically meaningful processes: maintenance, defined as the tendency to remain in cooperative states once entered, and repair, defined as the tendency to re-enter cooperation following a lapse. These capture distinct aspects of cooperation dynamics that are also psychologically distinguishable. Maintenance depends on the capacity to sustain a collaborative stance toward the partner in the face of weakly negative or ambiguous cues; repair depends on the capacity to recover that stance after a breakdown, treating a signal of renewed cooperative intent as genuine rather than as manipulation or irrelevance (Bateman & Fonagy, 2004). Because these capacities are likely to draw on different psychological resources, they may be differentially disrupted across clinical groups, making the distinction between maintenance and repair especially important for empirical analysis.

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is characterised by unstable relationships, affective lability, and rapid oscillation between idealisation and devaluation of others (Gunderson, 2011; Skodol et al., 2002; Zanarini et al., 2007). Several features of BPD may plausibly shape the dynamics of cooperation, and in a mentalizing and epistemic trust framework these features are deeply interconnected rather than independent. Mentalizing difficulties, central to developmental accounts of BPD (Bateman & Fonagy, 2004; Fonagy et al., 2002; Fonagy & Luyten, 2009), may impair flexible interpretation of a partner's intentions, particularly when signals are ambiguous. Under conditions of epistemic hypervigilance (Fonagy et al., 2015 (Fonagy et al., , 2017;; Nolte et al., 2023), ambiguous cues such as low investment may be read not merely as low generosity but as socially meaningful signs of misattunement, disinterest, or impending abandonment. On this account, the core difficulty is not simply excessive affective reactivity, though emotion dysregulation (Linehan, 1993) may accelerate the process, but the collapse of a flexible interpretive stance toward the partner -a state in which the other person becomes harder to represent as having a mind that is both separate and benignly intelligible. Heightened rejection sensitivity (Gunderson, 2011; Staebler et al., 2011) may compound this by amplifying negative readings of ambiguous signals and precipitating transitions out of cooperative states. Consistent with this account, Trust Game studies have reported reduced trust and reciprocity in BPD (King-Casas et al., 2008; Unoka et al., 2009). King-Casas et al. (2008) found that individuals with BPD could initiate cooperation but struggled to sustain it, showing reduced coaxing behaviour. Whether this difficulty reflects a failure to signal repair after rupture, as the coaxing account implies, or a more specific fragility in sustaining cooperation when partner signals are weak or ambiguous, remains an open question. In HMM terms, the former would predict impaired re-entry into cooperative states, whereas the latter would predict impaired retention within them.

Affective disorders (AD), used here as a shorthand label for the treatment-seeking subgroup with depressive and/or anxiety presentations in this study, present a more heterogeneous picture. Depression is associated with low mood, anhedonia, and impaired social functioning (Gotlib & Joormann, 2010; Kupferberg et al., 2016), yet recent Trust Game evidence suggests intact or even heightened cooperative behaviour in people with remitted depression (Ong et al., 2017), possibly reflecting interpersonal over-accommodation -a tendency to preserve the interaction by absorbing the burden of repair, consistent with guilt, submissiveness, and conflict avoidance (O'Connor et al., 2002) -rather than finely calibrated responsiveness to partner signals. By contrast, social anxiety has been linked to reduced reciprocity, potentially reflecting interpersonal inhibition and difficulty responding to prosocial overtures (Anderl et al., 2018). These opposing tendencies raise the possibility that average reciprocity in a heterogeneous AD sample may conceal divergent underlying dynamics. A process-level analysis capable of distinguishing how cooperation is maintained and repaired is therefore needed to determine which aspects of cooperation are affected.

Guided by this framework, we analysed a large sample of community controls (CC), patients with BPD, and the affective-symptom subgroup (AD) who completed a 10-round MRT in either the investor or trustee role (1,300 participants; 898 trustee and 412 investor games). We combined trial-level mixed-effects models, which test how trust and reciprocity respond to partner behaviour from round to round, with HMMs, which characterise how cooperation is maintained and repaired through transitions between latent states. By examining both roles, we also asked whether clinical groups show role-dependent patterns in social exchange tasks (Nolte et al., n.d.). Because the investor and trustee roles were completed by different participants, however, these comparisons are limited to the group level and do not permit within-person cross-role inference.

We hypothesised that BPD would show reduced reciprocity together with a distinct dynamic signature of fragile cooperation, including lower retention in cooperative states and weaker cooperative repair following rupture, consistent with the fragile cooperation pattern reported by King-Casas et al. (2008). For AD, prior findings suggest competing possibilities: remitted depression has been associated with increased cooperation (Ong et al., 2017), whereas anxiety has been linked to reduced reciprocity (Anderl et al., 2018). We therefore adopted an exploratory approach to determine which pattern predominated in this sample. By contrasting maintenance and repair profiles across diagnostic groups, we aimed to identify distinct reciprocity phenotypes that may help refine mechanistic accounts of interpersonal dysfunction and inform more targeted clinical intervention.

MRT Paradigm

Participants completed a 10-round MRT (Berg et al., 1995; King-Casas et al., 2005). In each round, investors received 20 tokens and decided how many to send to a trustee. The transferred amount was tripled, after which the trustee decided how much to return. Participants remained in the same role throughout the task.

Participants were told that they were paired with another player. In fact, the partner was a computerised agent implementing a nearest-neighbour response algorithm. On each trial, the agent constructed a state representation from the current investment and interaction history, including the previous investment and previous return, searched a library of prior human responses for the closest matching state, and replayed the corresponding response. The agent could produce locally exploitative or generous responses from the library, but did not learn participant-specific policies across rounds. This version of the MRT therefore assessed responses to controlled social signals under presumed human agency, rather than genuinely reciprocal interpersonal dynamics in which both partners adapt to each other.

Behavioural feature definitions

In addition to mean investment and return levels, we defined trial-level behavioural events to capture dynamic reciprocity patterns. For descriptive summaries, these events were aggregated to participant-level rates; in the mixed-effects analyses, they entered as binary outcomes. A fair return equalises investor and trustee payoffs and was defined as fair return = 2 × investment -10 when investment ≥ 5 tokens, and 0 otherwise. For example, if investment = 10 tokens, the trustee receives 30; a fair return of 10 tokens would leave both parties with 20 tokens each. For investments < 5 tokens, equal payoffs are mathematically impossible under the tripling rule. In these low-investment cases, we therefore defined the fair-return floor as 0, meaning that any positive return was classified as "better than fair". Full feature formulae and coding details are provided in Supplement S1.1.

For investors, we examined two forms of adjustment to trustee behaviour. Responsiveness was defined as the tendency to increase investment following better-than-fair returns, indexing positive reciprocity. Retaliation was defined as the tendency to decrease investment following worse-than-fair returns, indexing negative reciprocity.

For trustees, we examined three forms of response to investor stakes. Coaxing was defined as returning at least the invested amount after low but non-zero investments (0 < investment ≤ 5 tokens). Encouragement was coded when the investor sent fewer tokens than in the preceding round but the trustee returned more tokens than in that round-for example, when investment fell from 10 to 5 tokens while the return rose from 8 to 9 tokens. Cooperation pick-up was coded when both the investment and the number of tokens returned increased from the preceding round-for example, from 5 tokens invested and 4 returned to 10 invested and 6 returned.

Statistical analyses

Linear mixed-effects models

We used linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) to analyse trial-level behaviour while accounting for withinparticipant dependence across the 10 rounds (Matuschek et al., 2017). Models were estimated in R (R Core Team, 2025) using the lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) packages. Unless otherwise stated, p-values were based on Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; spot checks using Kenward-Roger corrections yielded the same inferential pattern. We report fixed-effect estimates, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and tests of marginal means. Our primary diagnostic questions concerned whether each clinical group differed from CC. We therefore estimated two focal treatment-versus-control contrasts, BPD vs CC and AD vs CC, using the emmeans package (Lenth, 2025). For transparency, the corresponding estimates are reported in the main text, but inferential claims are based on Holm-adjusted p-values within each model family.

We report unstandardised coefficients with 95% CIs, which are directly interpretable in the original metric, tokens for investment and logit units for the proportion of the tripled amount returned, supplemented where informative by simple-slope comparisons and percentage-change metrics. We do not add a single standardised measure of explained variance because its interpretation depends on the partitioning of withinand between-participant variation (Supplement S1.2).

Investor investment model

We compared four investor models: (a) diagnosis only, (b) diagnosis plus round and lagged net return main effects, (c) diagnosis-by-round and diagnosis-by-lagged-return interactions, and (d) all two-way interactions plus the three-way interaction. The best-fitting model was selected using sequential likelihood-ratio tests. All models included participant-specific intercepts; models (b)-(d) also included slopes for lagged net return.

Trustee return model

We compared four nested trustee models: (a) group only, (b) group plus round and investment main effects, (c) all two-way interactions, and (d) the three-way interaction. The best-fitting model was selected using sequential likelihood-ratio tests across the nested hierarchy. We initially attempted random-slope specifications for investment, but the two-way and three-way interaction models failed to converge under that random-effects structure. Model-fitting details are provided in Supplement S1.2. To preserve valid likelihood-ratio comparisons on a common estimable random-effects structure, all trustee models in the hierarchy were therefore fitted with random intercepts only. Random-intercept-only models may underestimate standard errors for investment-related fixed effects, and the trustee LMM results should be interpreted with this caveat in mind.

Trial-level behavioural event models

To model the trial-level behavioural events without discarding contextual information, we fitted hierarchical logistic generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs) to each binary outcome: investor responsiveness, investor retaliation, trustee coaxing, trustee encouragement, and trustee cooperation pick-up. This approach accommodates varying denominators and allows partial pooling across sparse diagnostic groups.

For investor responsiveness and retaliation, the binary outcomes defined above were modelled as a function of the previous round's fairness gap, z-scored, prior investment, z-scored, round, modelled with a natural spline (2 df), and diagnosis.

For trustee coaxing, encouragement, and cooperation pick-up, the binary outcomes defined above were modelled as a function of current and prior investments, both z-scored, the previous round's fairness gap, z-scored, round, modelled with a natural spline (2 df), and diagnostic group.

For diagnosis effects in the GLMMs, we used the same two focal treatment-versus-control contrasts against CC estimated with emmeans. Estimates are reported in the main text, but inferential claims are based on Holm-adjusted p-values within model family. When random-slope specifications were singular or produced convergence warnings in sparse behavioural subsets, the models were simplified to random intercepts only, and the retained random-effects structure is reported.

Hidden Markov models

Because reciprocity unfolds over time and may reflect unobserved shifts in strategy, we used HMMs to identify latent states directly from behavioural sequences rather than imposing fixed categories. HMMs represent observed behaviour as emissions from a sequence of discrete latent states linked by probabilistic transitions, allowing both the number and the characteristics of these states to be estimated from the data. Models were fitted in R using the depmixS4 package (Visser & Speekenbrink, 2010), and interpretive labels were assigned only after estimation on the basis of the recovered emission profiles. Prior simulation work in an independent repeated Trust Game dataset demonstrated recovery of the ordered state policies and state-occupancy quantities used to interpret this model class (Guennouni, Dupret, et al., 2026).

Model specification and fitting

We fitted separate HMM families for investors and trustees, considering models with 2 to 7 latent states in each role. Trustee models included CC, BPD, and AD participants. Because the AD investor cell was small (n = 16), investor HMMs were fitted to CC and BPD sequences only. Within each role, models were estimated on pooled participant sequences. Trustee returns were modelled within their feasible range conditional on current investment, and investor emissions were bounded to the observed 0 to 20 token investment scale.

Transitions depended on partner investment on the following round for trustees and prior-round net return for investors. Initial state probabilities depended on first-round investment for trustees and were constant across investor sequences. At each state count, we fitted one model in which transitions depended on the role-specific trial variable and another that also included diagnostic group and its interaction with that variable. Each candidate was fitted from multiple starting values, retaining the converged solution with the highest likelihood. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) selected the number of states within the models that included diagnostic group. At the selected state count, we used a likelihood-ratio test to assess whether the group terms improved model fit and retained them when they did (Giudici et al., 2000). Full fitting details are provided in Supplement S1.3-S1.4.

Estimating and analysing latent states

An HMM does not determine a trial's latent state with certainty. After fitting, the forward-backward algorithm used the complete 10-round sequence to estimate the probability that each trial belonged to each state (Visser & Speekenbrink, 2010). We retained these probabilities when estimating transitions and their uncertainty, allowing each pair of consecutive trials to contribute partly to several possible transitions.

For other analyses, we assigned each trial to the state with the highest probability, considering each trial separately. We refer to analyses based on this sequence as most-likely-state analyses. To summarise how confidently trials could be assigned, we report the mean probability of each trial's most likely state and the proportion of trials assigned with a probability of at least .80.

In both roles, the most-likely-state sequences were used to calculate each participant's occupancy of each state, defined as the proportion of trials assigned to that state. We compared these proportions across groups using one-way analyses of variance and applied Holm correction to each set of occupancy comparisons. The comparisons included in each correction are reported in the Supplement.

We then examined how participants moved between states from one round to the next. For investors, each state was represented by its mean investment. Net earnings were defined as the amount returned minus the amount invested. We tested whether net earnings predicted the investment level of the next state and whether this relationship differed between BPD and CC. The model also accounted for the current state. For trustees, separate models examined remaining cooperative, returning to cooperation from the exploitative or neutral state, and leaving cooperation for either state. We tested whether these outcomes varied with the partner's next investment and diagnosis.

Because the selected models allowed transitions to differ by diagnosis, states assigned under them partly reflect the group differences under test. We therefore also assigned states under the diagnosis-free model with the same number of states in each role and compared groups on these states using trial-level mixed-effects logistic regressions with a participant random intercept and testing setting as a covariate. For trustees, the outcomes were cooperative-state occupancy, repair after the exploitative state, and retention in cooperative states; partner investment on the next round was a covariate for the two transition outcomes. For investors, the outcome was occupancy of the highest-investment state. Differences in predicted probability refer to a participant with an average random intercept. Their 95% intervals were based on 2,000 participant resamples, refitted with a faster, less exact estimation setting (nAGQ = 0 in lme4), whereas point estimates and p-values came from the full fits; p-values were Holm-adjusted across the three trustee group comparisons. We repeated these comparisons with BPD restricted to participants meeting interview criteria, without controls scoring 3 or above on the SAPAS, with age and gender as covariates, and, for trustees, among participants tested online.

Transition dynamics and reciprocity metrics

State occupancy describes which strategies are expressed, but reciprocity also depends on how strategies change over time. The cooperation-focused transition analyses therefore centred on the trustee HMMs, in which states with higher return emissions can be interpreted as more cooperative and states with lower return emissions as more exploitative. After ordering trustee states by their emission means, we treated the fitted transition model as a covariate-dependent Markov chain and evaluated it at representative investment levels of 5, 10, and 15 tokens. In the selected five-state model, State 1 returned well below one third of the tripled investment and was classified as exploitative. State 2 was centred near one third, which returned approximately the investor's original stake, and was classified as neutral. States 3-5 returned more than one third and were classified as cooperative. These labels describe the economic consequences of the fitted return distributions. From these transition matrices, we derived four metrics: retention, the probability of remaining in cooperative states; repair, the probability of moving from exploitative State 1 into cooperative States 3-5; exploitative exit, the probability of leaving cooperative states for State 1; and model-implied long-run cooperation, the eventual probability of occupying cooperative states if the partner repeated the same investment level across rounds.

This final quantity was calculated from the invariant distribution of the corresponding transition matrix. Movement from neutral State 2 into cooperation and movement from cooperation into State 2 were examined separately.

Because all four metrics are derived from the same transition matrix, they describe related aspects of the same transition pattern and were interpreted together. When retention or exploitative exit combined several cooperative starting states, we averaged the state-specific transition probabilities according to how frequently those states occurred in the model's long-run distribution. Unlike the mixed-model analyses, which focused on two focal clinical-versus-control contrasts, the HMM transition analyses were used to characterise relative reciprocity phenotypes across all diagnostic groups. We therefore retained all pairwise diagnostic comparisons in the HMM summaries, including AD vs BPD, and applied Holm correction within each HMM comparison family. The comparisons included in each family are reported in Supplement S1.3.

Bootstrap uncertainty for reciprocity dynamics

We quantified uncertainty in the trustee transition measures using a transition-only bootstrap. We obtained 10,000 successfully fitted bootstrap samples for the five-state model. Point estimates were taken directly from the fitted HMM. CIs were calculated as percentile intervals (2.5th-97.5th percentiles), and hypothesis tests were based on two-sided bootstrap p-values with Holm correction within the comparison families described above.

The bootstrap varied the transition counts and transition parameters while keeping the five-state structure and its state-specific return distributions fixed. The resulting intervals and p-values therefore describe uncertainty in the transition dynamics of the selected five-state model. We repeated the analyses with neighbouring fourand six-state models, using 10,000 successful bootstrap samples per model. Further details are reported in Supplement S1.3.

The primary trustee hidden-state analysis included all 898 trustees. For model-implied long-run cooperation, sensitivity analyses included online trustees (n = 686) and the online participants with complete demographic data (n = 600), analysed before and after age and gender adjustment. Each analysis used 10,000 successful transition-bootstrap samples. An additional demographic-adjusted analysis across all testing settings is reported in the supplement.

The attenuated BPD adaptation slopes above (46% smaller adjustments) coexisted with no reliable group differences in tactical response probabilities. BPD investors adjusted by smaller magnitudes, although the analysis did not detect a group difference in whether participants changed direction after feedback.

Comparisons involving the AD investor subgroup (n = 16) should be interpreted cautiously, given the limited power associated with this cell despite partial pooling. Both spline terms for round were significant in the responsiveness model (p = .001 and p = .041). The fitted model predicted that the probability of increasing stakes following generous returns declined modestly across rounds. Investors therefore became slightly less responsive to positive signals over time, possibly reflecting adaptation or progressive strategy stabilisation.

In summary, BPD investors showed substantially attenuated adjustment of investment following partner returns, whereas tactical response probabilities did not differ reliably across diagnostic groups. That is, BPD investors appeared to detect partner feedback -responding directionally -but to integrate it less strongly into subsequent investment decisions.

Discussion

This study used computational modelling to decompose trust and reciprocity in a large community and clinical sample including participants with BPD and an affective-symptom AD subgroup. By combining trial-level mixed-effects models with HMMs, we identified a clear dissociation between average behaviour and the dynamics through which cooperation unfolds. The trial-level analysis identified higher trustee returns in AD than in controls at the sample's average round and investment. Further differences emerged in how participants responded to their partners: first in round-by-round adjustment to the partner, and then in the maintenance and repair of cooperation across latent states. The findings differ in robustness. BPD was characterised by attenuated feedback-dependent adaptation in the investor role, alongside more model-dependent evidence of altered cooperative maintenance and repair in the trustee role. Among trustees, the most robust distinction was between the AD sample and BPD: AD trustees occupied cooperative states more often, repaired cooperation more readily, and showed higher model-implied long-run cooperation at low and moderate partner investments. The occupancy difference persisted across alternative state counts and in administration-mode and demographic checks, and the repair difference persisted at most investment levels. Both differences also persisted when states were estimated without diagnostic information, although not in that analysis when BPD was restricted to participants meeting interview criteria. Differences between AD and controls in cooperative patterns were weaker and did not persist among participants tested online. The BPD-CC trustee differences in maintenance and repair depended on the number of states and on whether transitions were taken from the fitted model or from most-likely-state sequences. The elevated AD tactical behaviours, and their interpretation as over-accommodation, remain provisional.

Maintenance and repair may index psychologically distinct interpersonal capacities rather than merely statistical decompositions of reciprocity. Maintenance may involve sustaining a flexible representation of the partner's intentions under ambiguous cues; repair may involve revising it after rupture and recognising renewed cooperative intent as genuine rather than manipulation or irrelevance (Fonagy et al., 2017; Fonagy & Luyten, 2009). We interpret these patterns through epistemic trust theory (Fonagy et al., 2017; Lashani et al., 2026; Nolte et al., 2023), although the proposed mechanisms were not directly measured. Differential reinforcement sensitivity, loss aversion, and strategic sophistication could also explain these signatures. We develop the epistemic trust account because we believe it generates the most differentiated predictions: directly testable differences in mentalizing capacity, epistemic stance, and social learning.

In the investor role, BPD was associated with significantly attenuated adaptation to partner returns. We detected no reliable group differences in directional responses to feedback, but BPD investors made substantially smaller adjustments. The hidden-state analysis supported this pattern: greater net earnings shifted BPD investors less strongly towards higher-investment states. BPD investors also occupied the highest-investment state less often than CC, but this difference did not survive correction across states and did not persist in most sensitivity checks. One possible interpretation is that BPD investors place less weight on partner feedback when revising expectations of the other person (Fonagy et al., 2015 (Fonagy et al., , 2017)). This is also consistent with evidence that individuals with BPD maintain more rigid self-other distinctions during social learning (Barnby et al., 2025).

Among trustees, the clearest evidence concerned the AD sample. The strongest evidence was that AD trustees occupied cooperative states more often than BPD and showed greater cooperative repair across investment levels and greater model-implied long-run cooperation at low and moderate investments. AD also occupied cooperative states, including the highest-return state, more often than CC and showed higher model-implied long-run cooperation at moderate investment, but the AD-CC occupancy difference did not persist among participants tested online. Because almost all AD trustees were tested online, AD-CC differences cannot be fully separated from testing setting. This was mirrored in the tactical analyses: encouragement was elevated relative to CC in the primary model, but this effect was no longer significant after adjustment for administration mode or in the demographic complete-case sample and should therefore be regarded as provisional. The AD-CC long-run cooperation differences in the primary model did not survive correction in the online and demographic sensitivity analyses. AD trustees, relative to BPD, returned to cooperation more readily after exploitative responses and had higher model-implied long-run cooperation at low and moderate investments. One hypothesis generated by this pattern is that elevated cooperative repair may in some individuals reflect compensatory interpersonal accommodation rather than more accurately calibrated social learning. The hypothesis draws on meta-analytic evidence that depression is associated with greater cooperation in the trustee role of the Trust Game (Jin et al., 2025), on the association between depression and guilt and submissive interpersonal tendencies (O' Connor et al., 2002), and on the concept of epistemic credulity, an indiscriminate receptivity to social demands that manifests as compliance rather than calibrated responsiveness (Bröcker et al., 2026). Future studies combining the task with measures of guilt, submissiveness, and epistemic credulity could distinguish this account from genuinely enhanced cooperative responsiveness. The present data cannot adjudicate between these accounts, and the AD investor subgroup (n = 16) was too small to test whether similar patterns appeared in both roles. The AD findings should therefore be treated as hypothesis-generating, with the encouragement result in particular requiring replication with fuller control for administration mode and demographic confounds.

The BPD-CC trustee pattern was less stable across model specifications. In the primary analysis, the BPD-related phenotype was a context-dependent fragility of cooperation. Retention in cooperative states was lower in BPD than CC at the lowest investment in both analyses, and also at moderate investment in the most-likely-state analysis; the difference narrowed at higher investments. BPD trustees also showed context-dependent differences in returning to cooperation from the clearly exploitative state, and their model-implied long-run cooperation was lower than that of CC at the lowest investment. BPD trustees did not leave cooperation for the exploitative state more often than other groups; in the fitted transition model they did so less often at moderate and high investments. Together, this pattern is most consistent with difficulty both sustaining cooperation when partner signals are weak and restoring it after an exploitative response, rather than with a global reduction in cooperative motivation. Low investment may make a partner's intentions harder to infer and increase vulnerability to hostile or rejecting interpretations (Gunderson, 2011; Staebler et al., 2011). Under conditions of unstable mentalizing, low investment may be read not as low generosity but as a socially meaningful sign of misattunement or impending abandonment -precisely the kind of ambiguous condition that places the greatest demands on flexible, controlled mentalizing (Fonagy & Luyten, 2009). On this account, cooperative maintenance may be more fragile when the partner's intentions are ambiguous. Higher investment may stabilise the maintenance of cooperation by reducing this interpretive ambiguity. Emotion dysregulation (Linehan, 1993) may further accelerate transitions out of cooperative states once a negative reading has taken hold.

Notably, BPD trustees did not show reliable evidence of reduced coaxing. This is worth underscoring given the prominence of coaxing deficits in earlier accounts (King-Casas et al., 2008). The absence of a coaxing difference argues against a general failure to emit repair signals. However, BPD trustees were less likely than CC to return to cooperation when their previous response was clearly exploitative at medium and high partner investments, whereas returning from the neutral state did not differ reliably. The results therefore suggest context-dependent differences in both maintenance and repair, rather than a difficulty confined to either process. The observed instability under low-stake conditions also resonates with the clinical phenomenology of rapid shifts in interpersonal stance: minor cues of ambiguous intent may precipitate an abrupt downgrading of the partner from potentially cooperative to insufficiently reliable (Skodol et al., 2002). This parallel should be treated with caution, as the present data capture behavioural transitions rather than representational shifts, but it suggests a point of contact between this computational phenotype and the oscillation in representations of others that characterises BPD.

Because the investor and trustee roles were completed by different participants, any cross-role synthesis must remain conceptual. Nonetheless, the two findings may reflect distinguishable manifestations of altered epistemic calibration under uncertainty. In the trustee role, ambiguous low investment may be over-read as rejection and destabilise cooperative maintenance -a pattern compatible with epistemic hypervigilance, a defensive mode of intense scrutiny in which ambiguous cues are over-interpreted under conditions of expected interpersonal danger. In the investor role, partner feedback may be under-used as a basis for revising trust estimates -a pattern more consistent with epistemic mistrust in the narrower sense. Negative prior expectations are related but distinct: they would imply a more stable expectation that partners are unreliable across contexts, whereas hypervigilance and mistrust concern how ambiguous cues are interpreted and how feedback is used once encountered. Although related, these are not identical processes, and the present data cannot adjudicate between them directly.

Despite the marked differences in reciprocity dynamics, we detected no reliable group differences in payoffs. This does not establish equivalent social functioning. Behavioural differences need not translate into differences in total earnings: in repeated trust games with adaptive computer partners, participants scoring higher on self-serving personality traits returned less without obtaining significantly higher total payoffs (Guennouni, Korn, et al., 2026). Earnings therefore need to be interpreted in light of how partners respond to participants' choices. If the clinically relevant dysfunction lies in the temporal organisation of interaction-processes that become unstable, brittle, and difficult to repair over time-then a short, constrained exchange may fail to reveal outcome differences even when the underlying cooperative dynamics differ substantially. The present design constrained precisely those processes that would ordinarily amplify the consequences of fragile cooperation. The nearest-neighbour algorithm could replay exploitative or generous human responses in locally similar situations, but it did not learn a participant-specific policy over repeated interaction. It therefore limited cumulative partner adaptation, such as progressively exploiting a consistently generous participant, retaliating against instability, or building increasing trust with a reliably cooperative one. The interpersonal costs of these patterns are likely to emerge in contexts involving reputation formation, memory for prior behaviour, and cumulative mutual adaptation -conditions that were deliberately minimised here.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations warrant caution. First, the analyses were not preregistered and are hypothesis-generating. They used a fixed sample accumulated across parent studies rather than a prospectively powered design. Racial/ethnic identification, cultural or geographic background, and socioeconomic indicators were not collected in the existing dataset, limiting assessment of representativeness. The target populations are UK treatment-seeking patients with BPD and affective-symptom service users, compared with community participants recruited as described above. Findings should not be assumed to generalise to all people with BPD, depression or anxiety, or to other national, cultural or service contexts. The small AD investor subgroup warrants particular caution.

Second, combining depressive and anxiety presentations in AD limits inference about specific affectivedisorder mechanisms. Third, medication and comorbidity were uncontrolled; both are common in these populations and may influence social decision-making. Fourth, administration mode (remote vs. in-person) was uneven across groups as testing shifted to remote during recruitment. Mode-adjusted checks supported the primary mixed-model effects-attenuated BPD investor adaptation and elevated AD trustee returns-but AD encouragement became non-significant, consistent with demographic checks, and the AD-CC difference in cooperative-state occupancy did not persist among participants tested online. Fifth, although controls were meant to be screened out if they scored 3 or above on the SAPAS, many retained controls scored at or above this cut-off, and some had no recorded score. The control group therefore includes participants with high personality-disorder screening scores. Excluding them did not change the BPD-CC comparisons in the hidden-state analysis estimated without diagnostic information.

Sixth, the computerised partner allowed experimental control but limited participant-specific reputation formation and cumulative mutual adaptation. Participants believed they were interacting with humans; knowing the partner was artificial might have changed behaviour. The findings concern responses to controlled social signals rather than relationships in which both partners learn and adapt.

Finally, the HMM transition intervals are conditional on the selected state structure and fixed emission model, excluding uncertainty over state number and emission specification. The AD-BPD difference in cooperative-state occupancy persisted in four-and six-state models and when states were estimated without diagnostic information, and the long-run cooperation difference persisted at most investment levels; BPD-CC retention and repair varied with state count. This supports the broader AD-BPD difference but requires caution about the balance of maintenance and repair in BPD relative to CC. The states' psychological validity remains to be established, for example through associations with mentalizing (including reflective functioning), epistemic trust, mistrust and credulity, rejection sensitivity, and symptom severity.

Future work should use preregistered studies with more balanced samples, finer characterisation of affective symptoms, fuller medication and comorbidity assessment, symptom-severity associations, and live partners or adaptive agents that learn individual behavioural patterns. One way to test the role of ambiguous partner signals is to manipulate the information available about the partner. In a repeated trust-game experiment, displaying emotion cues linked to a computer investor's underlying response pattern increased trustee returns, with effects depending on presentation order (Guennouni et al., 2024). A clinical follow-up could compare visible and concealed cues while holding the partner's response rules constant, and measure participants' interpretations of the partner's intentions alongside their choices. This would test whether clearer partner signals alter cooperation and whether changes in interpretation accompany changes in behaviour.

Clinical Implications

These computational phenotypes may have preliminary translational relevance, although any clinical implications remain provisional and require replication. If the pattern of over-accommodating prosocial responding in this AD sample proves robust, affected individuals may be especially vulnerable to exploitation in unstructured relationships, even if those risks are not readily apparent in standardised therapeutic or experimental contexts. This vulnerability may be compounded if the over-accommodation observed here reflects epistemic credulity -a reduced capacity to discriminate between social demands that warrant compliance and those that do not (Bröcker et al., 2026) -a pattern associated with both depressive and anxiety symptoms. Interventions might then focus on helping patients identify trigger contexts, particularly partner hesitation or reduced investment, and legitimising proportional rather than compensatory reciprocity, in line with work identifying guilt and submissive responding as clinically salient in depression ( O'Connor et al., 2002). For BPD, if fragile cooperation proves replicable, mentalization-based treatment approaches (Bateman & Fonagy, 2004; Fonagy et al., 2015) may be well placed to address two role-specific challenges suggested by the present data: in the trustee role, interpreting ambiguous partner signals without over-inferring rejection; and in the investor role, updating models of the partner progressively on the basis of accumulating evidence. One potential therapeutic target suggested by these findings, pending replication, is helping patients recognise and use renewed cooperative signals from others, rather than focusing exclusively on signalling their own cooperative intent. The differential pattern across groups also suggests that intervention may need to be tailored, with guilt-focused work potentially more relevant for AD and epistemic-trust-oriented work more relevant for BPD (Nolte et al., 2023), although symptom-level correlates remain to be established. Experimental evidence suggests that responses to a withdrawal of trust can be modified: a brief cognitive intervention increased trustee returns and reduced retaliation following a programmed low investment in non-clinical adults (Guennouni, Dupret, et al., 2026). Whether comparable changes occur in clinical populations remains to be tested. Computational measures derived from Trust Games may eventually complement conventional symptom assessments by providing quantitative indicators of social functioning that are sensitive to treatment-related change (Huys et al., 2016).

Conclusion

In conclusion, diagnostic groups differed in how they adjusted trust and maintained or restored cooperation across repeated social exchange. BPD was most consistently characterised by attenuated adjustment of investment following partner feedback, whereas the trustee analyses indicated more context-and modeldependent differences in cooperative maintenance and repair. The most robust trustee distinction was between BPD and the affective-symptom sample, which showed greater cooperative-state occupancy, repair, and, at most partner investment levels, model-implied long-run cooperation. These dynamic phenotypes are candidate behavioural markers of altered social learning and interpersonal adaptation. The proposed links to mentalizing, epistemic stance, rejection sensitivity, and interpersonal accommodation remain interpretations of the behavioural pattern that require direct measurement in future studies.

S1.3 Bootstrap Algorithm for Trustee HMM Transition Dynamics

For the trustee hidden Markov model (HMM), we implemented an approximate, model-conditional transition bootstrap with 10,000 successful draws to quantify uncertainty in retention, repair, transitions involving the neutral state, and long-run cooperation. The selected state structure and emission distributions remained fixed.

Algorithm Steps

First, we extracted probability-weighted current-to-next-state transition counts from the fitted HMM using the forward-backward algorithm. We then fitted a multinomial transition model for each starting state, including investment, diagnostic group, and their interaction.

Before fitting these models, fractional transition counts were multiplied by 2,000 and rounded to whole counts. For simulation, each row was included with a probability equal to its rounded row total divided by 2,000, capped at one. Each included row contributed one destination state sampled from the fitted transition probabilities.

For each bootstrap sample, we: 1. simulated transitions from these models; 2. refitted the transition models; 3. recalculated retention, repair, transitions involving the neutral state, and long-run cooperation at investments of 5, 10, and 15 tokens; and 4. stored the results when every refitted model was successful.

We obtained 10,000 successful samples in eight independently seeded batches of 1,250 samples (seeds 11-18), pooling the samples before calculating confidence intervals and tests.

The reported point estimates were calculated directly from the fitted HMM rather than from the bootstrap transition models.

Confidence Intervals and Testing

• 95% confidence interval (CI): Percentile method [q 0.025 , q 0.975 ] of bootstrap distribution • Hypothesis tests: Two-sided bootstrap p-values with a finite-sample correction and Holm correction within each comparison family. Retention and repair from State 1 formed an 18-comparison family for the primary results (Table S29), and long-run cooperation formed a nine-comparison family. For the secondary neutral-state results (Table S33), movement from neutral State 2 into cooperation was adjusted together with retention and repair from State 1, forming a 27-comparison family. Exits to States 1 and 2 were adjusted together across 18 comparisons.

The bootstrap simulated transitions within the fitted five-state model while holding the state structure and emission distributions fixed. The resulting intervals represent uncertainty in the transition model. Separate simulation of each starting-state model omits some covariance across transition blocks, and interval coverage remains unverified. Analyses with the neighbouring four-and six-state trustee models assessed sensitivity to state count, using 10,000 successful bootstrap samples per model. For each model, eight independently seeded batches of 1,250 samples were pooled before calculating confidence intervals and applying the original comparison-family corrections.

The coefficients describe baseline transition probabilities, the effect of the trial-level predictor, group differences, and group-by-predictor differences. The denominator makes the probabilities across all possible next states sum to one. Initial state probabilities depended on first-round investment for trustees and were constant across investor sequences.

Parameter Initialization and Multistart Search

Investor and trustee HMMs used the same multistart search and fit-validity criteria. For each state count and model family, we screened 20 randomized statemembership starts for 20 iterations and continued the 3 highest-likelihood candidates for up to 2000 iterations. We also fitted a structured start that placed the starting state means evenly across the feasible response range. Diagnosis-dependent models were additionally fitted from warm starts based on the highest-likelihood and structured diagnosis-free fits, with the new diagnostic-group coefficients initially set to zero. 5 small perturbations were fitted around each of the structured diagnosis-dependent and structured warm starts. If no candidate passed the validity criteria, up to 4 additional rounds continued the best finite candidates.

A fit was retained only if the fitting algorithm reported convergence, all likelihoods and parameters were finite, every response distribution had non-zero spread, and every state accounted for at least 0.5% of the total posterior state probability across trials. This final requirement excluded effectively unused states. Among the valid candidates, we retained the fit with the highest log likelihood. States were then ordered by mean return proportion for trustees and mean investment for investors.

Model Selection

At the selected state count, the diagnosis-free model was nested within the diagnosisdependent model because it could be obtained by constraining the group coefficients to zero. Standard likelihood-ratio theory applies to such fixed-state nested HMM comparisons under regularity conditions, but not to comparisons that change the number of hidden states (Giudici et al., 2000).

As a robustness check, we refit the diagnosis-relevant mixed models on the subset with valid age and binary gender data (Male/Female only), adding centered age and gender as additive covariates. Because the AD investor subgroup remained small even in the matched sample, investor sensitivity analyses should be interpreted descriptively.

Hidden-State Sensitivity to Administration Mode and Demographics

We also examined whether the central AD-BPD trustee hidden-state findings persisted when the comparison was restricted to the administration mode shared by both groups and when age and gender were included as covariates. These were post-estimation sensitivity analyses: hidden states were obtained from the validated five-state model fitted to the full trustee sample, after which probability-weighted cooperative-state occupancy, decoded transition outcomes, and model-implied long-run cooperation were compared in the restricted or adjusted samples. Thus, the online-only analyses address whether the observed administration-mode difference alone accounts for the second-stage group contrasts; they do not remove differences in recruitment or diagnostic ascertainment.

For long-run cooperation, we first analysed all online trustees (n = 686). We then analysed the online participants with complete age and gender data (n = 600), both before and after demographic adjustment. Using the same participants for these two analyses separated the effect of adjustment from the effect of excluding participants with missing demographic data. An additional demographic-adjusted analysis included participants from all testing settings with complete demographic data (n = 806; Table S34).

The five-state definitions, state-specific return distributions, and posterior state probabilities remained fixed at their full-sample estimates. Transition models were re-estimated within each analysis sample, including diagnosis, investment, and their interaction; adjusted models additionally included age and gender. Adjusted predictions at investments of 5, 10, and 15 tokens used the full sample's observed mean age. Gender-specific long-run cooperative-state probabilities were averaged using the analysis sample's pooled gender proportions, with identical weights across diagnostic groups.

Each analysis used 10,000 successful transition-bootstrap samples, percentile 95% confidence intervals, and two-sided bootstrap tests, following Section S1.3. Holm correction covered nine comparisons per analysis: three diagnostic-group pairs at three investment levels.

The AD-BPD difference persisted when analysis was restricted to online trustees. AD trustees had 22.2 percentage points greater probability-weighted cooperative-state occupancy than BPD trustees (95% CI [14.8, 29.6]; Holm-adjusted p < .001). On the online subset with complete demographic data, the age-and gender-adjusted difference was 21.8 percentage points (95% CI [13.6, 30.0]; Holm-adjusted p < .001). The corresponding AD advantage in returning from the clearly low-return state was also present at partner investments of 5, 10, and 15 tokens in every sensitivity analysis (Table S18).

AD trustees showed greater model-implied long-run cooperation than BPD trustees at 5 and 10 tokens in every analysis: among online participants, in the matched online sample before and after demographic adjustment, and across all testing settings after adjustment (largest Holm-adjusted p = .034; Table S34). At 15 tokens, the difference was not significant in any of these analyses (smallest Holm-adjusted p = .452).

At 10 tokens, the difference in the matched online sample was 24.1 percentage points (95% CI [13.5, 30.7]) before adjustment and 23.4 points (95% CI [12.0, 30.1]) after adjustment. Among comparisons involving controls, only one survived Holm correction: in the all-settings adjusted analysis, long-run cooperation at 5 tokens was 13.6 points lower in BPD than in CC (95% CI [-24.1, -4.2], Holm-adjusted p = .024).

Sensitivity to the Number of Trustee States

To examine whether the findings depended on the number of hidden states, we repeated the occupancy and transition analyses using neighbouring four-and six-state diagnosis-dependent models. In each model, the state closest to returning one third of the tripled investment was classified as neutral. Lower-return states were classified as exploitative and higher-return states as cooperative. This defined States 3-4 as cooperative in the four-state model and States 4-6 in the six-state model.

The AD-BPD difference was the most consistent result. AD trustees occupied cooperative states more often than BPD trustees in both alternative models. They also showed greater long-run cooperation at every investment level in the four-state model and at 10 and 15 tokens in the six-state model. Repair after exploitation was also higher in AD than BPD at every investment level in the four-state model and at 10 and 15 tokens in the six-state model.

The BPD-CC transition pattern depended on the number of states. In the six-state model, BPD trustees showed lower cooperative retention than controls at 5 and 10 tokens after adjustment; the difference at 15 tokens did not remain reliable. The four-state model showed no reliable BPD retention deficit and instead identified lower BPD repair at 10 and 15 tokens. Thus, the AD-BPD occupancy difference was stable and the long-run cooperation difference held at most investment levels, whereas whether BPD-CC differences appeared in cooperative maintenance or repair depended on how finely behaviour was divided into states.

AD trustees showed higher repair than controls at 5 and 10 tokens in the four-state model; the remaining AD-CC repair comparisons, including all comparisons in the six-state model, did not remain reliable after adjustment. In the six-state model, AD retention was lower than that of controls at 10 tokens.

S1.1 Behavioural Feature Definitions: Complete Formulas

Beyond mean investment amounts and return proportions, we derived behavioural features capturing dynamic reciprocity patterns and strategic adjustment. While these can be summarised as participant-level proportions for descriptive purposes, our primary analysis treated them as binary outcomes in trial-level generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs). Diagnostic groups were community controls (CC), participants with borderline personality disorder (BPD), and affective-symptom service users (AD).

Descriptive proportions used the opportunity conditions below. Trial-level models additionally applied the stated eligibility conditions to exclude rounds in which the response was impossible. All trial-level models used rounds with the required previous-round information and observed outcomes, excluding the first round.

Fair Return Benchmark We defined a "fair return" as the amount equalising payoffs between investor and trustee: fair_return = 2 × investment -10 if investment ≥ 5 0 otherwise

When investment < 5, any positive return exceeds fairness because the tripled amount is insufficient to equalise payoffs. For example, if investment is 0, the trustee receives 0, and if investment is 1, the trustee receives 3, such that any return leaves unequal totals.

We used this boundary because at 5 tokens the equal-payoff return is 0, whereas investments above 5 tokens have a positive fair-return benchmark and were analysed through the general return and fairness variables.
