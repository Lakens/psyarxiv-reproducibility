Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Confidence is an imperfect guide to accuracy, but a group need not use it to identify its best member or assign a precise weight to every answer. Confidence can instead determine whether an answer contributes at all. This article examines confidence filtering: responses below a threshold abstain, and the remaining responses are combined by an equally weighted majority vote. Three studies applied specified decision rules to existing behavioral judgments. In binary-choice inference, filtering improved the exact expected accuracy of groups of 30 respondents at every tested threshold from 10 to 70 on a 0-100 confidence scale. The largest improvement was 2.73 percentage points, and a fixed threshold also benefited larger groups. A second study extended the analysis to counterfactual transmission chains constructed from physicians' diagnostic judgments. The modified rule allowed lowconfidence disagreement to suspend an inherited answer without asserting an alternative. This reduced overall accuracy but partially mitigated the deterioration found in cases where the original majority was wrong. The improvement depended on resolving final undecided states at chance; it did not reflect an increase in committed correct answers. A third study compared filtering with the surprisingly popular method. In general knowledge, every integer threshold above the confidence-scale minimum outperformed both ordinary majority voting and the surprisingly popular benchmark. The highest accuracy was 61%, compared with 51% and 50%, respectively. NFL forecasting provided a contrasting result: filtering did not improve ordinary majority voting, and strict thresholds reduced accuracy. All items remained in the analyses, with unresolved outcomes receiving half credit. Confidence filtering therefore worked across broad threshold ranges in some settings, rather than providing a universally superior rule. Its value lies in using confidence to decide which answers should contribute and, in sequential transmission, when an inherited answer should cease to be treated as settled.

1 Introduction

Combining judgments from several people can produce a more accurate answer than relying on one person. This wisdom-of-crowds effect is possible because people do not all possess the same information or make the same mistakes. Aggregation can bring their information together while allowing some errors to cancel. A group may therefore reach an accurate answer without first identifying its most knowledgeable member (Galton, 1907; Surowiecki, 2004; Davis-Stober et al., 2014).

The success of aggregation does not, however, imply that every available judgment should always receive a vote. A response from someone who is guessing enters an ordinary majority vote on the same terms as a response from someone who knows the answer. Excluding the former might improve the decision. The difficulty is to identify which responses can be set aside without also discarding information that the group needs.

Research on selecting crowds has approached this problem in several ways. Small groups selected on the basis of past performance can outperform much larger groups (Mannes et al., 2014; Goldstein et al., 2014). Other approaches assess how much an individual contributes to the group's accuracy and use that contribution to select or weight judgments (Budescu & Chen, 2015; Chen et al., 2016; see also Himmelstein et al., 2023). These results show that access to a large crowd and the decision to use all its members are separate matters. The available crowd can contain a smaller set of judgments that produces a better answer.

Even random reductions in crowd size can sometimes improve accuracy. Galesic et al. (2018) examined environments containing both problems on which most people were correct and problems on which most were wrong. Increasing group size made a correct majority more reliable, but it also made an incorrect majority more reliable. Moderately sized random crowds could consequently outperform larger crowds. The problem was not simply that some individuals were poor judges. It arose because adding judgments reinforced different majorities on different problems.

The surprisingly popular method offers another way to identify information that ordinary voting can miss. Prelec et al. (2017) asked respondents both to answer a question and to predict how others would answer it. An answer that is more common than respondents predict may reflect knowledge held by a minority. Comparing observed and expected support can then recover an answer that would lose a conventional majority vote. This approach established that the distribution of answers alone need not reveal the best answer available within a crowd.

These approaches raise a question about confidence. People can often report whether they feel certain about a particular answer or are merely guessing. Could that information help a group decide which answers to use, even when it does not reliably identify the best individual?

1.1 Confidence as an imperfect guide to individual accuracy

Confidence is an appealing cue because it is available from the person making the judgment. In principle, someone who recognizes a gap in their knowledge can report that uncertainty. In practice, people can be overconfident or underconfident, and these tendencies vary across individuals, tasks, and methods of elicitation (Klayman et al., 1999; Moore & Healy, 2008). A meta-analysis of selfassessed ability found an average association with objective performance of approximately r = .29 (Zell & Krizan, 2014). That estimate concerns general assessments of ability, not confidence in individual answers. It nevertheless illustrates why self-assessment cannot simply be substituted for an independent measure of competence.

Different properties of confidence also need to be distinguished. One concerns a person's general tendency to report high or low confidence. Another concerns whether reported confidence matches actual accuracy. A third concerns whether the person assigns higher confidence to correct answers than to errors. These properties need not coincide (Fleming & Lau, 2014). Someone who is generally overconfident may still be relatively more confident when correct than when mistaken. Conversely, two people with similar average confidence may differ considerably in their ability to recognize their own errors. A high confidence rating is therefore not, by itself, evidence that a respondent evaluates their judgments accurately.

This distinction matters for collective decisions. A group might use confidence to decide whose answer to follow, but it might also use confidence to identify answers that should not influence the decision. These uses ask different things of the same report. The former places considerable responsibility on the person who expresses the greatest confidence. The latter leaves several respondents involved and uses confidence only to determine whether a particular response enters their vote.

1.2 From confident individuals to better collective judgments

Following the most confident person can improve a decision when confidence identifies the better answer. It can also reinforce an error. Confidence sometimes reflects how widely an answer is shared, rather than knowledge that distinguishes it from a plausible mistake. When the widely shared answer is wrong, selecting the more confident respondent can favor that wrong answer (Koriat, 2008 (Koriat, , 2012)). The fact that confidence is socially informative does not establish that the most confident person should determine the outcome.

Confidence-weighted voting distributes influence more gradually. Several answers still contribute, but more confident responses receive greater weight. This can improve collective accuracy (Meyen et al., 2021), although the advantage is not consistent across tasks. In face matching, for example, confidence weighting did not reliably outperform an ordinary majority vote (Kramer & Javorková, 2025). Retaining more of the numerical information in confidence ratings therefore does not guarantee a better collective decision.

Selecting confident people on the basis of previous questions presents a different difficulty. Shirasuna and Honda (2023) used a training-test design to examine whether confidence in one set of questions could identify groups that would perform well on another set. Groups selected for high confidence often performed well, but confidence on the training questions did not reliably predict test performance. Selection could also reduce the diversity of judgments, and some selected groups showed substantial declines on the test questions. Including people with different confidence levels could help avoid these losses.

That finding provides a reason to be cautious about excluding people whose confidence is generally low. It does not settle whether an uncertain answer to the current question should receive a vote. A person who lacks confidence on one question may be well informed on another. Selecting a fixed group from previous responses and deciding whether to retain a current response are therefore different procedures. The latter does not require the same people to contribute to every decision.

The question pursued here is whether this response-level use of confidence can improve collective accuracy without requiring a finely tuned numerical weight. Rather than asking who is the most confident or how much more a highly confident answer should count, the procedure asks whether an answer is confident enough to enter the vote at all.

1.3 Confidence filtering with a simple rule

Under confidence filtering, responses below a threshold abstain. The remaining responses are combined by majority voting, with one vote assigned to each retained answer. Confidence determines participation in the vote, but additional confidence above the threshold brings no additional influence.

Figure 1 shows two ways in which confidence can change a collective decision. Figure 1a shows the filtering rule used for simultaneous voting. Five respondents choose between A and B. From left to right, their answers and confidence ratings are B (80), A (30), B (70), B (40), and A (90). With a threshold of 50, the responses rated 30 and 40 abstain. The remaining votes are B, B, and A. The majority therefore chooses B. The most confident respondent chooses A, but does not determine the result. Ordinary majority voting would also choose B in this example, because three of the five original respondents choose B. The diagram illustrates which responses enter the vote. It does not illustrate an improvement in accuracy. Filtering keeps the collective decision rule while changing the responses to which it is applied.

Filtering deliberately ignores differences between retained confidence ratings. An answer rated at 70 counts as much as one rated at 90 when both exceed the threshold. This may appear wasteful, but a more detailed use of information need not improve a decision. Hastie and Kameda's (2005) account of the "robust beauty" of majority rules showed how simple collective rules could remain competitive across varied decision environments, including comparisons with procedures that used additional information. Confidence filtering retains the equal weighting of majority voting while changing which responses enter it.

Abstention has already proved useful in related research. Bennett et al. (2018) allowed respondents to decide which questions to answer rather than requiring an answer to every question. This improved crowd performance. Douven et al. (2026) more recently compared voting rules that converted probability judgments into endorsement, rejection, or abstention. Majority voting among respondents who did not abstain achieved substantial accuracy gains over a range of confidence thresholds. These findings establish that withholding uncertain responses can do more than protect a group against an unsuccessful selection procedure: it can improve the answer produced by aggregation.

The present research examines how widely such an improvement extends within and across tasks. Finding one effective threshold is not the same as finding a procedure that is relatively insensitive to its threshold. A cutoff chosen because it performs best in one dataset may be difficult to apply elsewhere. A broad range of effective cutoffs would provide stronger evidence that the benefit does not depend on identifying one precise value. It would not establish that the same threshold transfers to every new task, but it would distinguish a stable within-dataset advantage from a narrow optimum.

1.4 From abstention to suspended judgment

The role of uncertainty changes when judgments are passed sequentially rather than collected for a simultaneous vote. In a transmission chain, each person receives an answer from a predecessor and can leave it in place or replace it. An uncertain respondent who refrains from contributing a new answer does not thereby remove an answer from the process. The inherited answer may continue to travel unchanged.

Transmission research has examined how messages are transformed as they pass between people, what limits the propagation of quantitative judgments, and when sequential problem solving improves on independent search (Moussaïd et al., 2015 (Moussaïd et al., , 2017;; Seyed Yahosseini & Moussaïd, 2020). These studies make clear that a chain does not merely collect additional opinions. What later respondents receive depends on how earlier respondents have used, altered, or preserved the information passed to them. Moussaïd and Seyed Yahosseini (2016) examined a simple confidence-based transmission rule. A sufficiently confident respondent replaces the inherited answer with their own answer; a less confident respondent leaves the inherited answer unchanged. Their analyses identified conditions under which this rule could perform well, although conditions favoring chains over the other aggregation methods were rare in the empirical datasets they examined.

The rule leaves an important distinction unexpressed. Suppose a respondent receives A but tentatively favors B. Their confidence may be too low to justify replacing A with B. It does not follow that they endorse A. Nevertheless, leaving the inherited answer unchanged allows A to continue as the chain's answer. Uncertainty about an alternative is thus treated procedurally as a reason to preserve the existing conclusion.

The extension examined here introduces an undecided state, represented by "?". A respondent who disagrees with the inherited answer but lacks sufficient confidence to replace it can instead suspend it. The chain then carries "?" rather than A or B. Further low-confidence responses leave the answer unresolved. Only a sufficiently confident response can establish an answer again. This gives uncertain disagreement a limited role. It can withdraw commitment to an answer without promoting the respondent's tentative alternative.

Figure 1b shows how this works. The first respondent chooses A with confidence 4. The second chooses B but reports confidence 1. This disagreement changes the inherited A to "?". The third respondent also chooses B, but reports confidence 2. Because the transmitted state is already undecided, this low-confidence response leaves it undecided. The fourth respondent chooses B with confidence 4 and establishes B. The transmitted states are therefore A → ? → ? → B. Here, "?" is a state of the chain. In Figure 1a, by contrast, it marks an individual response that has abstained from voting.

Consider an inherited answer that is wrong. Under the binary rule, it may persist because no later respondent is confident enough to replace it. Changing that answer to "?" can prevent the chain from retaining a definite error. If an unresolved final state is resolved by a fair binary choice, its expected accuracy is one half rather than zero. This gain does not require anyone to discover the correct answer. It results from no longer preserving a wrong answer as settled. Whether the same rule improves accuracy across a set of cases remains an empirical question, because correct inherited answers can also be suspended.

Simultaneous filtering and suspended judgment therefore perform different operations. One excludes an uncertain response from a vote. The other changes the status of an answer that is already in circulation. Their common feature is that uncertainty need not be converted immediately into definite support for either alternative.

1.5 The present study

Three studies evaluated these procedures by applying specified aggregation or update rules to existing behavioral data. Study 1 examined confidence-filtered majority voting in binary-choice inference. Exact expected accuracies were calculated across confidence thresholds and group sizes. The main question was whether improvement over ordinary majority voting would extend across a broad range of settings rather than appearing only at one selected cutoff.

Study 2 used physicians' diagnostic judgments to construct counterfactual transmission chains. It compared binary transmission with the rule allowing uncertain disagreement to suspend an inherited answer. The analysis considered both all diagnostic cases and the subset in which the original majority was wrong. This distinction was necessary to determine whether the new rule improved transmission generally or instead mitigated a particular kind of failure.

Study 3 compared confidence filtering with the surprisingly popular method in general knowledge and NFL forecasting. Although surprisingly popular aggregation can reveal information missed by majority voting, its benefits have varied across applications and respondent populations (Lee et al., 2018; Rutchick et al., 2020; Kramer & Javorková, 2025). The comparison asked whether confidence in one's own answer could provide an effective alternative to predictions about others' answers, and whether any advantage persisted across thresholds.

Together, the studies examine a use of confidence that does not depend on assigning a different degree of influence to every reported value. They also separate two possible benefits of withholding a judgment: improving the vote formed from current answers and interrupting the continued transmission of an inherited error. Both benefits are evaluated alongside the cases in which withholding information reduces accuracy.

2 Study 1: Confidence filtering across thresholds and group sizes

2.1 Method

Study 1 reanalyzed the binary-choice responses in the dataset accompanying Shirasuna and Honda (2023). Participants answered inference questions and reported confidence on a scale from 0 to 100. The analysis included 28,120 judgments from 592 participants. Responses were organized into 190 question-by-version pools, each containing between 146 and 150 respondents.

Within each pool, ordinary majority voting was compared with confidence-filtered majority voting. Ordinary voting gave every response one vote. Under filtering, responses with confidence below the threshold abstained, and each remaining response received one vote. Ratings equal to the threshold were retained. Thus, a threshold of 0 reproduced ordinary majority voting, whereas a threshold of 100 retained only responses assigned the maximum confidence rating.

I calculated the exact expected accuracy of groups sampled without replacement from each response pool. Group size refers throughout to the number of respondents sampled before filtering. Filtering could reduce the number of votes cast, but it did not replace excluded responses with additional respondents. This makes the comparison one between two rules applied to groups of the same initial size, rather than between groups recruited to contain the same number of retained votes.

All 190 pools received equal weight. A correct collective answer received a score of 1, and an incorrect answer received 0. Ties and outcomes in which every sampled respondent abstained received half credit. The latter scoring rule corresponds to choosing randomly between the two answers when voting does not determine a winner.

Two parameter sweeps examined the procedure's sensitivity to its settings. The first held group size at 30 and varied the confidence threshold from 0 to 100 in increments of 10. The second held the threshold at 50 and varied group size from 1 to 146. A maximum of 146 allowed every response pool to remain in the analysis at every group size.

The resulting values are exact expectations conditional on the observed response pools. They describe how the specified rules would perform across possible samples from those pools. Hypothetical groups were not treated as additional, independent empirical observations.

2.2 Results

Figure 2a shows how the advantage of filtering changes with the abstention threshold for groups of 30 respondents. The vertical axis gives the difference in expected accuracy from ordinary majority voting. Values above zero mean that filtering performs better. Ordinary majority voting achieved an expected accuracy of 82.60%. Filtering exceeded this value at every tested threshold from 10 to 70. The highest accuracy on the tested grid was 85.33% at a threshold of 50, an improvement of 2.73 percentage points.

Several other thresholds produced similar gains. Thresholds of 20, 30, and 40 each yielded approximately 84.6% accuracy. The advantage was therefore not confined to the best-performing cutoff. A range of rules that differed in how many low-confidence responses they excluded nevertheless improved on counting every response equally.

More stringent filtering eventually reversed this benefit. At a threshold of 80, accuracy fell to 81.35%, below ordinary majority voting. Retaining only responses rated at 100 reduced accuracy further, to 74.13%. Excluding uncertain responses and retaining only maximally confident responses were therefore not equivalent strategies in this dataset. Moderate filtering helped, whereas restricting the vote to the most confident responses did not.

Figure 2b shows expected accuracy as the sampled group size increases, with the threshold fixed at 50. The black line represents filtering, and the gray dashed line represents ordinary majority voting. The advantage extended well beyond the groups of 30 used in Figure 2a. Filtering improved expected accuracy by 1.08 percentage points for groups of 10, 3.49 points for groups of 50, and 4.08 points for groups of 100. At the largest group size, 146 respondents, the filtered vote achieved 87.07% accuracy, compared with 82.86% for ordinary majority voting.

Very small groups showed a different pattern. For a single respondent, filtering reduced expected accuracy from 71.29% to 66.57%. Small gains and losses alternated over the next few group sizes. With so few respondents, excluding a response could remove much of the information on which the decision depended. In larger groups, the advantage became sustained and more pronounced. At the threshold examined here, filtering did not merely preserve accuracy while reducing the number of votes. It increased the accuracy obtained from the sampled groups.

3 Study 2: Suspending inherited judgments in transmission chains

3.1 Method

Study 2 examined the sequential setting in which an uncertain response can affect an answer inherited from a predecessor. The analysis used a medical diagnosis dataset containing 4,320 judgments from 40 physicians on 108 cases. Confidence was recorded on a four-point scale.

Counterfactual chains were evaluated within the binary-chain framework of Moussaïd and Seyed Yahosseini (2016). These chains were calculated from individual judgments; they were not observed exchanges between physicians.

Two transmission rules were compared. Under binary transmission, the physician at the first position supplied the initial answer regardless of confidence. At each subsequent position, a physician reporting confidence of 3 or 4 supplied their own answer. A physician reporting confidence of 1 or 2 left the inherited answer unchanged. Thus, only a high-confidence response could replace an answer already in the chain.

The modified rule retained this treatment of the first position and of subsequent high-confidence responses. Its change concerned low-confidence disagreement. When a low-confidence respondent disagreed with an inherited answer, the transmitted state changed to "?" rather than remaining at the inherited answer or switching to the respondent's alternative. Low-confidence agreement left a definite answer unchanged. Once the state became "?", another low-confidence response could not establish an answer, whether that respondent favored A or B. Only a high-confidence response could resolve the undecided state. This procedure is referred to as the locked rule. Figure 1b shows an example of these transitions.

Physicians were sampled independently with replacement within each diagnostic case. I calculated exact expected outcomes at positions 1 through 10 using the transition probabilities implied by the two rules. A chain ending with a correct answer received a score of 1, and one ending with an incorrect answer received 0. A final "?" received half credit, corresponding to a random choice between the two substantive answers. Every diagnostic case received equal weight.

Accuracy was examined across all 108 cases and separately for wrong-majority cases. The latter were defined as cases in which fewer than half of the physicians initially answered correctly. This subset was identified retrospectively from the recorded judgments and the correct answers, not from a procedure available to the physicians during diagnosis.

3.2 Results

Figure 3a shows the result across all 108 diagnostic cases. The locked rule performed worse than binary transmission after the first position. At position 10, binary transmission achieved 85.14% expected accuracy, whereas the locked rule achieved 84.01%. The difference was -1.13 percentage points. Allowing judgments to become undecided therefore did not improve transmission across the dataset as a whole.

Figure 3b shows a different pattern for the nine wrong-majority cases. In these cases, binary transmission reduced expected accuracy from 31.94% at the first position to 27.09% at position 10. Under the locked rule, expected accuracy at position 10 was 30.06%. Suspending inherited judgments thus mitigated the loss by 2.97 percentage points relative to binary transmission. At this position, the locked rule had higher expected accuracy in each of the nine cases.

This improvement was a partial rescue rather than a reversal of the original problem. Accuracy remained below chance and below the accuracy at the first position. Its interpretation also depends on separating definite answers from undecided outcomes. At position 10, 22.25% of locked chains ended with a correct answer, while 15.62% remained undecided. Half credit for the undecided outcomes contributed 7.81 percentage points, bringing expected final accuracy to 30.06%. The proportion remaining undecided is shown in the inset of Figure 3b.

The probability of ending with a committed correct answer was therefore lower under the locked rule than under binary transmission, whose final accuracy was 27.09%. The advantage arose only after accounting for the expected accuracy of randomly resolving "?". The rule reduced the harm associated with preserving definite errors in wrong-majority cases, but it did not increase the transmission of committed correct answers or improve accuracy across all cases. The general-knowledge dataset comprised responses to 50 questions from 32 participants in the no-information condition of He et al. (2026). Participants judged whether statements were true or false and reported confidence as an integer from 50 to 100.

The NFL dataset contained predictions collected by Lee et al. (2018). Following that study's focus on self-rated expertise, the analysis used respondents who described themselves as extremely knowledgeable about the sport. This subset contained 4,676 predictions across 256 games. Confidence was measured on a scale from 1 to 5.

Every question or game was evaluated using all available responses in the relevant pool. Confidence filtering followed the same response-level rule as in Study 1: responses below the threshold abstained, and all retained responses received one vote. Ordinary majority voting counted every response, irrespective of confidence.

For SP, the observed proportion choosing each answer was compared with the mean predicted proportion choosing that answer. The collective decision favored the answer whose observed support exceeded its predicted support. Both SP and ordinary majority voting were calculated from the full response pool. These benchmarks therefore remained fixed while the confidence-filtering threshold varied.

Filtering was evaluated at every integer threshold on each dataset's original scale: 50 through 100 for general knowledge and 1 through 5 for NFL forecasting. All questions and games remained in the accuracy denominator, including items for which no respondent's confidence reached the threshold. Each item received equal weight. Correct and incorrect decisions received scores of 1 and 0, respectively, while ties and all-abstention outcomes received half credit.

These comparisons describe sensitivity to the threshold within each dataset. The thresholds yielding the highest observed accuracies were identified and evaluated on the same data. They were not selected on an independent training set, and their reported performance should not be interpreted as an out-of-sample estimate.

4.2 Results

Figure 4a shows accuracy across the confidence thresholds in the general-knowledge dataset. The dotted horizontal line represents SP, which achieved 50.00% accuracy. Ordinary majority voting achieved 51.00%. It is the leftmost point of the filtering curve, because a threshold of 50 excludes no responses. Confidence filtering outperformed both benchmarks at every integer threshold from 51 to 100. The highest observed accuracy was 61.00%, obtained at thresholds of 79 and 80. These values exceeded SP by 11 percentage points and ordinary majority voting by 10 points.

The improvement therefore did not depend on locating the best-performing cutoff. Any tested threshold above the minimum of the confidence scale produced higher accuracy than either benchmark. Even the maximum threshold, which retained only answers assigned confidence of 100, yielded 56.00% accuracy.

At that maximum threshold, 21.5% of the original responses were retained. One of the 50 questions had no retained response, but it remained in the analysis and received half credit. The advantage was consequently not an artifact of removing unanswered questions from the denominator. It reflected the expected accuracy of a procedure required to produce a final outcome for the same set of questions as the benchmark methods.

Figure 4b shows that NFL forecasting produced a different result. Ordinary majority voting achieved 67.77% accuracy and SP achieved 66.60% under the same scoring rule. As in panel a, ordinary majority voting is the leftmost point of the filtering curve. Here it corresponds to a threshold of 1. Raising the threshold to 2 excluded responses at the lowest confidence level. Accuracy then fell slightly to 67.58%. This was higher than SP but lower than ordinary majority voting. No threshold improved ordinary majority voting.

Stricter filtering progressively reduced performance. A threshold of 3 matched SP at 66.60%, while thresholds of 4 and 5 yielded 65.63% and 60.74%, respectively. At the maximum threshold, 29 of the 256 games had no retained response. These games remained in the denominator with half credit.

The two datasets thus provided different answers to the question of threshold robustness. In general knowledge, the advantage over both benchmarks extended across the entire tested range above the scale minimum. In NFL forecasting, only mild filtering remained competitive, and no threshold improved ordinary majority voting. The broad benefit in general knowledge did not generalize to increasingly strict filtering of sports forecasts.

5 Discussion

The question motivating this research was how a group might use confidence without treating it as a precise measure of who is right. The findings show that, in some settings, confidence can help by determining which responses enter a majority vote. Study 1 found improvements across a broad range of tested thresholds, with an advantage that also extended to larger groups. The generalknowledge analysis in Study 3 showed a similarly broad advantage over ordinary majority voting and the surprisingly popular method. These improvements were obtained while giving all retained answers equal influence.

The other analyses establish important limits to that result. Confidence filtering did not improve ordinary majority voting in NFL forecasting. In sequential transmission, allowing uncertain disagreement to suspend an inherited answer reduced accuracy across the diagnostic dataset as a whole. It nevertheless mitigated part of the deterioration in cases where the original majority was wrong. The usefulness of confidence therefore cannot be assessed only by asking whether it is an accurate self-report. It also depends on what a collective procedure does with that report, including what happens when a judgment is withheld.

5.1 A useful filtering rule need not depend on one optimal threshold

The main evidence for robustness is not the accuracy of the single best threshold. It is the range over which filtering improved on the benchmark. In Study 1, groups of 30 performed better than ordinary majority voting at every tested threshold from 10 to 70. In the general-knowledge analysis of Study 3, every integer threshold above the confidence-scale minimum outperformed ordinary majority voting. These results are more informative than a comparison made only at the cutoff yielding the largest gain. They show that an improvement could survive substantial changes in how strictly uncertain responses were excluded. This is the sense in which confidence filtering connects with Hastie and Kameda's (2005) account of simple majority rules. Their argument was not that less information must always be better. It was that a simple procedure can perform competitively without extracting a distinct weight from every available cue. The procedure examined here does not abandon majority voting. It allows confidence to determine entry into the vote and then retains the equal treatment of participating responses. A person who barely exceeds the threshold counts as much as a person who reports maximum confidence.

There is already evidence that abstention can improve aggregation. Douven et al. (2026) converted probability judgments into endorsement, rejection, or abstention and compared the resulting voting rules. Their analysis considered both accuracy and the frequency with which each rule reached a conclusion. The present research extends that direction by examining different tasks and confidence measures and by considering a related rule for sequential transmission. The emphasis is on whether a procedure remains effective across a range of cutoffs, rather than solely on how well it performs at its best cutoff.

This definition of robustness leaves room for substantial failures. In Study 1, filtering became detrimental at high thresholds, and it could be harmful in very small groups. In NFL forecasting, filtering offered no improvement over ordinary majority voting. The results identify broad effective ranges in particular datasets, not a rule that can safely ignore its task or the number of available responses. Nor does a broad within-dataset advantage establish that a threshold selected in advance will transfer unchanged to new questions. It reduces concern about dependence on one precise cutoff, but it does not replace an independent evaluation.

5.2 Imperfect confidence can still inform a collective decision

People often need to evaluate advice without being able to observe an advisor's competence directly. Expressed confidence provides one available cue. Research shows that it affects judgments of knowledge and competence and can produce a preference for overconfident advisors (Price & Stone, 2004). The possibility of being misled is real, but it does not make every use of confidence misguided.

Ecological rationality provides a useful way to formulate the question. A decision rule is evaluated in relation to the conditions under which it operates, rather than by how much information it uses in the abstract. A procedure that ignores some distinctions can perform well when those distinctions are unhelpful or difficult to use reliably (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). For confidence filtering, the relevant issue is whether a coarse division between retained and excluded responses produces a better vote than counting all responses equally.

The distinction between calibration and discrimination helps explain why confidence might support such a division. A person's reported confidence need not match their probability of being correct for it to distinguish their more reliable answers from their less reliable ones. Someone who reports confidence that is generally too high may nevertheless assign relatively lower values to errors. Filtering does not require interpreting every confidence value as a calibrated probability. It uses the location of a response relative to the threshold. Whether that division improves collective accuracy also depends on the judgments that are lost when responses are excluded.

The present findings are consistent with this use of confidence, but they do not establish the psychological process by which people judge another person's confidence. No participant was observed deciding whether to defer to the most confident member. Following one confident respondent and aggregating several responses after excluding uncertain ones are different procedures. The results concern the latter and show that it can be useful even without graded differences in the influence assigned to retained answers.

Voluntary participation offers a related, but distinct, comparison. Kuroda et al. (2023) allowed participants to choose between joining a group majority vote and receiving a payoff determined by their own judgment. More confident participants tended to leave the group. Yet on difficult tasks, the majority of those who remained outperformed the individuals who opted out. Their analyses distinguished changes in group size from changes in the accuracy of the remaining members, showing why the consequences of participation cannot be read directly from the confidence of the people who leave.

It would be misleading to treat their finding and the present results as interchangeable evidence that abstention helps. In Kuroda et al.'s (2023) setting, relatively confident people tended to opt out in pursuit of individual rewards. In the present simultaneous-aggregation analyses, a specified rule excluded relatively uncertain responses. One procedure concerns a person's choice of how to obtain a payoff; the other concerns how a group uses the answers it has collected. Whose answers remain, and the reason they remain, are central to interpreting the outcome.

Sequential transmission adds another distinction. Withholding one's own answer can leave the inherited answer untouched. Suspending an inherited answer changes what the next respondent receives. Thus, a person can refrain from proposing a replacement while still affecting whether the current conclusion remains in force. Study 2 examined that possibility rather than simply repeating the simultaneous filtering procedure in a different order.

5.3 Uncertain disagreement can suspend a judgment without supplying a replacement

In the binary-chain framework of Moussaïd and Seyed Yahosseini (2016), the inherited answer is the default. A high-confidence respondent can replace it, whereas a low-confidence respondent passes it onward unchanged. The authors themselves described this mechanism as a confidence-based filter. Its effect is to prevent insufficiently confident new answers from displacing the answer already in the chain.

The locked rule introduced here changes the treatment of one particular response: uncertain disagreement. Someone who receives A and tentatively favors B need not be entitled to replace A with B. Yet requiring that person to pass A onward gives the inherited answer a status that their own judgment does not support. Transmitting "?" provides an intermediate possibility. The respondent can withdraw commitment to A without establishing B.

This distinction gives an uncertain objection a role that is absent from ordinary abstention. A low-confidence response excluded from a simultaneous vote has no direct effect on the votes already cast. Under the locked rule, low-confidence disagreement can alter the inherited state. Its effect is deliberately limited, because it cannot establish the objector's alternative. Once the state becomes undecided, a further low-confidence response cannot turn it back into a definite answer. A sufficiently confident response is needed to do so.

This question about the status of an inherited answer belongs to a broader concern with what survives transmission. In experimental diffusion chains, Moussaïd et al. (2015) found that messages about a potential health risk changed as they passed between respondents. Information was lost, while the relative emphasis on harmful consequences increased. The transmitted message also interacted with recipients' risk perceptions. Passing a message onward therefore did more than preserve its original content. It changed what later recipients encountered and how the risk was represented. Moussaïd et al. (2017) examined a different aspect of transmission: the reach and speed with which quantitative judgments propagated. Propagation depended not only on the network but also on how respondents used others' judgments and learned about their competence. Distortion of judgments and the time needed to learn about others limited their spread. That work asks how influence travels. The present chain analysis asks whether a judgment should continue to travel as a committed answer when a successor doubts it but cannot confidently supply a replacement.

The value of passing a solution onward also depends on the problem being solved. Seyed Yahosseini and Moussaïd (2020) compared transmission chains with groups of independent solvers. Chains performed better on relatively smooth problems, whereas independent search was preferable on rugged problems. An inherited solution could provide a useful basis for improvement in one environment without being the best starting point in another. Sequential accumulation is therefore not beneficial independently of the way successors use what they inherit.

These findings concern different objects of transmission. A risk message is not a binary answer, and improving a multidimensional solution is not equivalent to replacing a diagnosis. The present analysis does not demonstrate that an undecided state would improve each of those processes. Its contribution is narrower: a binary update rule can distinguish between offering a new answer and declining to preserve the current one as settled.

That distinction did not improve overall diagnostic accuracy. The benefit was confined to the nine cases in which the original majority was wrong, and even there accuracy remained below chance and below the initial individual accuracy. Moreover, the locked rule did not increase the probability of a committed correct answer. It improved expected final accuracy only when undecided states were resolved at chance. The result shows a way to mitigate the continued transmission of errors, not a way to generate the knowledge needed to replace them with correct diagnoses.

5.4 The value of additional judgments depends on the task and the crowd

The SP method makes use of a distinctive source of information. Respondents report not only what they believe but also how common they expect their answer to be. An answer can then be informative because it is more prevalent than those expectations suggest, even if it is not the majority answer. Prelec et al. (2017) showed how this discrepancy can reveal knowledge that would otherwise be missed by aggregation.

Subsequent findings have qualified when this additional report improves a collective judgment. Lee et al. (2018) applied SP to NFL forecasting. Their corrected analysis showed that SP among respondents who rated themselves as extremely knowledgeable did not outperform those respondents' modal prediction. This finding does not imply that SP lacks value. It shows that its advantage over ordinary aggregation could not be assumed in that forecasting application (Lee et al., 2018, and accompanying corrigendum). Rutchick et al. (2020) obtained a more favorable, although still conditional, pattern across studies of football, elections, and basketball. SP showed modest advantages when applied to knowledgeable respondents. Objective knowledge assessments were important in identifying these respondents; selfreported expertise did not provide the same basis for selecting useful crowds. Their findings indicate that forecasting need not be inherently unsuitable for SP, while emphasizing the importance of who provides both the answers and the predictions about others.

Face matching supplies a different boundary condition. Across three tests, Kramer and Javorková (2025) found that SP performed substantially worse than majority voting and confidence-weighted aggregation. Confidence weighting itself did not consistently improve on an ordinary majority. In that setting, neither eliciting a prediction about others nor retaining more detailed differences between confidence ratings guaranteed an advantage.

These comparisons suggest a distinction between knowing an answer and knowing how that answer is distributed across a population. SP draws on both. Confidence filtering does not ask respondents to estimate the distribution of other people's answers. It asks whether their confidence in their own answer reaches a threshold. The difference could matter when confidence distinguishes useful from unhelpful responses more effectively than predictions about the crowd reveal minority knowledge. That remains a possible explanation, rather than a mechanism demonstrated by the present analyses.

Study 3 found room to improve the general-knowledge vote using confidence without relying on predictions about others. The advantage over SP and ordinary majority voting extended across a broad range of thresholds, although the highest observed accuracy was still 61%. The NFL analysis did not reproduce that pattern. Mild filtering remained competitive, but stricter filtering performed worse than both benchmarks.

The contrast does not establish that simple procedures are generally preferable to more elaborate ones. It identifies settings in which different reports from the same respondents contributed differently to accuracy. General knowledge and NFL forecasting also differed in their participants and confidence scales, not just in whether the answers concerned established facts or future events. The present design cannot separate these differences. Variation in the relationship between confidence and accuracy, the quality of predictions about others, or the extent of shared errors could contribute to the contrasting results. Further comparisons are needed to determine which of these possibilities matters.

5.5 Confidence differs across people and can change through interaction

A common numerical threshold can reflect more than uncertainty about the current answer. It can also reflect how a person generally uses the response scale. A respondent who usually reports high confidence may contribute frequently, while a respondent who uses the scale conservatively may be excluded more often. That difference in participation need not correspond to an equally large difference in accuracy.

Future research can compare a common threshold with rules based on each respondent's relative confidence across their answers. One option would be to exclude the answers a person rates lowest within their own set of judgments. The comparison would help distinguish the contribution of response-specific uncertainty from the effect of a generally high or low reporting style. Other adjustments for scale use could be evaluated for the same purpose, rather than assuming that a common value means the same thing for every respondent.

The use of several confidence scales in the present datasets does not itself resolve this measurement issue. The findings show how filtering performed with the confidence reports recorded in those studies. They do not show that changing the scale while holding the task constant would leave the result unchanged. A direct comparison of elicitation formats, with task and participant conditions held constant, is needed to assess that possibility.

Confidence is also shaped by interaction. Bang et al. (2017) showed that people can align their expressed confidence with a partner's. Confidence matching can support good collective decisions when partners have similar ability, but it can impair them when their abilities differ. A confidence value expressed during interaction is therefore not necessarily the same report the person would have supplied independently. Pescetelli and Yeung (2022) examined spontaneous confidence alignment between dyad members. Participants moved their average confidence toward one another even when they were not required to make a joint decision. The alignment persisted beyond the interaction period and was associated with benefits to judgment accuracy. Shared ways of expressing confidence can thus emerge without being adopted explicitly as a strategy for collective choice.

Confidence can further affect the process by which people learn whom to trust. Pescetelli and Yeung (2021) examined how agreement with an advisor is evaluated in light of confidence in one's own initial judgment. When judgments were independent, this process could support learning about advisor accuracy without objective feedback. When judgments and errors were correlated, trust could become distorted. Confidence therefore helps determine not only the influence assigned to a current answer but also the advisors from whom later answers are sought or accepted.

These findings raise a practical question for repeated filtering. If participants adjust their confidence through interaction, a fixed threshold may retain different responses over time even when the numerical rule remains unchanged. A shift could reflect learning, a change in reporting style, or both. Following answers and confidence across repeated exchanges would make it possible to examine how alignment and trust change the responses that are retained or excluded. In a chain, the same processes could change when an inherited answer is suspended. The present calculations hold recorded judgments fixed and do not model these feedback effects.

5.6 Confidence ranges may reveal uncertainty that point ratings miss

The present analyses used one confidence value for each answer. That value may not fully express how settled the confidence judgment is. A respondent who reports 70% might regard values close to 70% as plausible and other values as unlikely. Another respondent might use 70% to summarize a much less definite assessment. Treating both reports identically leaves this difference unexpressed.

Research on interval estimates demonstrates why richer response formats deserve consideration without assuming that they solve the problem of overconfidence. Soll and Klayman (2004) examined interval estimates of uncertain quantities and showed that overconfidence depended on how the intervals were elicited. Their findings did not establish that allowing a range eliminates miscalibration. They showed that the procedure used to express uncertainty matters.

Related work has addressed imprecision in subjective probabilities themselves. Giustinelli et al. (2022) found that respondents may think of an event's probability as a range rather than a single number. Ellerby et al. (2022) examined response formats that allow a person to specify both a position and a width on a scale. Such formats can capture uncertainty or ambiguity that a point response cannot directly represent.

These approaches motivate a corresponding distinction for confidence in one's own answer. A report of "between 68% and 72%" differs from "somewhere between 50% and 90%," even though either might otherwise be reduced to 70%. Here, the range concerns the confidence value the person regards as plausible, rather than an interval intended to contain an unknown quantity. Its width could matter when deciding whether an answer is sufficiently settled to contribute. Future studies can test whether that width adds useful information beyond the central confidence level.

Eliciting a range must also be distinguished from simply measuring a range that was already present. The act of asking about confidence can change the underlying judgment. Double et al. (2026) found that confidence elicitation increased extremity in subjective evaluations but reduced extremity in an objective date-estimation task. The direction differed across tasks, but both results caution against treating confidence measurement as an entirely passive observation.

A range-based measure consequently raises two separate questions. Does it give respondents a more adequate way to express uncertainty they already experience? And does constructing a range change their awareness of uncertainty, thereby changing whether they are willing to commit to an answer? The second possibility concerns uncertainty induced or made salient by the measurement procedure itself. Asking for a single value and asking for a range could differ not only in what they record but also in how they lead a person to represent the judgment.

Nothing in the present data establishes that effect. It is a direction for new research suggested by the distinction between being uncertain and being required to issue a definite response. Understanding that transition could help explain which judgments a group should filter and how an answer comes to be regarded as settled in the first place.

5.7 Scope of the evidence and the treatment of unresolved outcomes

The present studies evaluate specified procedures applied to existing responses. They do not observe people choosing whether to abstain, reacting to an inherited answer, or deciding how to respond to "?". Learning another person's answer could change a respondent's own answer and confidence. The same is true of learning one's position in a group or chain. Experiments in which participants actually use these procedures are needed to establish how those behavioral changes affect their performance.

The calculations also remain conditional on the observed datasets. Exact expectations over possible groups or chains describe the consequences of the rules for these response pools; they do not turn the hypothetical groups into independent observations. Thresholds were evaluated within the same data, including those yielding the highest accuracy. Broad effective ranges provide evidence against dependence on one narrowly selected cutoff, but they do not guarantee performance for new respondents or new questions. Likewise, the wrong-majority cases in Study 2 were identified retrospectively using the recorded judgments and correct diagnoses. The analysis did not show how to identify the cases likely to benefit before knowing the answer.

The comparisons are also limited to the alternatives actually evaluated. The simultaneousaggregation results concern ordinary majority voting and, in Study 3, SP. They do not establish that confidence filtering is generally superior to procedures that assign graded weights to confidence. The findings support a coarse use of confidence as a viable possibility in some settings, not the claim that more detailed weighting must be unhelpful.

Finally, the value of an unresolved judgment depends on what follows it. Ties, all-abstention outcomes, and final undecided chain states received half credit. This represents the expected accuracy of random binary resolution, not an empirical claim about what people do when a procedure fails to choose. Keeping these outcomes in the denominator ensured that filtering was not credited only for the items on which it retained enough information to reach a conclusion.

In practice, withholding a judgment might permit further evidence to be obtained, or it might impose a delay that is costly in its own right. Accuracy under random resolution does not represent either consequence. Evaluating a procedure for use therefore requires attention to how often it reaches a definite conclusion and to what happens when it does not. Douven et al.'s (2026) joint consideration of accuracy and decisiveness provides a related basis for this broader evaluation.

5.8 Conclusion

A crowd need not use every available answer in the same way. Excluding low-confidence responses improved majority-vote accuracy across broad threshold ranges in binary-choice inference and general knowledge. The benefit was limited in very small groups, disappeared under overly strict filtering in Study 1, and did not improve ordinary majority voting in NFL forecasting. In transmission chains, allowing uncertain disagreement to suspend an inherited answer reduced overall accuracy but partially mitigated the losses found in wrong-majority cases.

These results locate the value of confidence in the procedure that uses it. A confidence rating need not determine exactly how much an answer should count. It can determine whether that answer contributes at all. In a chain, uncertainty can also provide a reason to stop treating an inherited answer as settled without requiring a respondent to supply a better alternative. Confidence filtering gives these possibilities an explicit place in collective decision making, while leaving their usefulness to be evaluated against the task and the consequences of an unresolved answer.
