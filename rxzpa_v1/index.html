Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. Does Complementarity Depend on the Conversational Context?

At the same time, complementarity varies with acquaintance, relationship duration, conversational content, and interaction goals (Markey & Kurtz, 2006; Moskowitz et al., 2007; Rehman et al., 2017; Slotter et al., 2023). Is Complementarity Associated with Relationship Satisfaction?

Warmth complementarity has been associated with liking, positive affect, task performance, and relationship satisfaction (Halberstadt et al., 2023; Kurzius et al., 2022; Markey et al., 2010; Slotter et al., 2023), consistent with the idea that coordinated warmth enacts mutual responsiveness (Reis et al., 2004). Evidence for dominance complementarity is mixed. Observed indices have often shown weak or null associations with relationship evaluations (Cundiff et al., 2015; Slotter et al., 2023), and dominance levels and trajectories were unrelated to perceived conflict resolution in a recent CAID study (Gibson et al., 2024). In parent-child dyads, control (dominance) complementarity was related to observed cooperation but not to parents' or children's reports of the relationship (Shewark et al., 2022). Thomas (2015), however, found that stronger moment-to-moment dominance reciprocity accompanied higher marital satisfaction.

Once partners' average warmth entered the model, the association held only in interaction with the combination of husbands' and wives' warmth. In parent-adolescent dyads, by contrast, dominance complementarity accompanied more adaptive and less maladaptive parenting, whereas warmth complementarity was largely unrelated to parenting (Meisel et al., 2022). Two further considerations bear on how complementarity should relate to satisfaction. First, Orford (1986) argued that dominance invites submission mainly when interactions are warm, so any association between dominance reciprocity and satisfaction could depend on how warm a couple is. Second, a warmth correspondence index pools two patterns that the couples literature treats as opposites: partners becoming warmer together and partners becoming colder together. The latter resembles negative reciprocity, in which one partner's negativity is followed by the other's, a marker of distressed relationships (Gottman & Levenson, 1992; Ramos Salazar, 2015). A pooled index could therefore obscure associations that run in opposite directions, and it is at best an INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY indirect indicator of mutual responsiveness. We examined how complementarity is related to relationship satisfaction, expecting a positive association for moment-to-moment warmth complementarity and leaving dominance associations open.

The Present Study

We studied 137 Swiss romantic couples who completed four videotaped, 5-min conversations coded with CAID. Couples discussed the best and worst aspects of their relationship and completed two persuasion conversations concerning a standing dietary disagreement. In the persuasion conversations, each partner was assigned the persuasion role in turn. Both partners also reported relationship satisfaction. Because the study was not preregistered, we present theory-guided research questions (RQs) rather than confirmatory hypotheses.

For consistency (RQ1), we expected dominance complementarity to show stronger crossconversation consistency than warmth complementarity, replicating the pattern reported by Thomas (2015), and used generalizability analyses to distinguish stable couple differences from conversation and observer effects. For context (RQ2), we expected warmth complementarity to be strongest during the positive relationship conversation and dominance complementarity to be strongest when influence was contested (RQ2a). We also expected assigned persuasion roles to reverse which partner was more dominant, without predicting whether role assignment would change the strength of dominance complementarity itself (RQ2b). For satisfaction (RQ3), we expected stronger moment-to-moment warmth complementarity to be associated with greater relationship satisfaction and treated dominance associations as open questions. Exploratory analyses extended RQ3. One tested whether warmth moderates the dominance association, as Orford's (1986) proposal implies, and another separated warmth correspondence in warm INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY moments from correspondence in cold moments. Two further analyses asked whether variability in warmth complementarity relates to satisfaction and whether reciprocity predicts how fully the dominance asymmetry reverses under assigned roles. Finally, continuous ratings of dominance could in principle track the alternation of speaking turns rather than the regulation of influence (Sadler et al., 2009). Because speaking turns typically last seconds rather than minutes, reciprocity produced by turn-taking alone would appear as fast fluctuations in the ratings, so we examined the timescales at which dominance reciprocity operates before interpreting it.

Couples were recruited through online and community advertisements in the Zürich region. Eligibility required that both partners were adults in a romantic relationship, regularly shared meals, and differed in their habitual consumption of meat or other animal products.

Procedure

Couples attended a single laboratory session, provided written informed consent, and completed four video-recorded 5-min conversations while alone in the laboratory. Conversation order was randomized. Topics included the best aspects of the relationship, the most challenging aspects of the relationship, a discussion in which the lower-animal-consuming partner argued that the other partner should eat less meat, and a second discussion in which the higher-animalconsuming partner argued that the other partner should eat more meat. Participants were instructed to interact as naturally as they would at home. The two persuasion conversations therefore experimentally manipulated which partner occupied the persuasion role while maintaining an otherwise observational, within-couple design. All materials were administered in German. Participants were compensated CHF 120 for completing the full Partner Diet Study protocol.

Observed Interpersonal Behavior

Interactions were coded using the Continuous Assessment of Interpersonal Dynamics (CAID; Lizdek et al., 2012; Sadler et al., 2009) implemented in DARMA (Girard & Wright, 2018). Trained observers continuously rated one partner at a time on warmth and dominance by moving a joystick within the interpersonal circumplex. Ratings ranged from -1,000 to +1,000 and were sampled every 0.5 s. Ten observers completed standardized CAID training following published procedures (Fox et al., 2021; Ramer et al., 2023). Consistent with the standard CAID protocol (Sadler et al., 2009; see also Halberstadt & Pincus, 2025), training defined dominance as the regulation of influence rather than as speech, and observers tracked variation in dominance within as well as across speaking turns. Directing the conversation, asserting a position, yielding, and remaining silent during a lull were therefore rated for their interpersonal meaning rather than for who was speaking. Each recording was coded by four observers, except for one recording coded by three. The first and last 10 s of each recording were removed to minimize instruction lag and recording artifacts. Indices computed with 5-s trimming correlated .99 with those used here, and the satisfaction estimates for the moment-to-moment indices were essentially unchanged (Section S2).

Momentary composite series were calculated by averaging observer ratings at each time point, requiring at least two observer streams. Twenty-three observer streams (0.5%) whose ratings, after linear trends were removed, showed negative average agreement with the remaining observers of the same recording were excluded, consistent with screening in previous CAID work, although our criterion was more conservative, excluding only clearly aberrant streams. Without these exclusions, indices correlated .97 or higher with the screened indices and satisfaction estimates were essentially unchanged (Section S2). Following exclusions, 1,071 recordings were represented by four observer streams, 22 by three streams, and one by two streams. Mean coefficient alpha across recordings was .79 for dominance and .40 for warmth, with lower observer agreement for warmth consistent with previous CAID studies. Agreement for dominance was stable across the months of coding, whereas agreement for warmth was lower for recordings coded later in the period (Section S7). The consequences of this level of INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY agreement for the reliability of the complementarity indices were evaluated directly through the generalizability analyses.

For moment-to-moment analyses, each composite time series was linearly detrended within person and conversation so that shared linear trends could not inflate the moment-tomoment indices. Means and within-conversation variability were calculated from the trimmed, undetrended series. Four complementarity indices were computed for each couple and conversation (Table 1): moment-to-moment warmth complementarity (correspondence), moment-to-moment dominance complementarity (reciprocity), mean-level warmth complementarity, and mean-level dominance complementarity. Signed differences in partners' average warmth and dominance were retained as indicators of role asymmetry (which partner was warmer or more dominant); because persuasion roles were experimentally assigned, shifts in dominance asymmetry between these conversations reflect the role manipulation.

Analytic Strategy

Analyses followed the three research questions. Moment-to-moment complementarity correlations were Fisher z-transformed whenever they were averaged across conversations or entered into statistical models because dominance complementarity correlations were substantially skewed. The consistency and generalizability analyses (Table 2, Section S4) used the untransformed correlations; the consistency statistics were similar on the Fisher z scale (Section S2). Descriptive statistics reported by conversation are presented as raw correlations for ease of interpretation. Missing data were minimal. Four participants lacked baseline questionnaire data, one laboratory conversation was not recorded, one conversation ended early (137 s) but was retained, and six couples could not be classified unambiguously into lowerversus higher-animal-consuming roles for analyses requiring that distinction. One participant did not complete the relationship satisfaction questionnaire, leaving 269 participants in the satisfaction analyses. All models used the maximum available data.

To address RQ1, we evaluated the consistency of each dyad-level behavioral index across conversations using cross-conversation correlations, coefficient alpha, and the intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC(2,k), from a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement model (McGraw & Wong, 1996). We also conducted generalizability and decision-study analyses (Cranford et al., 2006) on the observer-level indices (each index recomputed from a single observer's ratings; see Section S4) to partition variance attributable to couples, conversations, observers, and their interactions. Complete variance-component estimates are reported in the supplemental materials (Section S4).

To address RQ2, we examined contextual differences in complementarity using linear mixed-effects models with conversation type as a fixed effect and random intercepts for couples.

To address RQ3, relationship satisfaction was regressed separately on each standardized dyad-level complementarity index using mixed-effects models with random intercepts for couples. Complementarity indices were averaged across conversations because the aggregate demonstrated substantially greater reliability than any single-conversation estimate, particularly for warmth complementarity. All indices were coded so that higher values reflected greater complementarity. Correlations among complementarity indices and satisfaction are reported in the supplemental materials (Section S5).

Across the four satisfaction models, p values were adjusted using Holm's procedure. We additionally conducted a permutation test (1,000 permutations) evaluating whether the average absolute association between complementarity and couple-level relationship satisfaction exceeded chance expectations. As a benchmark for spurious complementarity, moment-tomoment indices were recomputed for 1,000 randomly re-paired pseudo-couples (Gurtman, 2001; Markey et al., 2010; Sadler et al., 2009). To examine whether dominance reciprocity was concentrated in rapid fluctuations of the kind that turn exchange would produce, we also partitioned each couple's moment-to-moment dominance correlation by timescale through the co-spectrum (Sadler et al., 2009; Warner, 1998; Section S10). This decomposition attributes the correlation exactly to cycles of different lengths. This check and the four analyses described next were added after the primary analyses and are exploratory.

Four exploratory analyses extended RQ3 (Sections S11 to S14). To test Orford's (1986) proposal, we computed dominance reciprocity within moments when both partners were warm, INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY both were cold, or one was warm and one cold. We also tested warmth as a moderator of the association between reciprocity and satisfaction. To separate coordinated warmth from coordinated coldness, we computed warmth correspondence within warm and within cold moments, along with the share of each conversation in which both partners were on the cold side of the circumplex. We indexed variability in warmth complementarity across conversations by its standard deviation. Finally, we related reciprocity in the two relationship conversations to how fully the dominance asymmetry reversed between the persuasion conversations, an outcome of the conversation itself.

All analyses were conducted in R 4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026) using lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017), and psych (Revelle, 2026). Statistical significance was evaluated at α = .05 using two-tailed tests, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported throughout, and all models converged without warnings.

Discussion

Interpersonal theory proposes that social behavior reflects an ongoing process of mutual influence in which each person's behavior shapes and is shaped by the other (Carson, 1969; Kiesler, 1983; Sadler et al., 2009). Complementarity is one of the defining principles of these exchanges, but whether it is a stable characteristic of couples or a process that changes from one interaction to the next has rarely been tested. We examined it across four conversations that differed in emotional tone and interpersonal demands. The answer depended on the dimension.

Dominance complementarity generalized across conversations, whereas warmth complementarity varied systematically with conversational context. Only warmth complementarity was associated with relationship satisfaction. Mean-level indices showed a different pattern of consistency and did not predict satisfaction, so average interpersonal position and moment-to-moment coordination capture different aspects of how couples interact.

Interpersonal complementarity is both a stable dyadic characteristic and a situational process, and the balance between the two depends on the interpersonal dimension. One possible reason for this difference is that dominance and warmth solve different interpersonal problems.

Reciprocal regulation of influence may provide relationships with a stable organizational structure that allows partners to negotiate decisions efficiently across situations. In contrast, communal behavior may function primarily to communicate responsiveness to immediate relational needs, so that sensitivity to context, rather than consistency, may be its characteristic form. Although speculative, this perspective suggests that different interpersonal dimensions may have evolved to balance stability and flexibility in different ways.

One implication of these findings is that stable interactional tendencies may exist at the level of the relationship rather than solely within individuals. Personality psychology has largely moved beyond asking whether behavior is stable across situations. Instead, contemporary perspectives emphasize that individuals display characteristic patterns of behavior across varying contexts, with stability emerging from recurring patterns rather than identical actions (Fleeson, 2001; Fleeson & Noftle, 2008). The same framework fits dyads. Couples differed reliably in the extent to which they exhibited reciprocal exchanges of dominance and submission, and these differences generalized across conversations despite substantial changes in the topics discussed and the roles partners occupied. Rather than behaving identically across situations, couples appeared to maintain characteristic ways of organizing influence within their interactions.

Dominance complementarity may be an interactional analogue of a personality trait, a behavioral signature that recurs across situations.

The persuasion conversations make this point directly. During these discussions, partners explicitly exchanged persuasion roles, producing large shifts in who occupied the dominant interpersonal position. Dominance complementarity nevertheless remained consistent. Studies of complementarity have rarely separated interpersonal position from interpersonal process.

Individuals may move to very different locations within interpersonal space depending on situational demands, yet still coordinate their behavior according to stable interpersonal principles. The present findings suggest that reciprocal influence may represent one such principle. Whether one partner led because they possessed greater expertise, held the assigned persuasion role, or simply initiated discussion, the broader process through which partners coordinated dominance and submission remained comparatively stable. Complementarity therefore reflects more than similarity in observable behavior; it captures how partners regulate influence across situations.

The persuasion conversations also bear on Orford's (1986) proposal that hostile dominance provokes dominance rather than submission. Reciprocity was strongest in the persuasion conversations, which were also the least warm, and within conversations it was not significantly weaker during mutually cold moments than during mutually warm ones. As Thomas (2015) noted, Orford's review drew largely on complementarity aggregated over whole interactions, and the moment-level evidence here does not show the breakdown of reciprocity he described. Because mutually cold moments occupied only a small fraction of these conversations, however, the test has limited power, and couples whose interactions tend to be cold may still show the pattern he proposed.

Perhaps the most theoretically informative finding concerned warmth complementarity.

Generalizability analyses suggested that part of the variability in warmth complementarity arose from systematic interactions between particular couples and particular conversational contexts, although the variance attributable to observers and to residual error was larger. The same pattern appeared in earlier CAID generalizability studies, where the couple × conversation component for warmth was two to four times the couple component (Fox et al., 2021; Ramer et al., 2023; Table S10). These findings show both that warmth and warmth complementarity tend to be less reliable in CAID data and that couples reliably differed in how their warmth coordination changed across situations. Substantively, the results suggest that communal coordination is more context-sensitive than dominance reciprocity. Whereas reciprocal influence may constitute a relatively stable organizational feature of dyadic interaction across situations, warmth coordination appears to shift with relational demands.

The conversation effects fit this account. Warmth complementarity was highest when partners discussed the best aspects of their relationship and lowest during the persuasion conversations. These contexts differed in emotional valence and in the goals they imposed.

Positive relationship discussions encouraged mutual validation and shared emotional engagement, whereas persuasion required partners to advocate competing positions while maintaining the relationship. Successful interpersonal functioning may therefore involve the capacity to adjust communal behavior appropriately as conversational demands change. Whether such variability is adaptive remains an open question. Couples whose warmth complementarity varied more across conversations were no more satisfied than couples whose complementarity was steadier (Section S13).

The satisfaction findings point the same way. Only moment-to-moment warmth complementarity predicted satisfaction, and this association remained after controlling for couples' average warmth. Average warmth reflects how positively partners behave toward one another overall, whereas warmth complementarity reflects how effectively partners coordinate those behaviors over time. Satisfaction was associated with how closely partners coordinated warmth, over and above how warm they were. Relationship research has long treated responsiveness and coordinated emotional exchange as central to satisfying relationships (Gottman & Levenson, 1992; Reis et al., 2004). Because the design was cross-sectional, the direction of this association remains open; reversed-direction models, in which couple-mean satisfaction predicted complementarity, produced the same warmth-satisfaction association (Section S6).

No comparable association emerged for dominance complementarity. In supplementary analyses, couples differed reliably and substantially in reciprocity (between-couple SD = .14; INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY aggregate values from -.97 to -.19), so the null cannot be attributed to a lack of variation. The confidence interval for the satisfaction association (B = 0.00, 95% CI [-0.12, 0.13]) excludes an association as large as the one observed for warmth. Nor did this variation predict satisfaction conditional on warmth or how fully the dominance asymmetry reversed under assigned roles.

The published record is divided between null associations (Cundiff et al., 2015; Slotter et al., 2023) and the positive association reported by Thomas (2015), which held only in interaction with husbands' and wives' warmth; no interaction with couples' average warmth emerged here.

One possible explanation is that reciprocal influence is primarily an organizational feature of interaction rather than a determinant of relationship quality. Most couples may settle into a stable way of negotiating influence, and many different arrangements may work equally well. If so, couples differ in the form their coordination takes rather than in how well it works.

Whether reciprocity also carries evaluative weight may depend on the power difference between partners. In parent-adolescent discussions, where that difference is built in, dominance complementarity accompanied more adaptive parenting while warmth complementarity was largely unrelated to it (Meisel et al., 2022). In parent-child dyads, dominance complementarity was likewise related to observed cooperation during the task but not to reports of the relationship (Shewark et al., 2022). In couples the pattern is reversed, and reciprocity may describe how influence is organized without indicating how well. If so, reciprocity should be tied more closely to outcomes of the conversation itself than to global evaluations of the relationship. Such outcomes include how efficiently partners reach decisions, how responsive each partner perceives the other to be, and whether contested influence escalates into demand and withdrawal when interactions turn cold (Christensen & Heavey, 1990; Thomas, 2015). In contrast, communal coordination may more directly determine whether partners experience interactions as supportive, validating, and emotionally rewarding. Interpersonal theory has long held that warmth is tied to relationship satisfaction whereas dominance structures interaction (Carson, 1969; Leary, 1957). The present findings show that the same division holds for the temporal coordination of behavior, not just for mean levels, and they identify the kinds of outcomes against which the reliable variation in reciprocity should next be tested.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the sample consisted primarily of young, German-speaking couples who differed in animal-product consumption and were willing to participate in an intensive observational study. Although the diversity of conversation topics increases confidence that the findings are not specific to a single interaction, replication in older couples, couples experiencing greater relationship distress, and more culturally diverse samples will be important. In addition, dietary role was confounded with gender (85.3% of lower-animal-consuming partners were women), so role-specific effects cannot be separated from gender effects. Interpersonal coordination is also likely to be shaped by shared INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY history, by cultural norms regarding emotional expression and power, and by relationship duration, which was not assessed here. Duration may matter more for dominance than for warmth: in 180 couples, dominance complementarity rose steeply over the first years of a relationship and then leveled off, whereas warmth complementarity did not change with duration (Slotter et al., 2023).

Second, the laboratory conversations represent only a small sample of couples' everyday interactions. They differed substantially in interpersonal demands and included an experimentally manipulated disagreement, but everyday relationships unfold across a far broader range of situations than a laboratory can recreate. Recent developments in ambulatory assessment, passive sensing, and repeated observational methods provide an opportunity to examine complementarity across hundreds or even thousands of naturally occurring interactions.

Such designs would estimate dyadic signatures more precisely and identify the contexts that strengthen or weaken coordination. Practically, researchers interested in dominance complementarity may obtain usable estimates from a single well-sampled interaction (ICC = .45 for one conversation vs. .76 for four), whereas assessing warmth complementarity requires sampling multiple interpersonal contexts.

Third, although the present findings demonstrate that complementarity varies systematically across contexts, they do not explain why this occurs. The observed changes may reflect characteristics of the situation, stable individual differences, properties of the relationship, or interactions among all three. For example, some couples may become more interpersonally coordinated under conditions of stress, whereas others may lose coordination. Similarly, personality traits, attachment styles, conflict resolution strategies, or relationship goals may INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY influence both average levels of complementarity and the extent to which complementarity changes across situations. Future research should examine these potential moderators directly.

Fourth, there are two potential limitations related to the dominance findings. The first is that dominance reciprocity may partly reflect the alternation of speaking turns. The CAID protocol treats interrupting, holding the floor, and declining to speak as expressions of influence (Lizdek et al., 2012; Sadler et al., 2009), but who speaks when also depends on politeness, conversational habits, and the task instructions. Speaking turns were not coded in these data, so we examined how much of the reciprocity lay at each timescale. Only 5% of the dominance correlation lay in cycles of 10 s or shorter, so rapid turn exchange does not account for the findings. Slower alternation is not excluded, however: single turns last roughly 3 to 45 s (Buder, 1996), and the amount each partner talks alternates over cycles of 1 to 2 min (Warner, 1996) that overlap the slow cycles carrying most of the reciprocity (Sadler et al., 2009). Two studies have tested this directly. Cappella (1996) removed each person's floor holding (the time spent keeping the speaking turn) from time-series models of speaking-role behaviors such as vocalizing, gesturing, and looking away, and partners still shifted toward listening as the other took up speaking. Kurzius et al. (2022) coded who was speaking alongside 10-s status ratings and found that covarying speech reduced status complementarity from -.47 to -.20 but did not remove it. A comparable study with CAID ratings has not, to our knowledge, been published.

The second concern is convergent validity. Ross et al. (2017) related CAID ratings to affect codes from the Specific Affect Coding System in 67 couples. Predicted associations were supported more often for warmth than for dominance, and dominance showed more unexpected associations; the Domineering code, for example, was negatively rather than positively associated with the same person's CAID dominance. The two systems were designed to measure INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY different constructs, which may contribute to these discrepancies. Validating CAID dominance against independent, construct-matched measures of momentary influence, alongside direct coding of speech and silence, therefore remains a priority for the method.

Fifth, the warmth complementarity index pools correspondence on the warm side of the circumplex with correspondence on the cold side. The latter resembles negative reciprocity, a marker of distress in the couples literature (Gottman & Levenson, 1992; Ramos Salazar, 2015).

In the present sample the pooled index was in practice a warm-side index. Partners were mutually cold during 6% or less of each conversation type on average, and correspondence within cold moments could be estimated for only 32 couples. Samples with more distressed couples, or tasks that reliably elicit sustained cold behavior, will be needed to establish whether coordinated coldness relates to satisfaction in the opposite direction, as the negative reciprocity literature predicts. Such samples would also show whether reciprocity on the cold side follows Orford's (1986) predictions.

The next step is to model interpersonal dynamics as a property of relationships rather than only to estimate average levels of complementarity. Contemporary personality theory increasingly conceptualizes personality as patterns of behavior expressed across situations rather than as static average tendencies (Kuper et al., 2024). The same approach can be applied to dyads. Relationships can be described by average behavior and by stable signatures of how partners regulate influence, coordinate affiliation, and adjust to changing contexts. Such signatures could ultimately provide a richer description of relationship functioning than either self-report questionnaires or mean behavioral scores alone.

The same questions arise outside romantic relationships. Complementarity has been studied in psychotherapy, friendships, parent-child interactions, and work teams, yet these INTERPERSONAL COMPLEMENTARITY literatures have generally focused on whether complementarity occurs rather than whether it represents a stable characteristic of particular dyads. Stable interactional tendencies and systematic contextual adaptation are unlikely to be unique to romantic couples and may be a general property of enduring relationships. Asking when coordination reflects stable dyadic organization and when it reflects adaptation to the situation would give these literatures a common question.

Conclusion

Personality psychologists spent decades debating whether behavior reflects enduring dispositions or situational influences. That debate has largely been settled for individuals, but the parallel question for interpersonal processes, whether complementarity is a stable property of relationships or arises anew in each interaction, has remained open.
