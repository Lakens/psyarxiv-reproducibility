What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Social presence, particularly awareness of being observed by a tutor, may enhance learners' engagement and attention during foreign language practice, but its effects on learning and associated neural activity remain poorly understood. We investigated whether learners' (n = 32) awareness of being observed by a tutor influences foreign speech learning in a short (11-min) video-mediated listen-and-repeat training for Mandarin words. In the online group, learners knew the tutor could see them, whereas in the offline group, they knew the tutor could neither see nor hear them. Tone production was assessed before, during, and after training, and tone identification before and after training. Electroencephalography and autonomic activity were recorded during training. Tone production accuracy improved from pre-to post-training in the online group only, whereas tone identification improved similarly in both groups.

Posterior alpha power increased during training in the offline group but did not change significantly in the online group. Together, these findings suggest that awareness of being observed by a tutor may support foreign language speech production learning in video-mediated settings. The accompanying differences in posterior alpha activity further indicate that the social context of learning is associated with differences in neural activity during practice.

1 Introduction

Adult foreign speech learning is strongly shaped by prior language experience. Nonnative contrasts can be particularly difficult to acquire when the acoustic dimensions that distinguish them are not used to distinguish word meaning, or are used differently, in the learner's first language (Best, 1995; Flege, 1995). Mandarin lexical tone provides a useful model for examining how adults learn non-native phonological contrasts (e.g., Gandour, 1983). In Mandarin, lexical identity depends on pitch height and dynamic fundamental-frequency (F0) contours, dimensions that do not distinguish word meaning in many non-tonal languages (Wang et al., 1999; Antoniou & Chin, 2018).

Experimental studies have shown that training can improve non-native tone perception in a short time (for Mandarin tone, e.g., Jiang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Wang et al., 1999) and learners' ability to use pitch patterns for lexical identification (Wong & Perrachione, 2007; Chandrasekaran et al., 2010), although outcomes vary substantially across training paradigms and learning contexts (for a review, see Antoniou & Chin, 2018). This motivates examining whether features of the learning context influence learning outcomes. Social context is one such feature. Social-neurocognitive accounts propose that learning with a social partner engages attentional, motivational, and socialcognitive processes that shape how new linguistic information is encoded (Verga & Kotz, 2013 , 2017; Li & Jeong, 2020). Learning foreign words through simulated social interaction also recruits brain regions associated with social cognition more strongly than learning through translation (Jeong et al., 2021). In speech sound learning, practising Mandarin tones alongside a peer even without instructed interaction boosted early learning: participants discriminated the tones better at the beginning of training than those who practised alone, although the learners practising alone caught up as training progressed (Li et al., 2026). This suggests that the mere presence of another person might initially improve learning. Although social aspects can support language learning, it remains unclear which specific mechanisms contribute to learning. Recent work has highlighted multimodal information, bodily engagement, and social feedback as potentially important factors, while emphasising the need for further research to disentangle their respective contributions (Zappa et al., 2025). One potentially relevant aspect is learners' awareness of being observed by another person during learning.

In social cognition studies, being the focus of another person's attention, as reflected in direct eye contact with a live person, can direct attention towards the self and elicit selfreferential processing (Conty et al., 2016; Hietanen & Hietanen, 2017). Importantly, these effects depend on the belief that one is being seen by another person. When participants believed that another person could see them, direct gaze elicited stronger autonomic responses and higher public self-awareness than averted gaze (Myllyneva & Hietanen, 2015). These effects were not observed when participants believed that the other person could not see them (Myllyneva & Hietanen, 2015). A follow-up study showed that physiological responses to eye contact additionally required seeing the other person, whereas public self-awareness depended on the belief of being seen (Myllyneva & Hietanen, 2016). Eye contact also increases autonomic arousal in video calls, but not when viewing a pre-recorded video of the same person (Hietanen et al., 2020). Audience effects, referring to changes in behaviour when people believe they are being observed, have been linked to reputation management (Hamilton & Lind, 2016). However, it remains unclear whether and how being observed influences learning processes, particularly in language learning contexts. Knowing that a tutor can observe one's performance may, for example, engage self-referential processes, such as self-awareness and monitoring of one's own performance.

Online and offline learning provide a useful contrast for examining the role of awareness of being observed. In offline learning based on recorded materials, learners can see the instructor, but the instructor cannot see them. In synchronous online learning, the tutor and learner can typically see each other. However, it remains unknown whether awareness of being observed by a tutor influences adult foreign-language speech learning, including both speech production and speech perception.

The present study addressed this question using video-mediated listen-and-repeat training of Mandarin tones. The training focused on tone production, while auditory tone identification was assessed to examine whether learning transferred to perception.

In the online group, the tutor could see the learner via the video connection, whereas in the offline group, the tutor could not see the learner, as in a recorded learning situation.

The tutor could neither hear the learners nor provide feedback in either group, thereby minimising the possibility that the tutor's behaviour would be influenced by the learners' speech production. If awareness of being observed engages self-awareness and self-monitoring, these processes may be particularly relevant during training, when learners produce speech knowing that the tutor can see them. Electroencephalography (EEG) recorded during training allows this to be examined. Posterior alpha oscillations index the allocation of attention between external sensory input and internal processing (Klimesch et al., 2007; Jensen & Mazaheri, 2010; Foxe & Snyder, 2011). Alpha power decreases when attention is directed to task-relevant external input and increases with the inhibition of irrelevant input and with internally directed attention (Cooper et al., 2003; Benedek et al., 2014). Alpha-band activity has also been linked to self-referential processing (Knyazev, 2013), and it tends to increase with time on task (Boksem et al., 2005). The primary outcome in the present study was posterior alpha power following each spoken word produced by the learner during the listen-and-repeat task, examined across the three training blocks (three successive parts of the training, in each of which all training items were practised once). Cardiac and electrodermal activity, which index autonomic arousal and respond to direct gaze in video calls (Task Force, 1996; Boucsein, 2012; Hietanen et al., 2020), were recorded as complementary measures. Because learners repeated each word immediately after the tutor, their productions during training could be supported by short-term auditory memory and were therefore not used as measures of learning outcomes. Instead, learning was assessed using two behavioural tests administered separately from the training: tone production based on a written word accompanied by a tone mark, without an auditory model, and auditory tone identification.

We hypothesised that, if awareness of being observed influences learners' selfawareness and monitoring of their own performance, posterior alpha power would follow different trajectories across training blocks in the two groups. Self-referential processing has been associated with higher alpha power, but how such an effect would develop over repeated practice is unclear; this hypothesis was therefore tested as a Group × Block interaction without a directional prediction. Because monitoring of selfproduced speech supports auditory-motor learning (Guenther, 2006), heightened selfmonitoring could also benefit tone production; we therefore expected the change in production accuracy across the four assessments to differ between the groups. Tone identification was expected to improve because listen-and-repeat training also involves listening to the tutor's productions (Li & DeKeyser, 2017), and autonomic activity was examined to test whether being observed increased arousal.

2 Method 2.1 Participants Participants were recruited through university mailing lists, university web pages, notice boards at the University of Jyväskylä, and social media advertisements.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to estimate the sample size required to detect a Group × Session interaction in production performance.

Because the planned analysis used a mixed-effects model, the power calculation was approximated using a repeated-measures ANOVA with a within-between interaction.

Assuming a medium interaction effect size (f = 0.25), α = .05, power of .80, two groups, two measurements (pre-and post-test), a correlation of .50 between repeated measures, and a nonsphericity correction of ε = 1, the analysis indicated a required total sample of 34 participants. The final sample comprised 32 participants.

Eligibility was assessed by self-report during participant screening. Participants were required to be right-handed native speakers of Finnish aged 18-40 years with normal hearing and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Exclusion criteria were prior formal study of a tonal language, such as Mandarin Chinese or Thai; previous participation in studies involving Chinese speech perception, production, or learning; professional musicianship; any history of neurological, psychiatric, or language-related disorders; and use of medication affecting the central nervous system. Brief exposure to tonallanguage environments during travel was permitted when it did not exceed two weeks and did not involve formal tonal-language instruction. Participants were randomly assigned to either the offline or online group. Valid EEG data could not be recorded for two online-group participants because of equipment problems. To match the number of EEG datasets across groups, two additional participants were therefore assigned to the online group. The posterior EEG alpha analysis included 30 participants (offline: 15 participants, 12 women, 3 men, Mage = 25.20 years, SD = 4.75; online: 15 participants, 13 women, 2 men, Mage = 21.20 years, SD = 1.97). The behavioural and autonomic analyses included all 32 participants (offline: 12 women, 3 men; Mage = 25.20 years, SD = 4.75; online: 15 women, 2 men; Mage = 21.35 years, SD = 2.09). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Jyväskylä. All participants provided written informed consent before participation.

2.2 Study design and procedure

The study used a between-participants design with two groups (online and offline; Section 2.3) and repeated measures within participants. The experiment was controlled using PsychoPy (version 2022.2.4) and comprised five main phases (Figure 1): restingstate recording, tone instruction, pre-test assessments, listen-and-repeat training interleaved with two interim production tests, and post-test assessments. For all participants, both the pre-and post-test phases followed the same fixed task order: attentive listening of isolated tones, identification of tones, and tone production. The attentive-listening task was intended for event-related potential (ERP) analysis of responses to Mandarin tones; because of a technical problem in the recording (trial-totrial variability in auditory stimulus onset exceeded 80 ms), these data could not be used and are not reported further (see Supplementary Section S3 for task details).

2.2.1 Resting-state recording

Resting-state activity was recorded before the experimental tasks to provide baseline measures for the autonomic and EEG analyses. The recording comprised two alternating cycles of a 1.5-min eyes-open period and a 1.5-min eyes-closed period.

During the eyes-open periods, participants fixated on a central cross and were instructed to remain still. Only the eyes-open periods were used to derive resting-state baselines because this visual state was more comparable to the training phase. The two eyes-open periods were processed separately and then averaged to obtain one resting-state baseline for each physiological or EEG outcome.

2.2.2 Tone instruction

After the resting-state recording, participants received brief prerecorded audiovisual instruction on the four Mandarin lexical tones. The tones were introduced using the example word /ma/ and schematic pitch contours representing the flat, rising, dipping, and falling tones. Subsequent identification, production, and training tasks focused on the rising, dipping, and falling tones; the flat tone was not included in these tasks.

2.3 Listen-and-repeat training

Training was divided into three blocks. A block was one complete run through the training items: each of the 63 tone-word combinations was practised once, giving 63 trials per block. The three blocks were separated by the two interim production tests (Figure 1). These combinations were formed from 21 words (seven CV [consonantvowel], seven CVV [consonant-diphthong, e.g., gao], and seven CVC [consonantvowel-consonant]; Supplementary Table S1), each combined with the three target Mandarin tones (rising, dipping, and falling). Three of the 15 production-test words (lu, mi, and mai) also occurred in the training materials, whereas none of the identificationtest words did.

Each trial had a fixed duration of 3.5 s, comprising the tutor's production followed by a response interval in which the participant repeated the word. The next trial started automatically, independently of the participant's response. The tutor followed synchronized on-screen prompts specifying the target item and trial timing and therefore did not rely on the participant's behaviour to advance the task. Productions that extended beyond the end of the trial were rare (no more than five trials per participant across the whole training). Participants completed approximately 11 minutes of listen-and-repeat practice in total over the three training blocks.

Training was conducted through a real-time audiovisual communication system.

Participants wore standardized in-ear headphones, and a brief audio check was completed before training to ensure that the tutor's speech was clearly audible for the learner. On each trial, the tutor produced the prompted word in real time, and the participant repeated it aloud immediately afterwards, attempting to imitate both the segmental pronunciation and lexical tone as accurately as possible. These productions were not scored; tone-production learning was assessed with the separate tests described in Section 2.5. Participants in both groups could see and hear the tutor. In the online group, the tutor could see but not hear the participant (as in synchronous online learning), whereas in the offline group, the tutor could neither see nor hear the participant (as with recorded materials). At the start of training, participants were told whether the tutor could see them. The tutor was instructed to maintain a neutral facial expression and to direct their gaze toward the camera, and did not provide corrective, evaluative, verbal, or contingent nonverbal feedback. An experimenter monitored the tutor's behaviour throughout training to ensure adherence to the protocol.

2.4 EEG and autonomic measures during training EEG, electrodermal activity (EDA), electrocardiography (ECG), and vocal activity were recorded continuously and synchronously using a NeurOne system (Bittium Biosignals Ltd., Kuopio, Finland) at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz, with an online bandpass filter of 0.01-250 Hz. EEG was acquired using the EGI HydroCel 128-channel layout. Cz served as the online reference, and electrode impedances were maintained below 50 kΩ in accordance with the system specifications. EDA was recorded from the palmar surface of the left, non-dominant hand using two disposable Ag/AgCl isotonicgel electrodes (EL507; Biopac Systems Inc.) placed over the thenar and hypothenar eminences. The EDA recordings were subsequently used to derive mean skin conductance level (SCL) and skin conductance response (SCR) rate. ECG was recorded using a two-electrode configuration, with one electrode placed below the collarbone and the other on the rib cage below the heart. The ECG recordings were subsequently used to derive mean heart rate and the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) as an index of heart-rate variability. Vocal activity was recorded using a neckmounted three-axis accelerometer positioned over the laryngeal area. The accelerometer signal was used to identify vocal onset and offset and thereby define the EEG analysis windows; it was not treated as an outcome measure.

Posterior alpha power immediately after each of the learner's productions was the primary EEG measure (Section 2.6.2). Mean heart rate, RMSSD, mean SCL, and SCR rate were computed for each training block as complementary measures of autonomic arousal and task-related effort (Task Force, 1996; Boucsein, 2012). Their processing is summarised in Section 2.6.3 and reported in detail in Supplementary Section S1.

2.5 Behavioural measures: tone production and identification tests

Learning outcomes were assessed with two behavioural tests administered outside the listen-and-repeat training blocks, in the same way in both groups. The production test was administered four times: before training (pre-test), after the first and second training blocks (interim assessments 1 and 2), and after the third block as part of the post-test. The identification test was administered before and after training. The posttest followed the training session directly, and no delayed retention test was included.

2.5.1 Production test

The production-test stimulus set comprised 15 words (Supplementary Table S3), each combined with the three target tones, yielding 45 tone-word items in total. The words represented CV, CVV, and CVC structures and were selected to be readily pronounceable by native Finnish speakers. Participants could therefore focus primarily on tone production rather than unfamiliar segmental patterns. On each trial, a word was displayed together with a schematic tone-contour symbol indicating the target rising, dipping, or falling tone. Participants were instructed to pronounce the word aloud as accurately as possible, and the target was not presented auditorily. Each item was produced once, and all responses were recorded with a Zoom H2n Handy Recorder (Zoom Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The same 45 items were used in all four production assessments.

Productions from the four production assessments were evaluated offline by three native Mandarin speakers. Before rating, the raters completed the identification test used in the study, and each achieved an accuracy above 95%. Raters judged the perceived tone category of each production while focusing on lexical tone and disregarding loudness, duration, speaker identity, recording quality, and background or technical noise. For each production, they selected one of four response categories: rising, dipping, falling, or other. The "other" category was used when a production was not perceived as corresponding to any of the three target tones. Raters were blind to participants' group assignment. The final tone category was determined by majority agreement among the three raters. Productions for which all three raters selected different categories were treated as having no majority rating. Across the rated productions, inter-rater agreement was Fleiss' κ = .77.

2.5.2 Identification test

Stimuli for the identification test were generated using the iFLYTEK Xunfei Peiyin online text-to-speech platform and edited in Praat. Four synthetic Mandarin voices were used: two male voices, Tianming and Guanshan, and two female voices, Ling Xiaolan and Xiaoguo. The same synthesis platform has been used to generate Mandarin speech stimuli in previous neurolinguistic studies (Jin et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2023).

The stimulus set comprised words with three phonological structures: CV, CVV, and CVC. For each structure, two words were selected (Supplementary Table S2), and each word was synthesized in each of the four voices and three target tones (rising, dipping, and falling), yielding 72 stimuli in total, with 24 stimuli per tone category. Stimulus durations were matched as closely as possible across the three tone categories to minimize the possibility that participants could rely on durational cues. Mean stimulus durations were 365.08 ms (SD = 58.26) for the rising tone, 363.76 ms (SD = 64.08) for the dipping tone, and 359.49 ms (SD = 63.38) for the falling tone. A one-way betweengroups ANOVA revealed no significant difference in duration across the three tone categories, F(2, 69) = 0.053, p = .948.

Each of the 72 identification stimuli was presented once per test in a fixed order. On each trial, participants listened to a stimulus and identified the perceived tone by pressing one of three response keys (1, 2, or 3) using the index, middle, or ring finger, respectively. The response options for the rising, dipping, and falling tones were displayed on the screen throughout the task, and the mapping between tone categories and response keys was counterbalanced across participants. No feedback was provided.

Reaction time was measured from stimulus onset to the participant's key response.

2.6 Data processing and statistical analysis 2.6.1 General modelling procedures Statistical analyses were conducted in MATLAB (R2019a) using fitlme and fitglme.

Categorical predictors were effects coded, and fixed-effect structures were specified a priori. Random-effects structures were compared while holding the fixed effects constant. For each outcome, a random-intercept model was compared with a model additionally including by-participant random slopes for the repeated factor, and the random-slope model was retained when it converged and significantly improved model fit at α = .05. Linear mixed-effects models were compared using maximum likelihood and subsequently refitted using restricted maximum likelihood for final inference.

Generalized linear mixed-effects models were estimated using maximum likelihood with the Laplace approximation.

Fixed effects were evaluated using Type III tests. For linear mixed-effects models, denominator degrees of freedom were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation.

Model assumptions for linear mixed-effects models were assessed by inspecting residual distributions and fitted-versus-residual plots. Unless otherwise specified, models were fitted using all available observations after the data-processing and quality-control procedures described below; missing observations were not imputed.

When a Group × Session (behavioural outcomes) or Group × Block (training-phase outcomes) interaction was significant, it was followed up with contrasts estimated from the same model. These contrasts tested whether the change between two assessments or blocks differed between the groups (offline change minus online change) and estimated the change within each group; the contrasts for each outcome are specified below. The p values of these follow-up contrasts were Holm-corrected within each family of comparisons, whereas 95% confidence intervals were unadjusted. Effect sizes are reported as estimated differences in change on the model scale (empirical-logit units for production accuracy and log10-power units for alpha power). Approximate partial η² was additionally calculated for omnibus effects and contrasts as F × df₁/(F × df₁ + df₂), using the numerator and Satterthwaite denominator degrees of freedom; this is an approximation based on the test statistic rather than a partition of model variance.

2.6.2 EEG data processing and posterior alpha-power analysis EEG preprocessing EEG preprocessing was performed in MATLAB using EEGLAB (Delorme & Makeig, 2004) and FieldTrip (Oostenveld et al., 2011). Segments with 110-125-Hz power exceeding z = 10 were rejected as muscle-contaminated (Muthukumaraswamy, 2013).

Noisy channels were identified from broadband power, high-frequency activity, and low-frequency drift. Channels exceeding the across-channel median by more than five median absolute deviations on any measure were removed (Nolan et al., 2010; Bigdely-Shamlo et al., 2015). The data were band-pass filtered from 0.1 to 30 Hz, notch-filtered at 50 Hz using two-pass FieldTrip filters (Widmann et al., 2015), and resampled to 250 Hz. Extended-infomax independent component analysis was then applied (Lee et al., 1999). Components correlating with vertical or horizontal EOG at |r| ≥ .40 were removed (Jung et al., 2000). If more than five components met this criterion, the five components with the largest absolute EOG correlations were removed. Removed channels were reconstructed using spherical interpolation (Perrin et al., 1989), and the EEG data were re-referenced to the average reference.

Vocal-event detection

Vocal onset and offset during training were detected from the three-axis accelerometer recorded synchronously with EEG. Each axis was mean-centred, detrended, band-pass filtered from 70 to 400 Hz using a fourth-order bidirectional Butterworth filter, transformed into a Hilbert amplitude envelope, and smoothed using a 30-ms moving average. Axis-specific thresholds were defined as the median envelope plus five unscaled median absolute deviations, calculated from pooled activity 0.05-0.45 s after each trial trigger. Vocal activity was searched for from 0.50 to 3.50 s after the trigger. Candidate bursts were required to remain above threshold for at least 60 ms and reach a peak at least five median absolute deviations above the baseline median. The earliest valid onset across axes was selected. Vocal offset was defined as the end of the burst preceding at least 150 ms below threshold.

Posterior alpha-power calculation For each trial, EEG from 0.3 to 2.3 s after vocal offset was extracted. This postproduction interval captures the period immediately after learners had produced the word, when monitoring and evaluation of one's own performance, the processes assumed to be engaged by being observed, are likely to occur, and it excludes the learner's own vocalization. The 0.3-s delay reduces contamination from speech-related muscle and movement activity, which is broadband and can persist briefly after vocal offset (Muthukumaraswamy, 2013). The fixed 2-s length matches the resting-state epochs. Each 2-s segment was mean-centred and detrended and analysed using a full-length Hamming window, no overlap, and an FFT length of 512. These parameters provided a frequency resolution of approximately 0.49 Hz at the 250-Hz sampling rate. Mean 8-12-Hz power was calculated separately for each channel, log10-transformed, and then averaged across electrodes E67, E70, E71, E72, E75, E76, E77, and E83. Trial-level estimates were subsequently averaged within each training block.

For resting-state posterior alpha power, each 90-s eyes-open segment was divided into non-overlapping 2-s epochs and analysed using the same spectral parameters and posterior ROI.

Statistical model

Posterior alpha power was analysed with a linear mixed-effects model including Group, Block (three levels), and the Group × Block interaction as fixed effects; resting-state posterior alpha power was included as a covariate to account for individual differences in baseline alpha power. The random-effects structure included a random intercept for Participant, and by-participant random slopes for Block were tested as described in Section 2.6.1. When the Group × Block interaction was significant, it was followed up with between-group differences in change between each pair of blocks, within-group changes from the first to the third block, and between-group differences within each block, each as a separate Holm family. Because alpha power was log10-transformed, contrast estimates and their confidence intervals were back-transformed to power ratios for interpretation.

2.6.3 Autonomic data processing and analysis

ECG and EDA data were processed in Python using NeuroKit2 version 0.2.13 (Makowski et al., 2021). Resting-state baselines were derived from the eyes-open periods (Section 2.2.1). Each measure was analysed with a separate linear mixed-effects model including Group, Block, the Group × Block interaction, and the corresponding resting-state baseline as fixed effects. Signal processing, transformations, quality control, and model selection are reported in Supplementary Section S1.

2.6.4 Behavioural data processing and analysis

Outcome construction Production accuracy was calculated separately for each participant, assessment, and tone category as the number of productions rated as that tone category, irrespective of the tone mark of the item. This measure therefore indexes whether learners were able to produce each tone category, rather than whether each production matched its written tone mark. Each tone was represented by 15 items at each assessment. Productions without a majority rating contributed zero to the number of correct responses, while the denominator remained fixed at 15. Because a tone category could also be produced for items with a different tone mark, counts exceeding 15 were capped at 15. Thus, the number of correct responses, c, ranged from 0 to 15 for each participant × assessment × tone combination. Accuracy was transformed using an empirical-logit transformation, log[(c + 0.5)/(n -c + 0.5)], where c was the number of correct productions and n = 15. The 0.5 adjustment allowed the transformation to be defined for observations at the boundaries of the accuracy scale.

Identification accuracy was coded at the trial level as correct or incorrect for all 72 trials in each test. A valid response was required before the task advanced to the next trial, so no trials were missing because of response omissions. Reaction-time analyses included correct trials only. Trials with reaction times below 200 ms or above 3000 ms were excluded. Reaction times were natural-log transformed. Within each participant and session, observations more than three unscaled median absolute deviations from the median log reaction time were removed.

Statistical models

Empirical-logit-transformed production accuracy was analysed using a linear mixedeffects model with Group, Session (four levels: pre-test, two interim assessments, posttest), the Group × Session interaction, and Tone as fixed effects; Tone was included as a control variable. The random-effects structure included a random intercept for Participant, and by-participant random slopes for Session were tested as described in Section 2.6.1. When the Group × Session interaction was significant, it was followed up with between-group differences in change for all six pairs of assessments and with within-group pre-to post-test changes, each as a separate Holm family.

Identification accuracy was analysed using a binomial-logit generalized linear mixedeffects model with Group, Session (pre-and post-test), the Group × Session interaction, Tone, and Speaker as fixed effects; Tone and Speaker were included as control variables to account for systematic differences between tone categories and between the four synthetic voices. The random-effects structure included random intercepts for Participant and Item, and a correlated by-participant random slope for Session was tested as described in Section 2.6.1. Natural-log-transformed identification reaction times were analysed with the same fixed-and random-effects structures.

3 Results

3.1 Posterior EEG alpha power during listen-and-repeat training

For posterior alpha power, the random-slope model did not significantly improve model fit, χ²(5) = 9.87, p = .079, and the random-intercept model was retained. The Group × Block interaction was significant, F(2, 55.05) = 4.92, p = .011, approximate ηₚ² = .152, indicating that posterior alpha power changed differently across the three training blocks in the two groups. Neither Group, F(1, 27.03) = 0.38, p = .544, nor Block, F(2, 55.05) = 2.40, p = .100, was significant. Resting-state posterior alpha power, included as a covariate, was positively associated with alpha power during training, F(1, 27.03) = 33.58, p < .001, approximate ηₚ² = .554.

From Block 1 to Block 3, posterior alpha power increased by an estimated 17.0% in the offline group (B3/B1 power ratio = 1.170, unadjusted 95% CI [1.071, 1.278], Holmadjusted p = .002, approximate ηₚ² = .187), whereas the online group showed a small, non-significant decrease of 3.9% (ratio = 0.961, unadjusted 95% CI [0.878, 1.052], Holm-adjusted p = .382; Figure 2B; Table 2). This Block 1-to-Block 3 change differed significantly between the groups (difference in change = 0.086 log10-power units, SE = 0.027, unadjusted 95% CI [0.031, 0.141], Holm-adjusted p = .009, approximate ηₚ² = .150; Table 1); back-transformed, the B3/B1 power ratio was 21.8% larger in the offline than in the online group (ratio of ratios = 1.218, unadjusted 95% CI [1.073, 1.382]). The B2-B1 and B3-B2 change differences were not significant (Holmadjusted p = .140 and .201; Table 1). Alpha power was descriptively higher in the online than in the offline group in Block 1, but the groups did not differ significantly within any individual block (all Holm-adjusted p ≥ .663).

Autonomic activity during training (mean heart rate, RMSSD, mean SCL, and SCR rate) showed no significant Group × Block interactions. Mean SCL decreased across the training blocks in both groups. The full autonomic results are reported in Supplementary Section S1.

3.2 Behavioural outcomes

3.2.1 Tone production

For production accuracy, the random-slope model did not improve fit, χ²(2) = 0.00, p = 1.000, and the random-intercept model was retained. The Group × Session interaction was significant, F(3, 368) = 2.81, p = .039, approximate ηₚ² = .022, indicating that production accuracy changed differently across the four assessments in the two groups.

Neither Group, F(1, 368) = 0.98, p = .323, nor Session, F(3, 368) = 0.87, p = .455, was significant. Tone, included as a control variable, was also significant, F(2, 368) = 533.19, p < .001, approximate ηₚ² = .743, with much lower accuracy for the dipping tone (.100) than for the rising (.954) and falling (.921) tones.

From the pre-test to the post-test, tone-averaged predicted accuracy increased from .628 to .682 in the online group (+5.4 percentage points) and decreased from .662 to .635 in the offline group (-2.7 percentage points; Figure 2A). This pre-to-post change differed significantly between the groups (Offline -Online: estimate = -1.185 empirical-logit units, SE = 0.410, unadjusted 95% CI [-1.992, -0.378], Holmadjusted p = .025, approximate ηₚ² = .022; Table 3). Within-group contrasts showed that accuracy increased significantly in the online group (estimate = 0.789, SE = 0.283, unadjusted 95% CI [0.232, 1.346], Holm-adjusted p = .011, approximate ηₚ² = .021), whereas the decrease in the offline group was not significant (estimate = -0.396, SE = 0.297, unadjusted 95% CI [-0.980, 0.188], Holm-adjusted p = .183, approximate ηₚ² = .005). None of the five change contrasts involving the interim assessments differed significantly between the groups (Holm-adjusted p = .442-.836; Table 3).

3.2.2 Tone identification

For identification accuracy, the random-slope model provided a better fit than the random-intercept model, χ²(2) = 24.88, p < .001, and was retained. Accuracy increased from the pre-test to the post-test, F(1, 4599) = 12.06, p < .001, and this increase did not differ between the groups (Group × Session: F(1, 4599) = 0.75, p = .386). Averaged across groups, tones, and speakers, model-predicted accuracy rose from .436 to .518 (chance level = .333), an increase of 8.2 percentage points. Overall accuracy did not differ between the groups, F(1, 4599) = 1.23, p = .267. Tone and Speaker were included as control variables, and both had significant effects (Tone: F(2, 4599) = 10.68, p < .001; Speaker: F(3, 4599) = 4.54, p = .004).

For reaction times, the random-slope model was retained, χ²(2) = 82.55, p < .001. Reaction times did not change from the pre-test to the post-test, F(1, 26.45) = 0.68, p = .418, and this did not differ between the groups (Group × Session: F(1, 26.22) = 1.49, p = .234). Overall, the online group responded more slowly than the offline group, F(1, 29.07) = 6.69, p = .015, approximate ηₚ² = .187, with back-transformed fitted reaction times of approximately 1679 ms and 1475 ms, respectively (a 13.8% difference). Tone and Speaker were included as control variables. Tone had a significant effect, F(2, 62.48) = 7.50, p = .001, approximate ηₚ² = .194, whereas Speaker did not, F(3, 61.45) = 0.41, p = .749.

4 Discussion

This study examined whether learners' awareness of being observed by a tutor influences learning outcomes and neural activity during video-mediated listen-andrepeat training of Mandarin tones. The change in tone-production accuracy from preto post-training differed between the groups, with accuracy increasing in the online group but not in the offline group. Tone identification improved from pre-to posttraining, but this improvement did not differ between the groups. During training, posterior EEG alpha power showed a significant Group × Block interaction: alpha power increased from the first to the last (third) block in the offline group, whereas it did not change significantly in the online group.

4.1 Tone production

Consistent with our hypothesis, the change in production accuracy differed between the groups: accuracy improved from pre-to post-test in the online group but not in the offline group. Because the groups differed only in whether learners knew that the tutor could see them, with no feedback in either group, this difference can be attributed to learners' awareness of being observed. Awareness of being observed may thus improve learning from a tutor's pronunciation. This awareness could have supported production learning in two ways, neither of which was measured directly. Learners may have monitored their own speech more closely, a process central to auditory-motor learning (Levelt, 1989; Postma, 2000; Guenther, 2006), or they may have attended more closely to the tutor's productions. Either process could strengthen the link between the tutor's model and learners' own articulation, which would benefit later production without a model. Section 4.2 considers how these possibilities relate to the alpha results.

The online group had lower raw production accuracy at the pre-test than the offline group (Figure 2A), which could, in principle, be related to different levels of anxiety.

However, the groups did not differ in anxiety measured with the Beck Anxiety Inventory (Beck et al., 1988) before the experiment (p = .717) or in self-rated test stress after training (p = .674; see Supplementary Materials). Heart rate and skin conductance recorded during training did not differ between the groups either (all ps ≥ .066; see Supplementary Materials). Furthermore, participants were randomly assigned to the groups and were informed whether the tutor could see them only after the pre-test, so differing knowledge about group allocation cannot explain the baseline difference. The baseline difference therefore most plausibly reflects chance variation.

4.2 Posterior alpha dynamics during training

The Group × Block interaction in posterior alpha power reflected a change across training in the offline group: alpha power increased by an estimated 17% from the first to the third training block, whereas the online group showed no significant change.

Because the underlying processes were not measured directly, we consider three possible explanations, which relate to different measures: self-referential processing and attention, which are associated with posterior alpha power, and arousal, which is indexed by the autonomic measures.

First, awareness of being observed may have engaged self-referential processing. Being the target of another person's attention directs attention to the self, and public selfawareness increases when people believe that another person can see them (Myllyneva & Hietanen, 2015 , 2016), which was the case only in the online group. Because internally directed and self-referential processing is associated with increased alpha power (Cooper et al., 2003; Knyazev, 2013; Benedek et al., 2014), heightened selffocus could be expected to keep posterior alpha power relatively high in the online group from the beginning of training. However, the present data do not provide support for this account. The only observation in line with it is the descriptively higher alpha power in the online group in the first block (Figure 2B), and this difference was not significant. Moreover, the significant effect, the increase in alpha power in the offline group, does not require self-referential processing to be explained.

Second, the alpha effect may reflect attention to the tutor and the task. Posterior alpha power tends to increase as a repetitive task continues and attention to external input declines (a time-on-task effect; Boksem et al., 2005), which offers a parsimonious explanation for the increase in the offline group. Learners who knew that the tutor could see them may have kept attending to the tutor's productions across blocks, which could have prevented such an increase. This account is consistent with the alpha pattern, but attention was not measured independently of alpha power. Third, being observed may have increased arousal. The data do not support this account, because heart rate, heart-rate variability, and electrodermal activity, which index autonomic arousal, did not differ between the groups, although autonomic measures may be less sensitive than EEG to subtle changes in state. Taken together, the alpha effect shows that the neural state of the two groups developed differently over training, but the present data do not identify its cognitive source.

4.3 Tone identification

Identification accuracy improved by approximately 8 percentage points from the pretest to the post-test, and this improvement did not differ between the two groups. This improvement is consistent with evidence that production practice can transfer to tone perception (Li & DeKeyser, 2017) and with identification gains reported in previous tone-training studies (Wang et al., 1999; Sadakata & McQueen, 2014; Li et al., 2026).

Because the design included no untrained control group, repeated testing may also have contributed.

The absence of a group difference in identification suggests that awareness of being observed mainly affected learners' own production performance, which was practised during training and visible to the tutor in the online group, rather than auditory tone perception, which does not involve observable performance. This interpretation should be made cautiously, however, because a non-significant interaction for identification does not demonstrate that the groups improved equally (Altman & Bland, 1995), and the group effects on production and identification were not compared statistically. Identification reaction times also showed no significant Group × Session interaction.

The online group responded more slowly overall, but this difference was already present at the pre-test and most likely reflects pre-existing group differences.

4.4 Implications for online language learning

These findings bear on online language learning. Social presence is related to engagement, self-regulation, and perceived learning in online education (Gunawardena & Zittle, 1997; Richardson & Swan, 2003; Miao & Ma, 2022). Studies of instructional videos have mainly examined whether learners benefit from seeing the instructor: instructor visibility affects perceived social presence, motivation, and affect, whereas its effects on learning outcomes are inconsistent (Kizilcec et al., 2014; Alemdag, 2022; Beege et al., 2023). Here, we examined the reverse direction. The present results suggest that learners' awareness of being observed, which distinguishes synchronous online learning from learning with recorded materials, may matter in its own right. Even without verbal interaction or feedback, learning with a tutor whom learners knew could see them differed from learning in a situation corresponding to recorded materials. The value of synchronous online instruction may therefore depend not only on the exchange of linguistic information but also on the social situation in which learners perform.

4.5 Future directions

Two extensions follow from the present findings. First, self-awareness, self-monitoring, and attention should be measured directly, for example with self-report measures of self-awareness or perceived observation and behavioural measures of attention combined with EEG, to distinguish between the possible mechanisms. Second, larger samples, delayed retention tests, and acoustic analyses of F0 contours, including productions made during training, would characterise the time course and durability of production learning.

5 Conclusions

In conclusion, learners' awareness that the tutor could see them was associated with improved tone-production accuracy from pre-to post-training, whereas no significant improvement was observed in the offline group. In addition, differences in posterior alpha dynamics were observed during video-mediated listen-and-repeat training of Mandarin tones. The cognitive processes underlying these alpha differences remain to be established. These findings suggest that being observed by a tutor may improve pronunciation learning even when no feedback is given.
