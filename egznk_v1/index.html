statistical_tests BSI item responses will be dichotomized in the corresponding symptom being present (item score of 2 – “moderately”, 3 – “quite a bit”, or 4 – “extremely”) or not present (item score of 0 – “not at all”, or 1 – “a little bit”). The planned analysis comprises 1) model development and 2) network construction based on estimated coefficients from the best-fitting model.

1) MODEL DEVELOPMENT:
For each of the 6 core symptoms, a Bayesian multilevel logistic regression model will be fitted. Specifically, each model will predict the presence of a symptom at timepoint t based on the presence of that symptom and all other symptoms at the previous timepoint (t-1, i.e., lagged symptoms). Additionally, a linear interaction term between the lagged symptom (at t-1) and timepoint (t) will be included to capture linear changes in symptom interactions over the course of therapy.

Several variations of this model will be estimated:

1. Random Intercept Model: Includes only random intercepts, allowing intercepts to vary between persons. The model provides an estimate for the mean changes in symptom interactions during therapy.

2. Random Baseline Symptom Association Model: Includes random effects for the intercept and the lagged-symptom presence (i.e., the presence of symptoms at 𝑡−1), allowing intercepts and temporal symptom associations to vary between persons.

3. Random Baseline & Change Symptom Association Model: Includes random effects for all predictors (i.e., the presence of symptoms at 𝑡−1 and their interaction with timepoint), allowing all intercepts and symptom associations and their change vary between persons. As this model allows change rates to be person-specific, it facilitates the construction of time-variant patient-specific networks.

For all models, data from all treatment groups are analyzed simultaneously. To empirically identify the best-fitting model, two versions are estimated, respectively: (a) a sparse model without differentiation between treatment groups and (b) a full model that includes the treatment effect as an additional fixed effect interaction term. In the full model, the treatment effect is assessed through a three-way interaction between lagged symptoms, timepoint, and treatment group. Consequently, each of the three models described above is estimated in both (a) and (b) versions.

For all parameters, weakly informative priors are used: Normal(0,3) for population intercept and population slopes (fixed effects) and HalfCauchy(3) for the standard deviations of the population effect (random effects). Convergence will be checked using trace plots and the Gelman-Rubin potential scale reduction statistic. If all models provide estimable solutions, model fit will be evaluated using Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC), R², and Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOO). The model yielding the best fit will be selected for subsequent network construction. Additionally, for the final model, each symptom will be evaluated by comparing observed and predicted symptom frequencies over time.

2) NETWORK CONSTRUCTION:
Using the coefficient estimates from the superior model identified in step 1, symptom networks will be constructed for each of the eight timepoints (pre-treatment baseline and weekly assessments during the 7-week treatment period). If a full model version provides the best fit, symptom networks will be constructed for each timepoint and treatment group. The regression coefficients describing the direction and strength of the longitudinal associations between symptoms will serve as edge weights in the network. The robustness of all edges will be evaluated using 95% credibility intervals. To evaluate network structure, average raw strength, average absolute strength, instrength, outstrength, out-expected influence, and in-expected influence metrics for each node (symptom) are to be assessed. These metrics will be compared across symptoms, timepoints, and treatment groups (if applicable) to evaluate changes during therapy.
assumption_violation_model_non_convergence If none of the above stated models provides estimable solutions, a cross-lagged network model will be applied (see Wysocki et al., 2022). This approach allows for evaluating change in symptom interactions between core symptoms of depression and anxiety but does not enable person-specific networks to be estimated.
exploratory_analyses 1. Analysis of Person-Specific Networks: If Model 3 will show the best model fit, person-specific networks will be constructed to explore whether symptom interactions and their changes during treatment differ between individuals.

2. Network Structure and Treatment Response: If Model 3 demonstrates the best fit, we will exploratorily investigate the relationship between symptom network structure and treatment response. Specifically, depression severity (assessed using the BDI-II) at the end of treatment and at follow-up timepoints will be regressed on parameters describing the structure of person-specific networks at baseline (t0) and their changes during treatment, while accounting for baseline and end of treatment symptom severity. This analysis will follow the machine learning approach outlined by Schumacher et al. (2024).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Testing implications of the network theory of mental disorders in intensive shortterm treatment

The network theory of mental disorders 1 conceptualizes psychopathology as a system of causally interacting symptoms. 1, 2 When symptom interactions are numerous or strong, symptoms may mutually reinforce one another and stabilize a disordered state; when interactions are fewer or weaker, the system may be less self-sustaining and more compatible with healthy functioning. 1, 3 The network theory has motivated various proposals for targeted and personalized treatment by identifying symptoms and symptom interactions that are expected to maintain psychopathology. [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] These include network-supported case conceptualization 7 and personalized interventions that use person-specific networks to select and prioritize treatment targets and match them to evidence-based treatment modules. 4, [8] [9] [10] Such applications rely on a central treatment-related premise of the network theory: if strong symptom interactions are central to maintaining psychopathology, they should be modifiable through treatment.

Specifically, successful treatment should not only reduce symptom severity but also alter the pattern and reduce the strength of symptom interactions, potentially shifting the system toward a less connected, healthier state. 1, 11 Empirical support for this prediction is needed before network structure can confidently guide intervention targets. 12 However, empirical tests of these treatment-related propositions remain scarce. Most studies compare cross-sectional networks before and after treatment [13] [14] [15] [16] or use models assuming a stable network structure over time, 17, 18 precluding direct tests of how within-person symptom interactions change during treatment. Intensive longitudinal studies comparing preand post-treatment periods provide a first indication but have yielded mixed findings. [19] [20] [21] Two studies repeatedly assessed network structure throughout treatment, 12, 22 although only one examined changes in overall connectivity. 22 Using a time-varying longitudinal network model, Schumacher et al. 12 provided the most direct test during 1 year of outpatient psychotherapy for chronic depression. Specific symptom interactions changed across treatment conditions and individuals. However, contrary to network theory's prediction, absolute connectivity did not decrease, partly because negative (inhibitory) interactions emerged in successful treatment.

To further test the validity of the network theory's treatment-related implications, additional evidence is crucial, particularly regarding their generalizability across settings, timescales, and symptoms. As prior work 12 focused on long-term outpatient psychotherapy for chronic depression, short but intensive inpatient or day-clinic treatment provides an informative complementary test across a compressed timescale. Given the frequent co-occurrence of depression and anxiety, 23, 24 a transdiagnostic perspective is particularly relevant in such highly comorbid populations. 25, 26 A key strength of the network framework is that symptoms across diagnostic domains can be modeled within the same system, making it particularly suited to transdiagnostic questions. 1, 27, 28 In this preregistered secondary analysis, we apply a time-varying longitudinal network model 12 to test whether network structure changes systematically during intensive short-term treatment, jointly evaluating anxiety and depression symptoms. 1, 11 Following the network theory, we hypothesize that successful treatment corresponds to reduced overall connectivity (ie, fewer and/or weaker symptom interactions). Thereby, we hope to extend evidence for the treatmentrelated claims of the network theory across settings, timescales, and symptoms.

Methods

Study Design

This study is a preregistered secondary analysis (https://osf.io/bkngs) of the OPTIMA trial (OPtimized Treatment Identification at the MAx Planck Institute of Psychiatry), a monocentric, rater-blinded, prospective, parallel-group, block-randomized trial, approved by the ethics committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Ludwig-Maximilians-University of Munich (No. 17-395). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Details on trial procedures and primary outcomes are reported elsewhere. 29, 30 Patients aged 18 to 75 years were recruited between September 2017 and October 2020, and the present secondary analysis was conducted between January 2025 and May 2026. Eligible participants had a primary diagnosis of moderate to severe depression without psychotic symptoms. Participants received 7 weeks of inpatient or day-clinic treatment and were randomized to schema therapy (ST), cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), or individual supportive therapy (IST) as a nonspecific active control intervention. Each condition included up to two individual and two group sessions per week, supplemented by concomitant care (eg, ergotherapy and relaxation training). Medication was permitted and managed as clinically indicated.

Sample

This secondary analysis included participants who met the inclusion criteria and contributed at least two symptom assessments to the network analysis, resulting in a final sample of N = 288 patients. Participants were aged 19 to 74 years (M = 43.6, SD = 13.6), and 55.6% identified as female. The sample exhibited high rates of comorbidity, including anxiety (62.5%), substance use (27.6%), and somatoform disorders (23.5%; based on n = 272 with available M-CIDI data). 31 Participants attended a mean of 20.31 sessions over the 7-week period and completed on average 6.45 of 8 weekly assessments (19.4% of assessment occasions fully missing). As reported in the primary trial, 30 depressive symptom severity decreased significantly over the 7-week treatment, with mean reductions on the Beck Depression Inventory-II 32 ranging from 13.53 to 16.49 points across treatment groups and modest differences between treatment arms. 30

Measures and Node Selection

Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed at baseline (t0) and weekly through treatment (t1-t7) using the 53-item Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), 33 rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Item responses were dichotomized to indicate symptom presence (item score ≥ 2: "moderately", "quite a bit", or "extremely") or absence (item score ≤ 1: "not at all" or "a little bit"), as network analyses with ordinal data face severe limitations. 34, 35 For network estimation, we selected six symptoms intended to reflect the core symptoms of major depressive disorder (MDD) and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) as described in the ICD-10 criteria. 36 MDD nodes comprise (1) depressed mood, (2) loss of interest or pleasure, and (3) decrease in activity or fatigue. GAD nodes represent (1) excessive worry, nervousness, or fear, (2) physical tension, and (3) autonomic arousal. The item-to-criterion mapping is provided in eTable1 in the Supplement.

Statistical Analyses

Network Model: To examine whether symptom interactions changed during treatment, we used a time-varying multilevel vector autoregressive (VAR) model. 12 For each symptom, presence at timepoint t is predicted by its own and all other symptoms at t-1 (ie, lagged symptoms) and by the interaction between each lagged symptom and treatment week, allowing temporal interactions to change linearly across treatment. This framework allows for the explicit modeling of change in symptom interactions over time. A random intercept captured betweenperson differences in baseline symptom presence. This model was selected over candidate models allowing differential treatment effects and person-specific symptom interactions or change rates based on model fit. As a sensitivity analysis, we added time as a main effect to the final model to account for overall temporal trends in symptom probabilities that are not captured through changes in temporal interactions alone. Details on all model specifications, estimation procedures, and model comparisons are provided in the eMethods and eTables 2 and 3 in the Supplement.

Network Estimation:

We used Bayesian multilevel logistic regression with Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, fitting one model per symptom in R (version 4.4.2) 37 using brms (version 2.22.0). 38 Convergence was assessed using trace plots and the Gelman-Rubin statistic 39 , and model performance was evaluated for each symptom via Bayesian pseudo-R² and by comparing predicted with observed symptom frequencies across timepoints.

Network Description:

Posterior estimates were used to derive one symptom network for each treatment week (t0-t7). Connectivity indices were based on cross-lagged edges only, excluding autoregressive effects to capture connectivity between symptoms. Global connectivity was summarized using mean absolute strength (mean of all edge weights regardless of valence) and mean raw strength (mean of signed associations, allowing positive and negative edges to cancel each other out). Node-specific in-and out-strength and in-and out-expected influence summarized incoming and outgoing absolute and signed edge weights, respectively.

These node-specific metrics reflect the relative influence a symptom receives from or exerts on other symptoms over time. All analysis code is available on https://github.com/BeSabrin/dynamic-network-intensive-treatment.

Stability Landscapes:

We explored simulation-based stability landscapes to examine the stability of different mental health states which were defined by the number of present symptoms. 40, 41 Stability landscapes characterize the network-implied symptom dynamics and can identify attractor states toward which the system most strongly leans. 42 Clinically, they can complement connectivity measures by indicating whether treatment-related changes in network structure are accompanied by a shift toward healthier symptom dynamics, ie, more stable states of few present symptoms (eMethods in the Supplement).

Results

The final model allowed all symptom interactions to change linearly over time, with effects pooled across individuals and treatment arms. It explained about half of the variation in symptom presence during treatment, with Bayesian pseudo-R² values ranging from 0.43 to 0.54 across symptoms. Model-predicted symptom frequencies captured the observed decline in symptom presence across treatment well, although reductions tended to be slightly overestimated toward the end of treatment (eFigure 1 in the Supplement). Thus, overall model fit was good, and the model reproduced broad mean-level symptom improvement by entirely modeling change through lagged symptom interactions. Figure 1 visualizes the estimated temporal symptom networks at baseline (A), mid-treatment (B), and end of treatment (C); networks for all 8 timepoints are shown in eFigure 3 in the Supplement. At baseline, 12 of 36 lagged effects (6/6 autoregressive; 6/30 cross-lagged) were substantial (ie, their 95% credibility intervals did not include 0) compared with 20 of 36 at mid-treatment (6/6; 14/30) and 7 of 36 after the 7-week treatment (3/6; 4/30). All substantial edges were positive.

Limited change in lagged symptom interactions

Estimated changes in symptom interactions were small. Of 36 change rates, point estimates suggested decreasing associations over time for 21 edges, essentially no change for 4, and increasing associations for 11 (eTable 4 in the Supplement). However, only 3 change rates were substantial, ie, supported with 95% credibility (Figure 1). The autoregressive effects of arousal and loss of interest became less strongly self-predictive over time (β arousal = -0.19; 95% CI, -0.37 to -0.02; β loss of interest = -0.23; 95% CI, -0.42 to -0.03), whereas the crosslagged effect from arousal to physical tension increased (β = 0.16; 95% CI, 0.01 to 0.30).

Consistent with the limited evidence for changing symptom interactions through treatment, global network metrics based on cross-lagged effects showed only small shifts over time. Mean absolute strength of temporal symptom associations declined from 0.55 (95% CI, 0.47-0.64) at treatment start to 0.46 (95% CI, 0.38-0.54) at treatment end, but overlapping credibility intervals indicate no robust effect (Figure 2A). Mean raw strength of temporal symptom associations showed a similar pattern, decreasing from 0.45 (95% CI, 0.38-0.51) to 0.37 (95% CI, 0.30-0.43; Figure 2B). Node-specific metrics (in-strength, out-strength, in-/outexpected influence) likewise remained largely stable (eFigure 5 in the Supplement).

Exploratory inspection suggested qualitative shifts in a small set of dyadic feedback patterns. Several bidirectional associations evident at baseline (eg, depressed mood ↔ loss of interest; loss of interest ↔ decreased activity; nervousness ↔ physical tension) were not present with 95% certainty posttreatment (Figure 1A and C).

Sensitivity Analysis

Including time as a main effect yielded similar overall fit (Bayesian pseudo-R², 0.43-0.55) and a comparable pattern of symptom decline, with slight overestimation of improvement toward treatment end (eFigure 2 in the Supplement). Time showed a substantial main effect for 3 of 6 symptoms, all indicating a decreasing probability of symptom presence over time. Two of the 3 change rates identified in the primary model (autoregressive changes for loss of interest and autonomic arousal) were no longer supported by 95% credibility (eTable 5; eFigure 4).

Stability landscapes

Exploratory stability landscape analyses showed an apparent shift toward lowersymptom states during treatment (Figure 3). At treatment start, states with 3 to 4 present symptoms were most likely, whereas the symptom network tended toward 1 to 2 present symptoms at mid-treatment and 0 to 1 active symptoms at treatment end. However, the end-oftreatment basin was relatively shallow, indicating only a weak preference for the attractor state of few symptoms present. A similar qualitative shift emerged in the sensitivity analysis including time as a main effect (eFigure 6 in the Supplement). Thus, despite limited evidence for changes in network structure, the landscapes suggested a shift toward healthier system configurations over treatment.

Discussion

In this preregistered secondary analysis of a 7-week inpatient/day-clinic randomized controlled trial for depression, 29, 30 we examined the treatment-related implications of the network theory across settings, timescales, and symptoms. Despite substantial symptom improvement, 30 we found little evidence of systematic change in temporal symptom interactions or reduced network connectivity, providing limited support for the prediction that successful treatment should lead to a less connected symptom network. Most lagged effects remained stable in magnitude and valence, and unlike Schumacher et al, 12 no negative (ie, inhibitory) cross-lagged interactions emerged. Nevertheless, model-predicted symptom frequencies captured the overall decline in symptom presence well, and exploratory stability landscapes shifted toward healthier, lower-symptom states by treatment end. This apparent dissociation raises the question of how treatment-related change was represented in our models. In the primary model, systematic change through treatment could enter only through changing symptom interactions, and even small and uncertain changes (most point estimates differed from the null and tended toward decreases) allowed the model to reproduce the symptom decline well. The sensitivity model fitted similarly when each symptom's general activation tendency was additionally allowed to change over time, making activation tendency a potentially relevant additional indicator of change. We use this term to denote the likelihood of symptom occurrence that is not explained by symptom interactions, conceptually opposite to its "threshold". 43 Lower activation tendency can be interpreted as greater resilience to activation, requiring stronger input from the system or external field. 42, 43 Observed symptom presence is the realized state at a specific timepoint and is strongly influenced, but not determined, by activation tendency.

Several factors may explain the contrast with prior findings showing substantial changes in symptom interactions through treatment. 12 One concerns the timescale of change. Patients received broadly comparable numbers of psychotherapy sessions in OPTIMA 30 and the trial analyzed by Schumacher et al. 12, 44 (20 vs 28 sessions), but over 7 weeks versus 1 year. Higher session frequency may accelerate depressive symptom reduction even at constant treatment dose, 45, 46 although this advantage may not extend to longer-term outcomes 47 and is not consistently supported after adjusting for study characteristics (eg, baseline depression severity). 48 Faster symptom improvement, however, does not need to imply rapid network restructuring. Intensive inpatient treatment may rapidly reduce symptom occurrence in acutely ill patients, whereas changes in maintaining symptom interactions may lag behind and require longer periods of (re-)learning and consolidation. Such changes may become apparent only with longer observation or more sustained treatment, including subsequent outpatient care.

Continued care after discharge is crucial for maintaining treatment gains and reducing relapse risk, 49 and clinical guidelines recommend continuation of psychological treatment and transition to outpatient care. [50] [51] [52] [53] Repeated network assessments beyond discharge and across treatment settings differing in duration and intensity could help distinguish these explanations.

More importantly, the distinction between symptom interactions and general activation tendency may matter for the durability of symptom relief. Our findings may resemble a "silent disorder," in which symptoms are currently less active while pathways of mutual reinforcement persist, potentially leaving the system vulnerable to reactivation under external stress. 2, 54 Whether lower activation tendency reflects sustained recovery or primarily transient symptom relief with remaining relapse risk, and whether changes in symptom interactions carry different prognostic information, should be tested against follow-up outcomes. Prior work showed incremental prognostic value of symptom interaction changes beyond changes in symptom activation tendency. 55 In contrast, our results might suggest that a healthier symptom system may need to be characterized more broadly than by reduced connectivity alone. Lower activation tendencies may mark meaningful improvement even when interactions change little, highlighting the need to distinguish activation tendency, symptom interactions, and symptom severity when evaluating treatment success. Future models should estimate these components jointly and examine their independent and incremental value for evaluating treatment success. Network theory may also need greater specificity about which structural properties should change with successful treatment. Although the theory predicts weaker symptom interactions and lower overall connectivity with successful treatment, 1, 11 global connectivity did not substantially decline despite marked symptom improvement. Short-term improvement may therefore not require a detectable reduction in global connectivity, or global metrics may be too coarse to capture clinically relevant change. More fine-grained indicators, including changes in feedback loops, autoregressive dynamics, or the stability of symptom states, may be informative. 3, [40] [41] [42] [43] 56 Limitations Several limitations warrant consideration. First, despite theoretical emphasis on individual-level symptom dynamics, 1, 11 the best-fitting model pooled symptom interactions and change rates across participants and treatment arms. Although supported by model fit indices and similar estimates across alternative specifications, group-level averages may obscure clinically relevant heterogeneity 57 and limit inference about person-specific mechanisms of change. Second, weekly assessments may have been insensitive to faster symptom dynamics.

With several therapy sessions per week, each interval spanned multiple therapeutic events, so session-to-session changes could be averaged over or missed. Whether this underestimated treatment-related change depends on the timescale of interaction dynamics, which remains insufficiently understood. Third, although clinically relevant in a highly comorbid sample, the transdiagnostic inclusion of depression and anxiety symptoms may limit comparability with prior depression-specific networks. 12 Restricting each disorder to 3 core symptoms ensured estimability 17 but may have captured only part of the underlying symptom dynamics. Yet the few observed changes were not confined to depressive symptoms, supporting the clinical relevance of considering anxiety symptoms in patients treated for depression. 26 Finally, some change estimates were imprecise, with wide credibility intervals limiting distinction between small treatment-related changes and estimation uncertainty. A larger sample may have improved precision of these estimates.

Conclusion

Findings from this intensive short-term treatment invite a broader view of treatment success beyond symptom severity and global connectivity, including activation tendency, specific interaction patterns, and their stability over time. The observed pattern may reflect greater change in activation tendency than in temporal interaction structure, potentially indicating residual vulnerability. For the network theory, the limited support for reduced connectivity highlights the need to define more clearly what characterizes a healthy symptom system and how activation tendency and symptom interactions independently or jointly relate to sustained improvement. Research across assessment timescales, treatment durations, intensities, and settings should determine whether short-term improvement without detectable network restructuring reflects sustained recovery or heightened relapse risk, ultimately informing decisions about treatment duration and continuation of care.
