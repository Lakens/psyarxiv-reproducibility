What we found

We found 12 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Ensemble perception is a fundamental mechanism enabling the visual system to rapidly extract summary statistical properties (e.g., mean orientation) from a large number of objects. This study investigated the influence of attentional templates for targets and distractors which are formed during visual search, on subsequent ensemble perception. Participants first completed a visual search task involving target and distractor colors, followed by an ensemble orientation estimation task in which these colors recurred in various combinations. The first experiment revealed that target template systematically biased ensemble estimates, with the strongest effect occurring when both template colors recurred together. Furthermore, on these trials the bias exceeded the sum of the individual effects of target and distractor conditions, which demonstrates that combined effect of target enhancement and distractor suppression may be greater than predicted by their individual effects. In the second experiment, presentation duration (150 ms vs. 500 ms) was manipulated. While our results showed that duration did not alter the magnitude of the biases, a longer presentation time improved response precision. These findings suggest that ensemble perception, can be modulated by recently formed attentional templates and systematically shape the formation of summary statistical representations.

Introduction

In any natural scene, observers are constantly faced with a vast number of stimuli. However, working memory capacity and attentional resources are limited which prevents the simultaneous or parallel processing of visual input. When we look at a tree, we effortlessly perceive the leaves as uniformly "green" (Ahissar & Hochstein, 2004; Chong & Treisman, 2005). This impression arises even though we cannot simultaneously encode the properties of every individual leaf. Selectively processing or attending to the specific properties of each leaf is computationally demanding and severely capacity-limited (Tsotsos, 1988; Cowan, 2001). To overcome these capacity limitations the visual system rapidly extracts statistical summaries, such as mean or variance, of a group of similar items to circumvent these limitations, a phenomenon known as ensemble perception (Ariely, 2001; Alvarez & Oliva, 2008; Alvarez, 2011; Whitney & Yamanashi Leib, 2018; Corbett et al., 2023). Ensemble perception spans across feature levels: from low-level stimuli such as orientation (Dakin & Watt, 1997; Alvarez & Oliva, 2009), color (Maule et al., 2014; Bronfman, Brezis, Jacobson, & Usher, 2014), to mid-level stimuli such as size (Ariely, 2001), position (Morgan & Glennerster, 1991), but also to high-level stimuli such as emotional expressions (Haberman & Whitney, 2007; Haberman, Lee, & Whitney, 2015; Fischer & Whitney, 2011) and identity (de Fockert & Wolfenstein, 2009). Besides the mean of different features, second-order statistics such as variability (variance) or the range can also be extracted (Alvarez, 2011; Haberman et al., 2015; Khvostov & Utochkin, 2019) as well as more complex properties, such as the shapes of feature distributions (e.g., Chetverikov et al. 2016; Hansmann-Roth et al., 2021; see Chetverikov et al. 2019 for an overview).

Ensemble perception was initially thought to be an automatic process that operates in parallel across the visual field without requiring selective attention or detailed encoding of each stimulus feature (Chong & Treisman 2005; Alvarez & Oliva, 2008; Alvarez & Oliva, 2009; Bronfman et al., 2014; Huang, 2015; Jackson-Nielsen et al., 2017; Khayat & Hochstein, 2018; Talipsky et al., 2022). In their seminal paper, Ariely (2001) compared performance in two tasks: a member-identification and a mean-discrimination task, to understand how participants perceive individual circles or the mean size of the entire set. Results showed that participants performed well on the mean-identification task, but their performance was near chance-level for the memberidentification task. These results indicate that observers can extract summary statistics of a set without needing to remember all the details of the individual objects included in the set. Therefore, ensemble representations are primarily based on summary statistics rather than on explicit representations of individual items. In a follow-up study, Chong and Treisman (2005b) showed that averaging computations can occur automatically across perceptual groups, supporting parallel summary-statistic extraction rather than serial processing of individual items. Another study conducted by Alvarez and Oliva (2009) provided further evidence in favor of the attentionindependent nature of ensemble perception: While participants were attending and tracking a set of moving dots, they were still reliably able to detect orientation changes of an ensemble of Gabors in the unattended background. This indicates that spatial ensemble statistics can be extracted outside the focus of attention. Moreover, findings from Posner cueing showed that judgement of the average speed of moving dots is persistent even when spatial attention is directed away from the ensemble (Talipsky et al., 2022).

Conversely, a set of other studies showed that ensemble perception does sometimes require attention. Jackson-Nielsen et al. (2017) implemented inattentional blindness paradigm and found that attention is necessary for ensemble perception. In a separate study, Dakin et al. (2009) showed that performance in judging the mean orientation of Gabor patches was significantly impaired when observers performed a concurrent attention-demanding task. Similar findings were reported by Lukashevich et al. (2025), who examined whether ensemble perception requires attention. Participants either attended to changes in the mean orientation of ensembles or focused on a demanding central task while ignoring background ensembles that occasionally changed orientation. Neural responses indicating change detection were only observed when participants attended to the ensembles. The mean orientation of unattended ensemble was not automatically detected when attention was diverted from the central task, indicating that attention might be a key factor in the process of ensemble discrimination. Another recent study by Kimchi and Sabary (2025) examined whether ensemble properties, such as the average size or average orientation, can be extracted from unattended stimuli using an inattention paradigm. Participants performed a central change-detection task while a task-irrelevant background ensemble of circles or lines was presented simultaneously. Across trials, average size or orientation changed independently, while individual elements continuously varied to prevent reliance on local cues. Their results showed that changes in the ensemble mean were not detected when the stimuli were unattended.

Regardless of whether attention is required for ensemble perception, attentional allocation appears to influence the precision of ensemble processing and induces potential biases. Chong and Treisman (2005a) showed that the ensemble average is extracted more efficiently when attention is spread across the entire set while locally attending to individual items lead to impaired performance. Chen et al. (2021) investigated whether ensemble perception can occur outside of focal attention by examining the influence of a to-be-ignored ensemble on judgments of an attended ensemble. They found that unattended ensembles affected performance only when their summary statistics matched the target category that the participants were searching for. The authors concluded that ensemble processing outside the focus of attention depends on the observer's attentional control settings. In a further exploration of the role of attention in ensemble perception, Baek and Chong (2020) proposed two attentional modes: distributed attention, which controls how we summarize statistically redundant information, and focused attention, which facilitates detailed object recognition and filters irrelevant input. Taken together, there is no solid consensus on the role of attention in ensemble perception, with evidence supporting both its dependent and automatic nature.

Attention may also influence ensemble perception by altering how strongly individual items contribute to the final summary representation. Rather than weighting all items equally, certain stimuli appear to cause a disproportionate influence on ensemble estimates. De Fockert and Marchant (2008) used an extra localization task to direct attention to a single item within a set of differently sized circles. In Experiment 1, the participants were asked to look for the smallest or largest circle, thereby making size task relevant. In Experiment 2, they instead looked for a circle defined by its brightness, which was either the smallest or the largest item in the group. In both experiments, participants' average size estimates were systematically biased toward the size of the attended circle when attention was directed to the smallest or largest circle. To directly examine whether salient items contribute disproportionately to ensemble representations, Kanaya et al. (2018) studied ensemble perception in both spatial and temporal ensembles to directly examine the role of saliency. They investigated how observers perceive the mean size and temporal frequency of a set of discs. Their findings indicated that objects which are highly salient are weighted more heavily (are amplified) within summary statistical representations and subsequently bias observers' estimates. These findings suggest that ensemble perception is not a process of equal weighting across all items but instead, items that capture attention through their saliency contribute disproportionately to the ensemble representation.

Moreover, attentional biases can also arise from selection history (Theeuwes, 2018 (Theeuwes, , 2019)), whereby previous history of attentional selection influence subsequent attentional allocation independent of current goals or physical salience (Kristjánsson & Ásgeirsson, 2019; Maljkovic & Nakayama, 1994). Selection history encompasses several experience-based influences on attention, including intertrial priming and reward learning (Theeuwes, 2018). One example of selection history on ensemble processing was studied by Munneke et al. (2022). Their study focused on value-driven attentional capture through which previously rewarded stimuli can continue to attract attention even when they are irrelevant to the current task. Participants estimated the average size of a group of circles, while one circle (either the smallest or largest) was presented in a color that had previously been associated with high, low or no reward. The largest biases in perceived average size occurred when the largest circle had the highest reward value, with the amount of bias increasing linearly as reward magnitude increased even when dual-task conditions prevented the observer from fixating on the rewarded circle. This research demonstrates that selection history can influence ensemble representations, with previously rewarded items contributing disproportionately to perceived ensemble averages.

Beyond attentional biases arising from selection history or visual salience, ensemble perception may also be shaped by representations actively maintained in visual working memory (VWM). While selection history is the result of the influence of former experiences, representations in VWM can influence the processing of currently encountered information. Selective attention relies on feature representations actively held in working memory to guide goaldirected behavior. Williams et al. (2021) examined whether VWM influences perceptual averaging when a subset of items matched a feature held in memory. Participants performed a mean orientation task while concurrently maintaining a colored shape in VWM, which could match the color of a subset of items in the orientation ensemble display. Specifically, they found that the perceived mean orientation was biased toward the average of the memory-matching colored subset of lines rather than the true mean of all items. The information about memory-matching elements was more heavily weighed into the ensemble processing. Therefore, the ensembles' contributions of individual items can be influenced by representations maintained for a concurrent VWM task. Importantly, however, memory-matching representations constitute only one source of top-down attentional guidance. Another fundamental mechanism is the use of attentional templates that guide visual search toward behaviorally relevant information. Attentional templates, unlike the one maintained for the concurrent memory task in the paper by Williams et al. (2021), are representations of features used to select relevant information for visual search. However, far less is known about the influence of these attentional templates on ensemble perception. Attentional templates are defined as mental representations of target-defining features, which are used to guide attention and determine whether any given stimulus in the scene qualifies as a target. Attentional templates can be inferred and modified over time with a search task and indicate features that differentiate targets from distractors. This idea originates from early theories of visual search, which proposed that attention is guided by an internal description of target-defining features. According to Duncan and Humphreys (1989), visual inputs receive greater attentional weight to the extent that they match this internal representation, thereby facilitating the selection of behaviorally relevant information during visual search.

Attentional control may be mediated by positive templates that increase target-matching information and by negative templates (or templates for rejection) that facilitate the exclusion of information about known distractors (Arita et al., 2012; Carlisle, 2023). Imagine you have lost and are searching for your cat (target template) amongst other cats (distractor templates) on the street. To do so, you need to rely on your templates about your own cat like the color of its collar, the specific feature of its fur, etc. Top-down knowledge about the target guides visual search by biasing attention toward stimuli that match target-defining features (Vickery et al., 2005). Hout and Goldinger (2015) demonstrated that the precision and sharpness of target templates directly influence visual search performance. Before search, participants were given a visual cue that constituted the target template and the similarity between the cue and the target to be presented during search was manipulated. More precise and distinct target representations optimize attentional guidance and accelerate decision-making processes. When template precision decreased, participants' performance decreased systematically, and this cost was proportional to the dissimilarity between the target cue and the actual target. Using eye tracking, the authors further showed that degraded templates increased the number of locations observers fixated before finding the target and reduced the efficiency of target verification after fixation. Similarly, Lau et al. (2021) showed that target-distractor discriminability shapes template formation, with lower discriminability leading observers to develop higher-resolution target templates. The targets were repeatedly searched against distractors that varied in their visual similarity to the targets. Search among easily discriminable targets and distractors led to relatively coarse templates of the targets, while difficult search (which required target-similar distractors) led to the formation of higherresolution target templates. This is also consistent with the findings of Geng et al., (2017), and Yu and Geng (2019) that the target template can also be systematically shifted by distractor context. Geng et al. (2017) found that frequent exposure to highly target similar distractors can produced a narrower target representation. Building on these findings, Yu and Geng (2019) demonstrated that predictable distractor context caused a shift in the representation of the target away from the distractor features, whereas highly similar distractors also produce asymmetric sharpening of the target template. These changes made the targets stand out more from the distractors and enhanced the visual search. Besides target templates, there are also negative or rejection templates that can be used to help reject distractors by providing information about irrelevant features, as previously discussed. Arita et al. (2012) proposed that attention can be actively directed away from irrelevant items, providing one of the first demonstrations that observers can use distractor information to guide selection. Evidence from Carlisle and Nitka (2019) further suggests that both positive and negative templates can guide attention, however, there are mechanisms that may be different with negative templates than with the positive templates. (Carlisle, 2023). Together, these studies demonstrate that attentional guidance can use the information from both targets and distractors during visual search. However, it remains unknown whether such template-based biases in attentional selection also apply to the integration of multiple objects such as when ensemble representations are formed. Attentional templates could influence the processing of individual ensemble items, and items that match the target and distractor features could contribute differently to the computation of the perceived average, leading to systematic biases in ensemble estimates. Some previous work showed that attentional selection continues to influence perception following visual search trials (Rafiei et al., 2021). The study showed that information selected and rejected during visual search biased subsequent perceptual judgments. This suggests that attentional selection leaves persistent traces in perception. It remains unclear, however, whether these opposing effect of target selection and distractor rejection extend to carry over to judgements of the statistical summaries of ensembles.

Building on this question, our study investigates whether attentional templates, both templates for distractors and targets, jointly influence ensemble processing. To explore this, the two subsets of oriented lines within each ensemble display were oriented in different directions, one 15° clockwise, and the other 15° counterclockwise, from the global ensemble mean. Each ensemble judgment was preceded by 4 to 5 visual search trials where both the target and distractor colors were fixed so that representations of the same colors, allowing target-and distractor-related representations to develop across successive searches. We predicted that attentional templates formed during searches would bias subsequent ensemble processing. Specifically, if target and distractor representations differentially influence the weighting of ensemble items, perceived mean orientation should be systematically shifted depending on whether the target color, distractor color, or both reappeared in the subsequent ensemble display.

Experiment 1

Material & Methods Participants 25 observers participated in the experiment. 17 participants were undergraduates (17 females; M = 25.5 years, SD = 5.99, 8 males; M = 27.8 years, SD = 6.08) from the University of Iceland who participated in this experiment for course credit and 8 staff members from the University of Iceland participated the experiment for gift card (3000 isk). This sample size was equal to the study conducted by Williams et al. (2021). All participants provided written informed consent and were tested for colorblindness with Ishihara's test for color deficiency (Ishihara, 2004) before the experiment. All experiments were done in agreement with the local ethics committee from the University of Iceland and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Apparatus

Participants were seated 57 cm away from a 24-inch monitor (ASUS, VX248h, resolution: 1920 x 1080 pixels; refresh rate: 60 Hz). The experiment was carried out using Psychopy 2021.1.4 (Peirce et al., 2019) and stimuli were presented on a dark gray background (luminance = 20 cd/m²). The display was color-calibrated using a Cambridge Research Systems ColorCal MKII photometer.

Procedure

Each block consisted of 4-5 search trials and a single ensemble task where observers were asked to report the average orientation of the ensembles (See Figure 1). Participants were instructed to find the odd-colored target and report the location of the black dot (left or right) using the corresponding arrow keys (left/right arrow) on the keyboard. In the proceeding ensemble trial, participants were instructed to estimate the average orientation of the entire ensemble. Immediately after the ensemble display disappeared, a white adjustment line (line length: 2.5°; line width = 3 pixels) appeared at the center of the screen. This line served as a response tool and remained visible until the participant confirmed their adjustment by pressing the space bar. The initial orientation of the adjustment line was randomly selected on each trial from a uniform distribution between 0° and 179°. The entire experiment lasted around 80 minutes. The experiment was preceded by an initial training consisting of 10 blocks, but the data was not analyzed from this training. Participants could take breaks every 10 to 12 minutes. Each trial started with a 500 ms fixation cross followed by 4-5 search displays. Afterwards, the ensemble display appeared for 200 ms followed by an adjustment task which participants required to adjust the white line in the center of the display to match the average orientation of the entire ensemble from the previous display.

Stimuli

Each block began with a fixation on a white thin central cross (0.5° × 0.5°) for 500 ms, followed by four to five visual search trials and a single orientation ensemble display. In each visual search trial, nine diamond-shaped stimuli (1.5° × 1.5°) appeared on the screen: one target and eight distractors. Each diamond had a small black dot (0.1° × 0.1°) located either on its left or right corner. The target was defined by its color, which differed from that of the distractors. Four colors were selected to serve as potential target and distractor colors, corresponding to four positions on a 48 isoluminant (Witzel & Gegenfurtner, 2013 , 2015) DKL color wheel: 0, 12, 24, and 36 JNDs (red, green, yellow and purple). Distractor colors were always 12 JND away from the target color. Therefore, the experiment included eight color combinations (Target-Distractor) from the color wheel: 0-12, 12-24, 24-36, 36-48, 12-0, 24-12, 36-24, 48-36. All diamonds were presented at random locations within an invisible square on the screen. Stimulus positions were generated from two coordinate sets, each spanning -12° to +12° of visual angle in 2° increments. The x-and y-coordinate sets were independently shuffled on each trial. This ensured random but non-overlapping positions, while keeping all stimuli within a fixed display area. Following the search trials, an ensemble display consisting of twelve colored lines (2° × 0.3°) was presented for 200 ms. The spatial arrangement of the lines was determined using the same possible combinations as for the diamonds on the search trials. Two equally numbered subsets of lines made up this display (six lines per subset); the colors were determined by pairings of different colors. These colors were selected to create four different conditions: 1) the Both Repeat condition: the two subsets of lines contained the same colors as the targets and distractors from the preceding search tasks, 2) the Distractor Repeat condition: one of the two subsets contained the distractor color from the preceding search block and the second subset color was a new color, 3) the Target Repeat condition: one of the two subsets of lines had the target color from the preceding search block and the second subset color was a new color and 4) Control condition: both colors of the subset were new colors that were not presented on the preceding search task. For example, if the target color was purple and distractors were green, Target Repeat condition had purple and red, Distractor Repeat condition had green and yellow, Both Repeat condition had purple and green and lastly control condition red and yellow. Besides differing in color, the two subsets also had distinct average orientations following the design by Williams et al. (2021): One subset was oriented 15° clockwise (CW) from the global mean of the entire set, while the other subset was oriented 15° counterclockwise (CCW) from the global mean (see Figure 2). Within a subset, the orientation range spanned 30 degrees. The mean orientation of the overall set was selected from six predefined means: 15°, 45°, 75°, 105°, 135°, and 165° to ensure that the same global means were presented across all four conditions.

Discussion Experiment 1

Experiment 1 investigated how target and distractor templates formed during a visual search task affect the extraction of summary statistics. To analyze the effect of the templates simultaneously, observers completed search tasks which included 9 diamonds (one target and eight distractors) and 12 lines were shown in the ensemble display for 200 ms, right before the adjustment task. Perceptual biases were found in the experiment showing that orientation judgements of the participants were biased towards the target color or away from the distractor color, in other words, the encoded attentional templates formed during the search task affected observers' ensemble estimates. This indicates that the attentional templates formed during visual search remained even after the search task had ended and continued to shape how information was combined while performing ensemble perception tasks.

Our results showed that the perceptual bias was strongest in the Both Repeat (the target and distractor colors remain unchanged) condition, as indicated by the largest bias. In the Distractor Repeat and the Target Repeat condition both templates were active, but participants were only biased by the templates color which matched one of the subsets. However, in the Both Repeat condition participants were biased by both templates at the same time. The effect of using both templates simultaneously may lead to a larger bias in observers' averaging judgements. One possible explanation is that target enhancement and distractor suppression work simultaneously, each further enhancing the weighting of template-matching information in ensemble computations. Rather than working independently, the target enhancement and the distractor suppression appear to reinforce one another, resulting in a stronger bias when both templates are active together. In contrast, when neither color was previously seen (Control condition) no bias was observed. The conditions in which only one of the colors remained produced intermediate levels of bias. Notably, the Target Repeat condition yielded a stronger and statistically significant bias compared to the Distractor Repeat condition, which showed a smaller effect that did not survive Bonferroni correction. This finding may be attributed to the higher priority given to target information during visual search, and to the fact that target templates typically enhance attentional processing, whereas distractor templates can be used to guide attention away from irrelevant information. Importantly, previous studies have shown that the effects of negative-templates tend to be weaker than the effects of positive-templates (Arita et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2020). This imbalance in attentional guidance may therefore have transferred to the next ensemble task, leading to a stronger target-related than distractor-related bias. Overall, it can be concluded that previously formed attentional templates affected the subsequent ensemble estimation, the greatest bias being found when both the target-matching information and the distractor-matching information were present.

Additionally, no differences in the variability of ensemble orientation judgements were observed across conditions. The result showed that the effect of condition on CSD was not significant as reported. If observers were paying attention to a single color subset, the range of the orientations contributing to their estimate would have been smaller than if they were paying attention to orientations from both subsets. This is likely to result in less response variability in one-subset-based estimates than in estimates relying on the integration of information from both subsets (Khvostov and Utochkin, 2019; Khvostov et al., 2024). Nevertheless, no differences in response variability across conditions suggests that response variability was similar even though there was a template related bias in the perceived average orientation.

Experiment 2

Target and distractor templates from a search task can bias subsequent ensemble estimates. Our results from Experiment 1 suggest that observers' orientation averages were biased towards the target color and to a much weaker extent away from the distractor color. As a result, the estimated orientation averages were biased towards the average of the subset that contained the target color and away from the subset that contained the distractor color. Experiment 2 was thus designed to determine if these template-induced biases varied with the duration of the ensemble presentation. In Experiment 1, the ensemble display was presented for 200 ms. It has been shown that summary statistics can be extracted from briefly presented displays (Li et al., 2016), while the influence of items on ensemble judgments may encode differently across presentation times. With shorter presentation times, observers might not have enough time to distribute attention over the ensemble, and items in the template matching category might be given more attention. The longer the presentation time is, the more time the observers could process the rest of the items, which may minimize the template-matching subset bias. Or perhaps, if given more time to observe, the observers could continue to be biased toward template-matching items, leading to a stronger bias. Previous studies have examined how presentation duration affects biases in ensemble judgments, with mixed results. For example, Goldenberg et al. (2021) found that both positive and negative facial expressions were amplified in judgments of mean emotion. However, the amplification of positive expressions developed more slowly, requiring longer presentation times to reach a magnitude comparable to that observed for negative expressions. In contrast, Williams et al. (2021) compared ensemble presentation durations of 150 ms and 500 ms but did not find any differences in the magnitude of the VWM-related bias. These findings suggest that the influence of presentation duration on ensemble judgments may depend on the source of the bias. However, neither study directly addressed whether additional processing time strengthens biases arising from attentional templates established during visual search. We thus investigated whether attentional templates created during the visual search task affected subsequent ensemble estimation differently at a short (150 ms) and a longer (500 ms) presentation time in Experiment 2.

Methods

Methods 30 observers participated in the experiment (23 females; M = 25.6 years, SD = 5.63, 6 males; M = 27.1 years, SD = 4.40 and 1 nonbinary) from University of Iceland for course credit.

Participants

All participants provided written informed consent and were tested for colorblindness with Ishihara's test for color deficiency (Ishihara, 2004) before the experiment. All experiments were done in agreement with the local ethics committee from the University of Iceland and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Apparatus

Participants were seated 57 cm away from a 24-inch monitor (ASUS, VX248h, resolution: 1920 x 1080 pixels; refresh rate: 60 Hz). The experiment was carried out using Psychopy 2021.1.4 (Peirce et al., 2019) and stimuli were presented on a dark gray background (luminance = 20 cd/m²). The display was color-calibrated using a Cambridge Research Systems ColorCal MKII photometer.

Stimuli and Procedure

The procedure and the design of Experiment 2 was identical to Experiment 1. In Experiment 2, we manipulated the presentation time of the lines (150 ms vs 500 ms). To be able to equalize the number of trials (and keep the experiment length within a reasonable amount of time) in each condition we reduced the number of color combinations in Experiment 2 but the total number of blocks remained identical. Color combinations for targets and distractors that were used in Experiment 2 are: 0 -12, 36-24, 12-0, 24-36). The entire experiment lasted around 80 minutes. The experiment was preceded by an initial training phase consisting of 10 blocks but the data was not collected from participants during this training. Participants could take breaks every 10 to 12 minutes.

Results

On each block, participants completed an ensemble task which was preceded by 4-5 visual searches with identical target and distractor colors. As previously, this allowed us to test how prior attentional selection (target priming and distractor suppression) influenced perceived average orientation. These orientation judgments were performed under a short (150 ms) and a long (500 ms) presentation time across the same four within-subject conditions as in Experiment 1: Control, Distractor Repeat, Target Repeat, and Both Repeat. First, one-sample t-tests were conducted separately for each condition and collapsed across presentation times to determine if the biases toward or away from the subset were significantly different from zero. Results showed that in the Control condition there was no evidence of a bias, t(29) = -0.17, p = .87, 95% CI [-0.37, 0.33]. Additionally, in the Target Repeat condition, the observed bias was modest but significant before correction, t(29) = 2.24, p = .03, 95% CI [0.04, 0.79]. The Distractor Repeat condition did show a visibly small but not significant bias, t(29) = 1.79, p = .08, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.62]. Finally, results revealed a significant orientation bias in the Both Repeat condition, t(29) = 6.16, p < .01, 95% CI [1.14, 2.27]. This effect remained significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, indicating a reliable orientation bias in this condition (see Figure 5A). We next examined whether the magnitude of orientation bias differed across conditions and presentation times. A 2 (presentation time) × 4 (conditions) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on the average orientation adjustment bias. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of condition, F(3, 87) = 17.3, p < .001, η²G =.17, indicating that orientation bias differed across experimental conditions but there was no main effect of presentation time, F(1, 29) = 0.9, p = .34, η²G = .004, nor a significant interaction between condition and presentation time, F(3, 87) = 0.38, p = .76, η²G =.004. A Bayesian model comparison supported these results. The model including Condition alone received the strongest support, whereas adding presentation time or the Condition × Presentation Time interaction did not provide a better fit to the data. Thus, the Bayesian analysis provided further evidence that variation in orientation bias was primarily associated with condition rather than presentation time. Together, these results indicate that observed orientation biases were influenced by the four different conditions, while presentation time had no impact on the bias magnitude (see Figure 5B). To examine how stimulus duration and attentional templates influence the precision of ensemble orientation estimates, we analyzed the variability in the adjustment responses using the circular standard deviation (CSD) across conditions and presentation times (e.g., whether repeating the target or distractor color affected the variability of participants' orientation responses). A 2 (presentation time) × 4 (condition) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on the circular standard deviation of orientation responses. Discussion Experiment 2

The goal of Experiment 2 was to determine whether changes in the presentation time of the ensemble display modulate the bias exerted by the previously formed attentional templates from the search task, as observed in Experiment 1. The primary finding was that presentation time did not significantly affect the magnitude of the observed orientation biases. The orientation bias significantly differed between conditions as in Experiment 1. Participants' orientation judgements were more biased in the Both Repeat condition than in the other conditions, which was also evident in Experiment 1. replicating the pattern observed in Experiment 1. This suggests that the influence of attentional templates on ensemble judgements is relatively stable across the presentation durations tested, rather than becoming stronger with additional processing time. As in Experiment 1, response variability did not differ significantly between conditions, although it was generally lower at 500 ms than at 150 ms. Furthermore, although the super-additive effect reached significance only at 500 ms, there was no significant interaction with presentation time, suggesting that the magnitude of this effect did not reliably differ between presentation durations.

General Discussion

Across two experiments, we investigated whether judgments of mean orientation in ensembles of colored lines were biased by attentional templates formed during a preceding visual search task. If attentional templates of distractors and targets are formed during the visual search task and are still active in the ensemble task, we expected orientation averages to be biased when subsets of the ensemble match the active template colors. We implemented four conditions: 1) the Target Repeat condition where one of the two subsets of lines had the target color from the preceding search block and the second subset color was a new color, 2) the Distractor Repeat condition where one of the two subsets contained the distractor color from the preceding search block and the second subset color was a new color, 3) the Both Repeat condition where the two subsets of lines contained the same colors as the targets and distractors from the preceding search tasks, and 4) the Control condition where both colors of the subset were new colors that were not presented on the preceding search task. In the Both Repeat condition there are two biases simultaneously working together: one repulsive bias away from the distractor color and the other one is attractive bias towards the target color. Consequently, this condition exhibited the strongest bias in both experiments compared to the Target or Distractor Repeat conditions. Repeating the target color alone (Target Repeat condition) resulted in a larger bias than the distractor color alone (Distractor Repeat condition). However, only the Target Repeat bias remained significant when correcting for multiple comparisons, while the Distractor Repeat bias, although visible, was not significant when using Bonferroni correction. We examined how well the Both Repeat condition could be predicted by the combined effect of the Distractor Repeat and the Target Repeat conditions. The results showed that the bias was significantly larger when both colors remained identical than the additive prediction by summing the bias from the Target Repeat and the Distractor Repeat conditions in Experiment 1. In Experiment 2, the Both Repeat bias was numerically larger than the sum of the individual biases at both presentation times, although the difference reached statistical significance only at 500 ms. Furthermore, the different presentation times in the Experiment 2 showed no effect on the magnitude of the biases. The results of our study are in line with Williams et al. (2021). They showed that reports of mean orientation were biased toward the mean orientation of the same-color subset that participants needed to maintain in VWM. Critically, however, the present experiments involved active attentional templates for color subsets that were formed during a preceding visual search task. Accordingly, both of our experiments showed that when the target color reappeared in the ensemble display, reports of orientation averages were more biased toward the orientation of the target-color subset. A related effect of task-relevant color information was demonstrated by Brady and Alvarez (2011). Participants were asked to report the size of the target circle from the memorized array of colored circles. It was found that responses were biased by the sizes of the circles that shared the color of the target. Importantly, their findings indicated that when participants were asked to report the size of an individual item rather than an ensemble average, task-relevant color grouping affected the representation of individual items. Here, we build on that relationship by examining how attentional priority to a task-relevant color can affect the subsequent ensemble estimate. This template-driven weighting is in line with the evidence that ensemble perception is sensitive to the priority of items. Iakovlev and Utochkin (2020) showed that more perceptually salient items demonstrate a stronger influence on mean estimates than less salient items. Despite our focus on top-down attentional priority, and their focus on bottom-up perceptual salience, our results provide new evidence that unequal weighting can also occur when item priority is determined by top-down attentional goals. Specifically, items that matched the target template had more weight in the ensemble estimate, while items that matched the distractor template seemed to have less weight in the ensemble estimate. However, we cannot explain the strongest bias in the Both Repeat condition simply by unequal weighting. The bias in the Both Repeat condition was larger than the sum of the biases in the two conditions (Target Repeat and Distractor Repeat), suggesting that the simultaneous presence of both the target and distractor colors may reflect an interaction between target enhancement and distractor suppression.

Our data suggests that target-related templates bias ensemble judgements to a greater degree than distractor-related templates. This difference may be explained by how attentional templates work in visual search. Target templates can direct attention to task-relevant information, while distractor-related information can be used to suppress or reject irrelevant information (Gaspelin & Luck, 2018). While both target and distractor can be equally informative, positive templates could be more effective on attentional guidance, and negative templates have been found to be less effective and more task-dependent (Arita et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2020; Carlisle, 2023). The strong target-related bias found in the current study can be explained by this asymmetry between positive and negative templates, as the target template created in the visual search task might have had a greater carry-over effect for the subsequent ensemble judgement than the distractor template. At the same time, orientation judgments were not fully determined by the repeated (template-matching) colors, as evidenced by the intermediate biases in the Target Repeat and Distractor Repeat conditions. This is consistent with a weighted sampling mechanism. Attentional templates modulate how strongly each subset contributes to the ensemble representation, without the influence of the remaining items being completely overridden.

In Experiment 2 we manipulated the presentation times of the ensembles. In the present study, presentation time could affect template-based biases in two possible ways. If the additional processing time allows participants to continue prioritizing the subset where the template-matching occurs, the bias may become even larger at 500 ms. On the other hand, if the participants pay attention to the template-matching subset first, the extra time at 500 ms may redistribute attention across the entire ensemble, reducing the initial bias. We found no difference in the strength of the bias between the short and long presentation times. The template-based bias was already apparent at 150 ms and showed a little change at 500 ms, indicating that further processing time did not significantly alter the extent of the bias. Thus neither of the two predicted effects was observed: longer presentation time neither reinforced the template-based bias nor weakened it through a redistribution of attention across the ensemble display. On the other hand, Williams et al. (2021) investigated whether the memory-matching bias was due to the strategic allocation of attention using short and long ensemble presentation times. They assumed that voluntary attentional allocation would be more difficult at shorter presentation times, and that a strategically driven bias should, accordingly, grow stronger with longer presentation times. However, this strategic account was specific to their VWM task, in which participants could strategically attend to memorymatching items to improve their memory performance. Previous studies, however, indicate that the effects of attention on ensemble perception may vary depending on the presentation time (Haberman & Whitney, 2010; Li et al., 2016; Goldenberg et al., 2021). Goldenberg et al. (2021) found that while positive and negative faces were amplified in mean emotion judgements, longer presentation times were needed for the bias towards positive faces to reach the level of the bias towards the negative faces. Our results, however, indicate that template-based biases formed from earlier visual search are relatively stable over the different presentation times used here. The difference might be due to different types of attentional priority tested in the two studies or to the different presentation times employed.

Additionally, we tested whether the bias in the Both Repeat condition was merely an additive combination of the biases measured in the Target Repeat and the Distractor Repeat conditions. In the Target Repeat condition, the target color from the previous search task was present in the ensemble while the distractor color was replaced by a new color. In contrast, in the Distractor Repeat condition, the distractor color remained unchanged while the target color has changed. In the Both Repeat condition, both the target and distractor colors from the preceding search task were used to create the two subsets in the ensemble display. If target-related and distractor-related biases contribute independently, then the Both Repeat condition should be equal the sum of the biases observed in the Target and the Distractor Repeat conditions. In Experiment 1, the Both Repeat condition showed a super-additive pattern. This pattern emerged at the two presentation times (200 ms and 500 ms). However, at the shorter presentation time (150 ms) we did not observe a statistically significant super-additive pattern in the Both Repeat condition. Although a weak numerical trend towards the super-additivity was visible, the effect could not reach significance. However, the significance pattern should be treated with some care, since it is not always indicative of the difference in the magnitude of super-additivity for the two presentation times. The occurrence of the combined effect of the target and the distractor templates is suggested by the numerical trend at the 150 ms, suggesting that the combined effect is already present at the shorter presentation times, and not only at longer ones. The super-additive pattern may be explained by the fact that, when both the target and distractor colors were repeated, both feature representations for the target and distractor were re-activated concurrently in the same ensemble display. The present results do not directly support this mechanism but may have been the case if target-related enhancement and distractor-related rejection had acted together to increase the bias, rather than each having an independent effect. Finally, we examined the effects of the target and distractor templates on the variability of participants' responses. Response variability was examined because attentional templates might affect not only the mean of the ensemble estimates, but also the precision of those estimates. Attentional templates would change the contribution of the subsets, for instance: if one subset is prioritized over the other subset, prioritized subset may contribute more to the final ensemble orientation estimate. This may influence the systematic bias and the uniformity of participants' responses between trials. In both experiments, CSD is not significantly affected by the condition, although we observed systematic biases depending on the condition. This suggests that attentional templates influenced ensemble estimates mostly via a shift in the direction of the bias and not via measurable changes in response precision. Similar results were reported by Williams et al. (2021), noting that memory-matching biases impacted ensemble judgements without showing a significant influence of display type on CSD. These results indicate that ensemble estimates can be systematically altered by attentional prioritization without necessarily improving the response precision. In Experiment 2, longer presentation time led lower response variability than shorter presentation time. However, the level of template-based bias was not significantly altered with the length of the presentation. Tiurina et al. (2024) reported similar findings, showing that longer ensemble presentation times improved precision without changing the spatial weighting biases. Zepp and Dubé (2025) also showed that longer exposure times were associated with higher ensemble response precision. Together, these findings suggest that attentional-template repetition and presentation duration affect different aspects of ensemble judgments: template repetition systematically biases orientation estimates without significantly affecting response variability, whereas longer presentation times improve response precision without significantly altering the bias.

Limitations and future directions

In this study, templates were defined based on color. However, in real-world scenarios, target and distractor templates can be defined by various other features, such as shape, size, orientation, or motion. Our task required attentional selection guided by the color, and participants were expected to estimate average mean orientation of the lines. This implies that such attentionbased prioritization on one feature dimension may affect ensemble judgements over another dimension. Different features, however, may have different effectiveness in directing attention and in influencing later ensemble judgements. Consequently, it is not known if the bias is a general property of attentional templates or if it is specific to color-based attentional selection. This finding is consistent with the notion that template-based attentional weighting is applied across feature dimensions if similar biases were seen with templates defined by other features. Instead, interdimensional differences might suggest attentional templates' effects vary with feature type for ensemble perception. A related question that might be addressed in deeper research is whether templates based on other feature dimensions result in similar biases in the ensemble estimates.

Another limitation of this study is that we only compared the 150 ms and 500 ms presentation times. The findings indicate that attentional templates influence ensemble perception; however, this design cannot determine at which cognitive stage the effect arises. The present findings cannot determine whether attentional templates affect how the ensemble is encoded or whether they affect later processes, such as ensemble representation in working memory or the decision process involved in generating the response. The bias observed at 150 ms does not prove that it arises during early perceptual processing but does show that it can appear in short presentation times. Future studies might initially examine the time course of attentional template effect, by testing a wider range of presentation times. Independent manipulation of encoding, retention, and response stages could then be employed to help to resolve the questions of when template-related biases occur.

Conclusion

To conclude, our results show that ensemble perception can be modulated by attentional templates, such that items do not necessarily contribute equally to the estimated average. These templates were established during prior visual search. Importantly, it was found that the bias produced by target-related templates was stronger than the bias produced by distractor-related templates, suggesting that these two types of attentional templates might differ in their effect on ensemble estimates. These biases were already evident at short presentation times and did not increase with longer presentation times, showing that the influence of attentional templates was already present under brief presentation conditions. Moreover, when both target and distractor templates were present, their combined influence could exceed the effects observed for the individual templates. This finding suggests that target enhancement and distractor suppression may not operate independently but can jointly shape ensemble representations. More broadly, our results indicate that the global impression we form of a visual scene is influenced not only by the information that is currently available but also by attentional templates carried over from preceding visual search.
