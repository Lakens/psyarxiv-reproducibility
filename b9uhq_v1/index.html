Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 9 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

This study presents a conceptual replication of Popov, Popov, and Nastran (2023) (Study 2), examining whether workplace greenery is associated with work engagement in a novel occupational and seasonal context. Ninety-four live-in shiftworkers (55.3% women) employed in Norwegian assisted living facilities for adults with intellectual disabilities and/or autism spectrum disorders completed an online survey during autumn 2024. Work engagement was measured with the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale, and three forms of workplace greenery were assessed: window views of nature, access to outdoor natural environments, and the presence of indoor plants. Analyses of covariance, controlling for job demands and appreciation of natural beauty, largely replicated the original pattern of results. Access to outdoor natural environments-both during breaks and while accompanying service users-was significantly associated with higher work engagement, whereas window views and indoor plants showed no reliable effects. Gender did not consistently moderate these associations. The findings provide evidence that the association between outdoor nature exposure and work engagement may extend to live-in shiftworkers and an autumn context.

Introduction

Over the past decades, researchers have increasingly examined how exposure to natural environments influences employee well-being at work. Workplace greenery, such as views of nature, access to outdoor areas, and the presence of indoor plants, has been linked to reduced stress, greater job satisfaction, and better health outcomes (e.g., Lottrup, Grahn, & Stigsdotter, 2013; Largo-Wight, Wlyudka, Merten, & Cuvelier, 2017). From a theoretical standpoint, this can be understood in relation to Stress Reduction Theory (SRT), which posits that exposure to natural elements evokes rapid affective and physiological recovery from stress, offering a restorative counterbalance to demanding work environments (Ulrich et al., 1991), whereas Attention Restoration Theory (ART) suggests that nature's "soft fascination" replenishes depleted directed attention, thereby supporting cognitive functioning in occupational settings (Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989). Furthermore, the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model distinguishes between job demands and job resources, suggesting that high job demands are associated with exhaustion, while a lack of job resources is associated with disengagement. Both processes may possibly contribute to burnout. In contrast, job resources can promote positive outcomes such as motivation and well-being (Demerouti, Bakker, Nachreiner, & Schaufeli, 2001). Within this framework, access to nature at or near the workplace can be conceptualized as a potential job resource.

Despite promising findings, relatively few studies have focused on positive indicators of well-being such as work engagement, a motivational state characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption (Schaufeli, Salanova, González-Romá, & Bakker, 2002).

Moreover, replication studies remain scarce in this field, raising questions about the robustness and generalizability of initial findings.

A recent contribution by Popov et al. (2023) directly addressed this gap by examining workplace greenery and employee well-being in two seasonal and occupational contexts. In their Study 1 (Ljubljana, winter), no significant effects were found, whereas in Study 2 (Novi Sad, spring), outdoor exposure-through window views and the possibility to take breaks in natural settings-was positively associated with higher work engagement and lower burnout. Indoor plants showed no effect in either study, and gender did not consistently moderate these associations. These findings suggest that outdoor natural exposure may be a particularly important resource for employee well-being, but that contextual and seasonal factors could play a role.

The present study provides a conceptual replication of Study 2 from Popov et al. (2023) regarding work engagement in a novel occupational and seasonal context.

Specifically, we examined the relationship between workplace nature exposure and work engagement among employees working live-in shifts in assisted living facilities in Norway during the autumn season. The responsibilities of live-in shiftworkers include providing therapeutic care and supporting service users with activities of daily living, monitoring and documenting service users' needs and progress, and planning and implementing individualized interventions. The role also involves fostering a safe, structured, and predictable living environment while managing challenging situations through preventive and de-escalation strategies. Live-in shiftworkers (24-hour rotations across several days) represent a unique occupational group, as they spend extended periods of time at the workplace and are at elevated risk for strain and health problems associated with long shifts (Dall'Ora, Griffiths, Ball, Simon, & Aiken, 2015). Replicating findings in this group is therefore important for both theoretical generalization and applied occupational health.

Consistent with the original study, we investigated three forms of workplace greenery: 1.

Window views of nature during the workday.

2. Access to outdoor natural environments during the workday.

3. Presence of indoor plants during the workday.

As in Popov et al. (2023), we also tested whether gender moderated these associations, while controlling for job demands and appreciation of natural beauty, two factors previously linked to well-being at work.

Based on the original findings, we formulated the following replication hypotheses: • H1: Employees with window views of nature during breaks or while accompanying service users will report higher work engagement than employees without such views.

• H2: Employees with access to outdoor natural environments during breaks or while accompanying service users will report higher work engagement than those without.

• H3: The presence of indoor plants will not be associated with work engagement.

• H4: Gender will not significantly moderate the effects of workplace greenery on work engagement.

By conducting this replication in a different occupational and seasonal context, this study contributes to testing the robustness and boundary conditions of prior findings. It also extends the limited literature on workplace nature exposure and work engagement, particularly in the under-studied group of live-in shiftworkers. Because live-in shiftworkers may have more opportunities to interact with natural environments than the office workers studied by Popov et al. (2023), we expected associations in the same direction and of at least similar magnitude.

Appreciation of natural beauty.

The four-item Natural Beauty subscale from the Engagement with Beauty Scale (Diessner, Solom, Frost, Parsons, & Davidson, 2008) assessed sensitivity to and emotional engagement with natural beauty (𝛼 = .84).

Statistical analyses

Analyses of covariance were conducted in jamovi (Version 2.3.28) to test whether work engagement differed across levels of nature exposure while controlling for job demands and appreciation of natural beauty. Each form of exposure (view, outdoor access, indoor plants) was analyzed separately. Gender was included as a moderator in a second step. Normality and homogeneity assumptions were inspected; detailed diagnostics and robustness checks are provided in the https://osf.io/yuz73/.

Replication success was defined as conceptual consistency with the original effect direction and significance pattern reported by Popov et al. (2023). H1 was tested by examining the main effects of window views of nature (a) during breaks and (b) while accompanying service users. H2 was tested by examining the main effects of access to outdoor natural environments (a) during breaks and (b) while accompanying service users.

H3 was tested by examining the main effect of indoor plants. H4 was tested by examining the interaction effect of gender and all other independent variables listed above. Work engagement was the dependent variable for all analyses. Two-tailed tests were used with alpha level set to 0.05, uncorrected. Family-wise error correction was not utilized since all hypotheses were specified in advance.

Overall, the replication was partially successful. Consistent with Popov et al. (2023), employees who reported opportunities to spend time outdoors in natural environments-both during breaks and while accompanying service users-showed higher levels of work engagement. No significant effects were found for window views or indoor plants, and gender did not reliably moderate any relationships. The pattern of results is therefore consistent with the original spring study's central finding that outdoor exposure, rather than indoor greenery, was most strongly associated with work engagement.

These results provide further evidence that access to outdoor natural environments may represent an important job resource within the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model (Demerouti et al., 2001). At the same time, several factors temper this conclusion. The study relied on a small, self-selected sample (N = 94), which limits statistical power and the precision of effect estimates. In particular, very few respondents lacked access to nature, reducing sensitivity for detecting view-related effects. The cross-sectional and self-report design also precludes causal interpretation and may be influenced by response biases. More engaged employees might be more inclined to take outdoor breaks rather than the other way around.

Although such constraints mirror those of the original study, they highlight the need for future research using experimental or longitudinal designs to establish causality.

Despite these limitations, the replication demonstrates strong conceptual correspondence with Popov et al. (2023). Across the two studies, outdoor exposure was associated with moderate effect-size estimates (𝜂2 ≈ .06-.13), whereas findings for indoor plants and gender moderation were generally null or inconsistent. The overall pattern is consistent with a positive association between work engagement and workplace exposure to natural environments, particularly physical access to outdoor settings, across the contexts examined.

From a methodological perspective, this replication followed the original operationalizations closely while making justified contextual adaptations for live-in shiftwork. The inclusion of job demands and appreciation of natural beauty as covariates, as in the original design, enhances comparability. Conclusion

In sum, this partial conceptual replication provides evidence that aspects of Popov et al. (2023)'s findings may extend to a different occupational group and an autumn context.

The results suggest that opportunities for outdoor exposure in residential workplaces warrant further investigation as a potential job resource. At the same time, there remains a need for more systematic replication efforts using larger and more diverse samples, experimental design, and preferably incorporating more objective measures of exposure and engagement. Establishing reproducible evidence about the relationships between natural environments, employee motivation, and well-being remains a key step toward a more cumulative and theory-driven environmental psychology.

For live-in shiftworkers undertaking demanding rotations, the possibility to step outside into natural surroundings could serve as a low-cost means of replenishing energy and motivation. The weaker or absent associations for window views and indoor plants may be compatible with Attention Restoration Theory (ART) and Stress Reduction Theory (SRT), although the present study did not directly measure the restorative mechanisms proposed by these theories. Short visual contact or passive indoor elements may not provide the "soft fascination" and "being away" experiences required for attentional recovery. Importantly, this replication extends prior work to a new context and season. Popov et al. (2023) collected their data in spring from a mixed-industry sample; the current data were gathered in autumn from live-in shiftworkers in health and social care. Observing similar patterns under these distinct conditions provides evidence that the association between outdoor nature contact and work engagement may occur across different occupations and seasons. The autumn findings indicate that the association can also be observed under conditions of more limited daylight and colder weather, although seasons were not compared experimentally.
