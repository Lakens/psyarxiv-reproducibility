To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Abstract

Saccadic and fixation measures are widely used in psychiatry and neurology, yet no published centile norms exist for expressing a new recording as a centile or deviation score. We derived norms from GazeBase v2.0 (EyeLink 1000, 1000 Hz, left eye), using Round 1 recordings from 322 healthy young adults aged 18-46, most (284/322) aged 18-25. Twenty-nine features were extracted from three tasks-horizontal saccades (HSS), random oblique saccades (RAN), and central fixation (FXS). Norms are empirical centiles (5th-95th). We tested age and sex effects with generalized additive models for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS), assessed calibration with split-half resampling (50 random splits), and estimated 4-month test-retest reliability in 136 returners (ICC(2,1), absolute agreement). Median main-saccade latency was 121 ms in predictable HSS and 199 ms in unpredictable RAN, with median peak velocity 527 °/s and 440 °/s, respectively. Six of the 29 features differed by sex after Benjamini-Hochberg correction (q < .05), notably fixation small-saccade rate (female median 0.27/s vs. male 0.13/s). Split-half calibration was near-nominal except for ceiling and floor variables. Four features had good reliability (ICC ≥ .75), 8 moderate, and 17 poor (ICC < .50); fixation duration and small-saccade amplitude were least reliable (ICC = .11). At retest, HSS latency increased systematically (+53.0 ms), whereas RAN latency was unchanged. The HSS norms are therefore first-exposure norms.

These frozen v1 norms allow new recordings on the same EyeLink tasks to be expressed as centiles and deviation scores, but their clinical validity requires prospective validation in patient samples.

Introduction

Clinical neuroscience increasingly asks not whether a patient group differs from a control group on average, but where an individual patient falls relative to healthy variation. Normative modeling formalizes that question: a reference distribution is estimated in healthy individuals, and each new individual is expressed as a centile or deviation (z) score against it (Marquand et al., 2016; Rutherford et al., 2022; Wolfers et al., 2018). The approach has produced population brain charts for structural imaging across the lifespan (Bethlehem et al., 2022) -on a far larger scale, with more than 100,000 participants across the lifespan, while the present norms cover 322 young adults over about eight well-supported years -and it is attractive in psychiatry precisely because diagnostic categories are heterogeneous -two patients with the same diagnosis may deviate on entirely different measures, a pattern that group-average comparisons erase.

Eye movements are a natural candidate for the same treatment. Saccadic latency, amplitude, gain and peak velocity (the amplitude-peak velocity relation known as the main sequence; Bahill et al., 1975; Gibaldi & Sabatini, 2021), fixation stability and microsaccades (Martinez-Conde et al., 2004) can be quantified with millisecond and sub-degree precision, differ, on average, from healthy values in several psychiatric and neurological conditions (Gooding & Basso, 2008; Hutton & Ettinger, 2006), and can be recorded with identical hardware across sites. Yet interpretation still depends on ad hoc control groups: each study recruits its own comparison sample, with its own age range, device settings and feature definitions, so a latency of 210 ms may be unremarkable in one paper and pathological in another. No published set of centile norms -reference values that allow a new individual recording to be expressed as a centile or deviation score -exists for saccadic and fixation features. The closest large-sample reference, Bargary et al. (2017), reported normative means, distributions and reliabilities for 21 pro-saccade, anti-saccade and smooth-pursuit measures in 1,058 healthy young adults, but published no centile tables and no scoring procedure, and its measures were derived from different tasks and hardware. Antisaccade norms are outside the scope of this paper. Bargary et al. (2017) recorded pro-saccades, anti-saccades and smooth pursuit in 1,058 healthy young adults and reported normative means, distributions and test-retest reliabilities for 21 oculomotor measures, showing that the profile of measures for an individual constitutes a personal "oculomotor signature" (Bargary et al., 2017). That study reports test-retest reliabilities for its measures and substantial sex differences, both of which the present norms build on. It does not, however, provide what a scoring reference requires: it publishes no centile values for any measure, no procedure for expressing a new recording as a centile or deviation score, and no fixation or microsaccade measures, and its recordings were made with different hardware (JAZZ-novo infrared oculography) and a different task battery. The gap this study addresses is therefore specific: frozen centile norms, with calibration and test-retest reliability, for a defined set of saccadic and fixation features recorded with the EyeLink hardware family used in many clinical laboratories. GazeBase makes such norms feasible. It is a public, longitudinal dataset of 12,334 monocular recordings from 322 college-aged participants, collected with an EyeLink 1000 at 1000 Hz over nine rounds spanning 37 months (Griffith et al., 2021). Its size supports centile estimation, its repeated rounds support test-retest reliability, and because the recordings were made with the same EyeLink hardware family used in many clinical laboratories, norms derived from it can, in principle, be applied to new clinical recordings of the same tasks. GazeBase has also spawned derived resources, such as AnnotatedGazeBase, which provides annotated and imputed recordings from the random saccade task (Siradj, 2026); the norms reported here are built from the original unimputed recordings.

This study had three aims: (1) to construct and freeze centile norms for a defined set of saccadic and fixation features from three GazeBase tasks; (2) to validate the norms internally by split-half calibration and to test whether age-or sex-specific norms are required; and (3) to quantify 4-month test-retest reliability feature by feature, so that users know which features were stable over 4 months and which were not. The study is deliberately methodological: it establishes and validates a measurement reference, and makes no claim about any clinical group.

Method

Dataset and Participants

We used GazeBase v2.0 (Figshare record 12912257; MD5 checksum verified on download). All recordings are monocular (left eye), sampled at 1000 Hz with an EyeLink 1000 in desktop-mount configuration, with calibration and validation before each task (Griffith et al., 2021). Norms were built from Round 1 only, one recording per participant per task, using Session 1 only. The normative sample comprises all 322 Round 1 participants: healthy young adults aged 18-46 years (mean 21.9, median 21), 171 male and 151 female. The age distribution is dense at 18-25 years (n = 284) and sparse above 26 years (n = 38, 1-6 participants per year of age); the norms therefore describe young adults, and this constraint is carried through the interpretation. Test-retest analyses used the 136 participants who returned for Round 2, approximately 4 months later. Returners did not differ from the 186 participants who did not return on any of the 27 Round 1 eye-movement features tested (all p > .05, Mann-Whitney U tests) or in sex distribution (p = .40, χ² test); they were slightly younger (median 20 vs. 21 years, p < .001, rank-biserial r = .22). Round 1 uses Session 1 only (966/966 recordings); no participant contributes Session 2 data to Round 1. A 'round' is a study visit (approximately 4 months apart); a 'session' is a repeat recording within a visit. Across the 27 uncorrected Round 1 eye-feature comparisons, the smallest p-value was .096 (HSS saccade duration). Exact per-participant retest intervals are not recorded in the public release, so no interval distribution can be reported; the interval is approximately 4 months (Griffith et al., 2021).

Tasks

Three of the seven GazeBase tasks were used. In the horizontal saccade task (HSS), the target stepped between ±15° and 0°, giving 100 target steps per recording; step direction and timing are predictable, so the task elicits anticipatory saccades (Joiner & Shelhamer, 2006; Shelhamer & Joiner, 2003). In the random saccade task (RAN), the target stepped to unpredictable oblique positions, giving 99 target steps; anticipation is largely impossible. In the fixation task (FXS), participants fixated a central target for 15 s. HSS and RAN together provide a consistency check: a correct target-locked pipeline must show far more anticipation in HSS than in RAN, and it did (anticipatory rates 0.41 vs 0.04).

Feature Extraction

Features were extracted from the EyeLink online parser's event labels (fixation, saccade, blink) rather than from a re-implemented velocity algorithm, so that the pipeline can be applied unchanged to clinical EyeLink recordings exported the same way (for how event-detection approaches differ in their outputs, see Andersson et al., 2017; Holmqvist et al., 2011; Larsson et al., 2013; Nyström & Holmqvist, 2010; Salvucci & Goldberg, 2000). The EyeLink online parser classifies samples into fixations, saccades and blinks using velocity, acceleration and motion thresholds; Griffith et al. (2021) do not report the thresholds or the EyeLink software version used for GazeBase, and the public release contains no EDF files, so the recording-time parser settings cannot be recovered from the file headers. The features in this study are therefore the parser's event labels exactly as distributed in the GazeBase CSV release. For reference, the manufacturer's documented default ('cognitive') configuration is a velocity threshold of 30°/s, an acceleration threshold of 8000°/s² and a motion threshold of 0.15° (Nyström & Holmqvist, 2010); whether the GazeBase recordings used these defaults was not reported and could not be verified, so the norms are conditional on the GazeBase parser configuration as distributed.

Degenerate segments (duration < 8 ms or amplitude < 0.1°) and jump artifacts (mean velocity > sample velocities rather than the maximum, because single-sample spikes otherwise produced physiologically impossible values (up to 22,000 °/s). Peak-velocity values are therefore specific to this 95th-percentile definition and should not be compared directly with maximum-velocity estimates. We did not quantify how much higher maximum-definition values would be. For each target step in HSS and RAN, the main saccade was defined as the largest saccade within [-300, +600] ms of the step -a window chosen to include anticipatory saccades before the step and the full range of reactive latencies after it, while keeping each saccade assigned to a single step; latency is the interval from step to main-saccade onset and can be negative for anticipatory responses. Saccade segments with amplitude < 2° are referred to throughout as parser-detected small saccades (hereafter 'small saccades'); the 2° bound is the authors' operational cut-off for parser segments, not a microsaccade criterion from the literature. They are segments of the EyeLink online parser's output, not the product of a velocity-based microsaccade detector, and are not equivalent to microsaccades as defined by dedicated detection algorithms (Engbert & Kliegl, 2003; Engbert & Mergenthaler, 2006; Otero-Millan et al., 2008 , 2014). Twenty-nine features were defined a priori (fixed before the reliability and group analyses; the study was not preregistered): 12 for HSS, 12 for RAN and 5 for FXS (Table 2). Per-participant summaries are medians across trials unless stated otherwise.

Quality Control Exclusions

Five of 966 task recordings were excluded under rules fixed before analysis (the study was not preregistered), each documented in an exclusion log: three HSS recordings with median gain < 0.5 (the same participants' RAN gain was normal, indicating a task-specific problem rather than a participant-level one) and two RAN recordings with median peak velocity > 1000 °/s (physiologically impossible). Feature-level sample sizes in Table 2 reflect these exclusions, and the smaller n for FXS small-saccade features (n = 274) reflects the 48 participants who produced no small saccade in 15 s of fixation -an observed property of the data, not an exclusion.

Norm Construction

The frozen norms are empirical centiles (5th, 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, 90th, 95th) with means and standard deviations, computed on Round 1. In parallel, each feature was modeled with GAMLSS (Rigby & Stasinopoulos, 2005)-a generalization of the LMS approach to reference centile curves (Cole & Green, 1992)-comparing normal and Box-Cox t families crossed with null, age, sex and age + sex location models; the best-fitting combination was selected by GAIC with penalty k = 2. Only the location (mu) parameter was modelled with predictors; scale and shape (sigma, nu, tau) were held intercept-only, and the mu link is the identity, so age slopes are in original units per year. Age and sex effects were tested by likelihood-ratio tests against the same-family model without the term, with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction across the 29 features (q < .05). Where a sex effect survived correction, sex-stratified centiles are provided (

Table 3) and should be used in preference to the pooled values. Because the age range is narrow and concentrated in young adulthood, v1 norms are pooled across age; GAMLSS age slopes are reported in Supplementary Table S2 but are not used to age-adjust the frozen table. The frozen table is version 1 (v1). A revision would be triggered by a corrected error in the frozen values, an extension to a wider age range or a new device, or additional rounds of data; any future revision will carry a new version number and will not overwrite v1.

Internal Validation: Split-Half Calibration

Calibration was assessed with 50 unstratified random split-halves (seed 123): centiles were estimated in one half of the sample, and the proportions of the held-out half falling below the 5th and 10th centiles and above the 90th and 95th centiles were compared with the nominal 5%, 10%, 10% and 5%. The Monte Carlo standard error of the reported calibration proportions is at most 0.007 (0.7 percentage points). A well-calibrated norm yields proportions close to nominal.

Test-Retest Reliability

Round 2 features were extracted with the identical pipeline. Ettinger et al. (2003) reported reliability of saccadic, fixation, and pursuit measures over shorter intervals. For each feature we report the paired mean difference (paired t-test), Pearson correlation, ICC(2,1) (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979) -two-way random effects, absolute agreement, single measurementwith percentile-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (2000 resamples, resampling unit = participant pairs), Bland-Altman limits of agreement, and the proportion of Round 2 values falling inside the Round 1 [5th, 95th] normative interval. ICC values were graded following Koo and Li (2016): < 0.50 poor, 0.50-0.75 moderate, 0.75-0.90 good, > 0.90 excellent. Pearson r and Bland-Altman limits of agreement, paired t-statistics, and median paired differences with Wilcoxon tests are reported in Supplementary Table S3. A small-saccade pair required at least one small saccade at both rounds: 110 of 136 returners produced small saccades at Round 1, 107 at Round 2, and 89 at both; all other features use 134-136 pairs. The n = 89 small-saccade estimates are therefore conditional on producing a small saccade at both rounds; they describe producers, not all returners. The approximately 4-month interval means that these coefficients index temporal stability under real retest conditions -including any state change over the interval -rather than pure measurement noise.

Software and Reproducibility

Feature extraction was performed in Python 3.12 (NumPy 1.26.4, pandas 2.1.4), the versions under which the extraction was verified to reproduce the deposited feature tables bytefor-byte (the Python version of the original 2026-11-09 run was not recorded); norm construction, GAMLSS modeling, calibration and reliability analyses in R (version 4.3.3; gamlss 5.5.5, gamlss.dist 6.1.11, ggplot2 4.0.3, patchwork 1.3.2). The frozen norm table, exclusion log and the full pipeline -extraction, norm generation, and scoring that converts a new same-task EyeLink recording into centiles and deviation scores -are provided as supplementary material, with the resulting norms summarized in Tables 1 2 3 4, in line with calls for standardized automated oculomotor analysis (Komogortsev et al., 2010).

Scoring a new recording. To score a new same-task EyeLink recording, extract the same 29 features with the same pipeline (gazebase_extract_r12.py), then convert each feature value to a centile and a deviation (z) score against the frozen Round 1 norms (gazebase_zscore.R, provided as supplementary material). The grid is first mapped to z-scores through the standard normal quantile function, and each value is interpolated linearly in z-space between neighbouring grid points; values outside the grid are extrapolated with the end-segment slope and z is clipped to ±4. Where Table 3 provides sex-specific norms, the sex-specific grid is used; otherwise the pooled grid is used. Ceiling or discrete features (daggered in Table 2) have tied grids and no deviation score is defined for them. Worked example: a new HSS recording with main-saccade latency 121 ms scores at the 50th centile (z = 0.00) against the pooled norm; a female recording of 109 ms scores at the 50th centile of the female norm; a latency of 500 ms extrapolates beyond the grid and is clipped to z = +4. A RAN latency of 199 ms sits exactly on the pooled 50th centile (z = 0.00); scored sex-specifically, the same 199 ms is the 36th centile for a woman (z = -0.36) and the 59th for a man (z = +0.23), because the female median is 204 ms and the male median 195 ms.

Results

The Normative Sample and Headline Values

Table 1 summarizes the sample and Table 2 gives the frozen norms for all 29 features.

The contrast between the two saccade tasks is the dominant pattern. In predictable HSS, the median latency was 121 ms and 41% of main saccades were anticipatory (latency < 80 ms; Fischer & Ramsperger, 1984); in unpredictable RAN, median latency was 199 ms with only 4-6% anticipation. Median amplitude was 28.4° in HSS (for 30° target steps) and 12.5° in RAN; median peak velocity was 527 °/s and 440 °/s, and median gain 0.949 and 0.968, respectively.

During 15 s of central fixation (FXS), participants made a median of 8 fixations, with median fixation duration 781 ms and median fixation dispersion 0.18°. Features labelled 'small saccade' are saccade segments below 2° in amplitude as classified by the EyeLink online parser, rather than microsaccades identified by a velocity-based detector.

Age and Sex Effects

Age effects were small, as expected in a sample concentrated at 18-25 years; across the wider lifespan, saccadic latency and dynamics change systematically with age (Irving et al., 2006; Munoz et al., 1998). Sex effects survived false-discovery-rate correction for 6 of the 29 features, and sex-stratified norms for those features are given in Table 3 (see also Bargary et al., 2017, for sex differences across a large healthy-sample oculomotor battery). The largest effect was in fixation: among the 274 participants who produced at least one small saccade, median small-saccade rate in FXS was approximately twice as high in female as in male participants (0.27 vs 0.13/s, p = 0.004). The 48 participants who produced none were disproportionately male (31 of 171 men vs 17 of 151 women), so the sex difference partly reflects differential zeroinflation as well as a rate difference among producers. HSS latency was longer in male participants (median 138 vs 109 ms, p = 0.008), largely because female participants anticipated more (rate 0.45 vs 0.39); notably, the RAN latency sex difference ran in the opposite direction and was much smaller (female 204 vs male 195 ms, p < 0.001). Where Table 3 provides sexspecific values, pooled centiles should not be used for individual scoring. No age effect survived false-discovery-rate correction (smallest q = .097; Supplementary Table S2), supporting the decision to pool norms across age.

Split-Half Calibration

Calibration was close to nominal for almost all features: across features, 4.0-6.2% of held-out participants fell below the 5th centile (nominal 5%), 8.4-11.3% below the 10th (nominal 10%), 9.5-11.4% above the 90th (nominal 10%) and 4.9-6.4% above the 95th (nominal 5%). The exceptions were variables whose distributions are bounded or discrete by construction -the main-saccade detection rate (median 1.0, a ceiling variable), the RAN anticipatory rate (a mass at zero) and the FXS fixation count. For these variables, the centiles describe the observed distribution faithfully, but their tail proportions cannot match nominal values; they are flagged in Table 2 and should not be converted into deviation scores. Calibration was assessed within this sample; whether the centiles are equally calibrated in a new sample (transportability) was not tested.

Test-Retest Reliability Over Approximately 4 Months

Table 4 and Figure 1 give reliability for all 29 features in the 136 returning participants (89-136 complete pairs per feature). Four features reached the good range: HSS main-saccade duration (ICC 0.81, 95% CI 0.74-0.87), RAN peak velocity for 10-20° saccades (0.80, 0.73-0.86), RAN peak velocity (0.76, 0.67-0.83) and RAN latency (0.75, 0.64-0.82). Eight features were moderate (0.50-0.75), including HSS peak velocity (0.69), HSS fixation dispersion (0.70), RAN gain (0.73) and FXS fixation count (0.62). Seventeen features were poor (< 0.50); the lowest were FXS fixation duration (0.11) and small-saccade amplitude (0.11-0.16 across tasks).

Median ICC was 0.47 (HSS), 0.50 (RAN) and 0.41 (FXS). One poor value requires interpretation rather than alarm: the main-saccade detection rate sits at its ceiling (almost every participant scores 1.0 at both rounds), so there is almost no between-person variance for an ICC to capture. FXS fixation duration rose by a mean of +230 ms, but the median paired change was only +55 ms (95% CI -88 to +208 ms; Wilcoxon p = .35), and the mean is carried by a few participants with shifts of several seconds; we do not interpret the mean as a group-level change.

The clearest reliability finding is a systematic change, not noise. In HSS, mean latency increased by 53.0 ms from Round 1 to Round 2 (113.9 to 166.9 ms; p = 1.3 × 10 ¹ ) and the ⁻ ⁰ anticipatory rate fell from 0.41 to 0.26 (p < 0.001); only 71% of Round 2 anticipatory rates fell inside the Round 1 normative interval, the lowest coverage of any feature. 40) is lower than its consistency ICC (0.48): part of the disagreement is the systematic shift itself. By contrast, for the remaining features, 81-99% of Round 2 values fell inside the Round 1 [5th, 95th] interval, most of them between 85% and 95%. The HSS latency and anticipatory-rate norms in Table 2 are therefore first-exposure norms: they describe a first encounter with the task, not performance after practice. Discussion This study delivers what eye-movement research in psychiatry has lacked: a frozen, splithalf-calibrated set of reference centiles for saccadic and fixation features, built on 322 healthy young adults recorded with standard clinical hardware, together with an explicit account of which features can support individual-level interpretation. Three conclusions follow. First, reliability -not group difference -should decide which features lead a deviationscore analysis. RAN latency, RAN peak velocity and HSS saccade duration combine good 4month stability with tight normative distributions; they are the features on which an individual's deviation is most likely to reflect a genuine individual difference (cf. Bargary et al., 2017).

Fixation duration and small-saccade amplitude (see Rolfs, 2009; Rucci & Victor, 2015), by contrast, vary so much within the same person over 4 months that a single-session deviation score on them is mostly a measurement of the day, not of the person -the reliability paradox for individual differences (Hedge et al., 2018).

They remain legitimate as group-level or exploratory measures, but they should not headline an individual-level claim. Second, predictability changes what a latency measures. The disappearance of anticipation at retest -a 53 ms latency shift confined to the predictable task -shows that HSS latency and anticipatory rate index familiarity and strategy as much as oculomotor physiology (for latency as a decision variable, see Carpenter & Williams, 1995). Studies that compare a clinical group tested once against norms must therefore match participants on task exposure, or prefer the unpredictable task, whose latency was stable over the 4-month interval studied (ICC 0.75, no mean shift). This is a concrete, checkable prescription that follows directly from the reliability data -the kind of guidance a norms paper is meant to provide. For longitudinal use, a follow-up recording should be scored against these first-exposure norms only for features without an exposure effect -the good-reliability features RAN latency, RAN peak velocity, RAN peak velocity for 10-20° saccades and HSS saccade duration are the safest for tracking change; HSS latency and anticipatory rate require an exposure-matched comparison, or the unpredictable task, whose latency showed no exposure shift. Separating a novelty effect from a lasting practice effect would require retesting at two or more intervals, which the present tworound design cannot do; such retesting is a planned follow-up analysis using the later GazeBase rounds. Third, sex-specific norms are not optional for six of the features. A two-fold sex difference in fixation small-saccade rate means that pooled centiles would systematically misclassify one sex. Table 3 removes that bias for the six affected features; users should treat the stratified table as the default wherever it exists. Frozen norms and ad hoc local controls serve different purposes. Frozen norms give every new recording the same reference, so deviation scores are comparable across studies and sites; they are preferable when the aim is individual-level scoring or cross-study comparison and the new sample matches the norm sample (young adults, EyeLink 1000, same tasks). A local control group is preferable when the new sample differs from the norm sample in age, hardware or task parameters, or when the research question is a group contrast within a single study; local controls also absorb site-specific calibration and setting differences that frozen norms cannot.

The two are complementary: a study can report deviation scores against the frozen norms and, where a local control group exists, check that the same pattern appears against it.

The intended use of these norms is prospective: a new participant -in our program, a patient and a matched control recorded on an EyeLink with the same three tasks -is scored against the frozen centiles, yielding a profile of deviation scores across features rather than a single group contrast. Whether such profiles carry clinical information (for example, separating first-episode patients from healthy individuals, or tracking change in features without exposure effects) is an empirical question for the clinical study, and we deliberately do not prejudge it here. Normative references in neuroimaging followed this sequence: the reference and its validation came first, clinical applications second (Marquand et al., 2016; Bethlehem et al., 2022). These norms come from a single site and a single device model, so they should be treated as provisional until externally validated -in our program, against hospital recordings of adult patients and matched controls made with the same tasks on an EyeLink 1000 Plus, and against recordings from a second site.

Limitations

The norms describe healthy young adults (healthy as described in Griffith et al., 2021; no additional screening was applied in this secondary analysis). Ages 18-25 are well supported; above age 26, the sample thins to 1-6 participants per year of age, so centiles must not be extrapolated to middle-aged or older adults, and pediatric use is out of scope. Participants were college-aged volunteers at a single site, recorded with one device model and one parser configuration; applying the norms to other hardware or event-detection settings requires cross-device checking. The 4-month retest interval makes the reliability estimates conservativeshort-interval reliability will be higher -but it also means genuinely state-sensitive features cannot be distinguished from noisy ones on these data alone. Small-saccade features rest on the EyeLink parser's segments with a 2° amplitude cut-off (the authors' operational cut-off, not a literature microsaccade criterion) and are not equivalent to microsaccades as defined by dedicated detection algorithms (Engbert & Mergenthaler, 2006; Otero-Millan et al., 2008 , 2014); 48 of 322 participants produced no FXS small saccade at all. Finally, this is a methodological reference study: it contains no clinical sample, and no statement in it should be read as evidence that deviation scores diagnose or predict any disorder. One further caveat: the retest subsample was slightly younger than those who did not return (median 20 vs. 21 years), so the reliability estimates may generalize most directly to younger adults; the two groups did not differ on any baseline eye-movement measure, however, which argues against substantial selection bias in the retest estimates.
