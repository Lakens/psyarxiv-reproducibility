Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Introduction

One of the most fundamental tensions in empirical social science is the gap between the observational data researchers collect and the causal claims they make out of it. Plato already stipulated that " [e]verything that becomes or changes must do so owing to some cause; for nothing can come to be without a cause" (cited in Plato, 2000, p. 13). As humans are apparently bound to think in terms of causality, considering why things happen is an essential part of the human nature (Asendorpf, 2012, p. 391; Kaltenpoth, 2024, p. 1). Pearl et al. (2016, p. xii) put the problem technically: "the fundamental question at the core of a great deal of statistical inference is causal; do changes in one variable cause changes in another, and if so, how much change do they cause?". Yet the traditional language of statistics offers no direct route to answering such questions. Its tools were designed, as Ronald Fisher (1922, p. 311) already declared, for "the reduction of data", not for recovering the inference process that generated them. The issue at hand is exacerbated for " [a]nalysts seek causation, but the data, on their own, only communicate associations" (Elwert, 2013, p. 247). To overcome this constraint, experimental designs have been established as the current 'gold standard'. The conventional methodological solution of randomized experimental designs, however, often falls short due to physical (i.e., real-life constraints which cannot be modeled experimentally), economic, ethical or other constraints (Levy, 2023, p. iii; Pearl et al., 2016, p. xi; Rohrer, 2018, p. 27). Faced with these limitations, researchers heavily rely on observational data paired with statistical control of third variables as a "cornerstone of developing causal models" (Levy, 2023, p. 3). More precisely, regressing dependent variables (i.e., outcomes) on independent variables (i.e., exposures) while conditioning on (i.e., controlling for) measured covariates thought to account for potential confounding. In this context, the choice of control variables in practice "is determined by norms in the domain and by the variables available in the data set" (Rohrer, 2018, p. 28). As Tu et al. (2008, p. 1) show by examining certain statistical paradoxes, "the association between two variables can be reversed, diminished, or enhanced when another variable is statistically controlled for". Furthermore, even selecting the right variables might lead to wrong conclusions by certain kinds of measurement error (Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016, p. 1).

In this highly sensitive field of observational data, the effects of methodological biases are central for they potentially lead to systematic errors that abide with increasing sample sizes (Cole et al., 2010, p. 417). According to Oeberst & Imhoff (2023, p. 4), the key findings from years of psychological research show that fundamentally biased information processing is "not only ubiquitous [...] but also a conditio humana" (see e.g., Abelson et al., 1968; Feldman, 1966; Gawronski & Strack, 2012; Nickerson, 1998). Based on this observation, collider bias plays a crucial role for it "seems less intuitive" (Rohrer, 2018, p. 35) and is generally "difficult to grasp" (Pearl et al., 2016, p. 41). To put it simply, collider bias occurs if an analysis is conditioning on a variable that is a common effect of two or more causally preceding variables (Elwert & Winship, 2014, p. 31; Pearl et al., 2016, p. 39; Rohrer, 2018, p. 34). It is especially consequential when it operates undetected within an established research design. As its effects emerge systematically rather than randomly, genuine signal becomes indistinguishable from 'noise' without an explicit causal framework. In the absence of such a framework, researchers face a fundamental challenge in drawing valid causal inferences from observational data without making assumptions explicit (Wysocki et al., 2022, p. 2).

The present thesis examines this methodological problem in a specific and consequential empirical domain: the psychology of ideal partner preferences. As introduced by Eastwick & Neff (2012, p. 667) most individuals are able to readily describe the qualities they would like in an ideal romantic partner. These descriptions often vary considerably, with some people valuing a caring and supportive partner who provides affection during difficult times, while others place greater importance on traits such as confidence and social competence. Whether these individually stated preferences predict whom people end up with, and how positively they evaluate them, is the central question of the partner preference-matching literature. The preference-matching hypothesis states that people evaluate romantic partners more favorably the more closely a partner's attributes match their stated ideals (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 5). If one rates intelligence as highly desirable, is one more likely to evaluate an intelligent partner positively? Does this variation in preferences explain why people are romantically drawn to some individuals rather than others? These questions are central to influential theoretical frameworks such as the Ideal Standards Model (Fletcher et al., 1999 (Fletcher et al., , 2000;; Simpson et al., 2001) that have motivated decades of empirical investigation (Campbell & Stanton, 2014, p. 485). Despite this longevity, empirical support for the matching hypothesis has remained surprisingly inconsistent, and concerns about the causal validity of the underlying research designs have been largely unaddressed (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 5; Gerlach et al., 2019, p. 1).

Against this backdrop, Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 5) conducted the most comprehensive investigation of ideal partner preference-matching to date. Partnering with the Psychological Science Accelerator (PSA), they collected data from N = 10,358 participants across 43 countries and 22 languages, systematically evaluated four distinct analytic approaches to measuring preference-matching, and concluded that idiosyncratic matching effects exist but are modest in size and highly dependent on how they are measured. Their effort is laudable in many respects: the project was implemented as a preregistered report, draws on a sample of unparalleled scale and cross-cultural breadth, and makes the full dataset publicly available. The latter provided the condition without which the present re-analysis would not have been possible. At the same time, the impressive amount of data cannot resolve a structural shortcoming that is built into the sampling design itself. The primary analysis which is dedicated to considering the matching hypothesis utilized a sample of established couples and singles rating their (most desired) partner. As indicated earlier and described in much more detail in the theoretical framework, this configuration reflects conditioning on a collider variable. Stated ideal preferences and perceived partner attributes both influence romantic evaluation, which in turn determines relationship formation as a common effect. Romantic evaluation and the relationship status are therefore colliders inducing corresponding collider bias. Conditioning on them, as every study using (most desired) partners necessarily does, opens potentially spurious paths between ideals and partner attributes before any analysis begins. In other words, the analysis presupposes a selection event which represents a world where high romantic evaluation is given by construction. An impressively large sample conditioned on a collider variable does not yield less biased estimates; it yields the same structural bias with greater precision. That may even lead readers to endorse the wrong conclusions more confidently.

The present thesis operationalizes this critique by addressing the following research question: "To what extent does collider bias distort the predictive validity of ideal partner preferences in the Eastwick et al. (2024) data analysis?".

To approach this question, the primary regression analyses of Eastwick et al. (2024) are replicated as a validated benchmark. Subsequently, a hierarchical multivariate extension is developed to analytically consider the collider bias configuration. Finally, both results are compared and assessed regarding potential predictive validity and valid causal inference. The overall aim of this thesis is thus to develop an analytically rigorous approach to deal with the partner preferencematching hypothesis. The evaluation of the analysis conducted by Eastwick et al. (2024) revealed several additional sources of bias which primarily need to be addressed to properly account for collider bias in a subsequent analysis. Based on this observation, four hypotheses guide the empirical analysis. The theoretical baseline, formally H0, restates Eastwick et al.'s (2024, p. 5) preference-matching hypothesis as described above, predicting positive and significant interaction effects between partner and ideal trait ratings across all 35 attributes for the original regression analysis. As Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 48) put it, H0 states that ideal partner preference-matching "indeed have predictive power" regarding romantic evaluation. Extending the baseline, the first hypothesis, H1, is based on the observation that background characteristics such as age and sex, as well as several scales of individual differences, operate as causally antecedent confounders of both attribute ratings as well as of the dependent measure itself (i.e., romantic evaluation). Accordingly, H1 predicts that the inclusion of demographic and individualdifference covariates will produce significant effect sizes in the extended hierarchical regression analysis.

As the primary regression analysis conducted by Eastwick et al. (2024) is implemented in a bivariate trait-by-trait fashion, the second hypothesis, H2, posits that simultaneously entering all 35 ideal preferences and partner attributes into a multivariate regression model will substantially attenuate all estimated coefficients relative to the Eastwick et al. (2024) estimates. Because rated attributes are intercorrelated, an attenuated effect would reflect inherent intertrait-confounding. In other words, each trait-by-trait model is potentially confounded by either trait-specific mediation effects or causally antecedent mutual influences such as normative desirability standards. Based on the methodological modification, the third hypothesis, H3, predicts that even after full hierarchical multivariate adjustment, idiosyncratic preference-matching effects will cluster near zero or turn insignificant. This hypothesis is consistent with (at least) two theoretical arguments that cannot be distinguished within the utilized sample itself. Either collider bias arising from conditioning on (most desired) partners constitutes a structural property of the sample that full multivariate regression adjustment cannot correct or the study-design itself is limited to detect idiosyncratic matching effects in the first place. Together, H1, H2, and H3 test analytically two distinct components of the collider bias structure: what statistical adjustment can recover by properly incorporating all measured causally upstream variables; and where the sampling design imposes a ceiling on valid inference that no statistical model can compensate.

The thesis proceeds as follows. Chapter 2 introduces directed acyclic graphs as a framework for causal reasoning and the mechanics of collider bias, before applying both to the specific causal structure of partner preference research in Chapter 3. Chapter 4 translates this theoretical framework into a set of methodological implications for the Eastwick et al. (2024) research design and outlines the hierarchical multivariate extension developed in response. Chapter 5 describes the method, including the replication procedure, the model specification, and the analytical strategy. Chapter 6 presents the results. Chapter 7 discusses the findings in relation to the methodological framework and the broader implications for the preference-matching literature.

Theoretical framework

One solution to the causal inference problem in observational research described in the introduction is not to abandon the aspiration of causal claims altogether, but to make underlying assumptions explicit and formally transparent. In this context, graphical causal models offer a "direct and powerful way of thinking about full causal systems and the strategies that can be used to estimate the effects within them" (Morgan & Winship, 2014, p. 77).

2.1

Directed acyclic graphs: framework for causal reasoning Developed primarily by computer scientist Judea Pearl (1985 Pearl ( , 1988 Pearl ( , 1993 Pearl ( , 1995 Pearl ( , 2000)), directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) have increasingly become synonymous with graphical causal models to provide "visual representations of qualitative causal assumptions" that "encode researchers' expert knowledge and beliefs about how the world works" (Elwert, 2013, p. 246). DAGs are able to reveal what was otherwise omitted. Informal theoretical commitments by researchers are thereby translated into a nonparametric graphical notation from which statistical implications can be systematically derived. The nonparametric feature indicates that DAGs neither require a specific variable distribution (e.g., normal, Poisson), nor restrict the functional form of the direct effects such as linear, nonlinear etc. (Elwert, 2013, p. 248; Morgan & Winship, 2014, p. 94). Moreover, no magnitude of causal effects is assumed in the DAG framework. The wide applicability is especially helpful to answer the question introduced in the previous section. Concretely, " [w]hat third variables need to be controlled for? Which third variables can be ignored? And in which situations will statistical control worsen causal inference?" (Rohrer, 2018, p. 28).

Drawing from Elwert (2013, pp. 248-266), Morgan and Winship (2014, pp. 77-101), Pearl (2000, pp. 65-106), Pearl et al. (2016, pp. 35-52) and Rohrer (2018, pp. 27-40) the functionality of DAGs can be described as follows. A DAG consists of two basic elements: nodes (originally called 'vertices'), representing variables, and directed edges (i.e., single-headed arrows), representing direct causal effects.

An arrow from A to B asserts that a direct intervention on A would change B (in time). Critically, missing arrows carry equal weight. They encompass "the strong assumption of no direct causal effect between two variables for every member of the population", called the "strong null" hypothesis of no effect (Elwert, 2013, p. 248). This is a point that applied researchers often overlook. As Elwert (2013, p. 248) insists, "in contrast to conventional social theory, which focuses on justifying what relationships do exist, DAGs insistently redirect the analyst's attention to justifying what arrows do not exist". It is precisely these "exclusion restrictions" that enable the identification of causal effects (Pearl, 2000, p. 101). Taken together, adding arrows to an existing set of variables never helps to isolate the causal effect while adding variables might help.

DAGs are acyclic by design, meaning no variable can be its own ancestor. This enforces temporal consistency: the future cannot cause the past. To address variables that influence themselves in a different point in time, the temporal order is explicitly considered by adding another variable indicating repeated measurement. Childhood intelligence, for instance, will likely influence intelligence measured as an adult. Consequently, intelligence is split up into childhood and adult intelligence depicted as two separate nodes (variables) within the same DAG. Despite the fact that any data-generating process can become increasingly complex, causal models can be decomposed into three elementary causal structures. Firstly, a chain in the form of X → Z → Y transmits a genuine causal effect from X to Y through Z, whereas X, Y and Z can represent either single variables or a set of multiple variables. In this context, a single variable Z is called a mediator variable for it mediates a causal effect of interest from X to Y.

Mediator variables should generally not be controlled for to account for their causal function within the chain formation. There are exceptions, such as estimating the direct effect of X on Y by mediation analysis (for an introduction, as well as the distinction of mediator and moderator variables, see Baron & Kenny, 1986; Hayes, 2017; Rohrer et al., 2022). However, controlling for mediator variables usually leads to overcontrol bias and potential underestimation of the causal effect of X on Y (Rohrer, 2018, p. 38). The second elementary structure is called fork, depicted as X ← Z → Y, and generates a so called spurious (i.e., non-causal) association between X and Y through their common cause Z, introducing the canonical structure of confounding. If potentially confounding variables are unobserved (i.e., unmeasured), they are typically denoted by U (Elwert, 2013, p. 249). In some cases, common-cause confounding by an unobserved variable is represented graphically by a bi-headed dashed arrow between the affected variables (Rohrer, 2018, p. 29). Idiosyncratic factors that affect only a single node and have no effect on any other variable are usually omitted from the graphical representation. Such factors are conceptualized as exogenous non-common causes or independent error terms and generally do not affect causal associations because they do not open additional paths between variables. Lastly, an inverted fork in the form of X → Z ← Y, represents the structure of a common effect. Z in this case is called collider variable and caused by both X and Y (i.e., two arrows are pointing in Z). Crucially, in an unconditional analysis, Z blocks the path and induces no association between X and Y. The inverted fork structure is examined in more detail in the next section.

As Elwert (2013, p. 249) summarizes, "these [three] structures correspond exactly to causation, confounding, and endogenous selection" which represent the only sources of observable association in any dataset. However, causal models are usually comprised of a large variety of these elementary structures. In this context, causal and noncausal (spurious) paths are distinguished in the following way.

A causal path is one along which all arrows point consistently away from the exposure and toward the outcome. This corresponds with a (multiple) chain and thus transmits a genuine causal effect. A noncausal path contains at least one arrow pointing in the opposite direction. It may still transmit statistical association, but that association does not represent a causal effect. In this sense, a causal DAG is defined as a DAG that "include[s] all common measured and unmeasured causes of any pair of variables" (Spirtes et al., 1993 (Spirtes et al., /2001;; cited in Elwert, 2013, p. 249). This rather "bold claim" needs to be carefully investigated and tested "against the data as far as possible" (Elwert, 2013, p. 249). Concerning terminology, variables which are directly caused by a given variable are called children while variables directly or indirectly caused by a given variable are called its descendants. Vice versa, direct causes of a variable are called parents, and all direct and indirect causes of a variable are called ancestors.

The formal tool that unifies all three elementary structures is d-separation, which was introduced by Pearl (1988). D-separation is a graphical criterion for determining when a path between two variables transmits association and when it is blocked. In other words, d-separation indicates whether two variables are likely dependent or definitely independent (Pearl et al., 2016, p. 46). A path is d-separated (i.e., blocked) if it contains either a conditioned non-collider (a confounder or mediator that has been controlled for) or an unconditioned collider (an inverted fork that has not been controlled for).

A path is otherwise d-connected (i.e., open, unblocked). Pearl et al. (2016, p. 36) correspondingly demonstrate that the structure of a causal graph, combined with observational data, makes it possible "to predict quantitatively the results of interventions without actually performing them". Those assumptions lead to the backdoor criterion (Pearl, 1993). A set of variables Z satisfies the backdoor criterion for the causal effect of exposure X on outcome Y if Z blocks (i.e., closes) every non-causal backdoor path (i.e., every path that enters Y with an arrow) while containing no descendant of X (Pearl, 1993; Pearl, 2000, p. 79). The transmission of an association is thus cut by statistically controlling a node, the purpose of the statistical control of third variables which was introduced in the previous chapter. If such a set Z exists and is measured, the causal effect of X on Y can explicitly be determined from observational data. For an introduction of various control practices, such as stratification, adjustment (i.e., conditioning on or controlling for a third variable in regression analysis) and matching, see Rohrer (2018, pp. 32-33). It is important to note, that a variable that is statistically associated with both exposure and outcome is not necessarily a confounder for it may instead be a collider. Indicated by the inverted fork structure, controlling for it actively induces collider bias, as will be investigated in the next section.

Finally, causality is always "conditional on the validity of the stated causal model" (Elwert, 2013, p. 247). As Kaltenpoth (2024, p. 15) notes, "many processes with different causal relationships are consistent with the observed distribution". Consequently, the DAG does not eliminate the need for theoretical commitments. Rather, it makes those commitments visible and refutable against the data to the extent that their implications are empirically accessible. In this sense, assumptions "cannot be corrected unless they are noticed and understood" (Elwert, 2013, p. 270). Moreover, Rohrer (2018, p. 31) concludes that "the assumption that one has correctly captured the causal web and successfully blocked all back-door paths is in most cases a very strong one, because it posits that no relevant variables have been omitted from the causal graph. Whether this is plausible or not needs to be evaluated on a case-by-case basis".

2.2

Collider bias: mechanism and consequences

As indicated in the introduction, of the three structural sources of association identified in the previous section, collider variables play a crucial role for being intellectually less accessible and potentially elusive (Pearl et al., 2016 , p. 41, Rohrer, 2018, p. 35). Moreover, collider bias can occur at any stage of the research process, due to conditioning on an outcome, one of its descendants, intermediate variables, and even pretreatment variables (Elwert, 2013, p. 262). Crucially, two steps are required in order for bias to be avoided: "First, one must be aware of the collider variable, and second, one must be able to run analyses that are not conditional on the collider" (Rohrer, 2018, p. 36). The second step, however, presupposes that the collider variable has been measured (Kaltenpoth, 2024, p. 114). The DAG framework is especially supportive in increasing awareness and shows that conditioning on a collider proves systematically wrong in structural configurations of inverted forks (i.e., X → Z ← Y).

The critical feature of colliders, derived from d-separation, is their asymmetric behavior under conditioning. As mentioned, without conditioning, a collider blocks the path between its parents. As Pearl et al. (2016, p. 41) demonstrate, X and Y are dependent conditional on Z even when X and Y are unconditionally independent. This leads to the introduction of "unknown amounts of bias into the estimate of the total causal effect […] which was, in fact, correctly estimated when we did not control for them" (Kaltenpoth, 2024, p. 4). The reversal from independence to conditional dependence is essentially the definition of collider bias. In the DAG framework, these conditional or spurious association between X and Y are indicated by a dashed line without arrowheads (X---Y, sometimes also depicted as bi-headed arrows, Elwert, 2013, p. 250). Corresponding biases potentially increase when measurement error of third variables is taken into account. This issue is not further discussed in the paper, however, according to Westfall and Yarkoni (2016, p. 1) "the false positive rate can reach very high levels, approaching almost 100%" and counterintuitively "increases when sample sizes are large".

There are many different terms for collider bias. It has been labelled collider stratification bias (Greenland, 2003), selection bias (Hernán et al., 2004), endogenous selection bias (Elwert & Winship, 2014), Berkson's (1946) bias, explaining away effect (Kim & Pearl, 1983) or conditioning bias (Morgan & Winship, 2007). Recently, Lu et al. (2023, pp. 407-408) further differentiated collider bias by distinguishing between collider restriction bias and collider adjustment bias. The first form occurs when analysis is restricted to a subgroup defined by a collider (or its descendant; including stratification of subgroups while separately reporting the results). In this way, collider restriction bias usually "results from the sampling procedure" (Rohrer, 2018, p. 36). The latter bias is induced through inclusion of a collider (or its descendant) in a regression model. In this sense collider restriction bias can be treated as a form of (endogenous) selection bias while collider adjustment bias is rather understood as a specific overadjustment bias (Lu et al., 2021; Schisterman et al., 2009). Furthermore, several special cases of collider bias exist such as survivorship bias (Hudomiet et al., 2021), sample truncation bias (Hausman & Wise, 1977), non-response bias (Lin et al., 1999), ascertainment bias (Elwert & Winship, 2014), attrition bias (Rohrer, 2018, p. 36) and Heckman (1974 Heckman ( , 1979) ) selection bias. For a comprehensive systematic review of collider bias terminology and more detailed distinctions, see Levy (2023). The conclusion, however, reveals that, despite few specific exceptions, "all selection bias is collider bias" (Levy, 2023, p. 296).

In the context of this paper, the distinction of Lu et al. (2023) is adopted, nonetheless, the remedy to both biases is similarly "not to condition on such variables" (Elwert, 2013, p. 252). Though cautious of overcontrol bias, this paper's focus lies on collider restriction bias. The mechanism becomes concrete through example. Pearl et al. (2016, pp. 41-42) illustrate it with a scholarship scheme that awards places to students who are either musically talented (X) or academically high-achieving (Y). In the general population, musical talent and academic achievement may be wholly uncorrelated. Yet if one restricts analysis to scholarship recipients (Z), and thus conditions on a collider, a spurious negative association emerges without any causal effect. Among recipients, a student with low academic achievement implies high musical talent in average, and vice versa. Pearl et al. (2016, p. 41) propose to understand conditioning on a third variable as "filtering by the value of the conditioning variable". In this sense, by conditioning on Z, comparisons to cases in which Z takes the same value are limited. For the value of Z depends on X and Y (i.e., common effect), comparing cases where Z takes a specific value are compensated in the way that any change in the value of X must account for a change in the value of Y.

Otherwise, the value of Z would likewise have to change, which is impossible while controlling for it. After all, this mechanism induces a spurious correlation between X and Y. To put it generally, this procedure implies the "nonrepresentativeness of samples" which is often overlooked in scientific research (Rohrer, 2018, p. 36). The exemplified issue intensifies for "selecting on a collider always leads to bias, but this bias may not occur in the stratum that coincides with our analytical sample" (Levy, 2023, p. 105). This essentially means, that causal distortions due to collider bias may be invisible within the restricted dataset itself, detectable only from a point outside of it. If the analytical sample is itself defined by conditioning on a collider, the bias becomes structural, not statistical. The primary remedy in this case is the reconstitution of the sample. If this is not possible, however, spurious effects can be mitigated by conscientiously closing corresponding backdoor paths (Elwert, 2013, pp. 256-257; Hernán et al., 2004, pp. 620-621; Shpitser et al., 2010, p. 530). Taken together, the practice of making causal inferences on the basis of observational data "depends crucially on awareness" (Rohrer, 2018, p. 38). For more illustrative examples and further explanations see Elwert (2013), Elwert & Winship (2014 ), Morgan & Winship (2014), Pearl (2000), Pearl et al. (2016) and Rohrer (2018), amongst others.

3

The case of partner preference research and the Eastwick et al. (2024) dataset

Building on the theoretical explanations of the previous chapter, the aim of this section is to introduce the research field of partner preference research to exemplify potential collider restriction bias effects in the data collection as well as analysis process. A long-standing question in relationship research is whether people actually end up with partners who match what they say they want (Gerlach et al., 2019, p. 1). When asked, people tend to describe their ideal romantic partner in terms of attributes (i.e., traits) like physical attractiveness, warmth, intelligence, or financial security (Buss, 1989; Eagly & Wood, 1999; Eastwick et al., 2014; Fletcher et al., 1999; Hill, 1945). These so-called ideal partner preferences are frequently utilized to examine the corresponding preference-matching hypothesis as described in the Introduction. Based on the observation, that positive evaluation of romantic partners is a key predictor of health and mortality (Robles et al., 2014), its assumption is central to influential theoretical frameworks such as the Ideal Standards Model (Fletcher et al., 1999), which proposes that people incorporate ideal personality traits when evaluating both potential and current partners (Gerlach et al., 2019, p. 2).

Despite decades of research, empirical support for the matching hypotheses is surprisingly "murky" (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 5). Moreover, concerns of causal inference in this research field remain largely untackled (Campbell & Stanton, 2014, p. 485; Gerlach et al., 2019, p. 1). As mentioned in the introduction, Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 43) conducted the most comprehensive investigation of preference-matching to date. Partnering with the Psychological Science Accelerator (PSA), they collected data from N = 10,358 participants across 43 countries and 22 languages. Individuals in established romantic relationships as well as single individuals (who evaluated the person they most desired as a partner) took part. The primary planned analyses used multilevel models that accounted for the nesting of participants within the 60 samples which have been included in the dataset.

All participants similarly rated 35 partner attributes in terms of their ideal preferences and then rated their (most desired) partner on the same attributes, before reporting a romantic evaluation of that person as the dependent measure (by answering six questions such as: 'I am romantically interested in…'). The attributes covered a range of normatively desirable traits, including physical attractiveness, personality traits, and relational qualities, as well as four negatively worded Big Five items (e.g., critical, quarrelsome; anxious, easily upset). The full list of attributes is presented in Table 1 in the Result section.

This design allowed the authors to test the preference-matching hypothesis using four distinct analytic approaches of varying methodological rigor. These analytical approaches resulted from a systematic review performed by Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 6) themselves and is supposed to comprise the whole methodological spectrum of the literature of partner preference matching. The following explanations of each approach are based on Eastwick et al. (2024, pp. 6-8).

Ideal-trait correlations (ITC) simply examine whether participants who rate a particular trait as more desirable also perceive their partner as higher on that trait. Measured by the standardized regression coefficient, this test reveals moderate average effect sizes of β = .33 but is especially susceptible to alternative explanations for it lacks a dependent measure; e.g., attraction or romantic evaluation (Conroy-Beam & Buss, 2016, p. 8; Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 6; Gerlach et al., 2019, p. 2). The raw pattern metric (RPM) computes a within-person correlation across all 35 attributes between a participant's ideal ratings and their partner attribute ratings. This yields a single index of how well the partner matches the participant's profile of preferences. It is subsequently normalized and used to predict the evaluative outcome of romantic attraction. While this approach tends to produce substantial effect sizes (β = .37 in the full sample), it is confounded with the normative desirability of traits (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 7; Rogers et al., 2018; Wood & Furr, 2016). Partners perceived as having more positive traits will receive higher scores on this metric regardless of whether those traits specifically match the participant's stated priorities. Consequently, the corrected pattern metric (CPM) addresses this confound by mean-centering both ideal and attribute ratings at the sample level before computing the withinperson correlation (Eastwick et al., 2019 (Eastwick et al., , 2024, p. 6;, p. 6; Fletcher et al., 2020). This (supposedly) removes the shared normative component, so that only idiosyncratic matching accounts for the effect (i.e., the degree to which a person's unique preference profile matches their partner's unique trait profile). Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 28) report a corrected pattern metric effect of β = .19 in the full sample. Finally, the level metric (LM) refers to the trait-by-trait interaction between a participant's ideal for and their partner's rating on that trait, predicting romantic evaluation while controlling for both main effects (i.e., participant's ideal and partner's traits). This test asks, for each trait separately, whether the attributeevaluation association is stronger among participants who place greater importance on that trait. As an illustration, suppose that perceiving a partner as humorous is positively related to attraction toward that partner. The level metric examines whether this relationship is stronger among individuals who place greater importance on humor in an ideal partner than among those who consider humor less important.

In other words, it assesses whether people with stronger preferences for a particular trait assign greater evaluative significance to that trait when judging their partners. After all, average level metric interaction effects were very small. The average interaction term was β = .04 across all 35 traits, and effects for normatively highly desirable traits such as attractive or considerate were essentially zero (Eastwick et al., 2024, pp. 28-29). While Eastwick et al. (2024) do not report any main effects, the results reveal that attraction to these partners is driven almost entirely by participant's ideal and partner's traits rather than idiosyncratic weighting (i.e., interaction). Moreover, following an approach proposed by Sparks et al. (2020), Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 13) asked participants to nominate and rate additional peer targets of their preferred gender. Their primary analysis, however, incorporated the evaluation of the (most desired) partner only, as it was preregistered in their analysis plan. Overall, Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 48) concluded that (idiosyncratic) preference-matching effects exist but are modest in size and highly dependent on how they are measured. The study is notable for its scale, its preregistered design, and the transparent availability of data and analysis code. At the same time, the study raises a number of methodological concerns which will be shown in the next section.

Methodological framework

The theoretical groundwork for the central structural flaws in partner preference research has been established in the preceding two chapters. Stated in DAG terminology presented in Sections 2.1 and 2.2, the problem is straightforward: ideal partner preferences and the partner's actual attributes (rated by participants themselves) are both causes of relationship formation. Relationship formation is therefore a common effect or collider variable on the path between ideals and partner traits (ideal preferences → relationship formation ← partner attributes). As Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 6) themselves acknowledge, "the selection event is not used as a measured variable (i.e., there are no 'unselected' partners)". Notably, Eastwick et al. (2024) state this observation specifically in relation to ideal-trait correlations. The structural problem, however, equally concerns level metric and the corrected pattern metric. As mentioned above, Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 13) applied a study design which deliberately includes single participants (who rate their most desired partner) to address the issue of relationship status. They report multiple times that differences of partnered and single participants tended to be "very small" (Eastwick et al., 2024, pp. 25, 28, 30). While other explanations might be possible, this finding can be read as a hint that replacing established by most desired partners has no mitigating effect on collider restriction bias. In other words, established as well as most desired relationships both imply conditioning on a mutual collider variable of high romantic evaluation. The first layer of the DAG corresponds with the first hypothesis stated in the introduction (H1) and serves as the basis for investigating if measured individual background criteria have a confounding effect over and above main and interaction effects. Eastwick et al. (2024) indirectly control for some of these unmeasured confounders by applying the multilevel structure described in Chapter 3. Culture-dependent third variable influences are partly covered by the variance of varying effects. Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 22) reported relatively small differences in the evaluation of romantic evaluation of about 3% variance across samples (varying intercept) and 0.6 % variance accounting for varying slopes of each partner attribute rating. In the present multilevel context, the varying intercept captures the degree to which the average level of romantic evaluation differs systematically across the 60 laboratory samples. This reflects, for instance, culturally specific baseline tendencies toward positive or reserved partner evaluation. The varying slope, by contrast, captures the degree to which the association between a given partner attribute and romantic evaluation differs across samples. That is, whether perceiving a partner as, say, humorous predicts romantic evaluation more strongly in some cultural contexts than others.

That both components account for less than 4% of total variance combined indicates that neither baseline romantic evaluation nor the evaluative weight assigned to specific partner attributes varies substantially across cultures. The preference-matching structure is thus broadly consistent across the 60 samples, suggesting that the methodological concerns raised in the present thesis generalize beyond any single cultural context rather than being confined to specific subsamples.

At the second layer, ideal preferences and partner attributes are each depicted as clusters of variables connected by dashed bidirectional arrows within their respective groups. While these arrows are usually used to show the impact of spurious associations based on conditioning on a collider (see Section 2.2), they may as well indicate common-cause confounding by unobserved variables (see Section 2.1; Rohrer, 2018, p. 29). The second aspect will be discussed first for it affects the analysis irrespective of collider restriction bias which will be discussed afterwards. Within-cluster associations in this case capture at least two distinct phenomena: intertrait confounding and halo effects. Attributes rated in the Eastwick et al. (2024) participants survey are highly correlated among ideals as well as partner trait ratings. Participants who strongly value intelligence for instance, tend also to positively value humor (to pick another example). Attribute ratings in this case are in large parts driven by (unknown) mediating effects between corresponding variables or by shared background factors such as normative desirability standards or general positive orientation (indicated by the U node). Especially among partner attributes, they additionally reflect halo effects where global positive evaluations of targets (e.g., high relationship satisfaction or sympathy) artificially inflate the rating of specific individual attributes (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977). A (most desired) partner perceived as highly attractive for instance is likely to be rated as more intelligent or humorous, regardless of whether these traits are objectively related. For now, both phenomena are still forms of confounding rather than collider restriction bias, because they arise from shared common causes rather than from conditioning on a selection event. They represent open backdoor paths in the causal graph that the trait-by-trait level metric does not close. A more detailed exemplification is provided in Panel A in Figure 2. The second layer corresponds with the second hypothesis (H2) as stated in the introduction and captures if simultaneously entering all 35 ideal preferences and partner attributes into a multivariate regression model will substantially attenuate the estimated coefficients relative to the Eastwick et al. (2024) analysis. The first two layers and hypotheses thus represent concerns which are basically analysis-related, rather than sample or study-design related.

As depicted in the DAG in Figure 1, all three clusters are then causally associated with the romantic evaluation in the third layer, which in turn causes the selection event of becoming the (most desired) partner. The direction of these causal assumptions is as such debatable. The so-called 'relationship formation hypothesis', for instance, offers a reverse-causality explanation by suggesting that partner ideals may be shaped by existing relationships (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 20). Specifically, individuals may modify their ideal preferences to correspond more closely to their current partner's attributes, rather than choosing partners who already match their previously held ideals (Gerlach et al., 2019; Neff & Karney, 2003). Reverse causality as a further distinct methodological phenomenon, though no focus of this thesis, is briefly picked up in the next section. However, conditioning on (most desired) partners, as every study using established couples or most desired partner ratings necessarily does, activates the collider mechanism described in Section 2.2. This adds an additional layer of spurious covariance between ideal preferences and partner attributes on top of the pre-existing confounding issues described above. To use the same terminology as above, corresponding concerns are rather sample-related than analysis or study design-related. Figure 2 makes explicit that the (most desired) partner node is not merely a sampling criterion but an active part of the causal structure. The analysis is embedded in a world where high romantic evaluation is given by construction. This structural fact distorts estimated associations throughout: participants with high ideals and objectively low-scoring partners must still have high romantic evaluations to justify their status as (most desired) partners itself.

In other words, high romantic evaluations are the condition for being in the sample in the first place. The resulting 'upward pressure' on perceived partner trait ratings that follows is not deliberate misreporting; it is a structural artifact of collider conditioning. A positive interaction coefficient for a given trait indicates that the association between perceiving that trait in a partner and evaluating them positively is stronger for participants who place greater importance on that trait (reporting a higher ideal preference for that trait). In a trait-by-trait model, the interaction coefficient therefore absorbs variance from the open backdoor paths identified in Figure 1 and Panel A in Figure 2. 4.1

Methodological implications for partner preference research

Based on the considerations derived from the previous DAGs in Figure 1 and 2, this section comprises implications for partner preference research. As mentioned previously, the four distinct analytical approaches utilized by Eastwick et al. (2024) are of different methodological quality and show specific confounding effects of their own. Consequently, all four approaches are briefly addressed and evaluated in respect to potentially predict idiosyncratic partner preference-matching effects. Only after that, the three-layer structure is applied by incorporating analysis and sample-related concerns alike. These implications are followed by general methodological concerns as a fourth layer which require attention in the context of the Eastwick et al. (2024) study design. In this way, the aim of this section is to prepare the ground for establishing methodological extensions in the next section, keeping the overall goal in mind: to provide an analytical approach to offering high(er) predictive validity for partner preferencematching.

To begin with, ideal-trait correlations and the raw pattern metric are by Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 57) themselves characterize as methodologically "very weak" and "weak" respectively. As indicated in Chapter 3, ideal-trait-correlations do not incorporate any evaluative measure which means that they are limited in answering questions about (idiosyncratic) matching. The raw pattern metric as such is normatively confounded because people tend to positively evaluate their relationship (high romantic evaluation) when their (most desired) partner has positive traits, irrespective of their own ideal (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 7).

Secondly, the corrected pattern metric, despite the mean-centering procedure intended to remove normative desirability confounding, remains confounded with normative preferences in trait levels (Zhao et al., 2024, p. 19). Most traits which are assessed in the study are normatively desirable and, moreover, ideals are preferably rated near the ceiling of the response scale. Consequently, partners with normatively more desirable personalities will end up with higher corrected pattern metrics, irrespective of the mean centering procedure. As a result, it is not surprising that the corrected pattern metric correlates with positive evaluations (Schönbrodt et al., 2018, p. 628). Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 37) themselves acknowledge this by calculating an additional normative pattern metric (NPM). In this approach, the evaluative outcome is predicted by correlating the individual partner traits ratings with the sample's average ideal profile (instead of participant's unique ideals). The reported effect sizes for the NPM (β=.37) are virtually identical to the Raw Pattern Metric (RPM) estimates, pointing towards the conclusion that explicitly distinguishable idiosyncratic fit might be absent in these calculations. Taken together, as Edwards (1994) already observed, the corrected pattern metric remains confounded with the main effects of traits (i.e., the normative preferences). As such, CPM, although corrected, might not actually measure 'preference-matching', but rather something like perceived 'partner quality' (i.e., normatively desirable personality), which represents a different construct and raises concerns about the construct validity assumed in this analytical approach. In the context of NPM, Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 38) conclude themselves that "effect sizes suggest that, when participants perceived that partners had normatively 'ideal' traits, they evaluated those partners very positively, regardless of their own idiosyncratic ideal partner preferences".

The third implication concerns the level metric, which is the only 'strong' inference methodology left to properly predict idiosyncratic matching effects in the context of the Eastwick et al. ( 2024) study (as well as of large parts of partner preference research landscape). This metric minimizes normative confounding by controlling for ideal preference and partner trait main effects within the same analytical model. On a conceptual level, the level metric is generally implemented trait-by-trait, evaluating each ideal-trait interaction in isolation. While Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 8) argue that the trait-by-trait approach is crucial "when testing theories positing that ideals for specific attributes have functional outcomes", it is methodologically not well justified for approaching the matching hypothesis. Since traits are intercorrelated, a bivariate model for any single trait will potentially overestimate the matching effect for that trait with matching effects flowing through correlated traits (open backdoor path in DAG terms).

While this finding mirrors the finding in the second layer and primarily reveals the issue of intertraitconfounding, the level metric is additionally estimated within a sample conditioned on (most desired partner). As discussed within the context of the third layer, the bivariate model will probably exacerbate the inherent collider restriction bias due to the artificially high tendency of positive partner trait as well as romantic evaluations within the restricted dataset. The pooled histogram in Figure 3 illustrates the distributional properties of the three central rating scales: partner trait ratings, ideal preference ratings and romantic evaluations, not reported by Eastwick et al. (2024).

All three distributions exhibit a highly positive skew, revealing high average response tendencies and corresponding ceiling effects. For ideal preference ratings, the skew is largely a function of the normative desirability of the provided attributes which are almost universally valued -such as attractiveness, humor, and intelligence. This naturally produces a clustering of responses at the upper end of the scale. For partner trait ratings and romantic evaluations, the ceiling effect is more consequential and reflects the selective nature of the sample: participants rated only a (most desired) partner whom they had deliberately chosen, meaning that partners who were perceived as clearly undesirable were largely excluded from the outset. The degree of this truncation is substantial: 39.9% of partners received a romantic evaluation score of 10 or higher out of 11, to name just one example. This indicates that the majority of evaluations occupy only the uppermost range of the scale. The small but visible concentration of ratings at the lower end of the partner trait and ideal preference distributions is attributable to the four negatively worded Big Five items included to assess Agreeableness, Emotional Stability, Extraversion, and Conscientiousness (e.g., critical, quarrelsome; or anxious, easily upset; Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 13).

Moreover, the level metric relied exclusively on multilevel models without including further demographics or individual-difference measures as control covariates as indicated in the first layer and captured by the first hypothesis H1. As the DAG in Figures 1 suggests, results are potentially confounded with these further covariates (additionally to normative desirability confounding and mediating effects between different traits). While some important individual-difference variables have not been measured, such as subjective well-being at the time of study participation, others have only been measured but not utilized in the primary analysis. Therefore, as specified in Section 5.2, scales for individualism/collectivism, relational mobility and further individual different measures would need to be incorporated in the regression analysis to determine whether they offer predictive validity over and above main effects and interactions.

The following implications refer to study-design related issues as a fourth layer which have not been covered in the previously introduced three-layer structure. Study design related concerns, however, influence the four analytical approaches of partner preference-matching alike. To start with, all partner trait ratings are reported by the same participant who also provides the romantic evaluation outcome. Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 48) themselves show awareness of this concern, noting that "the participants' partners did not actually take part in this study, and effect sizes will likely decline across the board if the partner's (rather than the participant's) reports of the partner's traits are used instead" (see Hromatko et al., 2015). Research on relationship cognition demonstrates that global romantic satisfaction strongly motivates individuals to idealize their partners and perceive them in ways that confirm their pre-existing standards (Murray et al., 1996). As indicated in the previous section, additional biases based on halo effects (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977) may thus further influence the reported associations. This adds additional 'noise' to the dataset which is distinct from distortion due to conditioning on the collider of relationship status, however based on the same selection restriction of (most desired) partners. Moreover, the romantic evaluation occurs at the same time as the evaluation of traits and ideals, often only after relationship formation has already occurred (or the most desired partner is already imagined). This cross-sectional methodology raises the question in which way the study design is 'predictive' in the first place. When taking a closer look, the study might investigate rather into causal inference. Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 16) themselves consider a reverse causality aspect, which is formally known as 'relationship formation hypothesis', by compiling a supplementary longitudinal sample. This study design addresses the issue that people may a posteriori adjust their assessment of corresponding partner traits to (better) match their ideals (Gerlach et al., 2019; Neff & Karney, 2003). To test the hypothesis, participants first reported their partner ideals while they were single. After entering a new romantic relationship several months later, they completed follow-up measures concerning their current romantic partner (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 10). They conclude that "[e]stimates for the steadily partnered and newly partnered sample were similar, suggesting that both sets of participants maintained their ideals over the intervening months and drew from them when evaluating their partners, regardless of whether or not they were dating that partner when they reported their ideals at time 1" (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 30). In a similar study design, Gerlach et al. (2019, p. 1) come to a slightly different conclusion, observing that "participants adjusted their preferences downward when partners fell short of initial preferences, but showed no consistent adjustment when partners exceeded them". Crucially, steady single participants in the Eastwick et al. (2024, pp. 30-31) longitudinal sample showed (insignificant) corrected pattern and level metric effects near zero. This finding might be interpreted as supporting the presence of collider restriction bias. If the preference-matching hypothesis holds true, it should work for everyone. A match should predict high attraction, and a mismatch should predict low attraction, even if the interaction does not result in relationship formation. Without conditioning on the collider of relationship formation, and most desired partner respectively, the originally observed association between preference-matching and evaluative outcomes vanishes. While alternative explanations remain plausible, this finding might as well suggest that the predictive validity of preference matching in this particular study design may be a statistical artifact of selection.

A further study design-related implication refers to the romantic evaluation composite used by Eastwick et al. (2024). It comprises six items administered identically across all four nominated targetsincluding the three preferred-gender peers. A closer inspection of the item wording, however, reveals a fundamental asymmetry between the two target types. While items such as "I am romantically interested in [target]" and "[target] is the only person I want to be romantically involved with" are reasonably applicable to both partners and peers, several items are particularly anchored in established relational intimacy. Consequently, their application to mere acquaintances or strangers is limited.

Specifically, "[target] is the first person that I would turn to if I had a problem" and "if I achieved something good, [target] is the person that I would tell first" presuppose a degree of emotional closeness and trust that is, by definition, characteristic of formed relationships rather than the kind of newly encountered or casually known individuals who typify the peer targets. This further constitutes the structural presence of collider restriction bias. The romantic evaluation composite thus conflates genuine preference-matching with the relational consequences of having already chosen, rendering direct comparisons between (most desired) partners and peers problematic.

To summarize, the raised concerns can be classified as analysis-related, sample-related and study design-related implications. The first category contains confounding by measured background variables (layer one, H1), intertrait confounding among the 35 trait ideals and partner ratings (layer two, H2) and subsequent mitigation of collider restriction bias (layer three, H3). These concerns can potentially be solved by adapting the analytical approach. Sample-related implications primarily refer to the remaining collider restriction bias arising from conditioning on (most desired) partners which is not targetable through analytical means. The most direct remedy available is to apply the same model to the three unselected peer targets collected but not analyzed by Eastwick et al. (2024). As addressed in the Discussion in Chapter 7, this could further restore the excluded variation and allow a direct empirical test of whether preference-matching estimates change meaningfully outside the restricted sample. The last category is bound to the study design used by Eastwick et al. (2024) as well as by many others within the research field of partner preference research. Design-related implications comprise unmeasured covariates, reverse causality aspects and halo effects, as well as the potentially skewed romantic evaluation composite. These are inherent limitations of the research paradigm rather than of any particular analytical choice. Corresponding biases can only be targeted by changing the design approach accordingly.

4.2

Methodological extensions of the Eastwick et al. (2024) analysis

As demonstrated in the previous sections, several structurally distinct sources of bias have been identified in the partner preference research design as applied by Eastwick et al. (2024). The easiest way forward is to rule out ITC, RPM and CPM approaches for the reasons stated above and focus on the level metric as the 'gold standard' of partner preference matching effects. When doing so, the first step of methodological improvement concerns the analysis-related implications, as they serve as necessary prerequisites for properly estimating partner preference-matching effect. These analysis-related implications set the stage for methodological extension introduced in this section. In addition, samplerelated concerns are briefly introduced to prepare for future research.

The first layer implicates to address confounding by measured covariates. If these backdoor paths are left open, associations between ideal preferences, partner traits and romantic evaluation remain confounded by variables that have nothing to do with the actual matching effect itself. The first step of the methodological strategy of this paper is thus to close these paths by including all measured confounders simultaneously in the regression model. This is the logic of the backdoor criterion introduced in Section 2.1 (Pearl, 1993; 2000, p. 79). Including demographics and individual-difference variables as covariates satisfies this criterion for the measured portion of potential confounders. This does not resolve the unmeasured component, but it narrows the set of plausible alternative explanations, potentially reducing unsystematic 'noise'. Moreover, following Elwert's (2013, pp. 256-257) and Shpitser et al.'s (2010, p. 530) formalization of the adjustment criterion, conditioning on variables that are ancestors of the collider variable can partially mitigate collider restriction bias, provided those variables have been measured (see also Hernán et al., 2004, pp. 620-621). The part of bias which can be mitigated is highly dependent on the degree of variance which is provided by the model. Full recovery of collider restriction bias would theoretically occur at a coefficient of determination of R² = 1, which would mean that all unmeasured confounders would have been measured and incorporated into the analysis. To, at least partly, account for this confounding effect, the presented regression model includes three demographic and seven individual-difference predictors that were included in the Eastwick et al. (2024) survey. Among demographics, participant age was included as a continuous variable, and both sex (coded as man as reference category, woman, or other) and sexual orientation (coded as heterosexual as reference category, bisexual, gay, lesbian, or other) were included as categorical factors. Relationship status and length are intentionally excluded from regression modelling as they represent potential collider variables. Among individual-difference measures, relational mobility was assessed with the 12-item scale developed by Thomson et al. (2018), capturing the perceived availability of new relationship partners and the ease of ending existing ones in a given social environment. Individualism and collectivism were each measured at two levels using the 16-item scale by Triandis and Gelfand (1998). Equality-based interdependence (horizontal) and hierarchy-based orientations (vertical) are differentiated across both individualist and collectivist dimensions, yielding four subscale scores in total. Cultural tightness-looseness, referring to the strength of social norms and the tolerance of deviance within a given society, was assessed with the scale established by Gelfand et al. (2011). Finally, attitudes toward sex roles were measured using the instrument of Larsen and Long (1988), capturing endorsement of traditional versus egalitarian gender norms. All continuous individualdifference variables were entered as standardized scores to facilitate comparability of regression coefficients across predictors of differing scales.

The second layer is covered by particularly addressing intertrait confounding in the extended regression model as within-cluster associations are potentially confounded apart from collider-variableinduced dependence. As discussed, in the trait-by-trait level metric of Eastwick et al. (2024), each regression model includes only a single ideal preference and a single partner attribute. Every other trait in the respective cluster is therefore omitted, leaving its confounding contribution open. The estimated interaction coefficient absorbs variance from all these open paths, inflating the apparent preferencematching effect for that trait. To account for intertrait confounding, the regression model in this thesis closes these backdoor paths by entering all 35 ideal preferences and all 35 partner attributes as simultaneous main effects within a single model. Each interaction coefficient can then finally be interpreted as the (analytically) unique preference-matching effect for that trait, net of all other ideal preferences and partner attributes. As including all 35 ideal preferences and all 35 partner attributes as simultaneous main effects blocks many of these backdoor paths, it likely yields more conservative and interpretable interaction estimates. In the same logic that was outlined in the previous sections via layer three, this methodological step additionally helps to mitigate collider restriction bias in a way that ideal and partner trait ratings are separately controlled as distinct ancestors of the collider variable of relationship status alongside demographics and individual differences. In this sense, the hierarchical multivariate extension is the primary methodological contribution of the present study. The predictor block of interaction effects as part of Model 4 is consequently labelled corrected level metric (CLM).

The third layer additionally implies to deal with the remaining collider restriction bias which is left after analytical adaptations of the first two layers. Though no focus of this thesis, collider restriction bias is no property of which variables are included in the model. It is, after all, a property of which observations are included in the sample. Every participant in the dataset has already passed through a high-romantic-evaluation 'selection event'. The highly skewed distributions presented in Section 4.1 reflect the structural fact that the collider has been conditioned on before any analysis begins. Statistical adjustment can mitigate but not undo this structural flaw nor reconstitute large parts of the variation that was excluded at the point of sampling. The preference-matching effect estimated within this sample is therefore, irrespective of properly applied regression analysis, not the population preference-matching effect. It is the preference-matching effect among people who are already in or pursuing a positively evaluated relationship. Therefore, the most robust methodological solution to address the issue of collider restriction bias, apart from confounding issues, is to utilize a less conditional sample. Notably, this approach is possible due to the 'unselected' peer targets which have been collected but not used by Eastwick et al. (2024). To predict partner preference matching effects, however, the first two layers of the extended methodological approach are essential prerequisites for the third layer. Therefore, the thesis focuses on testing the multivariate regression model on the same dataset used by Eastwick et al. (2024). This is the crucial first step to examine corresponding changes in effect sizes between both analytical models. The resulting interaction coefficients should therefore be understood as more conservative, less biased estimates than those from the trait-by-trait approach, not as bias-free estimates of the true population parameter. As methodological extensions are bound to the current dataset and study design as applied by Eastwick et al. (2024), design-related implications are not further considered in the analysis to come and revisited in the Discussion. The following chapter specifies the method of the hierarchical extension in detail, from the baseline replication to the demographic model through to the full preference-matching interaction model.

5

Method

Based on previous considerations, the present study constituted a secondary analysis of the dataset collected by Eastwick et al. (2024) as described in Chapter 3. The analysis proceeded in two steps. The first step consisted of a full replication of the level metric analyses reported by Eastwick et al. (2024), serving as a validation of the analytical framework prior to the proposed methodological extension.

Secondly, the hierarchical multivariate regression model was developed to address the three analysisrelated structurally distinct sources of bias identified in the methodological framework in Chapter 3.

Primarily, the analysis addressed confounding by measured background variables and intertrait confounding among the 35 trait ratings. As described in Section 4.2 the analysis thereby potentially mitigated collider restriction bias by controlling for corresponding ancestors of the collider variable. All analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2026). Annotated R code for data processing, level metric replication, hierarchical regression analysis as well as model comparison is provided in Appendix A. In alignment with the analysis of Eastwick et al. (2024), all continuous variables were z-standardized within the full analytic sample prior to model estimation (M = 0, SD = 1). This included the 35 ideal preference ratings, all 35 partner attribute ratings, romantic evaluations, age as well as all individual difference scales.

5.1

Replication of the level metric

Following the original procedure, a series of multilevel models was estimated using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015), with participants nested within the k = 60 laboratory samples as the clustering unit.

For each of the 35 partner attributes separately, romantic evaluation was regressed on the standardized ideal rating, the standardized partner trait rating, and their interaction term, the latter constituting the actual level metric effect. According to the analysis by Eastwick et al. (2024) a varying intercept and a varying slope for the partner trait rating across laboratories were included. As notated by Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 28) the model was fitted with the following regression equation: Romantic evaluation = β0 + β1Ideal + β2PartnerAttribute + β3Ideal×PartnerAttribute + u0 + u1PartnerAttribute + ε; where u0 denotes the varying intercept, u1 the partner attribute slope and ! the residual. Consistent with Eastwick et al. (2024), all models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML, Bates et al., 2015, p. 16-17). Regarding the numerical optimization algorithm, Eastwick et al. (2024) relied on SPSS's internal optimizer, which cannot be directly replicated in R. In its place, the bobyqa algorithm was employed, as it supposedly offers superior convergence properties for models with complex varying effects structures (Powell, 2009). To assess whether this algorithmic difference introduced any meaningful discrepancy, two alternative optimizers were applied to two exemplary attributes, intelligence and religiosity, and the resulting fixed and varying effect estimates were compared directly. Differences between the two optimizers were negligible, ranging from the fifth to the seventh decimal place depending on the attribute, providing sufficient justification for the use of bobyqa throughout all subsequent analyses. The replication was successful, recovering the pattern of results reported in the original study, including the substantially larger level metric effect for religiosity (β = .13) relative to intelligence (β = .03), consistent with Eastwick et al. (2024, Table 3).

5.2

Hierarchical multivariate extension

To account for all three layers as presented in Section 4.2, the methodological extension was implemented as a hierarchical multilevel model built across four steps (Models 1-4). In each step one predictor block was added to the preceding model. Model 1 included only the demographic and individual-difference covariates alongside a varying intercept, establishing the baseline variance explained by background characteristics. Model 2 extended Model 1 by adding the standardized main effects of all 35 partner attributes simultaneously. In addition to the remaining varying intercept in Model 1, 35 varying slopes for each partner attribute were added to Model 2 in correspondence to the analytical approach of Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 22). Model 3 further added the standardized main effects of all 35 ideal preferences. Model 4 introduced all 35 Ideal × PartnerAttribute product terms as the preference-matching estimates of primary theoretical interest. Models 3 and 4 retained the same varying effects structure as Model 2. Because simultaneously estimating 35 varying slopes across k = 60 Level-2 units risks over-parameterization, the varying effects covariance matrix was specified as diagonal, constraining off-diagonal covariances to zero. In other words, samples are assumed to vary independently of one another in terms of intercept and slopes. This assumption is justified by the absence of a strong a priori theory regarding the specific co-variation of the 35 traits as well as by technical feasibility. Following this approach, the parameter space from over 600 covariance terms was reduced to 36 variance components while retaining the theoretically motivated heterogeneity of partner attribute slopes across cultural samples as proposed by Eastwick et al. (2024). The same REML estimation and bobyqa optimization used for the replication were applied to all four models. Utilizing the same notation as used by Eastwick et al. 6.5

Overall model comparison

Likelihood ratio tests confirmed that each successive predictor block improved model fit significantly.

The addition of 35 partner attribute main effects in Model 2 produced by far the largest improvement over the demographic baseline of Model 1, Δχ²(70) = 5790.73, p < .001, indicating that perceived partner characteristics account for substantial variance in romantic evaluation over and above participant-level covariates. The further inclusion of 35 ideal preference main effects in Model 3 yielded a statistically significant but considerably smaller improvement, Δχ²(35) = 132.32, p < .001. Most critically, the addition of the 35 Idealₖ × PartnerAttributeₖ interaction terms in Model 4 again improved fit over Model 3, Δχ²(35) = 88.34, p < .001, providing evidence that preference-matching effects account for some variance in romantic evaluation net of all main effects. However, information criteria AIC (Akaike information criterion) and BIC (Bayesian information criterion) revealed a diverging picture. AIC decreased consistently across all four models, indicating that each successive predictor block improved the tradeoff between fit and parsimony accordingly. The reduction from Model 1 to Model 2 was by far the largest (ΔAIC = -5650), with only modest further reductions when ideal preferences (ΔAIC = -63) and interactions (ΔAIC = -18) were added. Though BIC decreased substantially from Model 1 to Model 2 (ΔBIC = -5145), it slightly increased when ideal preferences were added in Model 3 (ΔBIC = +190) and again when interactions were added in Model 4 (ΔBIC = +234). From a BIC perspective, which applies a more stringent penalty for model complexity, neither ideal preferences nor preference-matching interactions improve the model sufficiently to offset the 'cost' of 35 additional parameters each.

Variance decomposition as reported in the four last rows of Table 1 logically supported likelihood ratio test results. Model 1 accounted for a fixed effects variance of R²fixed = .041 in romantic evaluation. The addition of partner attribute main effects in Model 2 increased this to R²fixed = .462, a gain of ΔR²fixed = .421. Adding ideal preferences and interactions in Models 3 and 4 yielded only marginal further gains (ΔR²fixed = .006 and .002, respectively), bringing the total fixed-effect variance explained to R²fixed = .470. By comparison, fixed effects for the Eastwick et al. (2024) trait-by-trait replication accounted on average for R²fixed = .102. The marginal contribution of demographic and individual-difference predictors is further underscored by a supplementary analysis in which they were removed from the full Model 4 specification. The resulting reduction in fixed-effect variance was ΔR²fixed = .009, indicating that participant-level background characteristics account for less than one percentage point of variance in romantic evaluation once partner attributes, ideals, and their interactions are included. The variance attributable to varying effects (cross-cultural heterogeneity in trait-rating across the 60 laboratory samples) was comparatively small (R²varying = .040) to overall variance and closely mirrored the average Eastwick et al. (2024) trait-by-trait replication (R²varying = .039). This near-identical share across both analytical approaches indicates that the shift to multivariate estimation does not substantially redistribute fixed-effect variance into the varying effects structure.

Finally, average standard errors (SE) for all partner attribute main effects were smaller in Model 4 (M = .0117) than in the Eastwick et al. (2024) trait-by-trait replication (M = .0151). For ideal preferences SEs were almost identical across both approaches. Discussion

The following sections contextualize the results presented in Chapter 6. The central findings are organized around the five analytical sections of the previous chapter, each corresponding to a specific layer of the methodological framework and an associated hypothesis (H1-H3). Subsequently, broader theoretical and methodological implications are derived from the central findings before directions for future research are presented.

7.1

Central findings

H1, in correspondence with layer 1 implications, receives mixed support. Effect sizes for some demographic predictors and individual-difference scales were relatively high, especially in comparison to the attenuated effect sizes of both main effects (partner trait and ideal preference ratings) as well as interaction effects (idiosyncratic share of actual matching effects). This finding is especially notable as Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 44) stated that preregistered tests of individual differences showed "no interpretable pattern" and that no functional outcomes could be derived from them. However, their collective contribution to preference-matching estimates is relatively marginal in terms of explained variance represented by a small coefficient of determination in Model 1 (R² ). Moreover, the two sign reversals of relational mobility and horizontal collectivism in Model 2 indicate that individual-difference effects on romantic evaluation are substantially mediated by perceived partner attributes rather than operating independently. This observation is further supported, when Model 4 was stripped off any background characteristics. The resulting loss of variance was negligible (ΔR²fixed = .009). After all, the combination of relatively high effects and marginal variance of individual preferences might partly be explained by the specific configuration of the sample. To name just one example, many participants were recruited in a university context resulting in a relatively low standard deviation and mean of participants age (M = 28.5, SD = 11.7 years) compared to true population parameters. Little variance in age consequently results in a relatively small potential for age to explain variance in romantic evaluations. replication model. Changes in unique ideal preference effects, however, were highly ambiguous. This additional noteworthy pattern shows that across both models, a substantial number of ideal preference coefficients were positive, many of them normatively highly desirable. The pattern theoretically indicates that participants who place greater importance on a given trait also evaluate their partner more positively, independently of how highly they actually rate their partner on that trait. This pattern is considerably more pronounced in the Eastwick et al. (2024) replication, but it persists in Model 4 as well.

Under the preference-matching hypothesis, this direction of effect is difficult to justify: if a participant values a trait highly and their partner falls short of that standard, the ideal should function as a critical benchmark, producing lower rather than higher romantic evaluation. A positive main effect of the ideal may reflect a general positivity artifact: participants who rate any given trait as highly important may also tend to perceive their (most desired) partner as highly desirable overall, producing a spurious positive association between ideal ratings and romantic evaluation that has little to do with idiosyncratic preference structure. Religiosity constitutes an instructive counterexample. Both the Eastwick et al.

(2024) replication and Model 4 report a negative ideal preference main effect for religiosity. It means that participants who place greater importance on religiosity in a partner evaluate their (most desired) partner less positively, net of their partner's religiosity rating. This is precisely the direction which is expected in the context of the ideal preference logic: religiosity is one of the few attributes in the set for which individual preferences vary substantially rather than clustering near the ceiling of the ideal scale, making it possible for high ideals to exert downward pressure on romantic evaluation. This theoretically expected negative effect emerges for religiosity, but not for most normatively desirable traits which points, at least in parts, toward a study design-related concern. The ceiling effects documented in Figure 3, combined with the restriction to (most desired) partners, may suppress the critical benchmark function of ideals for attributes that are near-universally valued. Only traits with genuine crossparticipant variance, such as religiosity, are consequently capable of producing the expected pattern.

Whether this reflects a structural limitation of the sample, the measurement instrument, or both warrants careful consideration in future research designs.

H3 is, in alignment with the third layer, supported by the near-zero effect sizes of interactions.

All 35 traits in Model 4 clustered between β = -.02 and β = .03 and, once again, were substantially attenuated. In this sense H2 receives additional support. With 95% confidence intervals broadly overlapping zero in Figure 4, few of them were significant. After all, only three idiosyncratic matching effect sizes remained significant at the level of p < .001 in Model 4. This finding reflects a general observation that once all 35 attributes are incorporated via the hierarchical regression analysis, a large majority of attributes which had been significant in the trait-by-trait approach, turned insignificant across all predictor blocks. As no multiple comparison correction was applied, it is worth noting that a Holm-Bonferroni correction (Holm, 1979) applied to the 35 interaction terms yields a corrected threshold of α = .05/35 = .00143, below which all three significant effects remain. A stricter correction across all fixed-effect model terms would, however, likely render these effects non-significant as well, further underscoring the overall fragility of the preference-matching signal. Additional support for H3 is provided by the stark attenuation of ideal preferences. Because the partner attribute rating is held constant in the same model, the ideal coefficient potentially captures the discrepancy between what the participant wants and what the partner provides. For any fixed level of perceived partner quality on a given trait, a higher ideal means a larger unmet gap between standard and reality. The mostly insignificant near-zero effects observed in Model 4 thus indicate that ideals do apparently not function as critical standards against which partners are evaluated. Consequently, H3 receives strong empirical support by utilizing the corrected level metric: even after full multivariate adjustment, idiosyncratic preference-matching effects are very small and statistically fragile.

7.2

Implications and reflections

The near-zero ideal preference matching effects (interactions) in Model 4 constitute one of the clearest findings of the present study (H3). Once general partner quality, approximated by the simultaneous inclusion of all 35 partner attribute ratings, is accounted for, stated ideal preferences and interactions carry extremely low residual predictive value for romantic evaluation. Adding all 35 partner attribute main effects in Model 2 yielded to an increase in variance explained (ΔR²fixed) of more than 70 times higher than the subsequent addition of ideal preferences in Model 3 and more than 105 times higher than the final addition of interactions in Model 4. The BIC penalty for adding ideal preferences in Model 3 and interactions in Model 4 underscores that neither block improves model fit sufficiently to justify the additional 35 parameters each. In this sense, partner trait ratings provide by far the largest share of variance to explain romantic evaluation. The likelihood ratio test, however, formally confirmed that the 35 interactions collectively improved model fit over Model 3, providing formal evidence that preferencematching is not wholly absent in the utilized study design. However so, the (uncorrected) level metric might in large parts measure perceived 'partner quality' as a different construct after all. As stated in the introduction, these findings are consistent with (at least) two theoretical arguments: either collider bias arising from conditioning on (most desired) partners constitutes a structural property of the sample that full multivariate regression adjustment cannot correct or the study-design itself is limited to detect idiosyncratic matching effects in the first place. The latter explanation points towards a third possible explanation: asked in the way the study design implies, people don't say (or know) what they really want after all. Consequently, what people say they want may itself reflect normative desirability standards or social presentation rather than idiosyncratic evaluative criteria that actually guide their romantic judgments. This finding connects directly to the distinction between stated and revealed preferences as conceptualized by Wood and Brumbaugh (2009). Stated preferences (ideal ratings) reflect what participants say they want. Revealed preferences (partner attributes main effects) represent what actually drives romantic evaluation, regardless of what participants report. Eastwick et al. (2024, pp. 32-34) demonstrate that these systematically diverge in ranking across many of the 35 attributes reported.

A stated preference for intelligence may reflect a normative belief about desirable partner qualities rather than an operative criterion that modulates how intelligent partners are actually evaluated.

The spread of Eastwick ideal preference effects in both directions is itself informative in this regard. The positive effects for traits like Confident potentially reflect general partner quality leaking into the ideal coefficient in the absence of full partner attribute controls. Taken together, the present results support the conclusion that romantic evaluation is driven primarily by who the partner is perceived to be, not by the alignment between that perception and what the evaluator claims to want. This explanation is supported by the fact that steady single participants in the Eastwick et al. (2024, pp. 30-31) longitudinal sample showed insignificant corrected pattern and level metric effects near zero (in contrast to newly or steadily partnered individuals). If the preference-matching hypothesis would properly be addressed by the current research design, it should work for everyone.

The strong compression of partner attribute main effects in Model 4 is a further notable result of the present study, and its structural explanation is transparent within the methodological framework. In the trait-by-trait approach, each partner attribute coefficient absorbs the variance it shares with all correlated traits omitted from the model. Entering all 35 attributes simultaneously in Model 4 yielded clearly attenuated effects net of their shared (or mediated) covariance. The substantial intercorrelation among partner attribute ratings reflects potential halo effects, shared normative desirability covariance and unknown mediating structure among the 35 attributes. After all, the reduction in apparent predictive weight is both expected and theoretically interpretable as direct evidence of Layer 2 intertrait confounding. The simultaneous entry of measured ancestors of the collider variable additionally achieves partial mitigation of Layer 3 collider restriction bias, meaning the two mechanisms operate simultaneously and cannot be fully separated within the present design. However, as additional variance of measured background characteristics is extremely low, the mitigating effect on collider restriction bias apart from intertrait confounding is likely to be relatively small in the analytical model. As discussed in the next section, this finding reflects the limitations of analysis-related methodological extensions and leads to the necessity to utilize another sample in order to reconstitute the variance lost by condition on (most) desired partner.

Importantly, sign reversals as well as small effect sizes in Model 4 carry a ceteris paribus interpretation that requires attention for all predictor blocks. A negative and negligible partner trait main effect for Sporty and athletic for instance, does not mean that athletic partners are evaluated less positively in general. The Eastwick et al. (2024) trait-by-trait replication shows the opposite. It means that conditional on all other 35 partner attributes being held constant, perceived athleticism contributes a small negative increment to romantic evaluation. This finding is consistent with an intertrait mediation structure and risks overcontrol bias as mentioned in Chapter 2 (see Rohrer, 2018, p. 38; Rohrer et al., 2022). The positive marginal effect of athletic, for instance, might flow through correlated appearancerelated traits such as attractive and nice body. Equally and additionally possible is the explanation that shared confounding background variance is removed by the multivariate approach, such as by normative desirability standards. The present design cannot differentiate between these explanations. The practical implication, however, is that near-zero and negative coefficients in Model 4 should not be interpreted as evidence that a specific trait is irrelevant to romantic evaluation, but that its effect is not uniquely distinguishable once the full trait space is controlled. This ceteris paribus constraint equally applies to the interaction terms, where near-zero estimates may reflect fully mediated matching effects rather than genuine absence.

Furthermore, the simultaneous inclusion of 35 traits, 35 ideals, and their 35 respective interaction terms in a single multivariate model theoretically raises concerns regarding multicollinearity.

The empirical inspection of the standard errors, however, shows the average standard errors for the level metric interactions in the comprehensive multivariate model were lower or equal than those obtained from the isolated, trait-by-trait replication. While multicollinearity typically inflates standard errors, this observed shrinkage indicates a substantial reduction in the model's residual variance.

Consequently, the saturated multivariate model does not 'suffer' from coefficient instability; rather, it yields slightly more robust estimates by successfully isolating the unique predictive validity of each preference-matching interaction. The observed pattern is consistent with a growing body of literature and underscores the analytic advantage of simultaneous estimation. (Feng et al., 2019; Graham, 2003; Gregorich et al., 2021; Morrissey & Ruxton, 2018; O'Brien, 2007; Vanhove, 2021).

7.3

Directions for future research Several structurally distinct issues remain unresolved by the present analysis and define the most productive directions for subsequent work. The most consequential is the residual layer 3 collider restriction bias. The analysis-related multivariate adjustment addresses Layers 1 and 2 but cannot properly reconstitute the sample-related variation excluded by restricting the analysis to (most desired) partners. The highly positively skewed distributions documented in Figure 3, with 39.9% of partners receiving romantic evaluation scores of 10 or higher out of 11, reflect this structural truncation. Ceiling effects of this magnitude risk sample-dependent influence on effect sizes whereby high romantic evaluation is given by construction. The most apparent available remedy is to apply the same hierarchical multivariate model to the three unselected peer targets collected but not analyzed by Eastwick et al. (2024). Inclusion of these targets likely reconstitutes parts of the excluded variation by drawing from a more representative evaluation range of romantic evaluation. Thereby, comparison of both samples with the corrected level metric provides a direct empirical test of whether preferencematching interactions are further distorted by collider restriction bias. In this sense, the sample comparison provides a deeper understanding of the research question which guided this thesis: "To what extent does collider bias distort the predictive validity of ideal partner preferences?".

Second, the six-item romantic evaluation composite includes items presupposing relational intimacy as demonstrated in Section 4.1. Applying this specific scale across selected and unselected targets risks introducing asymmetric measurement error that confounds comparisons across target types. A revised composite calibrated to evaluative function across both target types, or alternatively, separate composite specifications, would strengthen future extension to the unselected peer sample.

A third unresolved issue is the mediation problem introduced by simultaneous entry of all 35 partner attributes. Some intertrait correlations treated as confounding in Model 4 are plausibly causal (or predictive respectively). The attribute attractive, for instance, may partly cause perceptions of sporty and athletic or nice body; ambitious may causally precede a perceived successful and so on. Susceptible to overcontrol bias, near-zero coefficients in Model 4 may reflect full mediation rather than genuine zero effects as discussed previously. Addressing this formally requires a prespecified causal ordering among the 35 traits -a theoretically demanding step that would require dedicated theoretical and empirical work beyond the present scope.

Finally, design-related implications as introduced in Section 4.1 point towards additional directions for future research.

The collection of unmeasured covariates such as subjective well-being at the time of participation (or transsituational), reverse causality aspects and halo effects might additionally be addressed to reduce apparent confounding. Many more potential sources of biased estimation which have not explicitly been discussed would require further attention such as method variance, measurement error (Westfall & Yarkoni, 2016), rater effects, and IKEA effects (Norton et al., 2011). The overall utility of these potential considerations is highly dependent on the methodological quality of the underlying research design which, after all, is in question as concluded in the next chapter.

8

Conclusion

This thesis was motivated by a structural concern that has received inadequate attention in the partner preference literature up until today. As introduced by Eastwick & Neff (2012, p. 667) most individuals are able to readily describe the qualities they would like in an ideal romantic partner. These descriptions often vary considerably, with some people valuing a caring and supportive partner who provides affection during difficult times, while others place greater importance on traits such as confidence and social competence. Whether these individually stated preferences predict whom people end up with, and how positively they evaluate them, is the central question of the partner preference-matching literature. The preference-matching hypothesis states that people evaluate romantic partners more favorably the more closely a partner's attributes match their stated ideals (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 5). If one rates intelligence as highly desirable, is one more likely to evaluate an intelligent partner positively?

Does this variation in preferences explain why people are romantically drawn to some individuals rather than others? These questions are central to influential theoretical frameworks such as the Ideal Standards Model (Fletcher et al., 1999 (Fletcher et al., , 2000;; Simpson et al., 2001) that have motivated decades of empirical investigation (Campbell & Stanton, 2014, p. 485). Despite this longevity, empirical support for the matching hypothesis has remained surprisingly inconsistent, and concerns about the predictive validity of the underlying research designs have been largely unaddressed (Eastwick et al., 2024, p. 5; Gerlach et al., 2019, p. 1).

One possible explanation for this phenomenon is introduced in Chapter 1 of this thesis: commonly utilized research designs that find support for the preference-matching hypothesis, as conducted by Eastwick et al. (2024), inherently condition their analysis on romantic partners. Thereby, researcher test their hypotheses in an artificial world of high romantic evaluation, which is not representative of the true population. As a common effect of the predictors under investigation (partner attributes as well as ideal preferences), romantic partners represent a collider variable based on the classical inverted fork structure as formalized in Chapter 2. The following introduction of the directed acyclic graph methodology (DAG) as a framework for causal reasoning demonstrated that conditioning on a collider potentially introduces collider restriction bias (Lu et al., 2023, pp. 407-408). As the analysis by Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 43), which was described in Chapter 3, represents "the largest examination of ideal partner preference-matching to date", it is especially appropriate for further investigation into the ramifications of collider restriction bias in the context of partner preference research. Eastwick et al.

(2024, p. 13) applied a study design which deliberately included single participants into the analysis to address the issue of relationship status. However, as Eastwick et al. (2024, pp. 25, 28, 30) themselves reported multiple times, the differences of partnered participants, who rated their current romantic partner, and single participants, who rated their most desired partner, tended to be "very small".

In the present context, this finding can be read as a hint that replacing established by most desired partners has no substantial mitigating effect on collider restriction bias. Based on these observations, the current thesis approached the following research question: "To what extent does collider bias distort the predictive validity of ideal partner preferences in the Eastwick et al. (2024) data analysis?". To provide potential answers, the analysis conducted by Eastwick et al. (2024) was methodologically assessed by drawing inferences from three distinct DAGs in Chapter 4. The evaluation revealed several additional sources of bias which primarily need to be addressed to properly account for collider bias in a subsequent analysis.

Consequently, a three-layered framework with specific analytical implications for the analysis as applied by Eastwick et al. (2024) was introduced. The first two layers comprise analysis-related implications. The third layer marks the transition from analysis to sample-related concerns. A fourth layer was additionally introduced to capture study design-related issues which cannot be accounted for in the context of this thesis. Layer 1 concerns confounding by measured background variables. Layer 2 indicates intertrait confounding among the 35 ideal preference and partner attribute ratings. Layer 3 points towards collider restriction bias arising from conditioning on the most desired or established partner. The latter represents a structural property of the sample that regression adjustment can only partially correct by controlling for causally preceding influences of the collider variable. In response to the methodological assessment, a hierarchical multivariate extension of the level metric was developed in Chapter 5 to address Layers 1 and 2 analytically, while acknowledging that Layer 3 remains largely beyond the reach of within-sample statistical correction.

Four hypotheses guided the empirical analysis. H0 restated the preference-matching hypothesis of Eastwick et al. (2024) as the theoretical baseline, predicting positive and significant interaction effects between partner and ideal trait ratings for the original level metric. The following three hypotheses were conceptualized in a way that they correspond with the three layers introduced in Chapter 4. H1 predicted that demographic and individual-difference covariates would produce additional significant effect sizes in the full hierarchical model.

H2 predicted that simultaneous multivariate intertrait adjustment would substantially attenuate both attribute main effects and interaction coefficients. H3 stated that even after full multivariate adjustment, preference-matching interactions would remain near zero. As stated in the introduction, H3 is consistent with (at least) two theoretical arguments: either collider bias arising from conditioning on (most desired) partners constitutes a structural property of the sample that full multivariate regression adjustment cannot correct or the study-design itself is limited to detect idiosyncratic matching effects in the first place. The latter explanation is based on layer four implications and points towards a third possible explanation. Asked in the way the study design implies, people may not say, or even know, what they actually want.

To find support for the stated hypotheses, the present study pursued two interconnected goals.

The first aim was to replicate the level metric of Eastwick et al. (2024) as it was evaluated as potentially providing the strongest predictive inference amongst the four analytically distinct methods applied by Eastwick et al. (2024). Against this benchmark, the hierarchical multivariate regression model was developed to address the three structurally distinct sources of bias as mentioned above (Model 1 -Model 4). The results of the full hierarchical multivariate regression model, as well as of the consequently established corrected level metric, were presented in Chapter 6. The discussion in Chapter 7 provided the necessary contextualization of the results and subsequently reflected on implications and directions for future research. In this sense, the present thesis reached out to examine whether the original level metric of Eastwick et al. (2024) yields valid estimates of idiosyncratic preference-matching.

The findings across three analytical layers and four hypotheses converge into a methodologically properly incorporating all measured causally upstream variables (layer 1 and 2); and where the sampling design imposes a ceiling on valid inference that no statistical model can overcome (layer 3). So far, the methodological multivariate extension developed in this thesis is not able to distinguish between the two. Without considering a different sample to be compared via the corrected level metric, the answer cannot be provided within the utilized sample itself. As discussed in Section 7.3, this approach is a rigorous and highly recommended possibility to provide a deeper understanding of the research question which initially guided this thesis. Notably, the 'unselected' peer targets which have been collected but not used by Eastwick et al. (2024) provide the necessary data for future research. In this sense, the research question was only insufficiently answered in the context of this thesis.

Nevertheless, the present findings raise a fundamental challenge for H0 as conventionally conceptualized. The preference-matching hypothesis assumes that stated ideal preferences are valid proxies for the individual preference structures that guide romantic evaluation. The current analysisrelated results point towards the fact that the operationalization as applied by Eastwick et al. (2024) might address a different construct. As mostly normatively desirable partner trait ratings constitute the overwhelmingly dominant predictor block of romantic evaluation in the present sample, the uncorrected level metric might rather address the construct of perceived 'partner quality' when applied trait-by-trait.

This observation is supported by the near-zero interaction effects after all 35 attributes are controlled for. Moreover, the systematic divergence between stated preferences (i.e., ideal ratings) and revealed preferences (i.e., partner attributes main effects) point towards the possibility that what people say they want may itself reflect normative desirability standards rather than idiosyncratic evaluative criteria guiding their romantic judgments.

The issue at hand is intensified by complementary biases which have only briefly been discussed in the context of this thesis. Arising from the additional study-design related fourth layer, halo effects (Murray et al., 1996; Nisbett & Wilson, 1977), IKEA effects (Norton et al., 2011) and reverse causality aspects (Gerlach et al., 2019) inflate individual attribute ratings, especially when initial attraction is high.

As high romantic evaluations are the condition for being in the sample in the first place, the resulting 'upward pressure' on perceived partner trait ratings that follows is not deliberate misreporting; it is a structural artifact of collider conditioning. This dynamic is an essential reason why the above-mentioned sources of bias as well as intertrait confounding are potentially exacerbated when conditioning on a sample-restricting collider. Additionally, the cross-sectional study design raises the question in which way the study design is 'predictive' in the first place. As demonstrated in Chapter 4, these considerations likely also apply to the other three analytically distinct approaches which have been utilized by Eastwick et al. (2024) apart from the level metric. Note in this context that predictive validity, framed by Eastwick et al. (2024, p. 3), and valid causal inference are not necessarily the same. However, as causality presupposes predictivity, causal reasoning offers an adequate way of targeting aspects of predictive validity (see e.g., Kraemer et al., 1997, p. 341). As this issue reaches far beyond the scope of this thesis, no further attempt at differentiation is made at this point.

Finally, the overall utility of sample-and study design-related adaptations as proposed in Section 7.3 highly depends on the potential of the current research design to actually test partner preferencematching effects. The apparent dominance of normative partner desirability might at least partly reflect a consequence of study design-related measurement conditions rather than a feature of the underlying sample. This account is substantive and points towards the construct validity of the ideal preference model itself. Reduced to explicit single-item importance ratings with an unknown mediation structure, the 35-item conceptualization only captures a highly accessible and normatively common layer of partner preferences. The more idiosyncratic, non-verbal, contextually embedded, or potentially irrational dimensions of actual attraction are, the less likely they are to be adequately represented by such a measurement approach. If the present operationalization were to systematically underspecify the complexity of genuine individual preferences, preference-matching effects would be attenuated: not primarily because matching is irrelevant and less due to conditioning on the collider variable of (most desired) partners, but because the utilized instrument would lack the measurement depth required to detect it.
