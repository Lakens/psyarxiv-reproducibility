It is relevant to consider that statistical results that cannot reject the null hypotheses within the Null Hypothesis Significance Testing approach (the most common approach in the studies examined above), are not outright support of the null hypotheses. Instead, the correct reading is that the alternative hypotheses were not supported on those occasions. That is, the phenomena under investigation are not proved to be nonexistent: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Very often, authors interested in ESP and PK ignore or do not take into account the fact that expected effects are often small, as documented in the available meta-analyses (e.g., Storm & Tressoldi, 2023; see also Cardeña, 2018). For example, the expected effect sizes in tasks requiring a forced-choice in a normal state of consciousness range from .009 to .02 (Storm & Tressoldi, 2023; see also Cardeña, 2018). To achieve a statistical power of a minimum .80, and the expected effect size set to .05 above the expected chance of .5, with a one-tailed binomial test, requires 620 trials (or 620 participants performing a single trial).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

A flag means look at this column. Where the data is already de-identified (an ip column reading Anonymized, a name column the authors emptied), no action is needed.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

This review systematically investigated preregistered studies concerning extra-sensory perception (ESP) and mind-matter interaction (micropsychokinesis; i.e., micro-PK), available up to October 2026.

The primary aims were to evaluate their outcomes and compare the percentage of positive findings with those observed in mainstream psychology. Our methodology adhered to the PRISMA Guidelines.

We searched major preregistration repositories, including the Open Science Framework and the Koestler Parapsychology Unit (KPU) Study Registry, and relevant meta-analyses. Inclusion criteria focused on completed studies with available preregistration and final results only for confirmatory hypotheses. A positive outcome was defined as the support of at least one confirmatory hypothesis, strictly following preregistered procedures and statistics without deviation. A total of 50 studies with a total of 60 experiments, met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of these, 17 experiments (28.3%) reported a positive outcome, and 20 confirmatory hypotheses out of 97 (20.6%) were positive.

Interestingly, and contrary to some expectations, a broader comparison indicated that preregistered studies concerning ESP and PK did not necessarily exhibit a much lower proportion of confirmed hypotheses when compared with results for preregistered studies in mainstream psychology.

Introduction

Most disciplines, including psychology and the sub-discipline of parapsychology, recognize the concerns in recent years over research credibility and reproducibility (Bošnjak et al., 2021; Logg & Dorison, 2021; Sasaki & Yamada, 2020; Waldron & Allen, 2022). Consequently, there is an increased focus on preregistration as a means by which questionable research practices (QRPs) can be addressed.

These QRPs include p-hacking (e.g., preferencing some p values over others), HARKing (hypothesizing after the results are known and presenting them as if they were pre-planned rather than post hoc), and selective reporting (Krypotos et al., 2022; Nosek et al., 2019; Sasaki & Yamada, 2020; Simmons et al., 2021; van den Akker et al., 2024).

Preregistering a study allows the author to have his/her work go on public record prior to actual testing, which means the work is not only time-stamped, but it is also open to external scrutiny, which means changes to the original methodology, hypotheses, and statistical analysis, can all be spotted and even questioned (Bošnjak et al., 2021; Krypotos et al., 2022; Levine et al., 2023; Logg & Dorison, 2021). QRPs like p-hacking and HARKing are therefore easily identified (Levine et al., 2023; Nosek et al., 2019; van den Akker et al., 2024).

Being able to scrutinize a preregistered study also allows distinctions to be made between confirmatory and exploratory analyses (Dutilh et al., 2021; Nosek et al., 2019; Waldron & Allen, 2022).

Greater reliability of findings can therefore emerge from preregistration, but replicability in the disciplines-particularly psychology-is much needed given the low success rates in large-scale replication attempts (Logg & Dorison, 2021; Sasaki & Yamada, 2020; van den Akker et al., 2024).

Greater interest in preregistration by psychological organizations helps standardize research planning and reporting, thus fostering a culture of rigorous scientific inquiry (Bošnjak et al., 2021; Pownall et al., 2023; Simmons et al., 2021).

Comparisons of Preregistered and Non-Preregistered Studies

Research investigating the outcomes of preregistered studies compared to non-preregistered ones reveals both anticipated benefits and areas where the implementation of preregistration still faces challenges.

Observed Differences and Benefits

Statistical Power and Sample Size:

Power analyses and larger sample sizes are now expected in a preregistered submission, which was not usually the case in non-preregistered submissions (van den Akker et al., 2024). As a consequence, preregistered studies re more likely to find true effects.

Publishability and Impact:

Preregistration is often avoided because authors falsely believe the process hampers publication or can compromise a study's impact. However, there is no evidence that preregistered studies take longer to publish-in fact, they are seen to score better on various impact measures (van den Akker et al., 2024).

Counteracting Bias: Overall, preregistration and Registered Reports are recognized for their potential to counteract systematic bias, such as selective reporting and publication bias, and improve the general quality of studies by allowing transparent evaluation of claims (Lakens et al., 2024).

There are of course areas of concern where differences between preregistered and non-preregistered studies were less pronounced although challenges remain: Preventing P-hacking and HARKing: While preregistration is intended to combat practices like phacking and HARKing, some findings indicate that robust evidence for its direct prevention of these issues is not consistently found (van den Akker et al., 2024).

Detailed Preanalysis Plans:

Some research has shown that preregistration did not consistently reduce p-hacking and publication bias, but this problem is alleviated if detailed pre-analyses are provided in the submission (Brodeur et al., 2024).

Discrepancies and Incomplete Registrations: Quality and preregistrations guidelines are often offputting to researchers, and when they do preregister, it is noted that the submissions lack methodological detail, which gives considerable opportunity for researchers to deviate from their original plans (van den Akker et al., 2024). One review found that a very high number of studies showed discrepancies between their preregistered plans and the resultant publications, particularly in relation to primary outcomes and secondary outcomes (TARG Meta-Research Group & Collaborators, 2023). These inconsistencies were primarily observed in data collection procedures, statistical models, and exclusion criteria (van den Akker et al., 2024). However, the more comprehensive the registration templates, the more precise and effective are the preregistrations (van den Akker et al., 2024).

In summary, preregistration is a major contributor to transparency in research, and research designs are greatly improved in terms of statistical power and sample size. Also, increased reliability of statistical findings builds confidence, especially when biases like p-hacking and HARKing are eliminated, provided of course preregistration rules are followed and researchers adhere to the registered plan.

Preregistration in the Context of Controversial Phenomena

Preregistration also plays a crucial role in enhancing the credibility and trustworthiness of research dealing with controversial phenomena like extra-sensory perception (ESP) or mind-matter interaction (i.e., psychokinesis; PK), where findings often face heightened skepticism and challenges to reproducibility (Kekecs et al., 2023). Thus problems already mentioned, such as p-hacking and HARKing, also need addressing in parapsychology (Walleczek & Stillfried, 2020). Maintaining transparency in parapsychology is essential for fostering trust in the experimental designs, the findings, and the discipline itself. For example, a replication study on observer consciousness that employed data encryption to guard against p-hacking and HARKing, clearly indicated that any deviation from the preapproved protocol constituted a misrepresentation of the research process (Walleczek & Stillfried, 2020).

Parapsychology has not been slow to act. Wiseman, Watt, and Kornbrot (2019) pointed out that a prototype of the Registered Report model was initiated as early as the mid-1970s by Martin Johnson in the European Journal of Parapsychology. This early adoption was explicitly designed to mitigate methodological issues and selective reporting, anticipating modern open-science initiatives by several decades.

Highly contentious (paranormal) phenomena can be given fairer representation through preregistration as it allows for an open and verifiable research process. As noted above, transparency of experimental designs and analysis plan are established before data collection or observation, thus eliminating pre-empting the possibility of producing spurious outcomes (Simmons et al., 2020).

Preregistration also allows researchers and others to understand how claims are reached, thereby facilitating a more objective assessment of the evidence presented (Kekecs et al., 2023; Nosek et al., 2019). Protocols like the "Transparent Psi Project" exemplify efforts to apply credible study designs and transparent methods to investigate topics like ESP, involving multi-lab replications co-designed by both proponents and opponents of the original hypotheses (Kekecs et al., 2023).

There is also a need to address concerns about reproducibility. Many controversial areas have historically suffered from low reproducibility. Preregistration, particularly when coupled with robust methodologies and adherence to the registered plan, aims to increase the reproducibility of scientific effects (van den Akker et al., 2023). While studies on precognition have yielded mixed results, confusion and ambiguity are reduced when transparency and pre-defined approaches in such investigations are established (Kekecs et al., 2023; Muhmenthaler et al., 2024).

In essence, for controversial phenomena, preregistration acts as a safeguard against biases and enhances the verifiability of the phenomena, ultimately fostering greater trust and facilitating a more constructive scientific dialogue. However, the effectiveness still hinges on the completeness and adherence to the preregistered plan, as evidenced by discrepancies observed even in preregistered studies (van den Akker et al., 2024).

Aims of the Present Study

The aims of this study are the following: (i) conduct a systematic review of the outcomes of all completed preregistered studies up to October 2026, that feature confirmatory psi hypotheses; and (ii) compare the percentage of positive outcomes of these studies with those related to mainstream psychological phenomena.

Methods

General Methodology

We followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) Guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The PRISMA flow diagram is available in the Supplementary Material.

Preregistered Studies Search

We searched the two main repositories of preregistered studies used by authors interested in extra-sensory perception and mind-matter interaction: -the Open Science Framework (OSF), registration database (https://osf.io/search?tab=3), which entailed entry of these terms/keywords: extra-sensory perception OR mind-matter interaction OR parapsychology OR ESP OR PK OR paranormal OR PSI.

-the Koestler Parapsychology Unit (KPU) Study Registry (https://koestlerunit.wordpress.com/study-registry), a registry devoted to parapsychological lines of investigation and research launched in the fall of 2012 by Watt and Kennedy (2015).

The OSF is an open-source platform. Its mission is "to increase openness, integrity, and reproducibility of research"(https://osf.io/ymu94/overview).

The KPU website notes that the Registry "is primarily intended for confirmatory studies in parapsychological research, but exploratory studies can also be registered." (https://koestlerunit.wordpress.com/study-registry/). Naturally, registered studies are placed on the website for public viewing and tracking.

We also searched all preregistered studies that were included in the more recent meta-analyses (Storm & Tressoldi, 2023; Tressoldi & Storm, 2024; Tressoldi & Katz, 2023).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included all studies completed up to October 31, 2026, provided the original preregistration documents were accompanied by uploaded final results in the form of reports, preprints, or peerreviewed papers that featured test results on confirmatory hypotheses with the outcomes.

We excluded preregistered studies that only tested exploratory hypotheses regardless of outcomes.

We counted only the preregistered confirmatory hypotheses related to test ESP or PK, excluding those related to different phenomena for example, correlations with personality factors (e.g., Zdrenka, 2017), or if psi z-score of the target will correlate with an independent query about how certain the person is of his/her rating (Cardeña & Marcusson-Clavertz, 2020). (ix) overall outcome of the study (positive, negative); and (x) peer-review of the study (yes/no). The database was checked and confirmed independently by the second author (L.S.).

Regarding (ix), three studies had their hypothesis 'confirmed' but outcome is 0 (i.e., negative, Duma et al., 2019; Schlitz et al., 2021; Tressoldi et al., 2016). This is because we considered a study had a positive outcome if at least one confirmatory hypothesis was supported, and it followed the exact preregistered procedure and statistics, without any deviations from it. Krippner et al., (2019), study was excluded because the experimental hypotheses were completely different from those preregistered.

Results

All studies, according to inclusion and exclusion criteria, were analyzed by the first author (P.T.), who also conducted the statistical tests on the study data (L.S. checked the database and statistics).

Fifty studies met the inclusion and exclusion criteria: 38 studies (76%) of these were peer-reviewed, see (x) above. In terms of number of the studies with one (or more) legitimate confirmed hypothesis, see (viii) above, there are more confirmatory hypotheses than studies: 97 confirmatory hypotheses, and 60 experiments.

Positive Outcomes

In terms of 'outcomes', see (ix) above, only 20 confirmatory hypotheses out of 97 (20.6%) were 'positive, which is to say true to the preregistered wording (i.e., they followed the exact preregistered procedure and statistics, and did not deviate from it). If our count is based on experiments alone, 17 out of 60 (28.3%) were 'positive'.

A post-hoc observation, evidenced that the series of ten experiments related to the unconscious influence on the randomness of random number generators, carried out by Markus Maier and collaborators, resulted in 90% of failed confirmatory outcomes. Filtering out these experiments, the percentage of positive outcomes raised to 34%.

Task Investigated

The most investigated task (psi modality)-see (iii) above-was Precognition (including Precall, Retropriming, Retroactive Facilitation, and I Ching) with 22 of 60 experiments (36.6%), PK with 19 experiments (31.6%), Presentiment with 7 studies (11.6%), Clairvoyance (including Remote Viewing and Subtle Energy Detection) with 4 studies (6.6%), and Telepathy with 5 studies (8.3%). One unique study that tested Morphic Resonance (Vernon et al, 2021) was excluded from categorization (due to the difficulty of slotting it into a recognized category), but another tested Remote Viewing and PK (Mossbridge et al., 2024), so the final total of studies for this exercise was still 60.

Consciousness Condition

Many experiments tested participants in altered states of consciousness-see (iv) above. These 'altered' states included ganzfeld and hypnosis, or participants were simply 'unconscious'; there were 14 such experiments out of 60 (23.3%). However, the most common state was the 'Normal' (waking) state, 46 (76.6%).

Experimental Designs

The most common experimental design, see (v) above, was Forced-Choice (F-C) with 38 out of 60 (63.3%) experiments, followed by Free-Response (F-R) with 5 experiments (8.3%). Sixteen studies (35%), related mainly to presentiment investigations did not require a response (designated "NA"). One study tested forced-choice and free-response (Mossbridge et al., 2024).

Participant Type

Forty-three experiments out of 60 (71.7%) tested 'unseselected' participants, while 17 experiments used selected participants (28.3%), see (vi) above. A participant is usually designated 'selected' because they were intentionally recruited for possessing characteristics experimenters deem psi-conducive. Such participants often claim prior psi experience, or they practice a mental discipline (e.g., meditation, yoga, energy healing), or they are creative and/or open to new experiences, or they have strong emotional sensitivity or intuitive tendencies. There were 7 experiments out of 17 (41.1%) with at least one statistically supported hypothesis with selected participants, whereas there were 10 experiments out of 17 (58.8%) with at least one statistically supported hypothesis with unselected participants.

Discussion

The present systematic review aimed to critically evaluate the outcomes of preregistered studies investigating ESP and PK, and to compare their rate of positive outcomes against those observed in mainstream psychology. This inquiry is particularly pertinent given the broader scientific community's increasing emphasis on research credibility and reproducibility, with preregistration serving as a key strategy to mitigate QRPs such as p-hacking, HARKing, and selective reporting.

Our review, conducted in accordance with PRISMA Guidelines, systematically identified and analyzed 50 preregistered ESP and PK studies. Each study was rigorously assessed for variables including its results source, preregistration repository, task type, consciousness state, response format, participant selection, number of confirmatory hypotheses, confirmed hypotheses, overall outcome (positive or negative), and peer-review status. A positive outcome was specifically defined as the successful support of at least one confirmatory hypothesis, adhering strictly to the preregistered procedures and statistical analyses without deviations. Our search encompassed major preregistration repositories like the Open Science Framework and the KPU Study Registry, alongside relevant metaanalyses, ensuring comprehensive coverage of completed studies with available documentation for both preregistration and final results.

The findings indicate that 17 out of 60 experiments (28.3%) reported positive outcomes. On confirmatory hypotheses only, there are 20 out of 97 (20.6%) that were statistically supported (confirmed). We compared the above results with the positive outcome rates that can be found in mainstream preregistered psychological studies, as illustrated in Figure 1. Interestingly, and contrary to some expectations regarding the impact of preregistration, this review found that preregistered studies did not necessarily exhibit a much lower proportion of positive results when compared to mainstream studies. This is to say, there is a common belief that preregistration leads to fewer positive findings, because it reduces QRPs like p-hacking or HARKing. This belief is supported by historical analyses. Wiseman et al. (2019) compared Registered Reports (RRs) against non-RRs published in the European Journal of Parapsychology between 1976 and 1993. They found that only 8.4% of hypotheses in RRs were statistically significant, compared to 28.4% in non-RRs, suggesting that the introduction of registration effectively reduced false positives driven by QRPs during that era. However, the current landscape appears different.

Our review found that preregistration in contemporary studies did not see psi effects diminish to the same extent. This finding suggests that modern preregistration may not necessarily suppress positive outcomes as much as some critics or skeptics have assumed based on earlier implementation models. Parapsychologists can design effective studies under strict preregistration controls.

We also checked on psi modalities, altered states of consciousness, and experimental designs.

Precognition was the most common modality tested (36.6%). Participants were more commonly tested in normal (waking) states (76.6%), and the most common design was Forced-Choice (63.3%). Finally, unselected participants were tested almost twice as often as selected participants (71.7% vs. 28.3%, respectively), but there appeared to be no performance advantage in the latter group.

Study limitations

One limitation of this study is that at present there are no independent criteria to assess the quality of preregistrations. One solution is to preregister the study as a registered report (https://www.cos.io/initiatives/registered-reports), where in stage 1, the preregistration is peer reviewed.

Even if all preregistrations submitted to the KPU Registry are reviewed by Caroline Watt and Jim Kennedy, allowing the authors to have feedback about the clarity and the details of their study, there is no control of the adherence between what was preregistered and what is reported in the final reports or the published peer-reviewed papers.

Comparing preregistrations with final reports is difficult. It requires considerable time, careful checking, and expertise, and judgements. Checking the quality of preregistered methods is also difficult. It requires expertise, close attention to detail in particular in the definition on how the confirmatory hypotheses will be tested, with particular attention to the statistical power.

In conclusion, while preregistration significantly contributes to enhancing the transparency and methodological rigor of research in controversial areas like ESP and PK, its impact on the rate of positive findings or the direct prevention of specific biases like p-hacking and HARKing may be more nuanced than often assumed. The positive outcome rate observed in this systematic review provides valuable empirical data for ongoing discussions about research integrity and reproducibility within parapsychological studies.

It is relevant to consider that statistical results that cannot reject the null hypotheses within the Null Hypothesis Significance Testing approach (the most common approach in the studies examined above), are not outright support of the null hypotheses. Instead, the correct reading is that the alternative hypotheses were not supported on those occasions. That is, the phenomena under investigation are not proved to be nonexistent: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Very often, authors interested in ESP and PK ignore or do not take into account the fact that expected effects are often small, as documented in the available meta-analyses (e.g., Storm & Tressoldi, 2023; see also Cardeña, 2018). For example, the expected effect sizes in tasks requiring a forced-choice in a normal state of consciousness range from .009 to .02 (Storm & Tressoldi, 2023; see also Cardeña, 2018). To achieve a statistical power of a minimum .80, and the expected effect size set to .05 above the expected chance of .5, with a one-tailed binomial test, requires 620 trials (or 620 participants performing a single trial).

In closing, the outcomes observed in this systematic review can be used as a first benchmark for all future investigations.
