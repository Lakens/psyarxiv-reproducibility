Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .151 In M1 (Table S1 p = .151) but moved toward zero afterwards (0.0059 per year, p p = .004).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

In most of the world's countries, young adults report the highest well-being among different age groups. A notable exception has emerged in North America and Northern Europe, where this pattern has reversed, with older adults reporting greater well-being than the young. We analyzed Gallup World Poll data from 2006-2025, covering 2.9 million respondents worldwide and 17,007 in Norway, to examine shifts in the age gap in well-being (as measured with the life evaluation metric, the Cantril Ladder) and possible determinants. Norway is a particularly valuable case for illustrating this reversal since the country ranked as the world's happiest in 2017, but has declined in both absolute terms and relative rankings, with national statistics revealing a pronounced wellbeing gap between young and old. We found that the decline in life evaluation scores in Norway largely reflects lower scores among young adults (<30 years), whose scores have dropped significantly since 2017 (p < .001; d = 0.36) and are now markedly lower than those of older adults (60+; p < .001; d = 0.34). We further confirmed that this trend is limited to a small set of countries. Of all 2,648 correlations between age and life evaluation across 159 different countries and 20 years, 83% were negative, and only 3% exceeded .10, the magnitude observed in Norway 2023-2025. We examined potential explanations of the observed change in Norway. Only a few of the 50 candidate variables in the Gallup World Poll were found to be related to the increased age difference in life evaluations in Norway; the most important variables were i) affect-related factors (e.g., feeling well-rested and sadness), and ii) socioeconomic satisfaction (e.g., satisfaction with standard of living and household income, although not absolute income) iii) institutional trust (e.g., confidence in the government and judicial system). This study emphasizes the imperative for highly developed countries to prioritise the well-being of their young despite overall prosperity and high living standards.

Introduction

Who is happier, the young or the old? Findings on the association between age and wellbeing are mixed and range from no relationship to positive and negative age gradients, as well as reports of U-curves and J-curves (Bartram, 2021 (Bartram, , 2023;; Blanchflower et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2022; Helliwell et al., 2024). Recent studies suggest a decline in well-being among the young, in some countries and regions (Helliwell et al., 2024). However, there is limited knowledge about the relations between age and well-being worldwide, how these relations have changed over time, and the factors driving such changes. Here, we use Norway as a case for studying age differences in life evaluations. In 2017 Norway was ranked as the world's happiest country (Helliwell et al., 2017) but has since fallen to the lowest level of life evaluation among the Nordic countries (Helliwell et al., 2026). Which processes and factors have been involved in this development from summit to slope? We set out to examine whether Norway's relative decline in life evaluations is due to a particular decline among the young, when it happened, to what extent the developments in Norway are paralleled in other countries, and which factors may explain the recent trends.

Life evaluation refers to a person's reflective assessment of their life as a whole, rather than to a specific component of well-being such as positive affect, meaning, or functioning. It is typically measured using the Cantril Ladder, which asks respondents to place their lives on a ladder ranging from the worst possible life for them to the best possible life for them. The measure therefore captures a broad, global evaluation of one's life, but does not by itself provide a comprehensive assessment of well-being or its constituent dimensions. The Cantril Ladder is the primary metric reported in the World Happiness Report (WHR; Helliwell et al., 2026). However, its framing may lead respondents to think about power and wealth rather than broader well-being (Nilsson et al., 2024). It nevertheless remains widely used for global comparisons and shows strong empirical overlap with life satisfaction, especially in highly developed countries (Bjørnskov, 2010).

Several studies have reported a U-shaped relationship between age and well-being, with lower well-being among middle-aged adults than among younger and older adults (cf. Blanchflower, 2021; Helliwell et al., 2024). This finding has been criticized on methodological grounds, particularly for relying on analyses that include potentially inappropriate control variables (Bartram, 2021 (Bartram, , 2023;; Glenn, 2009; Hellevik, 2017). Even when these methodological concerns are taken into account, young people have generally reported high levels of well-being, for example in Norway (Hellevik, 2017), while older adults have reported levels of well-being comparable to, or even higher than, those of younger adults, despite age-related declines in physical health, functional capacity, and social resources (the well-being paradox of aging; Baltes & Baltes, 1990; Blanchflower & Oswald, 2008).

However, recent evidence paints an increasingly complex picture of how life evaluations vary with age and cohorts (Helliwell et al., 2024). In some regions, the U-shaped pattern seems to have shifted to a linear, or nearly linear, negative association, with younger adults reporting higher life evaluations than older ones (cf. Blanchflower et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2022; Helliwell et al., 2024). Other studies indicate a J-shaped pattern, with almost flat levels across young adulthood and midlife (with only slightly lower levels in midlife) while older adults show substantially higher levels (cf. Lomas et al., 2025). In contrast, in North America and Norway, younger adults report lower life evaluation than older adults, producing a positive linear association between age and life evaluation (Chen et al., 2022; Helliwell et al., 2024). The 2024 WHR underscores this rare reversal, showing that people under 30 reported the lowest life evaluation in 7 of 143 countries (Helliwell et al., 2024). Additionally, when ranking countries by changes in life evaluation among those under 25, the United States, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand (the NANZ region) fall near the bottom, placing them between 122nd and 133rd out of 136 countries (Helliwell et al., 2026). This indicates that young people in these countries have experienced some of the largest declines in life evaluation globally during the past years.

The onset and development of this shift, along with its underlying causes, remain unclear, sparking growing concern and debate about the well-being of young people. These patterns, however, are largely based on cross-sectional data, which conflate age, period, and cohort effects, a limitation that applies equally to the life evaluation findings reported above. Longitudinal evidence, by contrast, indicates that life satisfaction typically increases modestly but systematically from around age 16 into the 60s and then declines in late old age (Buecker et al., 2023). In sum, age differences may reflect both normative developmental change and cohortor period-specific influences. Notably, Buecker et al.'s study found no evidence that birth cohort (year of birth) moderated the slopes of change in life satisfaction, positive affect, or negative affect. Their database, however, is dominated by high-income, predominantly Western samples, and does not support strong conclusions about cohort differences in mean levels of well-being or recent sociohistorical shifts in younger generations' well-being (cf. Gerstorf et al., 2020). Further insights into this phenomenon are crucial both for advancing theories and understanding of the factors that shape population well-being, and for informing effective policy responses, which would require broader global data.

Norway represents a particularly interesting case for understanding young life evaluation and its change. It has a reputation as one of the world's happiest countries and topped the global rankings in 2017 (Helliwell et al., 2017). Less than a decade later, however, the picture looks different: while point estimates of life evaluation have risen across the other Nordic countries, Norway has declined by 0.30 points and now ranks as the least happy among them (Helliwell et al., 2026). At the same time, there are signs that young people fare worse than older adults. Recent studies indicate that Norwegian adolescents and young adults score lower on life evaluation and mental health than the rest of the adult population (Hellevik & Hellevik, 2021; Kozák et al., 2023; Nes & Ørstavik, 2022; Potrebny et al., 2019; Potrebny et al., 2025). In fact, a near-linear positive association with age has been observed for life evaluation and related wellbeing measures (e.g., meaning in life, positive emotions, trust) since 2019-2020, when comprehensive monitoring of national and regional well-being was introduced in Norway (Aarø et al., 2024). This pattern is striking because Norway is a wealthy welfare state with strong social institutions, and relative economic resilience which might be expected to buffer younger adults from economic pressures. Nevertheless, young people in Norway, as in other high-income countries, face a range of challenges. They navigate complex transitions through education and employment under increasing pressure from housing affordability and living costs (Cournède & Plouin, 2022; Eurofound, 2024; OECD, 2022, 2025). The transition to parenthood is occurring later than in previous cohorts (OECD, 2022), alongside changes in fertility intentions and expectations about family formation (Dommermuth et al., 2025), which may reflect a change in the content and timing of normative life scripts rather than their disappearance (Janssen & Rubin, 2011; Liefbroer & Billari, 2010). Digitalisation has also reshaped how young people communicate, work, and form and maintain relationships (OECD, 2019; Nesi et al., 2018). Taken together, these contrasting factors make Norway an informative context for examining age differences in life evaluation over time.

When the age gradient in life evaluations shifted, the extent to which declining life evaluations among young adults contributed to the overall decline, whether similar shifts are evident in other countries, and what factors underlie these developments remain unclear. Several candidate mechanisms have been proposed. The most widely discussed is perhaps social media use, but findings remain mixed. Meta-analytic, cross-national, and longitudinal evidence indicates that relations between social media use and reduced life evaluations tend to be small but heterogeneous (Helliwell et al., 2026; Huang, 2022; Orben & Przybylski, 2019; Twenge et al., 2020), although some scholars argue that the effects are larger than these syntheses indicate (Haidt & Allen, 2020). Another explanation, particularly relevant in Norway, may be a growing gap in financial stability between younger and older adults (Normann, In the present study, we examine changes in life evaluation across age groups in Norway between 2006 and 2025. Specifically, we pursue three main objectives. First, we aim to identify when the age gradient in life evaluation shifted in Norway. Second, we seek to place the Norwegian findings in an international context by examining whether similar age-related patterns in life evaluation can be observed in other countries. Finally, we aim to explore potential factors underlying the observed trend in Norway.

Methods

Data Collection and Sample

We capitalized on the Gallup World Poll (GWP), a global survey designed to measure public opinion and monitor key wellbeing indicators across countries (Gallup, 2011; 2019). It involves nationally representative samples of the adult population aged 15 and older, with approximately 1,000 people per country annually, with a different selection of people each year. The GWP survey usually takes 15-20 minutes to complete, and includes around 60-80 questions (the number varies across respondents based on screener items, filters and skip patterns). This paper is based on data spanning all years of the GWP from 2006 to 2025 with the Norwegian data lacking in 2007, 2009-2011 and 2013. There were a total of 3,012,810 participants in the sample. After excluding respondents with less than $100 in annual household income and respondents with no data on Cantril Ladder, age and gender, 2,933,981 participants remained, of which 17,007 were in Norway. Data collection was undertaken in accordance with the established protocol of the GWP. Data collection in Norway was conducted by phone every year. We used listwise deletion for missing data, given that missing values were rare. The 71 variables (67 candidate variables plus life evaluation, age, gender, and weights) used in the analysis comprised 893 variable-by-year measurements. The mean missingness, defined as responses of either "I don't know" or "refused to answer", was 1.73%, with a median of 0.5% (maximum 28%), across the 893 variable-by-year measurements.

We adopted the age groups used in the WHR special chapter (Helliwell et al., 2024): below 30 years, 30-60 years, and 60+ years (the distribution of these age groups can be seen in Figure S16). In the WHR, the middle-aged group was further divided into 30-45 and 46-60, but in the case of Norway this finer division made little difference (Supplemental Figure S5), and we hence retained three groups for clarity in the main manuscript. Although the age group distribution was imbalanced, with 15% of the data in the below 30 age group and 50% in the middle-aged group, Gallup has sampling weights for age, gender and socioeconomic status; hence, we chose to keep the three age groups despite the imbalance for ease of interpretation. We further ran analyses treating age both as a categorical (grouped) variable and as a continuous variable. Defining age groups by current age rather than birth cohort implies that group composition changes over time. For example, the sub 30 age group was born 1976-1991 in the 2006 assessment, but 1995-2010 in the 2025 assessment. Cohort-based analyses are shown in Supplemental Material Figure S1.

Items Analysed

Life Evaluation

The main item of interest was the life evaluation question in the GWP, the Cantril Ladder (Cantril, 1965), which is phrased as follows: "Please imagine a ladder, with steps numbered from 0 at the bottom to 10 at the top. The top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you. On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time?"

In most of our analyses, we used the Cantril Ladder as the WHR does, meaning that we took the average of the last three years to offer clean comparisons. The WHR also uses threeyear averages to increase statistical power -a rationale that is relevant in our study too, particularly when examining subgroups. We present yearly assessments in the Supplemental Material Figure S6.

Candidate Variables and Factors

We explored 67 other candidate variables in the GWP as potential explanatory factors of the strengthening association between age and life evaluation in Norway (see Table S4). These ranged from affective experiences (e.g., experienced enjoyment the previous day), to economic standards (e.g., actual household income and satisfaction with household income), to institutional trust (e.g., corruption in business and government) and safety (e.g., feeling safe walking at night) measures. Of note, some GWP items were only asked in a few years (e.g., liking what you do every day). We used all variables that were measured at least two years in or before 2017 and that were also measured at least two years in 2018 or later. Hence, we made use of all available Gallup variables with enough data before and after 2017.

Factor analysis

To reduce the 67 candidate variables to broader domains and reduce the number of statistical tests, we conducted a principal component analysis with oblimin rotation. To prevent specific years from shaping the structure, each variable was standardised within each survey year, and the analysis was run on the pairwise correlation matrix pooled across 2006-2025, so that no respondents were excluded for missing items. The scree plot levelled off after six components, which together explained 25% of the variance. Of these six, we selected three based on interpretability and stability. The components were Institutional Trust (10 items, e.g., confidence in the national government and the judicial system), Affect Balance (5 items: enjoyment, smiling, feeling well-rested, [low] worry and [low] sadness), and City Likeability (5 items, e.g., city satisfaction and the city being a good place for immigrants).

Component scores were the mean of the salient items (loading .40), ≥ standardised across all years so that change over time was preserved. To assess stability, we refitted the solution separately in 2015-2017 and 2023-2025 and compared the loadings with Tucker's congruence coefficient. Across periods, congruence for the three components was .94, .74 and .85 over all loadings, and .99, .89 and .99 over the items defining each component. Affect Balance was the least stable because smiling and enjoyment loaded more weakly in 2015-2017.

The remaining 47 items that did not make it into any of the factors were entered as single variables. Scree plot, loadings and stability statistics are reported in Table S3 and Figures S10-11.

Data analysis

The analysis focused primarily on the trajectory of life evaluation across age and time in Norway. When comparing different periods, we specifically examined the years 2015-2017 against 2023-2025. Norway ranked number 1 in the world in 2017 with an average life evaluation of 7.537 (data spanning 2014-2016) but was beaten by Finland (and Norway ranked number 2) in 2018 with data from 2015-2017 despite an increase in absolute average to 7.594. We thus compared Norway's 2015-2017 life evaluation peak with the most recent three-year period, i.e., 2023-2025.

Life evaluation across age and time in Norway

We first examined life evaluation trends for the three age groups i) below 30, ii) 30-60, and iii) 60+, between 2006 and 2025. To do this, we grouped survey responses into overlapping three-year periods (e.g., 2023-2025 representing 2025) and assigned each participant to the period in which they were interviewed. We then fitted a weighted linear regression predicting the Cantril Ladder score from year, age group, their interaction, and gender. From this model we extracted adjusted mean scores for each age group in each period, and calculated Cohen's d to quantify (1) how much each age group's mean changed from one period to the next, and (2) how large the gap was between age groups within each period. Finally, these adjusted means were plotted with smoothed trend lines.

Life evaluation and age in the world

Next, we examined developments in the life evaluation-age association across all countries. We applied weighted correlations in each country and year across the GWP dataset between 2006 and 2025; here, we did not average life evaluation across the last three years, to get independent correlations. This resulted in 2,648 unique correlations. We plotted the correlations against year with one smoothed trend line per country (n = 159). To highlight countries where older people have come to evaluate their life higher than young people (in a linear fashion), we chose two inclusion criteria. First, the correlation in 2023-2025 had to be at least .10 stronger than the correlation in 2015-2017. Second, the correlation in 2023-2025 had to be r ≥ .10 to align with at least small-sized positive correlation coefficients (Gignac and Szodorai, 2016).

Multilevel analysis

To confirm the patterns in the global correlation analysis, we estimated multilevel models that made use of all the data. These were linear mixed models with respondents nested in country-years within countries, and a crossed random intercept for survey year. We regressed life evaluation on standardised age and survey year. Year was entered as two linear slopes joined at 2017, the year Norway ranked first in the World Happiness Report. Age × year interactions captured whether the association between age and life evaluation changed before and after 2017. We allowed the age slope and its interactions with year to vary across countries, and the age slope to vary across country-years. A second model added age² and its interactions with year. From the first model, we derived each country's age gradient (fixed effects plus country random effects) with 95% intervals, averaged over the windows used in Figure 2. We applied Gallup weights throughout. Supplemental Material Tables S1-2 and Figures S8-9 give the full specification and results.

Explaining the change in the age-life evaluation association.

The final group of analyses aimed to explain the emerging difference in life evaluation change across age in Norway. We first looked at how much each of the variables (47 single items and three factors) increased in explained variance in a multiple regression with age and year for two different time periods. This analysis screened for variables that became more strongly associated with life evaluation as the age gap in life evaluation grew. We ran a multiple regression using the formula life evaluation ~ year + age + candidate variable. This analysis was performed for two time periods, 2006 to 2017 and 2018 to 2025. Our goal was to identify which candidate variable resulted in the largest adjusted R² increase. We established three cutoff years, 2015, 2016, and 2017, to enhance the robustness of the analysis and make sure no single year disproportionately influenced the results. We focused on the average of the adjusted R² differences. The results from the analysis showed how much additional variance each candidate variable explained in life evaluation next to age and time between the early and late time periods. A large difference indicates that the candidate variable explained more variance in the recent time period than in the earlier time period.

Second, we looked at the strength of the weighted correlation coefficient between life evaluation and each candidate variable among those under 30, comparing 2015-2017 and 2023-2025. We significance-tested the correlation strength differences and plotted those that were significantly stronger in 2023-2025 compared to 2015-2017. To test whether the association between each candidate variable and life evaluation changed over time, we additionally estimated variable × year interactions among the below 30 age group. Life evaluation and each candidate variable were z-standardised using weighted means and standard deviations, and all models were estimated with the Gallup sample weights. For each variable we fitted two models of the change in association: (i) a linear trend across the full series (variable × year); (ii) a piecewise trend allowing an additional change in slope after 2017, the year of the break in the age-life evaluation association. Because many variables were tested, we controlled for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure within each model type.

Third, we looked at the standardised mean difference (i.e., the difference in zstandardised group means) between the below 30 and above 60 age groups in the two different time periods. We compared the difference in 2015-2017 and 2023-2025 and plotted the 10 variables with the strongest change.

Finally, we evaluated the mediating effect of the candidate variables on the age-life evaluation relationship in 2023-2025, comparing the standardised beta of age on life evaluation when it was the sole predictor and how much the standardised beta of age shrank after adding each of the candidate variables. Since age cannot be caused by any psychological variables, we can assess the mediation on the age-life evaluation relationship simply by examining how much the relationship decreases when adding each candidate variable. This interpretation assumes that life evaluation does not cause the candidate variables and that they share no common causes, which cross-sectional data cannot establish. We present the mediating effect of the two candidate variables that had the strongest mediating effect.

Based on the results of the above analyses, we presented the piecewise linear trends before and after 2017 of the variables that showed the strongest indications of being involved in the change of the life evaluation-age association.

Effect size interpretation, multiple correction and software

We interpreted effect sizes based on empirically derived guidelines. These are, for correlations, small r = .10, medium = .20, and strong = .30 (Gignac and Szodorai, 2016). For Cohen's d, they are small d = 0.15, medium d = 0.36, and strong d = 0.65 (Lovakov & Agadullina, 2021). Whenever we ran multiple analyses at once, we made corrections for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). All analyses were done in R (R Core Team, 2021) and RStudio (RStudio Team, 2015). The code can be accessed at the Open Science Framework. The data can be accessed through Gallup.

Results

Using past three-year averages as employed in the World Happiness Report, we observed that the life evaluation of Norwegian young adults started to decline after 2017 (Figure 1). The representative data with sampling weights and controlling for gender did not indicate any significant differences between the three age groups i) below 30, ii) 30-60, and iii) above 60, between 2006 and 2020. In 2017, when Norway ranked number one in the world on life evaluation, its young adults also peaked. However, from 2018, the life evaluation score of the 60+ age group remained relatively stable at around 7.6, peaking at 7.66 in 2023 and scoring 7.55 in 2025, while scores for the middle-aged and young declined. The 30-60 age group declined from 7.59 in 2017 to a record low 7.16 in 2024 and 7.17 in 2025, resulting in a Cohen's d difference to the 60+ age group of 0.30 in 2024 and 0.23 in 2025. Explanations for the declining life evaluation among Norwegian young adults

Using an exploratory approach, we tested 50 variables in the Gallup World Poll to identify conditions that may explain the change in life evaluation across age after 2017. These comprised 47 single-item variables with sufficient data across all survey years, and three factors extracted from the original 67 variables (see Supplemental Material Figures S10-11 and Table S3). We initially tested how the change in stress, income, and social support levels related to the life evaluation change among age groups. As these did not show strong evidence of explaining the observed change (Supplemental Material Figure S18), we took a more exploratory and datadriven approach. Using principal component analysis, we placed 20 of the variables in three relatively parsimonious factors: Institutional Trust (e.g., confidence in the national government and the judicial system), Affect Balance (e.g., enjoyment, smiling, feeling well-rested), and City Likeability (e.g., city satisfaction and the city being a good place for immigrants).

In Figure 3, we show four ways to help understand the change. First, we tested how much additional variance each of the 50 variables (47 single candidates and three factors) explained beyond year and age, comparing 2006-2017 with 2018-2025. To ensure robustness around the 2015-2017 cutoff, we treated each of these three years as separate cutoffs, averaged the adjusted R² values from the early and late models, and then calculated their difference. We used the mean difference across the three cutoff years. First, the explained variance of the baseline models (with age and year) averaged 0% of life evaluation before 2018, but 1.05% (equivalent of r = .10) after 2018. The candidate variables explained on average 1.90% more variance beyond the baseline models in the early periods and 2.85% more in the later periods (difference was significant, p < .001), indicating that the candidate variables explained more variance overall in more recent years. Among the candidate variables, standard of living satisfaction increased the most in explained variance, from 6.1% in 2006-2017 to 12.8% in 2018-2025. This means that standard of living satisfaction explained 6.7% more variance in life evaluation during the past years and hence was more strongly associated with life evaluation, beyond year and age, in the latter period. Other variables whose association strengthened included the Institutional Trust and Affect Balance components. These variables explained 3.0-6.5% more variance next to year and age after 2017 than before 2017.

Next, we evaluated the change in (weighted) correlations among the Norwegians below 30 in the peak period 2015-2017 compared to the latest three-year period 2023-2025 (Figure 3B). Here, we only show the variables that had a significant change in correlation coefficient after controlling for multiple comparisons (p < .05). Anger showed the strongest shift, from a positive correlation of r = .06 to a negative correlation of r = -.29. Other affective experiences that are reported in Figure 3A that also appeared in this analysis were feelings of pain (from r = -.01 to -.24) and being respected (from r = -.03 to .17). Standard of living satisfaction also showed a significant change from r = .29 to .46. These variables related more strongly to life evaluation among the Norwegian younger adults in the latest time period, when the Norwegian younger adults reached the bottom in life evaluation, compared to the peak period of 2015-2017, when they were all more weakly related. To more formally test whether these associations changed over time, we regressed life evaluation among under-30s on year, each candidate variable and their interaction. Modelled as one linear trend over 2006-2025, the association with opportunities to make friends strengthened significantly. With separate trends before and after 2017, the association with anger changed significantly (Supplemental Material Figures S12 and S13). Hence, some variables became more strongly associated with the life evaluation of Norwegians below 30 in recent years, not least anger.

Next, we evaluated the variables that had the largest shifts in differences between people under 30 and those over 60 (Figure 3C). Among the most pronounced changes were those in landline phone access and internet access, where, in 2015-2017 Norwegians below 30 had a 0.99 points lower and 0.51 standardised points higher score, respectively, compared to Norwegians above 60, differences that decreased to -0.12 and 0.21 points, respectively. The change in these variables can be explained by decreased variance in landline phones and internet access (i.e., the older getting rid of landline phones and getting internet access; see Figure S15). Beyond landline phone and internet access, there were significant shifts in Institutional Trust and optimistic variables. For example, with regard to public institutions, in 2015-2017, young Norwegians scored 0.30 and 0.16 standardised points higher than older Norwegians on the Institutional Trust factor and confidence in the military, respectively. The difference swapped such that older Norwegians scored 0.05 and 0.19 points higher, respectively, in 2023-2025. In terms of optimism, the younger scored 1.05 and 0.06 points higher on standard of living getting better and good time to find a job, respectively, and this difference decreased to 0.62 and flipped to 0.48 in favour of older Norwegians in 2023-2025, respectively. Some other variables turned from young Norwegians scoring higher to older scoring higher, such as learning or doing something interesting yesterday and social support.

Lastly, we evaluated the mediating effect on the age-life evaluation association of each candidate variable in the 2023-2025 period (Figure 3D). In Supplemental Material Figure S17 we present the 10 variables with the strongest mediating effect on the age beta coefficient. Out of these, we picked the two strongest variables (however, we excluded married as it is a proxy for age) to visualise in Figure 3D. The standardised beta of age on life evaluation by itself was .12 (p < .001). When adding household income satisfaction to the model, the age beta shrank by 46%, to 0.07 (p < .001). After also adding the Affect Balance factor to the model, age's beta coefficient had shrunk by 66% from its original beta. The beta was still significant (p < .05), but smaller.

When treating age as three groups (as in Figure 1), the non-standardised beta coefficient for being below 30 was .56 (p < .001; resembling absolute point difference seen in Figure 1). When including household income satisfaction, this beta coefficient, or difference between old and young Norwegians in absolute terms, shrank to .34 (p < .001) and reduced to .25 (p < .05) when including the Affect Balance factor as well. Thus, household income satisfaction and Affect Balance partially mediated the relationship between age and life evaluation in 2023-2025.

All in all, it appears that affective experiences and satisfaction with material standards have become more strongly associated with life evaluation among Norwegian young adults, and optimism and confidence in public institutions have worsened for the young relative to older adults.

The slopes across age in the candidate variables

To illustrate these changes, we plotted the three factors and the five strongest candidate variables and their linear slopes for each of our three age groups with one slope 2006-2017 and one from 2017-2025 (Figure 4). First, the slopes for life evaluation are shown on top, confirming previous results with nearly flat lines for all age groups until 2017, with sharp negative turns for the 30-60 and below 30 age groups thereafter. Looking at our strongest candidate variable, standard of living satisfaction, a somewhat similar trend can be seen, which also was the case for household income satisfaction, Affect Balance, and opportunities to make friends. Until 2017, the standard of living satisfaction, household income satisfaction, Affect Balance, and opportunities to make friends variables were flat or mildly rising. After 2017, both younger age groups declined in scores.

We note that anger, while important for Norwegians below 30, has not changed much among them. The last three variables, Institutional Trust, City Likeability and learning or doing something interesting, all increased for the 60+ age group and decreased for the sub 30 age group. All in all, these final results show some descriptive statistics on the development of the candidate variables. While they indicate that emotions and standard of living satisfaction may be contributing factors, they are not more than indications.

Discussion

Amid growing international concern about the well-being of young people (Handa et al., 2023; Marquez et al., 2024) and recent shifts in the age distribution of well-being (Blanchflower et al., 2025; Twenge & Blanchflower, 2025), the present study set out to address three interrelated research questions using data from 2.9 million individuals across the world and 17,000 respondents in Norway, over 20 years. Our first research question concerned when the age gradient in well-being changed in Norway. We found that this shift occurred around 2017, after which young Norwegians reported lower life evaluation than their older 60+ counterparts. Our second research question placed the Norwegian findings in an international context by examining whether similar age-well-being patterns were evident in other countries. Only a few countries, predominantly Nordic and English-speaking, showed a pattern similar to that observed in Norway. Finally, our third research question investigated factors that may underlie this trend in Norway. We found some support for subjective economic standards, emotional health, and institutional trust factors as potential explanations.

Our findings document a historically recent change in the distribution of life evaluations across age groups in Norway. The findings are in line with observations based on The Norwegian Public Health Surveys (Aarø et al., 2024), which consistently report lower well-being among the young and a near-linear gradient across age groups, since they started collecting data in 2019. Data from the Ipsos Norwegian Monitor, ranging back to 1985, show a marked decline in happiness and life satisfaction ratings among the young samples (15-39 years) between 2009 and 2019, contrasting an earlier period (1985-2009) characterized by stable or modestly rising levels (Hellevik & Hellevik, 2021). This latter study also suggests that the downward trend among the young samples accounts for the observed negative trajectory at the Norwegian population level. Declines in mental health and increases in loneliness have also been reported among Norwegian adolescent samples (von Soest et al., 2022; Kozák et al., 2023).

Our analyses show that the age pattern observed in Norway is only evident in a few other countries such as Sweden, China, Canada, and the US. These findings are in line with studies using other data sets than the GWP, which also indicate a shifting age trend in such countries (Chen et al., 2022; Twenge & Blanchflower, 2025). Because these countries differ in welfare provision and affluence, the Norwegian decline is unlikely to be explained solely by factors unique to Norway. A corresponding pattern is also evident for mental ill-being (e.g., anxiety and depressive symptoms). Pooled cross-sectional Global Minds data across 44 countries from 2020-2025, including the US and the United Kingdom (UK), show that scores on five different indicators of mental ill-being are higher among younger respondents than among older ones (Blanchflower et al., 2025). In both the US and the UK, this shift appears to have begun before the pandemic, which accelerated an existing trajectory. Notably, these age comparisons are cross-sectional and therefore cannot establish whether the observed differences reflect agerelated changes or birth cohort differences. Nor can they fully disentangle age, cohort, and period effects.

What accounts for declining life evaluation among Norwegian young adults?

Several contextual features make Norway an informative case for understanding shifts in young adults' well-being. The country combines high average life evaluations (Statistics Norway, 2025b) with extensive welfare protection and comparatively low levels of material deprivation (OECD, 2025). Yet young adults face rising housing costs (OECD, 2022), growing social gradients (Goldblatt et al., 2023), rapid technological change requiring continuous reskilling (Fauske, 2020), and societal pressures relating to climate concerns (Hickman et al., 2021). Most of these pressures are shared with other high-income countries, which fits the similar age patterns we observed in, for example, Sweden, Canada, and the United States. Together they may help explain why the well-being of young Norwegians has declined despite the buffers a wealthy welfare state would be expected to provide.

Our findings point to three clusters of explanatory factors, which echo these broader pressures. The first is economic and material living conditions. The identified economic risk factors include dissatisfaction with household income and living standards, but not reported income. More than twice as many younger Norwegians (13%) were dissatisfied with their household income compared with older adults (5%) in 2023-2025 and this difference appeared to account for part of the difference in life evaluation scores. Given the subjective nature of the household income satisfaction item, the economic connotations of the Cantril Ladder (Nilsson et al., 2024), and the association between income and well-being (e.g., Killingsworth et al., 2023), it is not surprising that the two variables share some variance. Although young adults in Norway may be better off economically than many others worldwide, they face a more difficult financial situation than the middle-aged and older adults they may compare themselves to (Luttmer, 2005). Wealth among Norwegians aged 60+ has increased markedly, and older adults have benefited from income growth and labour participation, drawing on both pensions and earnings (Normann, 2022; Hattrem, 2022).

Consistent with our GWP findings, the national wellbeing surveys 2020-2025 show that 50% of adults under 25 report financial dissatisfaction compared with one third of those aged 45-66, and fewer than 20% of those aged 66 and older (Statistics Norway, 2025c). A national student survey likewise found high rates of financial difficulties, linked to elevated risks of mental health problems, self-harm, suicide attempts, failed exams (Bøe et al., 2021), and major depression at one-year follow-up (Sivertsen et al., 2025).

The second cluster concerns emotional health. Affective experiences became more closely tied to life evaluation among the young. Adding the Affect Balance factor to the model with age and life evaluation reduced the age coefficient by 43%, and anger showed the largest shift in association with life evaluation among Norwegians under 30, followed by pain. Anger levels themselves did not increase significantly over the period, but what changed was how closely anger experiences were tied to life evaluations. In general, the rise in emotional health problems among the young is consistent with other findings on the matter, in Norway as well as globally (Almeida et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022; Thapar et al., 2022).

Third, the trend in Institutional Trust reversed. In 2015-2017, young Norwegians scored higher than older ones on Institutional Trust; by 2023-2025, older Norwegians scored higher. Institutional trust is often seen as an explanation for why the Nordic countries score high on life evaluation (Martela et al., 2020).

A generation at risk?

Although younger Norwegian adults continue to report life evaluation ratings above the global average, the observed negative trend among this group is concerning. Our findings indicate a decline in life evaluation ratings among Norwegian young adults (i.e., <30), and the difference is statistically significant, with a Cohen's d of 0.34 relative to those aged 60 and above in 2025. This effect size is small to medium in absolute terms, but it is concerning because it did not exist some years ago.

This trend raises important societal concerns. Sustained decline in young people's wellbeing may impair the functioning of future generations, with potential consequences for social and economic productivity and national flourishing. Previous studies show that well-being is both associated with and causally related to higher labor productivity (cf. In long-lived societies such as Norway, younger adults may adjust their expectations to support sustained investment in education, career, and personal growth over a longer life course, particularly given that the life evaluation metric (the Cantril Ladder) is related to social status, hierarchy, and wealth (Nilsson et al., 2024). Such recalibration can be adaptive, promoting long-term planning and growth. Taken together, the observed age differences in life evaluation likely reflect a combination of adaptive developmental processes, societal context, and potential risk factors, highlighting the importance of monitoring trends while considering their nuanced interpretation.

Limitations and future research

Our analyses were based on a large data set spanning 20 years. The observed trends and age group differences are robust and statistically significant, although small to medium in size. However, when it comes to explanatory factors, our analyses are exploratory and cannot provide evidence of causality. Rather, these factors should be considered candidates for further exploration and testing. This is particularly important because the present analysis adopted a data-driven approach, after initially testing whether changes in stress, income, and social support explained the change based on theoretical assumptions, but found little evidence that they did. In addition, some conceptual overlap may exist between several of the affective candidate variables and life evaluation, as both capture aspects of well-being. The direction of the associations is also uncertain: a stronger association between, for example, anger and life evaluation could mean that life evaluation shapes how anger is reported, or that a third factor affects both. Notably, in some analyses, access to a landline phone and internet access emerged as explanatory variables. Although these variables most likely reflect broader technological changes, their appearance provides face validity for the analysis by capturing trends that would be expected to appear in the data. Further, additional factors not captured by our dataset may contribute to the decline in life evaluation among Norwegians under 30. These could include country-specific characteristics, such as economic conditions, social policies, cultural norms, or welfare systems. While cohort patterns are shown descriptively (see Supplemental Figure S1), our study primarily examines age differences over time, and explicitly disentangling age, period, and cohort effects is beyond the scope of this analysis.

Changes in the demographic composition of the population could in principle contribute to the age differences we observe. Immigration to Norway increased after the Russian invasion of Ukraine (Statistics Norway, 2026), and in the Gallup data the share of participants under 30 born abroad rose from 2020, more steeply than among those aged 60 and above. Those under 30 born abroad reported lower life evaluations than those born in Norway, but only in 2024. Three considerations argue against immigration as a major explanation. The Gallup data do not specify respondents' countries of origin; the difference appeared in only one survey year; and recent studies from Sweden show that being foreign-born is associated with little or no difference in well-being (Bittár et al., 2025; Nilsson et al., 2026). Demographic change may nevertheless contribute to shifts in the age-life evaluation association and warrants further investigation in data with more detailed information on migration background.

Furthermore, individuals entering young adulthood around 2017 -coinciding with the onset of the well-being decline among young populations -were the first generation to have spent their entire teenage years with social media and smartphones. Previous studies have linked social media use to lower well-being, particularly when it involves social comparison (e.g., Marciano et al., 2024), and increased social comparison may plausibly reduce perceived financial well-being -a factor consistently associated with higher life satisfaction (e.g., Ngamaba et al., 2020; She et al., 2021). We could not examine social media use directly, as the GWP does not include questions on social media use or social comparison. Future studies incorporating these variables could help clarify additional mechanisms underpinning the observed decline.

Moreover, our findings should be interpreted considering the analytical procedures employed. Specifically, we did some analyses by dividing the data into subgroups, which affected the effective sample size. However, analyses treating age as continuous, thus increasing statistical power, yielded similar results. Additionally, the use of probability-based sampling and the inclusion of weights enhance the robustness and representativeness of our results. Another limitation of our study is that several of the items used as predictors were relatively narrow in scope, and the binary response scales restricted our ability to detect nuanced variability. Finally, our assessment of well-being relied on a single life evaluation item, the Cantril Ladder. Scholars have argued that focusing solely on life evaluations provides an incomplete picture of well-being and advocate for a more comprehensive, multidimensional approach (Marsh et al., 2020; Martela & Ryan, 2023; OECD, 2013). Thus, future research should incorporate multidimensional indicators of well-being to gain a more comprehensive understanding of how well-being among young individuals has changed over time.

Conclusion

In 2017, Norway was ranked as the happiest country in the world. Since then, average life evaluations have declined, and by 2026 Norway ranked lowest among the Nordic countries. Our analyses suggest that the decline is concentrated among adults under 60 and is steepest among young adults, while older adults continue to report relatively high scores. The current Norwegian pattern, in which older adults report higher life evaluations than younger adults and life evaluation is almost linearly positively associated with age, also appears in a few other countries, most notably Sweden, Canada, the United States and China. Finally, age differences in economic satisfaction, emotional health, and institutional trust emerge as potential explanatory factors in Norway. We encourage future research to devote greater attention to understanding both the decrease in young adults' life evaluations and the sustained high life evaluation in older adults, in Norway as well as in other countries where the young's life evaluations appear to be declining.

Factor analysis

Here, we present some more information on the factor analysis regarding missingness, score construction and stability.

Missing items and score construction.

Because not all items were administered every year, the principal component analysis used pairwise correlations rather than listwise deletion. One item (likelihood of moving) was not asked in 2015-2017 and was excluded from that period's solution. Component scores were the mean of the salient items available for each respondent (standardised on the pooled mean and SD).

Stability of the Affect Balance score.

Congruence on the Affect Balance items was fair (φ = .89) rather than equivalent, because smiling and enjoyment loaded more strongly in 2023-2025 than in 2015-2017. Because component scores are unweighted means of a fixed set of items, we tested whether weighting the items by each period's loadings would change them. Unit-weighted and loading-weighted Affect Balance scores correlated r = .96 in 2015-2017 and r = .997 in 2023-2025, indicating that the Affect Balance score is comparable across periods (Table S3). Difference-in-difference-based approach

Method

We also tested a Difference-in-difference (DiD) approach where we predicted life evaluation from age group, a post-2017 indicator and their interaction (the DiD term) plus 46 mediators and their interaction with the DiD term. This was seen as the most appropriate model analysis to understand the increasing life evaluation-age gap, as the analysis determines whether a change in difference in one candidate variable follows the change in life evaluation between age groups. We first excluded any mediator with more than 25 % missing data, reducing the number of candidate variables to 46, then limited the sample to participants under 30 ("young") or 60 and over ("old") to get the strongest difference. Life evaluation scores were standardized and combined with a post-2017 indicator and their product formed the DiD term. Remaining mediators were standardised, and a model with age, period, mediators, and mediator×DID interactions as predictors was constructed. A survey-weighted, 10-fold cross-validated LASSO regression then identified which mediator×DID terms retained nonzero coefficients at the optimal penalty -those variables whose link to well-being shifted most between young and old after 2017. As the interaction terms were the ones of interest, we only penalised the interactions, to avoid main effects pushing the interaction terms' effects to 0. To make the analysis more robust, we did three models where we shifted the post-2017 indicator to be post-2016 in one model and post-2015 in another model, and then averaging the beta coefficients.

As the DiD approach was relatively complex and did not provide clear results, we focus more on the three other analyses that were simpler and may help in understanding the change in the life evaluation-age association.

Results

The Affect Balance factor variable had a negative beta, indicating that its relationship with life evaluation decreased more for young than older Norwegians. Looking at the correlations to life evaluation for Norwegian young adults, confidence in elections was related at r = .26 in 2015-2017 but increased to r = .40 in 2023-2025. For older Norwegians, the relationship increased from r = .41 in 2015-2017 to r = .43 in 2022-2024. Thus, the Affect Balance factor became more important for Norwegians below 30 compared to older Norwegian people and as the younger population decreased in absolute scores, the variable may explain a small portion of the emerging age-life evaluation gap.

In a developed country like Norway, the landline phone access effect might reflect the severe drop in landline phones among older people. In 2015-2017, 60% of Norwegians above 60 had landline phones, which dropped to 6% in 2023-2025. For Norwegians below 30, the drop was from 16% in 2015-2017 to 9% in 2023-2025. Thus, the change in difference between old and young in landline phone access showed a dramatic change over the same years as the difference in life evaluation between young and old expanded. The correlation between landline phone access and life evaluation decreased slightly for the young, r = .16 in 2015-2017 and r = .02 in 2023-2025. For elders, the correlation decreased from r = .03 in 2015-2017 to -.18 in 2023-2025, reflecting that landline phone access has become a negative marker for elders, among the few people who have it. In sum, the DID-based model may reflect that what truly explains the increased life evaluation gap between young and old in Norway is unclear in the GWP data. The few effects were small, and access to landline phones may just reflect a technological development, and may act as a benchmark and face validity of the analysis.
