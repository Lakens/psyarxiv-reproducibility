Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Understanding speech amid other sounds is challenging because background sounds can mask target speech and draw on attentional and memory resources.

Yet people frequently encounter and seek out background music in everyday settings, from cafes and classrooms to offices. Prior work suggests that background music may engage reward systems and enhance concurrent learning, but most of that evidence comes from performance on visual tasks where acoustic masking is not a concern. Here, we asked whether background music affects comprehension of spoken stories, and which musical characteristics matter.

Participants listened to short stories in silence and with five background conditions: intact, reversed, pitch-scrambled, rhythm-jittered, and combined pitch-scrambled and rhythm-jittered music. Performance was best in silence, but also better with intact music than with the combined pitch-and rhythm-disrupted music. Performance in music disrupted in only one dimension (pitch or rhythm) did not differ reliably from intact music. These findings suggest that music may be less disruptive than other background sounds when at least one dimension of predictability, pitch or rhythm, remains intact. Liking ratings and individual sensitivity to musical reward did not reliably predict outcomes, suggesting that structural predictability, rather than subjective preference, influences how background music affects speech comprehension.

Introduction

Understanding speech in noisy environments is difficult, and the presence of background sounds can impair performance on concurrent tasks such as writing, reading, or listening (Rabbitt, 1968; Braat-Eggen et al., 2019; Klatte et al., 2013).

Despite this, people often choose to engage in activities with at least some types of background noise, especially background music. In fact, in some situations, background noise or music appears to actually be beneficial, not detrimental, for task performance (e.g. Dosseville et al., 2012; Othman et al., 2019; Cardona et al., 2020). In the work presented here, we address this apparent contradiction by investigating how the interfering and rewarding aspects of background music impact the comprehension and memory of concurrently presented auditory stories.

Speech Perception in Noise

Background noise is ubiquitous in everyday life, from busy streets and loud bars to noisy classrooms, and it can have far-reaching effects on both perception and learning (e.g. Miller, 1974; Elliott, 1979; Kryter, 1984; Evans et al., 2001; Erickson & Newman, 2017). Noise can impair speech recognition itself, by disrupting acoustic-phonetic mapping to lexical items (failure of recognition), or by competing and overlapping with the target acoustically or linguistically (perceptual interference) or diverting attentional resources (reduced attentional capacity). However, it can also impact the ability to segregate a target signal from other sounds or to attend selectively. Even when speech identification is near-perfect, background noise can reduce memory for the information conveyed by limiting the working memory resources needed to integrate and recall speech content (Rabbitt 1968; Pichora-Fuller et al., 1995; Murphy et al., 2000; Ljung et al., 2009; see Mattys et al., 2012 for a review). Therefore, the challenge of listening in noise extends beyond identification alone, disrupting the encoding, retention, and recall of speech and placing additional demands on working memory and attention.

These disruptive effects are commonly described in terms of two primary forms of masking: energetic masking and informational masking. Energetic masking refers to a low-level perceptual phenomenon in which energy from noise (the masker) spectrally and temporally overlaps with the target, reducing its audibility (Brungart, 2001).

Informational masking occurs when the target's energy is detectable, but listeners fail to segregate it from the masker or to focus attention appropriately, often due to the masker's similarity or salience (Cooke et al., 2008). Intelligibility, then, depends on the degree of spectral overlap between speech and noise (Brungart et al., 2005).

Not all Noise is Equal

While noise is generally considered detrimental to speech processing, its impact depends strongly on the acoustic characteristics of the masker and the demands it places on attention. Under energetic masking, speech is easier to detect and segregate when the masker has a fluctuating amplitude envelope because the listener can take advantage of brief "glimpses" of the target during low-amplitude periods (Festen & Plomp, 1990; Cooke, 2006). In contrast, steady-state noise offers few or no such glimpses, requiring listeners to rely on other, often less robust, cues such as spectral contrast or harmonicity for separation.

Beyond audibility, maskers differ in how they engage attention and arousal.

Steady-state noise produces continuous masking but is relatively predictable and therefore less attentionally disruptive; at low levels, it may even enhance performance by masking intermittent distractors (Erickson & Newman, 2017). Fluctuating maskers such as music, although providing acoustic glimpses, may impose greater cognitive costs by capturing attention through unpredictability or sudden changes. This tension between predictability and disruption is also documented in the Irrelevant Sound Effect (ISE) literature, which shows that background sound disrupts memory more when it is acoustically "changing-state", i.e. composed of distinct, varying elements, than when it is "steady-state," or unchanging (Jones & Macken, 1993; Hughes et al., 2007; see Hughes, 2014, for a review). The degree of disruption scales with the number of distinct elements in the irrelevant sequence, or its "token-set size" (Tremblay & Jones, 1998), largely independent of the sound's meaning or valence. Consistent with this, background music in advertising has shown that more frequent harmonic and textural changes increase distraction and reduce recall of concurrent verbal content (Fraser & Bradford, 2013).

In contrast, some forms of background noise have been shown to enhance performance on certain cognitive tasks, including vocabulary learning (Ross, 2021), novel word acquisition (Angwin et al., 2019), studying (Braat-Eggen et al., 2021), and reading (Halin, 2016). These benefits are often attributed to arousal-based mechanisms (Szalma & Hancock, 2011; Reinten et al., 2017), consistent with the Yerkes-Dodson law (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908), which proposes an inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance. In this framework, mild, constant noise may elevate arousal to an optimal level, whereas highly variable or intense noise may exceed it, particularly for demanding tasks, which raises the possibility that attention and arousal accounts converge on similar predictions through different mechanisms. Indeed, Cloutier et al. (2020) found that background music's effects on attentional control depended on the specific acoustic and arousal qualities of the music rather than producing a uniform benefit or cost, suggesting that attention and arousal accounts may not be fully separable in practice.

Learning with Background Music

A particularly common form of self-selected background noise is music. Many people use music to regulate mood, manage stress, or evoke specific emotions (Juslin & Västfjäll, 2008; Cowen et al., 2020; Ferreri et al., 2021; Sloboda & Juslin, 2001), and it is frequently present as background sound in both work and study settings.

Empirical findings on the effects of background music are mixed, and a metaanalysis by Kämpfe et al. (2011) found that overall effects of background music on cognitive performance were small and inconsistent across studies, varying by task type and musical characteristics. Some studies report benefits of background music for second language acquisition (Kang & Williamson, 2014), story recall (Fassbender et al., 2012), trigonometry problem-solving (Taylor & Rowe, 2012), learning from a college lecture (Dosseville et al., 2012) and essay writing (Batur, 2016). These improvements are often attributed to music-induced positive affect and mood-arousal mechanisms.

Music has also been shown to support memory by acting as a contextual cue: matching background music between study and test improves recall (Smith, 1985; Balch et al., 1992), the emotional relationship between music and the material can influence memory (Tesoriero & Rickard, 2012; Panteleeva et al., 2021), and pairing verbal material with a repeated melody can aid its recall (Wallace, 1994). Neuroimaging evidence further suggests that music can reduce cognitive demands during encoding, as reflected in reduced prefrontal activation compared to silence (Ferreri et al., 2015; Ferreri & Rodriguez-Fornells, 2022).

In contrast, other studies report no difference between music and silence for comprehension or recall (Küssner et al., 2016; Nguyen & Grahn, 2017; Jäncke et al., 2014), and some find detrimental effects, particularly for tasks with high verbal working memory demands, such as geometric figure recall (Echaide et al., 2019), reading comprehension (Kasiri, 2015; Chou, 2010), and foreign-word learning (de Groot & Smedinga, 2014). Similarly, in digit recall tasks, both lyrical and instrumental music impair performance, with lyrical music producing greater disruption (Salamé & Baddeley, 1989).

Such inconsistencies may stem from multiple musical features, including the presence of lyrics, tempo, genre, key, and overall complexity, which can differentially influence task performance (see de la mora Velasco et al., 2023, for a review). Studies using rich excerpts from composers like Bach, Beethoven, or Sidney Bechet often report positive effects (de Groot, 2006; Judde & Rickard, 2010; Ferreri et al., 2013; 2015), whereas experiments employing controlled, "mechanical" stimuli, matched for tempo, mode, and other variables, tend to yield either mixed (Kang & Williamson, 2014), null, or negative effects (Jäncke & Sandmann, 2010; Verga et al., 2015). Tillmann et al. (2013) demonstrated that recognition memory was superior for expressive versus MIDI-rendered pieces, suggesting that ecological musical stimuli engage listeners more deeply. It is possible that complex music may impair performance by dividing attention or masking target signals in listening tasks while also enhancing memory through mood, arousal, and associative memory mechanisms (Ferreri & Verga, 2016).

How is music rewarding?

In addition to enhancing memory indirectly by improving motivation, emotional state, or by serving as a retrieval cue (Juslin & Västfjäll, 2008; Janata, 2009), music can also directly enhance memory through reward-related mechanisms (Blood & Zatorre, 2001; Menon & Levitin, 2005; Salimpoor et al., 2011). Music listening also alters functional connectivity among auditory, motor, and reward networks relative to rest (Han et al., 2026), consistent with an interaction between auditory and reward systems during listening. For example, Abrams et al. (2025) showed that listening to highly rewarding, self-selected music enhanced memory for unrelated images compared to elevator music, a validated low-reward control (Abrams et al., 2024), particularly for individuals more sensitive to musical reward (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013). Relatedly, subjectively pleasantrated music has been shown to enhance verbal recall (Cardona et al., 2020).

Musical reward is not limited to self-selected or highly pleasant music. Instead, it emerges from how the brain forms and updates predictions over time. Music is most engaging when it balances predictability and surprise: overly predictable music is perceived as boring, whereas highly unpredictable music can feel chaotic (Gold et al., 2019a; Gold et al., 2019b; Ferreri & Verga, 2016; Zatorre & Salimpoor, 2013; Shany et al., 2019).

Current Study

The current study investigates whether the rewarding properties of background music influence speech comprehension. This complements existing work finding beneficial effects of background music for non-auditory tasks (de Groot, 2006; Cardona et al., 2020; Abrams et al., 2025). Much less is known about how music interacts with auditory learning, where both the target material and the background compete within the same sensory domain. From a speech-in-noise perspective, background sounds can impair comprehension by drawing attentional resources and by producing energetic or informational masking. Yet, if background music engages the reward system, it may support learning, as measured through story comprehension, even in auditory contexts.

Therefore, a key question is whether the structural properties of background music, specifically its pitch and rhythmic predictability, shape its impact on comprehension of concurrent speech.

In this study, we investigated whether music's rewarding properties, understood as arising from listeners' ability to form and confirm predictions about musical structure, can support learning for concurrent verbal material presented in an auditory setting. Participants listened to short stories presented in silence or under five background conditions: intact music, reversed music, pitch-scrambled music, rhythmjittered music, and both pitch-scrambled and rhythm-jittered music. Reversing the music preserves its harmonic and rhythmic content while disrupting the forward temporal progression of melodic and phrase structure. Scrambling pitch and jittering rhythm, by contrast, directly and independently disrupt predictability along each of these two structural dimensions, alone or in combination. Because reward models of music emphasize prediction and surprise, we tested whether comprehension benefits are stronger when participants can track pitch and temporal information, and whether disrupting either or both dimensions eliminates any advantage. After each story, participants answered True-or-False comprehension questions, and we additionally assessed participants' sensitivity to musical reward and how much they liked each type of background sound, in line with evidence that musical reward and subjective liking can independently influence memory and learning (Cardona et al., 2020).

Given that background noise can interfere with speech perception, but music, unlike other types of background noise, can activate reward mechanisms that may offset this cost, we established two hypotheses: 1.

Sound hypothesis: If low-level acoustic masking drives performance, then all background sound conditions should reduce speech comprehension relative to silence, regardless of the specific structural manipulation, because all sounds will produce acoustic masking effects on speech. Because the different background music conditions (rhythm jittered, pitch scrambled, combined rhythm jittered & pitch scrambled, and reversed music) were all derived from the original, unscrambled background music, they maintained the same instrumentation and similar overall energy content.

2. Affective hypothesis: If the rewarding and predictive aspects of music offset these detrimental effects, comprehension should instead vary with structural predictability rather than acoustic variability, although these two constructs happen in parallel. Intact music, which retains its full pitch and rhythmic structure and its original temporal direction, should best support comprehension. Reversed music preserves the same inventory of pitches, durations, and instrumentation as the original, but playing the excerpt backward alters the local sequential patterns embedded within it. This results in reversed music sounding less natural, as the attack-and-decay shape of each note's timbre is inverted. Reversed music should therefore be somewhat less predictable than intact music. Pitch-scrambled and rhythm-jittered, which disrupt one structural dimension each, should each show reduced comprehension relative to intact music, and when both manipulations are combined, performance should show the greatest impairment. Method

Participants

A total of 112 participants recruited through the University of Maryland Psychology subject pool participated in this study in exchange for course credit. 1 person was excluded for missing data or technical issues; 16 were excluded for selfreporting non-English exposure in infancy; 2 were excluded for reporting hearing impairment, and 4 were excluded for being professional musicians. Participants were not screened for musical anhedonia prior to inclusion. Of the remaining 89 participants (mean age = 18.77, 15 men, 74 women), 30 (33.71%) reported simultaneous early exposure to another language from birth, and 32 (35.96%) identified as amateur musicians. All participants completed the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (M = 77.47, SD = 11.2), which is a 20-item measure of how much pleasure individuals derive from musical experiences, with total scores ranging from 20 to 100 (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013). Our participants' mean score of 77.47 falls within the typical range, in which 65 and 87 are the 10th and 90th percentiles respectively. Participants also completed a Short Test of Music Preferences (STOMP-R; Rentfrow et al., 2011), where they rank their preferred genres, to assess their genre preferences, including jazz (given that a jazz piece was used as the music stimulus). We also asked participants about their musical training and exposure, in questions adapted from the Goldsmith Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI; Müllensiefen et al., 2014): how many years of formal music training they have received (including vocal training) as well as the number of live music shows they have attended in the past year. Materials and Design

Stimuli

Six stories were adapted from various folk tales unlikely to be familiar to participants (Rickles, 2023). The stories were normalized to consist of approximately 50 sentences each, with each sentence containing about 10 words (10-13). Each story was recorded by a different female native speaker of American English and varied in length between 160-300 seconds. The stories were recorded using a Shure Model SM81 microphone at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz and 32 bits of precision on Adobe Audition (Version 14; Adobe Systems Incorporated, 2021) in a sound-proof booth. All stories were normalized to the same RMS amplitude. The six stories were each paired with one of six background sounds described below. Each participant heard one of the six stories in a silent condition, in which no background sound played concurrently. The pairing of stories with background sounds was counterbalanced using a 6 × 6 Latin square design, such that each story occurred with each background condition across participants.

Background sounds (excluding silence) were mixed with the stories at a single signalto-noise ratio (-10 dB SNR, with the background sound louder than the target speech).

This SNR was selected based on pilot testing to avoid ceiling effects on comprehension accuracy while keeping the task tractable; more favorable SNRs produced comprehension accuracy too close to ceiling to detect differences across background conditions.

The background music was a MIDI transcription of Nina Simone's (1959) Good Bait, a jazz standard composed by Tadd Dameron and Count Basie with no vocal or lyrical content. This piece was chosen to be of the same genre and to have comparable For the rhythm-jittered condition, we added a random offset between 0 and 1 beat to each note's onset. Onsets were then sorted in ascending order within each instrument to prevent overlapping notes. The note pitches were unchanged, but these shifts disrupted alignment with the beat and across instruments. As a result, notes that originally played together no longer always sounded simultaneously, sometimes creating new vertical pitch combinations, while keeping the overall note content and durations intact. Our goal was to produce a version of the music that would feel out of sync, as if a band were playing slightly off the beat. For the pitch-scrambled condition, we selected all pitches excluding the percussion channel and added random values sampled from a uniform distribution between -3 and +3 semitones. We then rounded the pitch values to valid MIDI semitones and randomly reassigned these new pitch values to note locations. This ensured that local pitch intervals and melodic contours were totally disrupted while preserving the rhythmic structure. At the global level, the pitch distribution was largely preserved: original MIDI pitch values ranged from 22-96 (or A#0 to C7) with a mean of 55.4 (approximately G3) and scrambled pitch values ranged from 20-96 with a mean of 55.62 (approximately G3-G#3). So, while local tonal relationships were disrupted, the overall pitch average and range remained nearly unchanged.

Finally, we combined the rhythm jittered and pitch scrambled manipulations to generate a timing and pitch disrupted version of the original Good Bait song. All of these background sounds and the code used to generate them are available on GitHub: https://github.com/minemuezzinoglu/backgroundmusic.

Energetic masking characterization

While all background conditions were derived from the same musical excerpt to maintain acoustic properties as much as possible, the pitch and rhythm manipulations we applied may have changed the acoustic properties of the background in ways that affect comprehension independently of musical structure. Therefore, we evaluated whether the conditions differed in two aspects: 1) the balance of speech and background energy across frequency, which bears on energetic masking, and 2) the regularity of the background's loudness fluctuations over time, which bears on whether our rhythm manipulations altered temporal structure as intended.

Spectral energy. The overall (broadband) SNR was fixed at the nominal -10 dB for every story-masker pairing by design. However, since some scrambled music conditions may carry more energy than others at specific frequencies, we analyzed the spectral energy of our maskers to rule out any acoustic masking imbalances that may affect speech intelligibility across conditions.

For each of the five background conditions (original music, reverse, pitch-scrambled, rhythm-jittered, and combined pitch-scrambled&rhythm-jittered) crossed with each of the six stories, we computed target-to-masker SNR (20log₁₀[rms(target)/rms(masker)]) within ten narrowband filters with logarithmically spaced center frequencies spanning 50-5000 Hz. The filters were Butterworth bandpass filters (design order 4) applied forward and backward (zero phase). Each filter's bandwidth was set equal to the spacing between adjacent center frequencies (approximately 0.74 octaves), so that the ten bands tiled the analyzed range. All signals were analyzed at 44.1 kHz. For each story-masker pairing, we analyzed the portion of the background that overlapped with the story, which began when the 1.5-second fadein ended and finished when the story ended. The background was therefore at full level throughout the analyzed window. Maskers were rescaled to enforce the nominal -10 dB broadband SNR prior to band-limited analysis.

Band SNR varied widely across frequency (from approximately -40 dB at the lowest bands to +1 dB at 5000 Hz), reflecting the different spectral shapes of speech and music. Across conditions, band SNR depended mainly on whether the music's pitches had been manipulated. The three maskers with unaltered pitches (original, reversed, and rhythm-jittered) produced nearly identical band SNRs, within 0.3 dB of one another in every band. The two pitch-manipulated maskers (pitch-scrambled and combined) were likewise within 0.1 dB of each other. The pitch-manipulated and unaltered-pitch maskers differed by no more than 1.5 dB in all bands except 50, 139, and 232 Hz. In the 139 and 232 Hz bands, the pitch-manipulated maskers had approximately 4.5 and 5.0 dB more energy relative to the story, respectively, whereas in the 50 Hz band they had approximately 3.9 dB less, suggesting that the pitch manipulation shifted low-frequency masker energy upward. The reduced masking by the pitch-manipulated maskers in the 50 Hz band is unlikely to matter for comprehension, since speech carries little energy at this frequency. The 139-232 Hz range overlaps the approximate fundamental frequency range of the female speakers' voices (Oliveira et al., 2019) rather than the higher frequencies most important for speech intelligibility (French & Steinberg, 1947), so the additional masker energy there is less likely to obscure phonetic cues, although it could make the voices harder to separate from the background.

Temporal Regularity and Modulation Masking.

Music with a regular beat rises and falls in level at the beat rate, whereas irregular note timing should spread these fluctuations across a range of rates. To verify that the rhythm manipulation reduced beat regularity, we computed each masker's modulation spectrum using the same storymatched segments as the spectral analysis. We extracted the amplitude envelope (Hilbert transform, low-pass filtered at 50 Hz), normalized it by its mean, and took its Fourier transform, which indicates how strongly the envelope fluctuates at each rate.

The original music showed a prominent peak near 2 Hz, corresponding to its beat rate.

As an index of beat regularity, we took the height of the largest peak between 1.5 and 2.5 Hz, averaged across stories. A regular beat concentrates fluctuations at a single rate and produces a tall, narrow peak; irregular timing distributes them across neighboring rates and lowers the peak.

The original music and the reverse music had nearly identical beat strength (0.266 vs. 0.266; reversed was 100.2% of the original). We expected this because playing a song backward does not change how regularly its loudness rises and falls, only the direction in which it plays. The original music and pitch-scrambled condition were also comparable in beat strength (0.266 vs 0.281; 105.5% of the original), which is also expected, since pitch scrambling changes which notes play rather than when they play. In contrast, beat strength in the rhythm-jittered version dropped to about a quarter of the original (0.073; 27.6%), and in the combined version to about a fifth (0.056; 21.0%). These patterns indicate that the manipulations worked as intended: the versions designed to preserve the beat (reversed, pitch-scrambled) did so, while the versions designed to disrupt it (rhythm-jittered, combined) showed substantially reduced beat strength.

Because speech intelligibility depends on slow fluctuations in the speech envelope, a masker with strong envelope modulation at similar rates could impair comprehension even when spectral energy is matched. Therefore, we computed targetto-masker modulation SNR in five octave-spaced modulation bands (centered at 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 Hz) for each story-masker pairing, using the same mean-normalized envelopes. In all bands and conditions, the speech envelope fluctuated more than the masker envelope (modulation SNRs of approximately 2-9 dB). Relative to the original music, the rhythm-jittered and combined maskers carried more modulation energy at 1 Hz (approximately 5.6 dB lower SNR) and 4 Hz (approximately 1.3-1.8 dB lower), and less at 2 and 8 Hz, consistent with jittering redistributing envelope energy away from the beat rate.

Procedure

The experiment was created using PsychoPy2 Experiment Builder (version 1.85.1; Peirce et al., 2019). Participants completed the experiment in person using a desktop Windows computer and listened to the stimuli diotically using circumaural Cowin E7 headphones.

Participants first completed a demographic survey using Qualtrics. Following the survey, participants listened to a sample audio file through the headphones and were instructed to adjust the volume to their comfort level and to keep it at that same level throughout the experiment. Each story started with the speaker's introductory sentence, "Now you'll listen to a short story," after which the background sound began to play concurrently with the recorded story. Participants listened to each of the six stories in a different background condition (intact music, reversed music, pitch-scrambled music, rhythm-jittered music, combined pitch-scrambled and rhythm-jittered music, or silence).

After each story, participants answered a set of 20 to 22 True-or-False questions to assess their comprehension.

Next, participants completed the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ; Mas-Herrero et al., 2013) to assess their degree of musical hedonia, as well as the Short Test of Music Preferences (STOMP-R; Rentfrow et al., 2011) and answered a few additional questions regarding their familiarity with the jazz piece they listened to, how often they listen to jazz music, and how much they enjoy listening to jazz music.

Additionally, participants listened to 15-second clips of each of the five background sound files (music, reversed music, pitch-scrambled music, rhythm-jittered music, and combined pitch-scrambled/rhythm-jittered music) used in the study and rated each sound from 1 (low) to 5 (high) in terms of how much they liked it. shows the different conditions, and the y axis shows the average rating participants have given for each type of background noise they heard in the experiment in terms of liking (1 = don't like it at all,5 = like it a lot).

Discussion

Background sound generally impairs speech comprehension and learning by masking the target and competing for attentional and memory resources (e.g., Mattys et al., 2012), yet music has sometimes been found to benefit learning and memory (e.g., Fassbender et al., 2012; Cardona et al., 2020). We tested two accounts of how background sound affects comprehension. The sound hypothesis predicts that comprehension should scale with the amount of low-level acoustic masking a background sound produces. The affective hypothesis predicts that comprehension should scale with the reward a background sound generates, which depends on how well listeners can predict its structure. In our study, participants' story comprehension was best in silence, perhaps unsurprisingly, since any background sound produces acoustic masking. Hence, our results provide partial support for the sound hypothesis.

Among the background conditions, music that kept predictable structure in at least one dimension (original, reversed, pitch-scrambled, and rhythm-jittered music) was less disruptive than music that kept little (the combined rhythm-jittered & pitch-scrambled condition). We first consider how well each account explains this pattern, then turn to an attentional account that makes similar predictions.

A stronger test of the sound hypothesis comes from comparing the background conditions acoustically with one another. If background sound impaired comprehension through low-level masking alone, comprehension should track how much each background overlapped acoustically with the target speech. Broadband SNR was equated across conditions by design, but the frequency-resolved analysis revealed one exception. Relative to the original, reversed, and rhythm-jittered music, the pitch-scrambled and combined conditions carried approximately 4.5-5 dB more energy relative to the target in two low-frequency bands (139 and 232 Hz) and approximately 4 dB less in the lowest band (50 Hz). The two pitch-manipulated conditions were spectrally near-identical to each other, and all conditions were within 1.5 dB of one another in the remaining bands. Because the 139 and 232 Hz bands overlap the fundamental frequency range of the female speakers, the two pitch-manipulated conditions may have produced somewhat more energetic masking than the other music conditions. For the rhythm manipulation, the modulation-spectrum analysis indicated that the rhythm-jittered and combined conditions had a substantially weaker regular beat than the original music. Thus, apart from the low-frequency difference introduced by pitch reassignment, the manipulations altered musical structure as intended while leaving the acoustics otherwise comparable.

Importantly, the behavioral results do not follow this masking pattern. Story comprehension, as measured through responses to True/False questions, in the pitchscrambled condition did not differ reliably from the original music, despite its greater low-frequency energy. However, the combined condition produced a reliable decrement despite being spectrally matched to the pitch-scrambled condition. Therefore, spectral masking by itself cannot account for the comprehension cost observed in the combined condition.

Turning to the affective hypothesis, predictive accounts of musical pleasure propose that listeners continuously generate expectations about upcoming events, and that reward arises as those expectations are confirmed or violated (Salimpoor et al., 2015; Hansen et al., 2017; Gold et al., 2019b). Consistent with this, striatal dopamine release occurs both in anticipation of and at the peak of pleasurable passages (Salimpoor et al., 2011), and musical reward prediction errors engage the nucleus accumbens (Gold et al., 2019a). If background music generates reward in this way, that reward could support learning from concurrent material (Cardona et al., 2020), and disrupting the structure listeners use to form predictions should make background music more disruptive.

In our background sound manipulations, the pitch-scrambled and rhythm-jittered conditions each disrupted one structural dimension, either pitch assignment or noteonset timing, whereas the combined condition disrupted both. Music that kept predictable structure in at least one dimension was less disruptive than music that kept little. Disrupting a single dimension had no reliable cost compared to the original music, whereas disrupting both did. Comprehension therefore did not decline gradually as disruption increased. Statistical learning accounts propose that listeners track regularities at multiple levels, from local transitional probabilities to the overall uncertainty of a sequence (Daikoku, 2018). On this view, listeners could still track the dimension left intact in each single-disruption condition (timing in the pitch-scrambled condition, pitch transitions in the rhythm-jittered condition), whereas the combined condition may leave little predictable structure to track.

However, two of our findings fit less well with a reward-based explanation.

First, pitch-scrambled and rhythm-jittered music were rated as less pleasant than the original, yet they did not reduce comprehension, and liking did not correlate with performance in any condition. Second, individual differences in music reward sensitivity (BMRQ) did not modulate the effects. If the relative advantage of structured music depended on reward, some relation to state or trait measures of musical pleasure would be expected. It is possible that reward can operate without explicit liking, since individuals high in musical hedonia may derive reward value from music they do not report enjoying (Kathios et al., 2023; Mas-Herrero et al., 2025). Alternatively, predictability may influence comprehension through a route that does not depend on reward. Although predictability is necessary for musical reward, pleasure does not increase steadily with it; instead, it appears to depend on the balance between uncertainty and surprise (Cheung et al., 2019; Gold et al., 2019b). If comprehension depended on reward, it should have followed pleasure. Yet the single-disruption conditions reduced pleasure ratings without reducing comprehension, suggesting that manipulating the predictability of the background sounds might be resulting in reduced learning due to other mechanisms, such as attention.

Research on the irrelevant sound effect, in which task-irrelevant sound impairs performance on a focal task, has identified two mechanisms that can operate together (Hughes, 2014). One is changing-state interference, which depends on acoustic change, suggesting that successive sounds that differ from one another are more disruptive than a single repeated sound (Jones et al., 1992; Jones & Macken, 1993). The other is attentional capture, which depends on predictability, suggesting that a sound that violates an expectation established by the preceding sequence diverts attention from the task (Hughes et al., 2005 (Hughes et al., , 2007)). Because all background conditions involved continuous acoustic change, changing-state interference may contribute to the general impairment relative to silence, but it does not explain why music that kept little predictable structure was more disruptive than music that kept some. Attentional capture may account for that difference. Perhaps, music with little predictable structure generates many expectation violations, each of which could draw attention away from the target speech. Relatedly, regularities within a sound stream can help support its segregation from competing sources (Winkler et al., 2009; Andreou et al., 2011; Bendixen, 2014), and temporally regular sequences allow attention to be allocated in anticipation of expected events (Large & Jones, 1999). Intact structure in either dimension may therefore have allowed listeners to segregate the background music from the target speech and suppress it, whereas the combined condition may have made this more challenging.

Because reward and attentional demands both vary with predictability, the two accounts make the same ordinal prediction for our conditions, which is that the less predictable the background sound, the poorer comprehension of target speech.

Unfortunately, our measures cannot separate the two accounts. The null effects of liking and BMRQ are somewhat more easily accommodated by the attentional account, which does not require the background to be pleasurable. However, because our sample's BMRQ scores (M = 77.47, SD = 11.2) clustered near the normative mean, with few participants at either extreme, it is not possible to say which account may have contributed more heavily to our findings. Therefore, we treat the two accounts as candidate explanations that may operate together.

We note that our scrambling manipulations were coarse, and we did not quantify predictability directly. Our spectral analyses confirmed that the rhythm manipulations reduced temporal regularity, but changes in pitch predictability were inferred from how the stimuli were constructed rather than measured. Symbolic models of melodic expectation such as IDyOM (Pearce, 2018) estimate note-by-note surprisal, but they model one melodic line at a time and are not straightforward to apply to polyphonic music such as ours without reducing it to a single line. Future work using more graded disruptions, together with direct estimates of surprisal, could determine whether the threshold pattern reflects a genuine nonlinearity or the small number of levels tested. In addition, our stimuli were drawn from a single instrumental jazz piece that received only moderate liking ratings (M = 3.16/5), so it remains unclear whether the effects generalize to other genres. We used non-lyrical music because lyrics would introduce linguistic content that competes more directly with the target speech, and may create semantic interference (Salamé & Baddeley, 1989).

Comprehension was also assessed immediately after each story instead of exploring long-term effects of background music on learning from speech. Previous studies suggest that music-related reward may particularly benefit delayed memory consolidation (Ferreri et al., 2013; Kang & Williamson, 2014; Cardona et al., 2020). For example, Cardona et al. (2020) found that pleasant music during encoding enhanced 24hour recall, especially among individuals high in musical hedonia. Testing longer retention intervals would clarify whether reward-related effects emerge after a delay.

Finally, liking was rated once per condition, after the experiment, rather than after each story. Musical pleasure can fluctuate with repeated exposure and context (Cardona et al., 2020; Curzel et al., 2024). Trial-level variation in pleasure or engagement may therefore have contributed to the comprehension results in ways a single retrospective rating could not capture.

To sum up, all background sounds impaired comprehension relative to silence, consistent with the view that background sound imposes energetic, informational, or attentional costs on speech comprehension (Mattys et al., 2012), even when that sound is potentially rewarding. However, among the background conditions, music that kept predictable structure in at least one dimension was less disruptive than music that kept little. This difference did not follow the acoustic differences we measured, suggesting that it reflects the predictability and potentially reward associated with the background music rather than its spectral or temporal properties.

Conclusion

In the current study, comprehension of spoken stories was highest without background sound. Among the background conditions, music that retained predictable structure in at least one dimension, whether the original, reversed, pitch-scrambled, or rhythm-jittered music, was less disruptive than music with both pitch and rhythm disrupted. These results suggest that the impact of background music on speech comprehension depends not only on masking but also on whether the music retains predictable structure.

Whether this reflects music-related reward, reduced attentional demands of a predictable background, or both remains to be tested. More broadly, structured background music may be less costly for speech comprehension than its acoustic complexity alone would predict.
