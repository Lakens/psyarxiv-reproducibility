Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

This paper introduces the concept of multiple, parallel specifications of hypothesis-testing flexibilities. Although its ideas are not entirely novel, the conception as a whole has not yet been fully explicated. It allows researchers other than the study conductors to test a given hypothesis according to their own specifications of how to formulate the hypothesis, collect data, and analyze them. In practice, Programmatic Registered Reports, which were originally designed for another purpose, already provide an implementation framework requiring only minor additions. Incorporating a priori specifications by others might necessitate small, feasible extensions to the study design, for example, collecting further outcome measures.

Conceptually, allowing different specifications in hypothesis testing accommodates scientific pluralism while extending the epistemic benefits of preregistration to researchers beyond the study conductors. Further flexibilities in testing involve data processing and the decision rule for determining whether a hypothesis is corroborated. Existing approaches, including secondary data analysis, multiverse analysis, and sensitivity analysis, do not address the flexibilities that occur before data processing. They also have epistemic drawbacks because they rely on post hoc assessments. The paper discusses the value and scope of the resulting multiple test results, which are conditional on the specifications and their underlying substantive assumptions. It concludes with a brief discussion of flexibilities in exploratory research.

Introduction

Flexibilities, also known as 'researchers' degrees of freedom', encompass the entire research process, from a hypothesis's formulation to deciding which results would corroborate the hypothesis and which would not. Flexibilities are both desired and undesired: desired because they offer adaptability in responding to various demands and uncertainties across different disciplines and particular fields of science; undesired because they can easily give rise to a vast number of results, thereby opening the door to fishing for the desired one (Gelman & Loken, 2013; Hendriks, 2026; Tyner et al., 2026).

After reviewing current approaches to disclosing flexibilities in confirmatory research and their limitations, I will introduce the concept of multiple, parallel specifications of flexibilities before data collection. The concept invites researchers other than those conducting the study to test the hypothesis under different specifications that reflect different underlying substantive assumptions. A toy example illustrates what this could look like and what can be inferred from the resulting parallel, potentially different, test results. I outline how the concept can be implemented within the existing framework of Programmatic Registered Reports (PRRs). The paper ends by showing that these discussions naturally yield implications for the role of exploratory research in this context.

Conceptual Framework Current Approaches to Flexibilities for Rigorous Hypothesis Testing

Disclosing Flexibilities

In practice, it is often unclear how several flexibilities in research procedures have been specified in arriving at a published claim that a hypothesis was supported (Scheel, 2022).

This introduces uncertainty about the claim's falsifiability. Such weak spots may conceal questionable research practices and may be identified (Höfler et al., 2025). The most rigorous way to prevent fishing expeditions when testing a given hypothesis is to disclose the degrees of freedom before the data are collected, or at least before researchers gain access to the data. This has well-known benefits: 1.

Decisions on the flexibilities are not influenced by what is found in the data (Lakens et al., 2024).

2. Researchers are held accountable for their predictions (Mayo, 2018) and can later take credit for them (Wagenmakers & Dutilh, 2016).

3. If a prediction turns out to be correct, researchers can point out how it could have turned out otherwise; that is, readers gain an idea of the hypothesis' falsifiability (Höfler et al., 2025; Lakens et al., 2024; Mayo, 2018).

To ensure that these benefits hold, one needs to verify that flexibilities have actually been disclosed. Preregistration documents the plan for disclosing the flexibilities, and registered reports promote the actual checking of preregistrations by requesting reviewers to do so (Lakens et al., 2024). According to Lakens and DeBruine (2021), rigorous hypothesis testing is guaranteed if all flexibilities in the entire research process are disclosed so that the result is determined after data collection is complete. 'Result' means that the hypothesis is either corroborated or not. A binary test result has been argued to be warranted because the decisions informed by statistical tests (e.g., on theory and intervention) are binary in nature (Lakens et al., 2018; Höfler et al., to appear). However, reporting a third possibility, 'mixed results' or 'partial evidence' (for example, one test variant yields p < α, but another p ≥ α), in a seemingly transparent way may still be accompanied by a lack of rigor. This happens if a binary decision actually has to be made outside the paper (Höfler, 2026). For instance, applying for a grant to fund a research project investigating the mechanisms of an effect instead of refraining from doing so entails a binary decision, in favor of the effect's existence.

Domains of Flexibilities in the Research Process

Full disclosure of flexibilities is accomplished if they are completely specified in computer code, such that, once the code is run, it returns one result or the other. This conceptual principle defines the ideal of rigor in the disclosure of flexibilities and is called 'machinereadability.' In the implementation of Lakens and DeBruine (2021), machine readability involves the research domains of hypothesis formulation, processing the raw data, analyzing the resulting variables, and deciding on whether the hypothesis is corroborated. Thus, after data collection is complete according to a predefined stopping rule (e.g. the desired sample size is achieved), the hypothesis as formulated in the preregistration is either corroborated or not. Here, I also consider the domain of data collection because leaving some flexibilities open in this domain may allow others to test meaningful variants of the hypothesis. Disclosing Nonessential Flexibilities from the Outset

Some flexibilities in the research process may be argued to be irrelevant because they appear likely to have no effect on the result, such as the exact wording of a stimulus instruction.

However, empirical studies show that seemingly arbitrary choices can alter test results, especially when combined in attempts to fish for the desired outcome (Simmons et al., 2011; Steegen et al., 2016). A more defensible argument for considering flexibilities nonessential is that some specifications might be clearly better justified on conceptual grounds than others.

For instance, it has been argued that choosing a robust analytic model from the outset often comes at a minor cost. The common but epistemically less advisable approach is to start with a conventional analysis and then obtain, by running a more resistant test, different results that must be integrated into a binary decision (Höfler et al., to appear).

Another conception to eliminating nonessential flexibilities is 'adversarial collaboration,' which calls on disputants 'to co-develop tests of competing hypotheses' (Clark et al., 2022).

Similarly, 'consensus building' has been advocated to reduce flexibilities in different research domains of personality science (Leising et al., 2022). For example, the large redundancy among supposedly distinct measures has been argued to sometimes render the choice among them substantively irrelevant (Leising et al., 2013). This suggests that variation in a test result due to flexibility in this choice would not reflect warranted differences in underlying theoretical assumptions, but would instead open the door to fishing expeditions. Systematic consensus building has been found to be exceptionally rare in psychological science (Etzel et al., 2025). Case examples suggest that it may often work well, and systematic ways of reaching consensus have been conceptualized (Clark et al., 2022; Leising et al., 2024, and references in both papers).

On the flip side, urging researchers to reach consensus carries the risk of groupthink, through which warranted flexibilities may be smoothed away (Clark et al., 2022). Such urging may also reflect power imbalances, yield vague agreement that hardly helps specify flexibilities, may not be adhered to, or even give misleading conclusions that hinder scientific progress.

Instead, where different specifications are substantiated and the justifications are transparent and thus invite discussion and refinement, they reflect scientific pluralism (Kellert, 2006; Ruphy, 2016). Examples in psychology include researchers who adjust for different covariates based on assumptions about which causal mechanisms have generated the data (Wysocki et al., 2022), and competing theories of emotion that justify different response formats in emotion recognition tasks (Barrett et al., 2019). Embracing such flexibility uncovers how a test result depends on theoretical assumptions, which helps decide which assumptions to prefer or whether particular research is needed to specify flexibilities meaningfully (Cartwright, 1999; Cartwright & Hardie, 2012; Höfler et al., 2018).

Multiverse Analysis

Other approaches open and explore flexibilities to unravel how results vary across them.

However, these concern only flexibilities from raw data processing onwards. Multiverse analysis was initially conceptualized and used to present the full range of variation in results across a wide range of flexibilities (such as all p-values that can be produced at a certain step of data processing or analysis; Steegen et al., 2016). Producing many results raises the question of how to arrive at a binary decision from the resulting multitude of sometimes 'billions of regressions' (Auspurg, 2025). Presenting all results has been criticized for assigning them, often implicitly, equal weight (Auspurg, 2025; Del Giudice & Gangestad, 2021). Readers may be misled by a huge number of different results that vary substantially in the justifiability of their underlying assumptions. A majority vote across specifications-that is, deciding in favor of the hypothesis if more specifications support it than contradict itmay be dominated by variants with weak theoretical foundations. An alternative use of multiverse analysis selects one or a few specifications that a researcher considers reasonable and bases the decision on these. However, such selections and their justifications are commonly made post hoc, and justifications made in light of the results have long been known to be susceptible to cognitive biases (Kerr, 1998; Klayman & Ha, 1987).

In recent conceptualizations and implementations, multiverse analysis has been recommended to be preregistered before access to raw data (Mazei et al., 2025; Short et al., 2026).

Moreover, the related approach of specification curve analysis explicitly restricts the specifications considered to those that are theoretically justified (Simonsohn et al., 2020).

Secondary Data Analysis and Sensitivity Analysis

Secondary data analysis is an umbrella term for any analysis of data that has previously been collected for another purpose and/or has already been analyzed, whether with the same or a different goal. A frequent purpose of secondary analysis resembles the principle of sensitivity analysis: assessing how robust a result is to justifiably different analytical choices (Aczel et al., 2026; Fox et al., 2021). A test result may be considered more robust if it has passed additional tests with alternative specifications. However, whenever specifications are post hoc, this argument hinges on results not being selectively reported in order to claim additional evidence in favor of the hypothesis (i.e., producing the same result) or to dismiss it (i.e., producing the opposite result) (Simmons et al., 2011).

Preregistered Secondary Data Analysis

Secondary data analysis may be preregistered after data have been collected but before access to the data (Mertens & Krypotos, 2019; Silverzahn et al., 2018). However, some results, perhaps the primary result for the hypothesis, may have been published by then, and an analytical plan may be influenced by what is already known about the data. The primary results may provide hints that shape an analysis in favor of one result over another. For example, Table 1 of a paper might report a considerable difference in the mean score of a variable across the two groups compared. A secondary analyst may then decide to adjust for that variable and come up with a justification for doing so that appears plausible, thereby challenging the evidence for the group effect in the first analysis. Alternatively, a secondary analyst may obtain such information from the authors through non-transparent communication.

For this reason, preregistration templates for secondary data analysis need to elicit self-reports on this. Van den Akker and colleagues (2021) ask: 'What prior knowledge do you have about the dataset that may be relevant for the proposed analysis?'

Novel approach: Parallel Specifications of Flexibilities

Before Data Collection

Concept I formulate a concept that overcomes the pitfalls of post hoc assessment and considers much fewer variants than commonly examined in multiverse analysis. It grounds on familiar ideas, but has not been fully explicated as a conception guided by the principle of extending the epistemic benefits of preregistering flexibilities before access to data to researchers other than the study conductors. This extends the idea of parallel specifications of an analysis within a given dataset, as exemplified by 'Many Analysts, One Dataset' (Silberzahn et al., 2018) and 'Synchronous Robustness Reports' (Bartoš et al., 2025), to the entire research process.

In my concept, the study conductors develop a study design and specify which flexibilities it accommodates and the extent to which each can be varied within a study's feasibility limits.

The range of possible variations is determined at this stage. For example, the study conductors may plan an experiment using a particular paradigm while allowing for the addition of another outcome measure (e.g., a questionnaire score). In contrast, including a different method (e.g., eye tracking) will commonly not be practicable within the study's budget. Such a substantial extension would instead require additional funding for a multimethod study (Campbell & Fiske, 1959) at the outset. Specifications may also vary in the exact formulation of the hypothesis, as long as the resulting variants can be tested within the same basic study design. Importantly, the range of possibilities is determined primarily by practical constraints, rather than by whether a variation is considered to alter the hypothesis substantively. For instance, replacing an outcome measure with another may be feasible but might genuinely change the hypothesis. Conversely, a modification in the sampled population may be considered a variation of the same hypothesis, but may not be feasible.

After the study conductors have completed their study protocol, a preregistration template is prepared. This template defines the range within which these flexibilities can be specified differently. The study plan is then distributed, and other researchers are invited to contribute alternative specifications and their theoretical justifications as a basis for testing the hypothesis. The next chapter will outline how this can be implemented in Stage 1 and Stage 2 PRR study protocols.

Most importantly, these results must be taken as what they are: different results that rely on different assumptions reflecting pluralism. Consider two misinterpretations that researchers may be tempted to make: 1.

Conditional results yield a marginal conclusion. No. Conditional tests are not conceptualized as informing an overall decision across the conditional results. They do not permit such aggregation per se; that is, without assigning them weights and justifying these. For example, majority rule assumes that the conditions underlying the different results are equally plausible. Formally, such a marginal result for an effect estimate could be obtained via: Marginal estimate = ∫ (Estimate|s) * P(s) ds, s = 1, 2, …, S where s denotes a set of specifications in a single test and P() denotes a probability or 'plausibility' distribution over specifications s (in the example S = 4). Such a distribution must somehow be derived and justified. Then, the resulting marginal estimate, together with its standard error, can serve as the basis of an informed overall decision on the hypothesis. If the distribution is, however, specified only after seeing the conditional results, this again carries the risk of being chosen and justified with the goal of arriving at the desired result.

2. Differences between binary test results across the s specification sets can be readily interpreted. No. While integrating different results through concepts like 'effect surface estimation' (Rubin, 1992) is highly valuable, caution is required. Without explicit tests between specification sets, apparent differences are based on comparisons of p-values and are therefore easily attributable to chance-a practice that lacks error control (Gelman & Stern, 2006).

Reduced Costs Through Conducting a Single Study

In addition to the epistemic benefits, the suggested concept reduces costs in comparison to the common approach. In particular, the comparison is between: 1.

Many studies: As is common, a hypothesis is tested across different studies (samples), each with a different specification; and 2. One study: The same study tests the hypothesis under the different specifications.

In the many-studies approach, each set of specifications of the research process necessitates an individual study involving a new sample, resulting in a total of S sets. If the expenses (money, personnel, recruitment) of a study roughly equal c (assuming similar designs, as in the one-study concept), the total cost of all studies is on the order of S × c. The actual total costs are expected to be somewhat larger than S × c if multiple specifications require minor extensions to the study design.

I propose a common review process-albeit a more extensive one-for stages 1 and 2 of a PRR. Having the same reviewers assess the stage 1 proposal and the different variants in stage 2 would increase consistency and create synergies, as reviewers could build on their familiarity with the study rather than independently familiarizing themselves with it. This approach would reduce the overall reviewing workload for the scientific community.

Shared and unshared random error and bias

Cost considerations should also take into account how statistical errors differ between the two approaches. This requires estimating a quantity of interest on which the hypothesis test is based, e.g., a mean difference between two randomized groups to estimate a population average effect, perhaps across repeated measurements in a multilevel model (Höfler, 2026).

Estimates are prone to two kinds of error. Random error arises from random sampling, randomization, and perhaps random aspects of measurement (e.g., random time order) (Greenland, 1990). It decreases and converges to zero as sample size increases. Bias, or systematic error, however, is independent of sample size (if the same design is used across smaller and larger samples) and may arise, among other things, from issues in measurement, selection, non-compliance or (in non-randomized studies) unconsidered common causes of the factor and outcome (Fox et al., 2021).

Although exact answers to the error comparison between the many-studies and one-study ap-360 proaches depend strongly on particular design issues, and a formal treatment would be re-361 quired to provide specific answers for any given design, some general arguments can be 362 made. What has not yet Been Covered Invites Exploration

It has been argued that data analysis that does not meet the requirements of rigorous hypothesis testing-that is, preregistration with sufficient detail-should be considered exploratory (Höfler et al., 2022; Lakens & de Bruine, 2021; Lakens et al., 2024; Nosek et al., 2012). This concerns instances in which flexibilities are too poorly understood to be disclosed meaningfully, making hypothesis testing seem premature. Exploration can help identify and specify these flexibilities where theory alone cannot. Such gaps may involve underdeveloped theories and their operationalizations (Scheel, 2022), as well as measurement (Anvari et al., 2025), prediction, and analysis. Filling these gaps renders hypotheses more falsifiable (Höfler et al., 2025; Scheel, 2022).

Exploratory research must be communicated in clearly distinguishable terms from confirmatory research, including explicit wording stating that its results can only serve to generate new proposals, for instance, for modified or novel hypotheses. If flexibilities are not misused to produce results presented as confirmatory but instead serve to openly explore the novel, they are no longer a burden. Instead, they become the foundation for an open-ended search for the heretofore unknown and unexpected (Höfler et al., 2022).bA study can either be exploratory from the outset or its data can be explored after a confirmatory attempt.

Regardless of the confirmatory result, exploration, accompanied by theoretical input, might suggest modifications that better fit the data. With rigorous confirmation and transparent exploration, both approaches enrich each other: exploration generates new proposals for modified and entirely new hypotheses and invites testing them with new data (Höfler et al., 2022; Höfler et al., 2023). Alternating between confirmation and exploration to refine hypotheses produces chains of research called 'concatenated exploration' (Stebbins, 1992).

Discussion

Science does not begin with tools, and research should not be designed or conducted solely according to the capacities that the tools offer. Maslow (1966, p. 15) wrote: 'I suppose it is tempting, if the only tool you have is a hammer, to treat everything as if it were a nail.' Instead, science begins with conceptions, and tools should be developed to implement them (Kaplan, 1964). This paper has elaborated on the concept of multiple, parallel specifications of research flexibilities, initially set up by the conductors of a study and then opened to others for alternative specifications. The concept makes use of an existing tool (PRRs), which was designed for another purpose and therefore lacks a detailed conceptual foundation for its application and modification in this context. This paper has sought to fill this gap, whereas existing approaches to hypothesis testing by others incorporate multiple specifications only from data processing onward.

The concept combines desirable pluralism through allowing flexibilities with the epistemic advantages of disclosing them before accessing the data. It invites multiple test results, each conditional on a set of specifications and their substantive justifications. Importantly, the epistemic advantages of these conditional test results apply only to the researchers who actually chose the relevant specifications a priori. For others, namely researchers who read the results in a paper, these advantages apply only if they, too, would have chosen the same specifications beforehand-a condition that cannot be verified.

It might also be tempting for recipients to aim for a majority vote in the research community on which of the tested specification sets appears most plausible. This can be misleading because such a vote would constitute a post hoc assessment made with knowledge of the results and might thus be affected by interests in arriving at one result over another. However, awareness of this issue and discussions about the investigated specification variants might motivate researchers either to participate in future studies involving parallel specifications of flexibilities or to engage in consensus initiatives to reduce flexibilities in the first place.

Another epistemic question should be investigated once parallel preregistrations are established to test the same hypothesis: is such external testing of a hypothesis less susceptible to investigator bias than the common approach of testing the hypothesis in one's own study? This advantage, if it exists, might be limited insofar as investigator bias is already inherent in the study design selected by the researcher and cannot be addressed through what can be specified in the preregistration template. Minimizing such risks through detailed study plans and preregistration templates is a key purpose of the review process, and reviewers should be instructed accordingly.

Many practical issues that are outside the scope of this conceptual paper remain to be tested and investigated. These mainly involve details of implementation in PRRs. Nevertheless, some general principles can be outlined from the outset: The distribution of an accepted Stage 1 PRR protocol should follow the principles of visibility and inclusivity. Thus, the 'crowdsourcing' (Silberzahn et al., 2018) of alternative specifications for testing the planned study should provide as many researchers as possible with the opportunity to participate.

Existing communication channels provide useful starting points for achieving this goal, including collaborative research networks such as the 'Psychological Science Accelerator' (Moshontz et al., 2018), society newsletters, academic blogs, conference and community mailing lists, social media, and dedicated announcements on preregistration platforms. In addition, rich, standardized, and machine-readable metadata are an important means of making the protocol discoverable (Bonino da Silva Santos et al., 2023; Wilkinson et al., 2016). Detailed documentation of procedures, at least those covered by the preregistration template, is essential to allow others to understand them and thus to specify them differently.

Communication opportunities through surveys, email, or online meetings may be provided to clarify issues and details and to allow for consensus where different specifications turn out to be unwarranted (Silberzahn et al., 2018).

These open practical aspects do not diminish the concept and its epistemic benefits. Once its idea reaches the scientific community, it may encourage researchers to participate. This would reduce the overall workload for the scientific community by rendering multiple separate studies to test the same hypothesis less necessary. Researchers may also be prompted to initiate such studies themselves and to contribute to their refinement and implementation.
