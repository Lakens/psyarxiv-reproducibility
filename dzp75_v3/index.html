What we found

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

In an increasingly digital age, knowing which kinds of screen use relate to mental health in adolescents could help guide prevention efforts for a vulnerable population. Earlier work has examined this question, but the findings have been difficult to interpret. For instance, most studies measure screen use in hours, while it may be more informative to know whether adolescents have difficulty controlling their use. This is especially important now that adolescents use screens for school, games and contact with friends. Newer studies are being published on these topics, including a recent ABCD study in which problematic social media use and depressive symptoms predicted each other over time. Our study aims to contribute more broadly, by looking at gaming as well as social media, at hours and problematic use in the same participants, and at brain measures alongside symptoms. ABCD Study release 7.0 (N=11,860) offered a chance for us to look at these together, since it profiles neuroimaging, behavioral and clinical measures in the same participants. In this preregistered multimodal study, our goal was to see how screen time, problematic social media use (SMAQ) and problematic video game use (VGAQ) relate to parent-rated mental health, brain structure and resting-state connectivity. All five confirmatory associations were positive but marginal (β = 0.051 to 0.173), and changed little across several robustness checks. In the same participants, the problematic use scales were more strongly related to attention problems than hours of use were. The association between screen time and anxiety problems was present in male but not female participants. More screen time was associated with slightly smaller putamen and caudate volumes, while resting-state connectivity did not predict SMAQ. In exploratory analyses, SMAQ predicted later attention problems more than the reverse, while for gaming, attention problems predicted later VGAQ more strongly. Most of the association seemed to come from problematic use rather than use time by itself. Overall, the effects were small but consistent, in line with earlier work in the ABCD Study. We hope this analysis can contribute to a better understanding of how social media and gaming relate to adolescent mental health and brain development.

1 Introduction

Screen use for adolescents has only increased over the years, from schoolwork to games and keeping in touch with friends, and questions about how this use relates to well-being and brain development have only grown. Large cohort studies have mostly found small associations. In the ABCD Study, a typical association between screen time and mental health is around r = 0.05, and an above-average one is around r = 0.09 (Nagata et al., 2024).

These findings may be difficult to compare due to how screen use is measured. For instance, many studies focus on hours of use which may not be problematic on its own, and may miss important signal from difficulty stopping, or whether use gets in the way of sleep, school, or friendships. over time. Most participants stayed below the borderline ADHD score from baseline to year 6, while smaller groups remitted, increased or persisted (Figure 1).

1.2 Small links in large samples

Large cohort studies find associations between hours of screen time and mental health to be marginal but consistent (Odgers & Jensen, 2020). Across 355,358 adolescents in many analyses, technology use explained variation in well-being by at most 0.4% (Orben & Przybylski, 2019).

In participants aged 12 or younger, a meta-analysis of 87 studies found small correlations of screen time with externalizing and internalizing problems (Eirich et al., 2022).

In the ABCD Study itself, baseline screen time related to later depressive symptoms most, with marginal effects throughout, near the typical and above-average ABCD effects given above (Nagata et al., 2024; Paulich et al., 2021). While these effects may have lower explanatory value per individual, this can be a relevant trend at the population level (Odgers & Jensen, 2020).

1.3 Screen time, depression and anxiety

Across 87 studies, in about 159,000 participants aged 12 or younger, screen time appears to correlate with internalizing problems at r = 0.07 (Eirich et al., 2022).

In ABCD, baseline screen time predicted later depressive symptoms (B = 0.10) (Nagata et al., 2024), and within each participant, increases in social media hours preceded depressive symptoms, with no path the other way (Nagata et al., 2025).

In secondary school students, increased social media and television use co-occurs with increased depressive symptoms (Boers et al., 2019); in 6,595 US adolescents, social media time co-occurs with internalizing problems, most at three hours a day or more (Riehm et al., 2019).

In the UK Millennium Cohort at 14, five or more hours of social media a day related to depressive scores 50% higher in girls and 35% higher in boys (Kelly et al., 2018).

One longitudinal study found no path from social media to depression, but depressive symptoms predicting later use in girls (Heffer et al., 2019).

At the ABCD baseline, screen time explained under 2% of the variance in any of the measures studied (Paulich et al., 2021).

1.4 Externalizing problems

Screen time correlates with externalizing problems at r = 0.11 across 87 studies (Eirich et al., 2022).

In ABCD, conduct disorder was most linked to social media use of all problematic screen use types (Nagata et al., 2023), and parent-rated problematic use accounted for the remaining association with externalizing problems that screen time did not (Eales et al., 2026).

In 6,595 US adolescents, social media time increases with internalizing problems but not with externalizing problems alone (Riehm et al., 2019).

1.5 Gender and age

In ABCD, girls report more problematic social media use and boys more problematic gaming, and VGAQ does not measure the same differences in both sexes (Bagot et al., 2022).

Windows of developmental sensitivity to social media appear at 11 to 13 in girls, 14 to 15 in boys and 19 in both (Orben et al., 2022).

In ABCD, problematic use occurs differentially in both genders: online social activity predicted later ADHD problems in girls only (Deng et al., 2025), problematic social media use predicted ADHD symptoms in males (Nagata et al., 2026a), and baseline links with depression and anxiety appeared mainly in boys for weekends (Paulich et al., 2021).

Heavy social media use increases with greater differences in depressive scores in girls than in boys at 14 (Kelly et al., 2018).

1.6 Brain structure

Children and adults with ADHD have slightly smaller accumbens, amygdala, caudate, hippocampus and putamen volumes (Hoogman et al., 2017). The caudate, putamen and nucleus accumbens are part of the striatum, which plays a role in reward and in forming habits. Prefrontal regions help with self-regulation. Because of this, we expected differences linked to problematic use in these areas.

1.7 Prediction from connectivity

In connectome-based predictive modeling, how connectivity data maps to traits is learned in one set of participants and used to predict a different set (Shen et al., 2017); it has predicted problematic social media use at r = 0.055 and problematic gaming at r = 0.09 from rest in ABCD (Park et al., 2026a (Park et al., , 2026b)).

A sustained attention network found this way in adults predicted ADHD ratings from rest in another dataset (Rosenberg et al., 2016).

In ABCD at baseline, weaker anticorrelation between the default-mode and dorsal attention networks related to attention problems, at about 1% of the variance (Duffy & Helwig, 2024); one account of ADHD links lapses of attention to the default-mode network interfering with task networks (Sonuga-Barke & Castellanos, 2007).

Selecting connections and testing them on the same participants inflates such results (Kriegeskorte et al., 2009), and noise in the measures limits how accurate brain-behavior prediction can be (Gell et al., 2024).

Machine learning approaches are increasingly applied to ABCD measures and to neuroimaging, for example to predict trajectories of depressive symptoms or to find patterns in fMRI data (Xiang et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2018).

The hypotheses, instruments, covariates and analysis plan were preregistered under the title "Machine Learning Approaches in Neural Connectivity to Understanding Social Media Use in Adolescents" (Preregistration, 2026). Since this study is observational, we did not change anything for the participants. We used screen use as a continuous predictor for mental health and brain measures. As we planned, the exploratory analyses are kept separate from the preregistered ones.

2.4 Measures

Problematic use was measured using two surveys. Problematic social media use was measured with SMAQ, which has six items rated from 1 (never) to 6 (very often) and is adapted from the Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale (Andreassen et al., 2012; Bagot et al., 2022). Items are summed to a total score that ranges from 6 to 36 where higher scores indicate more problematic use. Only samples with at least five of six items answered are included. Internal consistency was Cronbach's α = 0.857. Problematic gaming was measured and scored the same way with VGAQ and α = 0.869 (Bagot et al., 2022). As in the preregistered hypotheses, we used total scores instead of single items for our scope. Since both scales were internally consistent, the total scores were able to summarize each scale while keeping the number of comparisons small. These questions focus on quality of social media use rather than quantity, such as difficulty stopping use, or use getting in the way of daily life. Data for both questionnaires first begins at year 2. The six items cover one component each: salience, tolerance, mood modification, relapse, withdrawal and conflict (Andreassen et al., 2012; Bagot et al., 2022). 2.5 Neuroimaging

For H5, we used the FreeSurfer measures provided in the release from the ABCD imaging pipeline (Hagler et al., 2019; Fischl, 2012). Earlier studies have related these regions to reward and self-regulation (Haber & Knutson, 2010; Heatherton & Wagner, 2011; Somerville & Casey, 2010). For screen time, we used the baseline scan. SMAQ and VGAQ were first surveyed in year 2, so we used the year 2 scan for those.

For H6, we used the resting-state correlations provided in the release. First, there were correlations within and between the 13 Gordon networks (Gordon et al., 2016), which make up 91 pairs. 2.6 Statistical analysis

Our goal was to see if outcomes increased with each predictor when comparing participants of similar age, gender, background and site, while also accounting for siblings.

For H1 to H3, each outcome was regressed on its predictor and the covariates using linear regression. Site was included as a fixed effect, and standard errors were clustered by family, which corrects for siblings being more similar to each other than to unrelated participants. β is in standard deviations, since the predictor, outcome and continuous covariates were standardized. We adjusted all models for six covariates (age, gender, race and ethnicity, household income, parental education, and site). Table 2 shows their distribution in each sample. H5 also included intracranial volume, and H6 included head motion. The five confirmatory tests (H1, its VGAQ form, both outcomes of H2, and H3) were corrected together using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995), and every preregistered prediction was positive. Throughout, q is a p value after Benjamini-Hochberg correction within its set of tests. These results were checked with a linear mixed model with a family random intercept and site as a fixed effect.

We also ran three checks to ensure our results were consistent. First, we used daily hours of use in place of the scales for the same participants. Second, we included non-users in the analysis by labeling them with the lowest score on the scale. Third, we added ten genetic ancestry components for participants that included them. We sought to determine if our design could identify effects of a known size. For this, we took the H1 outcome and added an association of a set size, and kept the same participants, covariates and families. Some of the added noise was shared between siblings (intraclass correlation 0.31, from 673 sibling pairs), and the model was refit 200 times at each size.

In H4, we tested whether effects differed by gender. Each confirmatory model included gender and a gender × predictor term. The five resulting interactions were corrected as one family.

For H5, we used the same models but also included intracranial volume. Regional values were winsorized at 3.5 SD before analysis. Corrections were done within each predictor and measure, first across the preregistered regions only and then across the whole brain.

H6 used connectome-based predictive modeling (Shen et al., 2017). The preregistered targets were SMAQ and screen time, and we also predicted VGAQ, which was not preregistered. Models were trained on all sites but one and tested on the held-out site, one site at a time. We removed the effects of covariates and head motion from the target and the edges. This was only done using the training sites, which kept the held-out site separate. Next, we kept the edges that were related to the target at p < 0.01. Edges were assigned to two sets, one for positive and one for negative. The total strength of each set then gave the prediction for the held-out site. To attain a p-value, we shuffled the target 100 times and ran the entire model on each shuffle. Last, we added a simulated effect of r = 0.15 on ten edges, to check how much of this the model could find.

Beyond the preregistered tests, we ran three exploratory analyses. First, to see which measure tends to change first within participants, we used cross-lagged models. We tested whether SMAQ or VGAQ at one visit predicted attention or DSM ADHD problems at a later visit, and whether these problems predicted later SMAQ or VGAQ. This was done from year 2 to year 3, year 2 to year 4, and year 3 to year 4. Each model included the preregistered covariates. It also included the earlier value of the measure it predicted, to account for how stable that measure was over time. The tests were then corrected within each lag.

Second, we looked at the emotion and reward tasks, to see if they related to ADHD symptoms or to problematic use. For this, we used the emotional n-back and reward task measures provided in the release. We only analyzed sessions that passed the release's quality and inclusion flags. Brain measures were also adjusted for head motion during tasks. We also looked at emotional n-back, which measures accuracy and reaction time in remembering faces if their expression appeared emotional (positive and negative) vs neutral. We also used amygdala and insula activation to emotional faces. Fusiform activation to faces compared with places was included as a check, since faces should clearly activate this region. From the reward task, we used how much more quickly participants responded when they could win or lose money, compared with trials where nothing was at stake. We also used accumbens activation while participants waited for a possible reward and when they saw the result, and medial orbitofrontal activation when they saw the result.

This gave 20 measures in total (Table S9). We then estimated each task effect as d (mean divided by standard deviation) and stability of each measure over two years. Each measure was related to parent-rated ADHD problems, emotional dysregulation, and K-SADS inattention and hyperactivity at baseline. It was also related to SMAQ and VGAQ at year 2, both at the same visit and from the baseline measure, with baseline screen time held constant. Corrections were done within each set of tests.

Last, we repeated the ADHD biotype approach of Pan et al. (2026), to see if it would hold in a population cohort. Since the release only includes regional measures, we built morphometric similarity networks from ten measures per region. Normative models of each region's degree, nodal efficiency and participation were trained on participants with an ADHD t-score under 60. The deviations of the 655 participants with a parent-rated ADHD tscore of 65 or more were then clustered into two, three and four groups. To see if these clusters were stable, we compared clusterings fitted via varied folds using the adjusted Rand index (ARI), also varied shufflings of case and control variables. We also fitted the clusters on half of the sites and on the other half, and compared their labels for the same participants.

All analyses were run on a secure enclave environment via a cloud machine hosted by Nimbus Logic, as participant-level data could only be accessed there. We used Python for all coding with Numpy, Scipy, Statsmodels, and ScikitLearn.

2.7 Differences from our Preregistration

There were a few places where our analysis did not follow the preregistration, and Table 4 lists them next to the original plan. We had the opportunity to rerun our analysis with ABCD release 7.0 so this study uses this dataset instead of 6.0. The preregistration planned for family nested in site as random effects, but we used site as a fixed effect and clustered the standard errors on family. The preregistered mixed model is still reported next to each test. Because there are only 22 sites at baseline and 21 at year 2, we considered fixed effects a better way to account for differences between sites than treating site as random.

We did not use the American Community Survey (ACS) propensity weights, since they were built to describe the US population of 9-and 10-year-olds at baseline, while our tests are associations at later visits adjusted for the preregistered covariates. So our estimates describe the ABCD sample and not the US population.

Screen time totals above 18 hours a day were also kept. As planned, only brain values were winsorized. For H3, the preregistration named "social media use," so we used SMAQ as in H1 and added daily social media hours as a second measure. In this paper, H4 focused on gender, since earlier ABCD studies found gender differences in both problematic social media use and its links to symptoms (Bagot et al., 2022; Nagata et al., 2026b).

3.6 Brain structure (H5)

Participants with more screen time had very slightly smaller volumes in parts of the striatum, a set of deep brain structures involved in reward and habit. These differences were small, only a few hundredths of a standard deviation. Across 10,383 participants, baseline screen time was associated with smaller volumes in three of the six preregistered striatal structures. These were the right putamen (β = -0.038, q = 5.4 × 10⁻⁵), left putamen (β = -0.025, q = 0.015) and left caudate (β = -0.022, q = 0.037) (Table 8, Figure 4). More screen time was also associated with smaller surface area in two of the 36 preregistered prefrontal regions, the right orbital gyrus and right gyrus rectus (β = -0.024 and -0.027, q = 0.047). No region showed an association with cortical thickness.

A similar pattern was observed for VGAQ, which was associated with smaller volumes in five of the six preregistered striatal structures (β from -0.031 to -0.025). However, none of these remained significant when all 18 subcortical structures were corrected together. SMAQ was not associated with any region.

None of the regional associations, significant or not, was greater than 0.048 SD (Table S8). This is about what we expected, because brain-behavior associations are usually small (Marek et al., 2022).

3.8 Direction (exploratory)

Both measures were collected again one or two years later, so we could determine which measure came first. We did not preregister this analysis, so it is reported as exploratory (Figure 6). 4 Discussion

Overall, the confirmatory predictions of H1 to H3 were supported. The associations were marginal but consistent. This is what earlier ABCD work led us to expect (Nagata et al., 2024). How much adolescents struggled with their use seemed to matter more than how many hours they spent on screens (Boer et al., 2020; Eales et al., 2026). Screen time and anxiety appear to co-occur in male participants and much less in female participants, which may connect to windows of sensitivity that differ by gender and age (Orben et al., 2022). More screen time appears to relate to very slightly smaller striatal volumes, a few hundredths of a standard deviation, which may reflect a liability shared with ADHD as much as screen use (Shou et al., 2025; Hoogman et al., 2017). Resting connectivity seems to include a faint trace of screen time but not of problematic social media use, and the direction analysis suggests the links may be bidirectional (Thorell et al., 2024). Effects in large cohorts tend to be small, as we observe here, yet population-level insights can still inform recommendations at an individual level (Odgers & Jensen, 2020; Orben & Przybylski, 2019). Likewise, these ADHD labels in this dataset are observational, and while they shouldn't be interpreted as causal, they can provide meaningful associations.

Previous studies have examined related questions on their own, such as hours of screen use or problematic use alone, or symptoms without brain measures (Nagata et al., 2026a (Nagata et al., , 2026b)). Our goal was to see if examining many of them in a single preregistered design could show more clearly which types of screen use relate to which problems. ABCD Study release 7.0 offered a chance for us to do this. We related screen use and problematic social media and gaming to attention, depression, anxiety, and externalizing problems. We compared these measures by gender, and related the same measures to brain structure and resting-state connectivity. Since we had more than 10,000 participants for screen time and more than 5,000 for problematic use scales, we were able to check for consistency. These associations changed little in mixed models or with ten genetic ancestry components, and stayed positive when we included non-users by giving them the lowest score.

Below, each hypothesis is discussed, followed by exploratory analyses, the size of the effects, and the limitations.

4.1 Problematic use and attention problems (H1)

The clearest associations in this study involve problematic use and attention problems. In the same participants, VGAQ increases with attention problems at 0.173 vs 0.077 for gaming hours, and SMAQ at 0.107 vs 0.061 for social media hours. This suggests that whether adolescents find their use problematic may explain more than use time on its own (Boer et al., 2020; Eales et al., 2026). The DSM-oriented ADHD scale showed the same pattern too (SMAQ 0.107, VGAQ 0.149). Likewise, in four years of ABCD data, addictive use related to increased suicidal behavior, while total screen time did not (Xiao et al., 2025).

These associations are above the r = 0.09 benchmark for an above-average ABCD effect (Nagata et al., 2024), while well below the meta-analytic correlations with ADHD symptoms of r = 0.36 for problematic social media use (Ding et al., 2025) and r = 0.30 for gaming disorder (Koncz et al., 2023). Parts of this difference may be due to that ABCD may avoid shared-rater inflation by screen using being measured by adolescents and separately, symptoms by their parents. Most studies are typically rated by the same person. Here, adolescents reported their screen use while parents reported their symptoms, which may avoid shared-rater inflation. Smaller estimates have been found in other designs too. Across 45 studies, media use and ADHD-related behavior correlated at r = 0.12 (Nikkelen et al., 2014). Hence the size of the association may also depend on the sample and the design.

4.2 Screen time vs anxiety and depressive symptoms (H2)

Beyond attention problems, H2 focuses on whether hours of screen time relates to mood. Screen time relates to slightly more depressive problems (β = 0.070) and anxiety problems (β = 0.051). These are the smallest of the five confirmatory associations, and close to the pooled internalizing estimate of r = 0.07 (Eirich et al., 2022). Within ABCD participants, increases in social media hours preceded depressive symptoms (Nagata et al., 2025). However, one longitudinal study found depressive symptoms predicting later social media use in female participants (Heffer et al., 2019), so this association may run both ways.

Studies outside ABCD align with this. In secondary school students, increases in social media and television use co-occurred with increased depressive symptoms (Boers et al., 2019). Likewise, in 6,595 US adolescents, time spent on social media related to internalizing problems, most at three hours a day or more (Riehm et al., 2019). In ABCD, Paulich et al. (2021) also found that baseline screen time explained under 2% of the variance in each measure. These results are in line with the marginal estimates here.

However, compared to hours by itself, problematic use has greater correlation with depressive and anxiety symptoms, around r = 0.27 and 0.35 across 18 cross-sectional studies (Shannon et al., 2022). In ABCD, problematic social media use and depressive symptoms predict one another within participants from year 3 to 4 and from year 4 to 5, with no lagged associations for anxiety (Nagata et al., 2026b). This suggests that the mood associations may depend on all three of problematic use, hours, and age. This further suggests H2 may only show part of the link between screen use and mood.

4.3 Problematic social media use and externalizing problems (H3)

For behavior in H3, we tested whether problematic social media use co-occurs with externalizing problems. In the same participants, SMAQ relates to slightly more externalizing problems (β = 0.116) than daily social media hours (β = 0.104). This further supports the benefit of these scales beyond raw hours, though the difference was greater for attention problems. For gaming, there was a greater difference (VGAQ 0.126 vs 0.076 gaming hours, Table S5). This is also supported by previous ABCD work in which parent-rated problematic use, instead of screen time, accounts for externalizing problems (Eales et al., 2026), and with work in which social media was the screen type most strongly linked to later conduct disorder (Nagata et al., 2023). SMAQ also relates to aggressive behavior (β = 0.098). In 6,595 US adolescents, time on social media related to internalizing problems but not to externalizing problems alone (Riehm et al., 2019).

4.4 Gender-wise Comparisons (H4)

In H4, these measures are compared by gender. Screen time co-occurs with anxiety problems in male participants (0.080) and much less in female participants (0.016), close to earlier ABCD findings where links with depression and anxiety appeared mainly in male participants (Paulich et al., 2021). Windows of sensitivity to social media appear at different ages in female and male participants, which may be part of why this pattern differs (Orben et al., 2022). Earlier ABCD studies also demonstrate differential behavior: problematic social media use predicted ADHD symptoms in male participants (Nagata et al., 2026a), and online social activity predicted ADHD problems in female participants (Deng et al., 2025).

In female participants, VGAQ related to attention problems slightly more (0.223 vs 0.154, p = 0.048), though not after correction (q = 0.12), and the other three associations did not differ by gender.

Other cohorts found the opposite for depression. In 10,904 adolescents aged 14 in the UK Millennium Cohort, five or more hours of social media a day related to depressive scores 50% higher in female and 35% higher in male adolescents (Kelly et al., 2018). In ABCD, female participants reported more problematic social media use, while male participants reported more problematic gaming (Bagot et al., 2022). VGAQ may also not measure the same way for both. Hence part of the gender differences here may be due to the scales themselves.

Within participants in ABCD, problematic social media use and depressive symptoms were also more strongly related in female participants (Nagata et al., 2026b). Our anxiety association appeared in male participants, but it was based on hours at a single time point, not on problematic use.

4.5 Brain structure (H5)

In H5, we include brain-level data to compare screen use with volume and structure. Greater screen time relates to slightly smaller putamen and caudate volumes. Our right putamen value (-0.038) is similar to the -0.036 in release 5.0 (Shou et al., 2025). Since smaller volumes in these regions are also observed in ADHD (Hoogman et al., 2017), this may reflect problematic symptoms as a shared risk between ADHD and screen use, rather than either of these by themselves. Regional associations consistently did not surpass 0.048 standard deviations, which aligns with larger samples for brain-behavior associations (Marek et al., 2022).

More screen time also relates to smaller surface area in two of the 36 preregistered prefrontal regions, the right orbital gyrus and right gyrus rectus (β = -0.024 and -0.027, q = 0.047). No region relates to cortical thickness. VGAQ relates to smaller volumes in five of the six preregistered striatal structures (β from -0.031 to -0.025), but none remained significant when all 18 subcortical structures were corrected together. SMAQ relates to no region.

These results are consistent with earlier studies. In ABCD, social media use related to smaller hippocampus, caudate and thalamus volumes (He et al., 2023). Younger individuals with ADHD also tend to have smaller cortical surface area (Hoogman et al., 2019), which may be part of why screen time relates to smaller prefrontal surface area here. In contrast, frequent video game players had more left ventral striatal gray matter at age 14 (Kühn et al., 2011). Within ABCD participants, those with lower caudate activation to reward later had higher VGAQ scores (Lopez et al., 2024). This suggests that gaming may relate to the striatum through reward processing, and not only through its volume. In ABCD, screen media activity also related to brain structure in several networks, most strongly in visual cortex (Paulus et al., 2019). However, over four years, digital media use did not change cortical or striatal development (Nivins et al., 2024).

4.6 Prediction from connectivity (H6)

Beyond brain structure, our goal in H6 was to see if resting-state connectivity could predict screen use in sites left out of each model. While connectivity predicted screen time at r = 0.094, it did not predict SMAQ (r = 0.012).

Since the design recovered a planted effect of r = 0.15 at 0.100, an association of that size would likely have been found if present. VGAQ, which was not preregistered, was predicted at r = 0.062 too, and the results were similar with and without removing motion during the scan.

Earlier ABCD work found SMAQ to be predicted at r = 0.055 and VGAQ at r = 0.09 from many more edges (Park et al., 2026a (Park et al., , 2026b)). Our values may be lower due to using 338 network-level edges, which could miss information in the parcel-level connectome.

Connectome-based models have predicted ADHD ratings from a sustained attention network at rest (Rosenberg et al., 2016). In the ABCD Study itself, weaker anticorrelation between the default-mode and dorsal attention networks related to attention problems at about 1% of the variance (Duffy & Helwig, 2024). While associations of this size are easy to overstate when connections are selected and tested on the same participants (Kriegeskorte et al., 2009), testing in left-out sites avoids this. The effects at the network level were small, yet a parcel-level connectome can still be a natural next step to build on these results.

4.7 Direction (exploratory)

Because the tests above are associations at a single time point each, the exploratory cross-lagged models look at whether each measure predicts later change in the other. Higher SMAQ at year 2 predicted a greater increase in attention problems by year 4 (0.058) than attention problems predicted in SMAQ (0.035), while for gaming, attention problems predicted a greater increase in VGAQ (0.072 against 0.043). The DSM-oriented ADHD scale showed the same order (0.043 compared to 0.016).

When we looked only at the lag from year 3 to year 4, attention problems still predicted later problematic social media use, but problematic use no longer predicted later attention problems. Which measures change first may therefore change with age, and in ABCD, Nagata et al. (2026a)also found that the timing of these paths changes across early adolescence.

Like earlier longitudinal studies, our results support both directions (Thorell et al., 2024). In ABCD, hours of social media related to increasing inattention, while television and gaming did not (Nivins et al., 2026). This held with an ADHD polygenic score kept constant, and there was no path in the other direction. Likewise, in 2,587 adolescents followed for 24 months, each additional high-frequency digital media activity related to higher odds of later ADHD symptoms (Ra et al., 2018). In a longitudinal study of 228 female participants, inattention related to problematic social internet use at the same time, while the paths over time were marginal (Halkett & Hinshaw, 2024). For depressive symptoms in ABCD, the paths with problematic social media use only appeared from year 3 to 4 and from year 4 to 5 (Nagata et al., 2026b). This suggests that, as with attention problems here, the timing of these paths may depend on age.

4.8 Further exploratory analyses

Some analyses were not part of the preregistration and are reported here as exploratory. We applied the ADHD biotypes from Pan et al. (2026) on ABCD (Table S11). In 655 participants in the clinical range, our clusters were only slightly better than shuffled labels (ARI = 0.039 at k = 3, vs 0.21 in the original study). This suggests that these biotypes may not carry over to a population sample like ABCD. More fidelity may be needed as well, given we were limited to observation-based labels. Beyond the preregistered tests, we also compared problematic use with hours in the same participants, looked at whether the direction of the SMAQ paths changes with age, and predicted VGAQ from resting-state connectivity.

The emotion and reward tasks showed the expected effects, for example accumbens activation to reward feedback (d = 0.51). However, most of these measures were only weakly stable over two years (r of 0.09 or less). None of them related to ADHD symptoms, emotional dysregulation, SMAQ or VGAQ after correction (Tables S9 and S10, Figure S6). So the task measures may be limited by how stable they are across visits.

4.9 Size of the effects

All of the associations above were small, as is common in large cohorts (Odgers & Jensen, 2020; Orben & Przybylski, 2019). Across many well-founded analyses of 355,358 adolescents, technology use explained at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being (Orben & Przybylski, 2019). A β of 0.107 for SMAQ and attention problems is about half a t-score point per standard deviation of SMAQ. While this is low for a single adolescent, it could accumulate across many.

For intervention efforts, this suggests that understanding whether adolescents find it difficult to control their use may be more explanatory than hours of use on their own.

4.10 Limitations

Several limitations are worth keeping in mind when considering these results. Since the data are observational, these associations cannot be interpreted as causal. While cross-lagged models can test whether single measures predict later change in other measures, they cannot rule out other factors that affect both (Heffer et al., 2019). All outcomes were parent-rated too. Parents may see things differently than the adolescents themselves, and may miss experiences that participants cannot yet put into words, or that have not shown up yet developmentally. Teacher reports only covered a smaller sample, and youth self-reports were not collected at baseline, so we left these out of this analysis.

SMAQ and VGAQ could only survey year 2 participants with social media accounts or who played video games respectively. Screen time was also self-reported in hours, without the preregistered 18-hour cap. Self-reported hours may also differ from what phones record. Likewise, many participants scored at the lowest value of each symptom scale, from 39.4% to 69.3% and 54.5% on the ADHD scale, which limits how much these scores can vary. We used total SMAQ and VGAQ scores instead of single items, since looking at each item or at patterns of answers would add many comparisons. Our goal was to first see if the preregistered total scores relate to symptoms, yet item-level patterns and profiles of problematic use can still be explored in future work.

When non-users were given the lowest score, the link between SMAQ and attention problems dropped from 0.107 to 0.042. Every association remained positive, in the same direction. So the H1 finding may mostly apply to adolescents who already use social media. Our estimates were also unweighted. They describe the ABCD sample, and may not carry over to a broader population.

We were also limited by the brain data resolution. The structural associations were very small and cross-sectional, and we did not have the computing resources to analyze the detailed parcel-level connectome data. This study focuses on 338 network-level edges, though future work could extend this to greater resolution. Since there were only 100 permutations, our smallest p-values were also limited to 0.010.

Our analysis also differs from the preregistration in a few places, such as using release 7.0 (Table 4). Of the three machine learning methods named in the preregistration, we used connectome-based prediction for H6 and clustering for the exploratory ADHD biotypes, but not autoencoders. Recent ABCD studies have used withinperson models over more visits for ADHD, depressive and anxiety symptoms (Nagata et al., 2026a (Nagata et al., , 2026b)). A natural next step can be to extend these models to gaming, externalizing problems and brain measures.

5 Conclusion

In this preregistered study of ABCD release 7.0, problematic social media and gaming use appears to relate to parent-rated attention problems more than hours of screen time. All five confirmatory associations were positive but marginal. Screen time co-occurs with slightly more depressive and anxiety problems, the latter mainly in male participants, and relates to slightly smaller putamen and caudate volumes. While resting-state connectivity predicted screen time slightly, it did not predict problematic social media use.

For families, clinicians and researchers, asking whether adolescents find their use difficult to control may explain more than hours of use on their own (Boer et al., 2020; Eales et al., 2026). While effects of this size are small for any one adolescent, they can still be meaningful across the total population (Odgers & Jensen, 2020).

While the data are observational and should not be interpreted as causal, they can still provide meaningful associations within the ABCD sample. A natural next step can be to follow the same adolescents over more visits, which would allow us to see if these links increase, fade or reverse with age (Nagata et al., 2026a). Parcel-level connectivity could also give more detail.
