Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Full text

Abstract

Despite extensive medical, psychological, and social research and substantial healthcare investment, chronic pain remains highly prevalent, and a leading cause of years lived with disability. While multimodal and interdisciplinary treatments are recommended, progress may be limited by the lack of an integrative theoretical framework that captures the complexity of chronic pain across levels of analysis.

We introduce a complex dynamic systems perspective as a unifying theoretical framework in which chronic pain emerges from interactions among biological, psychological, and social components over time, and we present a methodological toolbox based on the network approach to operationalize this perspective by modeling such interactions.

We conducted the first narrative review of network studies in chronic pain, identifying 54 studies published up to June 30, 2026. Most studies used cross-sectional network models to examine associations among pain-related variables, comorbidity patterns, and group differences, whereas longitudinal and within-person approaches were rare. Across studies, findings support the view that chronic pain-related factors form interconnected systems; however, substantial heterogeneity in variable selection and study design limits comparability and generalizability.

Overall, current applications provide an important first step toward modeling the complexity of chronic pain but so far remain limited in capturing temporal dynamics and individual-level processes. Advancing this field will require theory-driven, multi-level, and longitudinal research that aligns network methods with system-level questions. In the longer term, this approach holds the promise to support the development of parsimonious models of key mechanisms, informing interventions that target not only symptoms but the dynamic processes that sustain chronic pain.

Introduction

The ICD-11 classification subsumes 19 different conditions as Chronic Primary Pain (CPP; Nicholas et al., 2019). CPP represents a highly heterogeneous population encompassing a wide range of pain types, substantial variation in personal functioning, and diverse mechanisms contributing to the development and maintenance of chronic pain [2]. Prevalence rates remain persistently high, with more than 30% of people worldwide affected despite biotechnical advances and extensive research and treatment efforts over the past decades [2] [3] [4]. Clinical guidelines recommend personalized, multimodal, interdisciplinary treatment approaches [5] [6] [7], reflecting the multifaceted nature of chronic pain. While bio-psycho-social models have been instrumental in conceptualizing this complexity [8] [9] [10], their constituent elements are often studied in relative isolation, limiting the analysis of their dynamic interactions at the individual level.

To address this challenge, we introduce a complex dynamic systems perspective as a unifying theoretical framework. Originating from fields such as ecology, physics, and systems biology, this perspective conceptualizes phenomena as arising from interactions among multiple interdependent components over time [11]. Applied to chronic pain, it provides a framework for integrating biological, psychological, and social processes into a coherent model of how pain arises and persists within people over time [12]. From this perspective, chronic pain can be understood as emerging from continuous interactions among components across various domains, giving rise to self-organizing patterns, feedback loops, and relatively stable states that shape the persistence and variability of pain. For example, the biopsychosocial model already recognizes that pain is influenced by various factors [9, 13].

The fear-avoidance model introduces feedback loops that connect these components, creating a standardized architecture of an interconnected system [14]. The heterogeneity in phenomenology and the dynamic complexity of chronic pain raise the question of how such systems can be studied empirically. It implies that the focus is shifted from isolated risk factors to the structure and dynamics of the system as a whole and calls for methodological approaches capable of capturing such interdependencies.

The aforementioned, quantitative disciplines offer many methods for analyzing patterns of emergence and recovery that could, in principle, be applied to enhance our understanding of chronic pain. One of them is the network approach that has become particularly popular in psychiatry and psychology as a methodological framework to model systems of interacting components by representing variables as nodes and their relationships as edges in the past decade [15, 16]. In this way, network models provide a basic statistical representation of interconnected components, as indicated by conditional dependencies. This lends itself well to a systems perspective. More recently, network models have been extended to incorporate within-person changes over time, offering a dynamic perspective on how these interactions evolve in one individual [17] [18] [19]. While widely adopted in mental health research [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] [25], this process-based approach has not yet been comprehensively introduced and discussed in chronic pain research [12, 26]. This narrative review introduces a complex dynamic systems perspective on chronic pain. It reviews how network approaches have been used thus far, and discusses what is needed to further develop the study of chronic pain within a dynamical systems framework.

First, we outline the systems perspective as a conceptual foundation for understanding chronic pain. Second, we provide an overview of the methodological toolbox of network analysis. Third, we review existing studies applying network approaches to chronic pain. Finally, we evaluate and discuss these findings to inform future directions in the field of chronic pain research from a dynamical complex systems perspective.

1. A complex dynamic systems perspective

Our ability to effect change within a system depends on understanding its underlying dynamics. Just as repairing a car or a cardiovascular disease demand insight into the workings of their respective systems, advancing treatments for chronic pain and mental health conditions requires a nuanced understanding of how biological, psychological, and social components self-organize and mutually reinforce one another [27]. It is essential to describe these system components and their dynamic interactions in order to design interventions that promote flexibility, meaningful change and resilience [28].

A system is a set of interrelated or interdependent components that form a unified whole with properties that emerge from their interactions [29]. Aligning with bio-psycho-social models of chronic pain [2, 8, 9, 30], a complex dynamic systems perspective draws upon theoretical principles from multiple pain disciplines to describe their mutual interactions [31].

Complex systems comprise many interacting components that collectively exhibit behaviors such as emergence, adaptation, and self-organization [32]. Numerous decentralized and simple interactions collectively generate complex behavior that cannot easily be predicted from the properties of individual parts. Biological ecosystems, social networks, and neural networks are typical examples of complex systems. Most complex systems are also dynamic systems, which specify precisely how components evolve and interact over time to show patterns of change, whether they reach a stable equilibrium, oscillate, or display chaotic patterns [11]. 'Attractor states' refer to an equilibrium around which a system tends to fluctuate and re-stabilize, reflecting either a healthy or disordered state [33]. Hysteresis, a hallmark of non-linear dynamic systems, means that a system's state depends not only on its current conditions but also on its history. Once an external input has pushed the system into a new state, removing that input does not necessarily restore the original state, because selfreinforcing interactions among the system's components stabilize the new state. Applied to pain, this means that once pain has become chronic, it can persist even when precipitating factors fall below the levels that originally triggered it. The pain system has, in a sense, "learned" pain, so removing the initial trigger is not sufficient to return to the previous state.

Viewing chronic pain through a complex dynamic systems lens highlights how factors operating at multiple levels -biological, psychological, interpersonal, and societal -interact and collectively give rise to emergent patterns of pain experience and behavior over time [12, 34]. Existing evidence indicates that subsystems at bio-psycho-social levels exist that resemble dynamical systems: From a neurobiological perspective, pain memories are formed through neurophysiological and biochemical plastic changes in associative learning mechanisms [35, 36]. The pain memory also shapes the perception of pain through expectations [37, 38]. Psychologically, pain is an emotional experience that activates fear and avoidance responses, guiding behavior to prevent harm [2, [39] [40] [41]. Socially, these behaviors can be reinforced or discouraged by interpersonal interactions and environmental contexts [42] [43] [44]. Over time, avoidance behaviors may interfere with valued daily life activities leading to disability and depressive symptoms, which in turn may amplify pain sensitivity and fear.

Theoretically, this could create feedback loops that maintain the system's organization around a stable, maladaptive attractor state reflecting chronicity [12, 45].

In contrast, resilient systems balance stability and flexibility [46, 47]. They are stable enough to absorb disturbances and reorganize around their attractor state after perturbations, yet flexible enough to transition and reorganize around a new attractor when conditions change [33, 47]. In the context of chronic pain, resilience might involve adaptive neuroplasticity, exploratory approach behavior, and supportive social interactions that enable recovery from pain episodes without becoming trapped in maladaptive cycles. Change, therefore, can be conceptualized as a transition from one stable state through a period of variability to a new, healthier stable state [48, 49]. In this way, a systems perspective advances our conceptual understanding of chronic pain and provides a foundation for identifying targets and pathways for effective intervention and recovery.

2. Network analysis approach

Network analysis offers a framework for modeling the interplay among many variables within complex and dynamic systems (for recent reviews see: Contreras et al., 2019; McNally, 2021; Robinaugh et al., 2020; Schemer et al., 2023). In this approach, components of the system, like biological markers, psychological symptoms, behaviors, or environmental factors are represented as nodes, and their associations as edges, forming a network structure (for an overview for mental health researchers see: Briganti et al., 2024). The earliest and simplest approach to construct a network is to observe and visualize connections between nodes. For instance, the London Underground map represents stations as nodes and lines as connections, and social networks depict individuals linked by their relationships. Based on the idea of such descriptive networks, other methods -such as perceived and inferential -were developed to construct networks.

Networks have the advantage that they do not rely on a priori distinctions between dependent and independent variables enabling one to capture conditional dependencies that can be interpreted as bidirectional and self-reinforcing relationships among multiple variables.

While other multivariate statistical methods share these features, what makes the network approach unique is its capacity to quantify local and global network properties, capturing system-level organization beyond the behavior of isolated components. [52, 53]. This systemsbased representation can generate hypotheses about the causal links between multiple variables, which can then be tested and potentially translated into within-person models.

Subsequently, we will introduce the three most common approaches to construct networks: cross-sectional networks, temporal networks, and perceived causal networks. We close with an overview of common network properties. To illustrate the approaches, we use a hypothetical running example from chronic pain, in which a sample of patients provides the cross-sectional data and one person from this sample is followed over time and reports their own perceived causal network. Each figure shows a different subset of the variables in this system.

Cross-sectional networks

From a complex dynamic systems perspective, cross-sectional networks represent how the equilibrium states of network components correlate with each other across persons (Ryan & Dablander, 2025; Fig. 1). They map the statistical associations among variables at a given point in time, most often using cross-sectional data aggregated across individuals. In these networks, connections reflect conditional co-occurring relationships [15]. Typically, connections between variables are represented as conditional associations, for instance, partial correlations if the data are multivariate normal [55]. Conversely, the absence of a connection between two nodes indicates that the corresponding variables are conditionally independent given the other variables in the network [56].

Cross-sectional networks provide a phenomenological snapshot of system organization, depicting how system components are interrelated and how they cluster within a defined context or population. For example, network models can be used to illustrate comorbidities [16] and some symptoms may be more closely related to each other, forming distinct clusters or communities within the network [57]. In the context of chronic pain, in a sample of patients, one cluster might include pain intensity, pain-related worry, and disability, whereas another (affective) cluster might comprise sadness and social withdrawal (Fig. 1). These clusters may be interconnected through bridge symptoms such as sleep problems, which link otherwise distinct communities [58].

By visualizing and quantifying such interrelations, cross-sectional networks can reveal connections, subcommunities, and how nodes are connected to other nodes in comparison to each other indicating their centrality to the network (for tutorials see Epskamp et al., 2018; Epskamp & Fried, 2018; Hevey, 2018). Cross-sectional network models have been estimated for various mental disorders, including Major Depression [22, 25], Posttraumatic Stress Disorder [20, 61], Social Anxiety Disorder [62], and Eating Disorders [63].

However, cross-sectional networks are limited in capturing system dynamics and supporting causal inference. Because they do not consider temporal precedence and conflate within-and between-person relations, inferences about within-person processes are valid only if the strong assumption of ergodicity is met. Ergodicity means that the same relations hold within every individual over time as in the group on average.

Temporal networks

Temporal networks extend this structural snapshot of how equilibrium states of system components co-occur across individuals by modeling how variables interact and influence one another over time within individuals aligning more closely to a complex, dynamical systems perspective. For example, we might find that, for one person in this sample, sleep problems are temporally preceded by avoidance and drinking alcohol, and that these in turn reinforce pain intensity and pain-related worry. This creates a vicious cycle where pain-related worry increases avoidance and drinking alcohol, which in turn exacerbate sleep problems (Fig. 2, Weeks 3-4). Rather than relying on cross-sectional data, temporal network models are based on longitudinal or time-series data collected from one or multiple individuals [17, 64, 65]. This allows researchers to capture temporal dependencies and dynamic feedback processes between components of the complex systems within individuals.

Whereas temporal network models can in principle take many shapes, the currently most commonly estimated model is the lag-1 vector autoregressive (VAR) model, consisting of cross-lagged and autoregressive relations [66] [67] [68]. In these networks, a directed edge (arrow) from one node to another indicates that variation in the first variable predicts variation in the second at the subsequent time point, controlling for all variables at the previous time point. These temporal associations do not establish definitive causation [69], but they can be interpreted within the framework of Granger causality, which assumes that causes precede their effects in time [70, 71]. However, more complicated models are also available for analyzing complex dynamics in intensive longitudinal data, such as non-linear processes [72], deterministic trends, variables' residual variances, and ordinal responses [73] [74] [75].

Because behavior and symptom dynamics often change across time, time-varying extensions of longitudinal network models have been proposed (Fig. 2 In contrast, time-varying VAR models relax this assumption by estimating a sequence of local models across successive time windows, resulting in time-varying networks [19, 77]. They capture gradual shifts in the strength or direction of associations and are particularly useful for examining within-person change processes, such as those occurring in response to interventions [19]. The example shows how stress at work initially feeds the vicious cycle by increasing drinking alcohol and sleep problems (Fig. 2, Weeks 1-2) and how providing exposure and emotion regulation changes the interconnections: exposure reduces avoidance, emotion regulation dampens drinking alcohol, sleep problems, and worry, and the paths from worry to avoidance and drinking alcohol disappear (Fig. 2, Weeks 5-6). Thus, time-varying network models descriptively track the local shape of the system as it transitions over time, but they cannot explain these transitions. Recent studies applying this approach to individuals with depressive symptoms have found evidence of substantial non-stationarity in most participants [53, 78, 79].

This temporal perspective provides insights into how stable or fluctuating patterns of functioning emerge, persist, or transition over time. Longitudinal-data networks have been applied to investigate the individual dynamics of Mood and Anxiety [67, 80, 81], Social Anxiety Disorder [82], Eating Disorders [83, 84], Posttraumatic Stress Disorder [85], and Personality Disorders [86]. By quantifying these dynamic interactions, temporal networks enable the study of system dynamics. They have the potential to explore how chronic maladaptive patterns of functioning self-organize and stabilize and to identify leverage points for intervention.

However, temporal networks are sensitive to the sampling rate [19, 76], and complex models such as time-varying models require large amounts of data to yield reliable estimates [87, 88]. Beyond these feasibility concerns, the complexity of interpretation further limits clinical implementation [53, 89, 90]. Perceived Causal Networks (PECANs) capture clients' personal beliefs about how the components of their system influence one another, thereby enriching case conceptualization and guiding clinical decision-making [91] [92] [93]. For example, the person in our example might notice that they experience more pain when they didn't sleep well, that pain intensity increases pain-related worry, and that worry leads to the decision to cancel their workout (i.e., avoidance; Fig. 3). Constructing a PECAN involves (1) identifying components such as pain intensity or sleep problems that are relevant to the individual and rating the perceived causal strength between them (e.g., "How strongly does component pain intensity cause sleep problems?"), and (2) visualizing these directed connections as a network, with symptoms as nodes and causal beliefs as edges of varying strength [94]. PECANs have demonstrated acceptable feasibility, and clinical usefulness in both adults and adolescents screening positive for depression [92, 95, 96]. However, findings regarding reliability are mixed [97, 98]. Because the connections in a PECAN are not tied to a specific time scale, they can reflect short-and long-term relations alike. However, this also means that the time scale of each connection remains unspecified unless it is explicitly assessed. To reduce retrospective bias and overestimation of causality, recent studies have extended this method to daily assessments of perceived causal relations [99]. Network Properties

A central feature of network analysis consistent with systems thinking is that complex patterns of behavior emerge from simple local interactions, as captured in the edges of crosssectional and temporal network models. These emergent dynamics illustrate how interconnected components collectively shape system-level functioning in ways that cannot be inferred from individual elements in isolation. They can be described at three levels: the topology of the network, the state of the network in terms of node activation, and the trajectories of system dynamics [56]. These levels can dissociate. Individuals with chronic pain may share a similar network topology yet differ substantially in node activation, which may be the more clinically meaningful variable. So-called network properties summarize some of these system-level aspects, primarily the topology, at both local and global levels.

Whereas local network properties summarize the position and connectivity of individual nodes within the network, global properties describe the organization of the network as a whole.

Local network properties comprise node predictability (proportion of variance of one node predicted by the other nodes of the network; [100] and centrality measures (identifying and quantifying critical positions within the network; [101]. In cross-sectional networks, strength centrality reflects the weighted sum of all edges connected to a node. For example, sleep problems are strongly connected to pain-related worry and sadness, and more weakly connected to pain intensity, disability, and social withdrawal, bridging the pain and affective clusters and making it a central node overall (Fig. 1). In temporal networks, InStrength and OutStrength centrality represent the weighted sums of all incoming and outgoing edges, respectively [102]. At the individual level, it becomes visible that sleep problems predict pain intensity and pain-related worry via strong outgoing edges, making it a node with high OutStrength (Fig. 2, Weeks 3-4). Pain intensity, in contrast, is predicted by sleep problems and pain-related worry but predicts only worry in return. It therefore receives more influence than it sends, meaning it has high InStrength relative to its OutStrength (Fig. 2, Weeks 3-4).

In non-statistical networks such as social networks or transportation systems, centrality more clearly identifies key components, for instance, the family member who coordinates communication among otherwise disconnected relatives, or the major station connecting multiple lines in the London Underground [102]. Relatedly, it has been hypothesized that targeting highly central nodes in psychological pain treatment could reduce overall symptom burden because influencing one central symptom might indirectly affect its connected symptoms [103]. Consequently, centrality measures have been proposed indicators for potential treatment targets [62, [104] [105] [106]. In the example of the temporal network above, we could conclude that a change in sleep problems might lead to changes in several other nodes in the network (Fig. 2, Weeks 3-4). However, their suitability for this role is still being debated, as statistical centrality has a weak correlation with causal effects in cross-sectional data [102, 107, 108].

Global network properties summarize features of the entire system, such as network predictability (mean average node predictability of a network; [109] and network density (proportion of the actual versus the possible number of edges; [110, 111]. Network predictability indicates the average proportion of variance explained by the nodes of the network. For example, if the person's symptoms were highly predictable from each other, sleep problems, pain, worry, and avoidance would largely sustain one another, and external factors such as stress at work would add little. Low predictability would indicate that these symptoms fluctuate mainly in response to influences outside the network (Fig. 2, Weeks 1-2).

Density, in turn, decreases from Weeks 3-4 to Weeks 5-6 as the interventions weaken the connections among the symptoms (Fig. 2).

Existing validity concerns and empirical inconsistencies warrant further investigation [56]. Nevertheless, network analysis may provide valuable insights into system-level processes of stability and change. It is a promising step to match our methods more closely to the complexity of our world. In our examples, the cross-sectional network showed that sleep problems might bridge pain and affective symptoms (Fig. 1). The temporal network of one person revealed that avoidance and drinking alcohol predicted the sleep problems, which in turn fed pain intensity and pain-related worry, shedding more light on the individual mechanisms driving pain intensity (Fig. 2). The person's own perception (Fig. 3) did not include the role of drinking alcohol but would have been sufficient to indicate a vicious cycle between sleep problems, pain intensity, pain-related worry, and avoidance. In the context of chronic pain, networks may help clarify mechanisms underlying the transition from acute to chronic pain, as well as individual differences in treatment response and symptom persistence.

3. Network analyses in chronic pain research: a review

Network reviews exist for many disorders [21, 24, 56, [112] [113] [114]. However, to the best of our knowledge this is the first narrative review of the network approach in chronic pain research. We conducted a search of multiple academic databases, including PubMed, APA PsycInfo, PSYNDEX, and MEDLINE (last update 30th of June 2026). The search strategy was informed by the conditions of chronic primary pain defined by the ICD-11 [1], which encompasses fibromyalgia, complex regional pain syndrome, chronic primary headache, orofacial pain, visceral pain, and musculoskeletal pain. Additionally, the terms "chronic primary pain" and "chronic secondary pain" were included in the search criteria. These chronic pain-related terms were systematically combined with keywords related to network analysis methodologies, including "network analysis," "Bayesian network analysis," and "idiographic network." The database searches were followed by a forward and backward citation search to identify additional relevant articles.

Our search initially returned 7,669 articles. Following deduplication and preliminary screening, 2,793 articles underwent detailed review of title, abstract and partly full text, from which 54 studies were included in this review. The first study was published in 2017 [115], and the most recent in November, 2025 [116, 117], highlighting that the network approach has only recently been introduced into the field of chronic pain (Table S1). The reviewed studies can be grouped according to their objectives. Cross-sectional networks aimed to describe network structure, compare networks across groups, examine comorbidity patterns, changes across time, and intervention-related changes. Temporal network models aim to capture how symptoms, cognitions, emotions, and behaviors predict one another over time, within and across individuals, in order to identify the dynamic processes that maintain or alleviate chronic pain.

Cross-sectional networks

Variable interactions within conditions

Studies in this category focused on how variables within pain-related systems are interconnected. Twenty-four studies analyzed existing datasets to explore the pattern and strength of interactions among pain and other symptoms or factors within a cross-sectional network model. Arthritis [118], chronic pain in general [115, 119], depression and cognitive impairments [120, 121], fibromyalgia [122] [123] [124], genital pain [125, 126], neck pain [127], nociplastic pain [128], rheumatic, osteoarthritis-related and musculoskeletal pain [129] [130] [131] [132], and samples with other pain-related measures [116, [133] [134] [135] [136] [137] [138] [139]. Two studies used network parameters to predict clinically relevant outcomes, including the intensity and frequency of genital pain [126] and long-term work ability [139]. Interpretation of these findings is limited by the fact that variable selection in the reviewed studies was largely driven by data availability rather than theory. As a result, there was substantial variability across studies in which variables were included, making it difficult to draw generalizable conclusions about overall network structure or specific variable interactions. Consequently, findings from single group-level networks based on atheoretical variable sets should be interpreted with caution.

Comorbidity patterns

These studies specifically focused on the interactions among symptoms from different disorders, aiming to provide insights into how comorbid conditions co-occur and influence one another. Ten studies applied the network approach to investigate comorbidity patterns. [144] [145] [146], sleep problems [147], and Subjective-Based Bruxism [148]. Three studies found that sleep problems were associated with pain [147], connecting symptoms of pain with Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder [146] and symptoms of Anxiety and Depression [140]. This was the only recurring pattern across studies. It highlights the potential relevance of sleep problems in comorbidity and the variability of identified cross-condition interactions. This variability is further compounded by the fact that all estimated relationships and network properties, including bridge centrality, depend on the specific set of variables included in the network. As a result, and given the heterogeneity in variable selection across studies, it remains difficult to interpret what these estimated interactions between conditions represent and how they should be understood in relation to underlying mechanisms.

Group comparisons

The sixteen studies grouped under this heading examined whether and how network structures differ between distinct groups. Studies compared networks of groups with acute and chronic pain [149], different levels of pain [150, 151], different patterns of pain resilience [152], different pain trajectories [153], different subtypes of Vulvodynia [125], women living with and at risk for Human Immunodeficiency Virus [154], across racial groups [119], employees in different institutions [139], symptomatic versus non-symptomatic individuals [134, 135, 147, 155, 156], and receiving treatment versus receiving advice [157, 158]. Most studies focused on node-level or edge-level differences between groups. Across studies, group differences were most observed in the strength and configuration of relationships, as well as in the centrality of specific variables, whereas differences in the mere presence or absence of associations were less consistently reported. Only a small number of studies reported statistical differences in connectivity. Overall, however, the specific patterns of group differences varied considerably across studies, with no consistent set of relationships or central variables emerging. Interpretation of these findings is further constrained by heterogeneity in both variable selection and the choice of comparison groups, limiting the extent to which results can be generalized across populations.

Network changes across time within groups

This line of research investigated whether the structure of symptom networks changes across different time points or periods within the same group, thereby assessing temporal stability or reorganization of networks. Three studies examined differences in networks of the same group across multiple time points: At baseline and two-year follow-up [116], across five consecutive time points during physiotherapy [158], prior to surgery, three and six weeks post-surgery, and six and twelve months post-surgery [159]. The latter two studies adopted a dynamic perspective in principle; however, rather than modeling individual-level changes over time, they aggregated data at the group level and conducted cross-sectional analyses at each time point.

Network changes in response to interventions

Four studies explicitly examined how therapeutic interventions perturb symptom networks over time, asking whether and how network structure reorganizes in response to treatment. Åkerblom and colleagues [160] compared networks prior to and after therapy.

Another study compared "early changes" during the intervention period with "late changes" during the follow-up period using a Bayesian model [157]. Another study used Mixed Graphical Models for a Network Intervention Analysis to explore the effects of a digital intervention for the prevention of depression in patients with chronic back pain at the symptom level [161]. Both interventions were accompanied by changes in network structure, suggesting a reorganization of symptom interactions over the course of treatment. A third study examined the interactions among chronic pain-related outcomes in adults undergoing complementary and integrative health and/or standard rehabilitative care interventions [162].

They found that sleep-related impairment, pain interference, physical function, and pain intensity exhibited the highest out-expected influence centrality in the cross-lagged panel network model. However, because other potentially relevant factors were not included in the models, the extent to which these changes can be causally attributed to the interventions remains unclear.

Temporal networks

Studies in this category examined temporal connections using multilevel and individuallevel network analyses. Multilevel studies modeled how pain-related variables predict one another over days to months. Carvalho et al. [163] followed women with endometriosis over six and twelve months and found that pain intensity and pain impact predicted subsequent increases in cognitive fusion, depression, and somatization, suggesting a directional effect of physical symptoms on psychological processes, while contemporaneous associations indicated that these changes occurred in clusters of intercorrelated symptoms. Using diary data collected twice daily over 14 days in adolescents with chronic pain, Beeckman et al. [164] found that afternoon pain-related fear and negative affect predicted subsequent increases in pain, catastrophizing, and activity avoidance, whereas activity engagement was predicted by positive affect and prior engagement. Psychological flexibility was associated with less avoidance and buffered the impact of pain on subsequent engagement. In an ecological momentary assessment study of adults with chronic noncancer pain, Tang et al. [165] showed that increases in mental defeat predicted subsequent increases in attention to pain and perceived pain impact, which in turn predicted lower physical activity. Nadinda et al. [166] found that at the group level the strongest contemporaneous connection was between fear and pain and the strongest temporal connection was between attention and pain.

Three studies modeled individuals separately. Sanabria-Mazo et al. [167] applied Group Iterative Multiple Model Estimation (GIMME) to six participants with chronic low back pain receiving Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT). Individual networks varied considerably. Nadinda et al. [166] also found wide variation in network structure across the individual graphical vector autoregressive models of 30 participants. Lavefjord et al. [168] tested the centrality hypothesis in a single-case experimental design with six participants by comparing interventions guided by the most and least central nodes. Although four participants improved overall, differences between interventions emerged in only three and were unrelated to centrality.

As with cross-sectional studies, these findings are limited, based on heterogeneous samples and cannot be generalized directly. However, the considerable variation across individual networks suggests that differences in dynamics between persons are the rule rather than the exception, which supports the individual as the appropriate level of analysis [166].

Summary

Eighty-nine percent of the studies relied on cross-sectional, static network models, providing snapshots of variable interactions, comorbidity patterns, and group differences. The quality of the studies appeared to improve over time, as reflected in the Table S1 in the Supplemental Material by more comprehensive reporting of key methodological details (e.g., model estimation, stability analyses, and visualization). In particular, an increasing number of studies adhered to established reporting standards for psychological network analyses in cross-sectional networks [169]. However, their interpretability is limited by small sample sizes, data-driven rather than theory-driven node selection, a reliance on self-report data, and limited replicability, as results are contingent on the specific set of variables included, which varies substantially across studies. The predominance of cross-sectional designs limits the ability of the network models used in this literature to capture temporal dynamics and to support causal interpretations of interactions. This highlights the need for longitudinal, temporally resolved approaches, which are currently reflected in only six studies.

Collectively, these limitations reveal a gap between current empirical practice and the complex, dynamic systems perspective that motivated this research. Closing this gap will require theory-driven, multi-level studies with intensive longitudinal designs that provide the time-series data needed to recover system dynamics. Beyond statistical modeling, it will also require building formal dynamical systems models that turn the conceptual framework proposed here into testable theory.

4. Moving forward

This review introduced complex dynamic systems as a unifying framework for chronic pain and examined the network approach as a method for operationalizing it. Across the reviewed studies, network models have provided initial evidence that symptoms and related factors form interconnected systems, capturing patterns of comorbidity, group differences, and temporal variations within persons. In particular, cross-sectional network analyses have offered insights into the overall structure of associations and may reflect longer-term or aggregated relationships that are difficult to capture in short-term within-person designs. At the same time, the findings highlight a substantial gap between the complex, dynamic systems perspective that motivates the use of network models and current empirical practices, which remain largely cross-sectional and group-based and are therefore limited in their ability to capture temporal dynamics and system-level processes.

In this section, we discuss how different network approaches align, or fail to align, with the system-level questions they are intended to address and argue that progress will depend on integrating multiple methodological approaches. This provides a basis for evaluating current practices and for identifying directions in which network research on chronic pain needs to evolve.

Current practice and limitations

The existing literature has provided several initial insights: First, across studies, symptoms and related factors consistently appear to form interconnected systems, supporting the idea that chronic pain and comorbid conditions can be conceptualized as networked phenomena rather than isolated variables in line with biopsychosocial and fear-avoidance models [9, 13, 14]. Second, some studies suggest that aspects of network structure may show a degree of stability or replicability when similar constructs are assessed across comparable populations, although this remains understudied and methodologically constrained (e.g., van der Wijk et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2023), although this remains understudied and methodologically constrained.

At the same time, the dominant reliance on cross-sectional designs means that most findings reflect aggregated or stable associations rather than within-person dynamics. This limitation was widely acknowledged by the reviewed studies themselves, with many explicitly calling for longitudinal designs and stronger causal inference in their limitations or future directions sections [118] [119] [120] [125] [126] [127] [128] 130, [132] [133] [134] [135] [136] 138, [140] [141] [142] [143] 145, 147, [151] [152] [153] [154] [155] [156] 159, 161, 163, 166]. Although group-level networks can describe population-level structure and system behavior, intervention necessarily unfolds at the individual level. Inference from cross-sectional networks about within-person systems is only valid under strong assumptions [170, 171]. A small number of studies have incorporated longitudinal designs or moved toward within-person modeling of symptom dynamics [158, 159, 161, 163, 164, 166], whereas the first studies of fully temporal network approaches have only recently begun to be published [166, 167, 172]. Advancing the field will therefore require a combination of approaches, including intensive longitudinal designs and other complementary methodologies that can better capture temporal dynamics while building on cross-sectional findings where appropriate.

Moreover, because network structures depend strongly on the selected nodes, measurement instruments, and sampled populations, variability across studies remains substantial, making it difficult to infer generalizable patterns of specific interactions. This limitation also concerns the interpretation of network properties. Although network analysis is often motivated by its ability to characterize system-level organization, most studies relied primarily on node strength centrality, with only occasional reporting of other centrality measures. These metrics were rarely interpreted in substantive terms, consistent with broader critiques of centrality in statistical networks [56, 102]. Whereas centrality is intuitive in observable networks such as transport or social networks, this intuition does not translate directly to statistical networks, where centrality primarily reflects the strength of conditional associations rather than causal importance [102]. As a result, the clinical or theoretical relevance of commonly reported network properties remains unclear.

Finally, despite the original promise of network models as clinically informative tools, only one of the reviewed studies have yet implemented them in routine clinical practice [168].

This gap is consistent with the early developmental stage of the methodology [166, 168].

Nevertheless, several authors have proposed potential applications, including monitoring intervention effects on symptom networks, supporting clinical decision-making, and identifying subgroups with distinct symptom dynamics [121, 127, 150, 158]. Åkerblom et al. [160] emphasize that a theoretically guided, ideographic approach to translational application is necessary to better capture individual treatment processes.

Aligning network methods with system-level questions

From a complex dynamic systems perspective, the central questions concern how systems behave, how patterns emerge from interactions, and how systems change over time rather than the functioning of isolated components. Applied to chronic pain, this perspective raises a set of core questions: What defines a pain system? How do biological, psychological and social components interact within a pain system in a particular context? How do these interactions give rise to emergent patterns, such as the transition from acute to chronic pain? Under what conditions can the system reorganize toward a more resilient and functional state, and what characterizes resilience in the context of recurrent pain and perturbation?

To answer the first question, we propose the following definition: A pain system, in the complex dynamic systems sense [11, 29], consists of (1) a set of biological, psychological, and social components [9, 13], (2) the interactions among them, such as feedback loops and causal pathways, and (3) a function, understood in the cybernetic rather than the intentional sense, as the state or pattern that the organization of these interactions tends to maintain [11]. In acute pain, this function is protection, that is, detecting threat and motivating behavior that prevents harm. In chronic pain, the system continues to operate in this protective mode but in a miscalibrated way, for example through persistent threat appraisal, avoidance, and heightened vigilance, which in turn stabilize the pain state [2]. Because of these interactions, the system shows emergent properties such as the pain experience, which cannot be reduced to any single component, and can exhibit self-organization and path-dependent transitions (hysteresis).

A pain system, in the complex dynamic systems sense, is defined not by its constituent parts alone but by the nature of their interactions. Following von Bertalanffy's systems theory and Engel's biopsychosocial model, a system requires four elements: (1) a set of components (biological, psychological, social factors); (2) interactions between the components (feedback loops, causal pathways); (3) discernible boundaries (what counts as 'the system' versus its environment); and (4) emergent properties (pain experience itself, which cannot be reduced to nociception, cognition, or social context in isolation) [13, 29]. Pain systems are also adaptive, capable of self-organization, learning, and path-dependent state transitions (hysteresis). This distinguishes them from linear biomedical models (such as the fear-avoidance model) and positions chronic pain as an emergent attractor state within a multi-level network of interacting factors.

Addressing the other questions requires a shift in the level of analysis. Chronic pain systems are embedded within individuals, and the processes of interest, interaction, emergence, stabilization, and change unfold over time within persons [26]. Accordingly, the individual represents the primary level of analysis for answering key system questions [173].

Studying individuals over time allows for the identification of within-person patterns that may subsequently replicate across individuals, thereby revealing both commonalities and meaningful heterogeneity. This logic has important implications for the choice and interpretation of network models used to study chronic pain.

Toward individual-level, temporal data

Crucially, different network approaches address fundamentally different system questions, yet these distinctions are often blurred in empirical practice. Cross-sectional networks describe how a selected set of components is statistically related within a specific group under the constraints of a given model [107]. Temporal networks, in contrast, are designed to elucidate how interactions between system components unfold and change over time or in response to intervention [17, 56]. Because clinical research and practice are ultimately concerned with how systems change, these temporal approaches are most directly aligned with system-level questions of intervention, resilience, and recovery. Starting at the individual level and building evidence through replication may ultimately allow for generalization and the identification of meaningful subgroups [173].

A central methodological challenge in longitudinal network research concerns the appropriate time scale of measurement. System components may operate on different temporal rhythms, and mismatches between item reference period, assessment frequency and process dynamics can obscure meaningful relationships [19, 76]. For example, constructs such as pain-related fear, avoidance behavior, or affect may fluctuate more rapidly than typical daily assessment protocols capture [164]. Hybrid multi-time ecological momentary assessments (EMA), combining fixed, random, and event-contingent prompts in the same protocol might be considered to capture this intraindividual variability. Another related challenge is the large amount of data required for reliable estimates [87]. Finally, commonly used vector autoregressive (VAR) models cannot represent central concepts of dynamical systems theory, such as transitions between multiple stable states or hysteresis, because they assume linear, stationary dynamics around a single equilibrium [52]. Future studies should therefore align measurement schedules, power considerations, and network models with theoretically expected process dynamics.

Perceived causal networks can complement temporal models by representing causal relations across different time scales while avoiding power requests [172, 174]. They can capture how patients and therapists understand chronic pain at the individual-level. In addition, these networks are independent of a particular assessment frequency, enabling reflection on short-and long-term connections [92, 93, 172]. Thus, they can inform case conceptualization by representing beliefs about causal relationships [93, 174].

Capturing more of the system: metrics and data sources

Future work should also move beyond a narrow focus on traditional centrality metrics toward the development and validation of network properties that better capture system-level dynamics. Of particular clinical relevance is the potential to detect early warning signals, such as rising variance and increasing temporal autocorrelation in symptom dynamics, that may precede critical transitions from acute to chronic disabling pain, offering a window for timely intervention before the system settles into a maladaptive stable state [175] [176] [177]. Sufficient intensive longitudinal data that can be assessed using continuous data sources other than selfreport such as accelerometer data, heart rate variability are a precondition to estimating early warning signals of critical transitions. However, empirical support for early warning signals based on critical slowing down in clinical psychology remains limited [33]. Whether these signals occur depends strongly on the specifics of the system, contrary to their reputation as generic indicators, and they are highly sensitive to noise, which may limit their practical utility [178].

From a measurement perspective, reliance on self-report should not be viewed as a weakness per se as stated by many of the reviewed studies. Instead, self-report remains essential for assessing subjective experiences such as pain, emotions, and cognitions at the psychological level of the system. However, integrating complementary data sources, such as behavioral, physiological, and social measures [157, 179, 180], can help capture additional system layers and intensive longitudinal data with less measurement burden [147, 166].

Wearables integrated into studies with intensive longitudinal data collection may provide valuable information that is difficult to obtain through self-reporting, such as motion and exploratory behavior [181].

Complementary methods for modeling complex systems

Finally, although network analysis provides a powerful framework for modeling multivariate systems, it is not the only approach consistent with system thinking. Methods such as Directed Acyclic Graphs, multilevel Hidden Markov Models, Equilibrium Causal Models or Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling may offer complementary insights, particularly for theory building [54, 73, [182] [183] [184]. Across methods, a shared challenge remains the reliance on linear assumptions, which may inadequately capture non-linear or chaotic system behavior. Addressing this limitation will be critical for advancing explanatory and clinically meaningful models of chronic pain.

In addition, qualitative measures are needed to provide meaning and context (De Smet et al., 2025). For instance, in chronic pain research, a network topic modelling approach was employed to examine well-being practices among adults experiencing chronic pain (Cintron et al., 2025), while in psychotherapy research, Large Language Models have been utilised to generate personalised networks based on therapy sessions (Ong et al., 2025). Importantly, measurement strategies should be matched to the system level under investigation rather than ranked by perceived objectivity.

Conclusion

Viewing chronic pain as a complex and dynamic system offers a powerful conceptual framework for integrating biological, psychological, and social processes and for understanding how persistent pain emerges, stabilizes, and changes over time. Network analysis holds considerable promise as a methodological approach to operationalize this perspective by modeling multivariate interactions and system-level organization. This review shows that current applications have begun to capture aspects of this complexity but remain limited in representing dynamic, individual-level processes. Closing this gap will require theory-driven, multi-level, and longitudinal research designs that align network methods more explicitly with the system-level questions they are meant to address. Starting from individuallevel models and accumulating evidence across persons and studies may reveal recurring system structures and clinically meaningful subgroups [185, 186], and ultimately support parsimonious, empirically grounded models of the key mechanisms and interactions that explain the emergence and maintenance of chronic pain [27]. Such models could inform interventions that target not only symptoms but the dynamic processes that sustain them, supporting more precise and effective treatment.
