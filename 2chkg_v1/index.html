To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a highly prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by clinical symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity. These symptoms are often accompanied by social stigmatization, including negative descriptors, animalistic characterizations, emotional reactions of anger, and desired social distance from individuals with ADHD. Stigmatization can negatively impact diagnosis, treatment, and behavioural functioning. This study investigates how diagnostic labels and the gender of individuals with ADHD influence stigma-related responses. Specifically, it examines whether the presence of an ADHD diagnostic label reduces or exacerbates stigma and how gender affects these perceptions. Utilizing a 2x3 factorial design, participants were presented with vignettes describing a 12-year-old child with ADHD behaviours. These vignettes varied by diagnostic label (Label vs No Label) and gender (Female, Male, and Gender-Neutral). Stigma was assessed through four outcome variables: positive-negative attitudes, humanistic-animalistic attitudes, emotional reactions, and social distance. Findings indicate that the presence of a diagnostic label significantly reduced negative attitudes, anger, and social distance. Gender effects were observed but were not statistically significant. Notably, the female vignette condition showed the strongest trends, suggesting that a diagnostic label may be particularly beneficial for females, who often go underdiagnosed. These findings highlight the potential of diagnostic labels to reduce stigma and promote social acceptance.

The Effect of an ADHD Diagnostic Label: Stigmatizing or Protective? Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that is characterized by clinical symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity (American Psychological Association [APA], 2013). Typical manifestations of inattention include difficulty concentrating, disorganization, and forgetfulness (American Psychological Association [APA], 2013). Hyperactivity and impulsivity are often expressed through excessive talking, fidgeting, and a tendency to become easily frustrated. ADHD has an approximate worldwide prevalence rate of 5-9% of children and 3-5% of adults, making it one of the most widespread and debilitating childhood disorders. In Canada, approximately 1 in 21 individuals, or about 1.8 million Canadians, have ADHD (CAMH, 2024). According to the Diagnostic Statistic Manual (DSM-5), an ADHD diagnosis requires that symptoms are exhibited across multiple contexts, such as home, school or work, and social settings, leading to significant challenges in many facets of an individual's life (American Psychological Association [APA], 2013). Despite ADHD's high prevalence, diagnosis rates are three to four times more common in males than females (CADDAC, 2024). Females with ADHD are more likely to present predominantly inattentive symptoms, contributing to the underdiagnosis and misperception of ADHD in females (Rucklidge, 2008). This disparity points to the influence of societal and clinical biases in identifying ADHD across genders. Examining how ADHD is perceived across genders may reveal biases that impact diagnosis and treatment.

For individuals with ADHD, navigating a world that often misunderstands their behaviour and labels them as "troublemakers" can feel isolating and disheartening (Wilens & Spencer, 2010).

Living with ADHD often entails facing different perceptions from others due to the disorder's symptoms, frequently leading to associated social stigma and isolation. Goffman (1963) defined stigma as a social identity that is devalued within a given social context. In ADHD, common stigmatizing characterizations include negative descriptors such as "foolish," "moody," or "pessimistic," and even animalistic terms like "wild" or "untamed" (Meza et al., 2019). These characteristics can evoke emotional reactions of anger in others and contribute to desired social distance from individuals with ADHD (Mueller et al., 2012). Such reactions affect the experiences and self-perception of those impacted, often leading to cognitive and social impairments, observed through broader social adjustment issues, fewer acquaintances, and relationship challenges (Godfrey et al., 2021).

Given how they are perceived, the social lives of individuals with ADHD often differ from individuals without ADHD. These perceptions can be shaped by their ADHD-related behaviours, the diagnostic label itself, or a combination of both (Law et al., 2007). While psychiatric labels, particularly for children, raise concerns about amplifying stigma, they can also validate experiences, enable treatment access, and provide relief (Ohan et al., 2011). Existing literature remains inconclusive on whether an ADHD diagnostic label provides protective benefits or exacerbates stigma, with studies showing mixed results (Werkhoven et al., 2022; Law et al., 2007; Hoza et al., 2005).

This study investigates three factors that may contribute to the stigmatization of ADHD: ADHD-related behaviours, the diagnostic label, and the impact of gender on these perceptions.

Specifically, it will aim to determine whether ADHD behaviours, alone or in conjunction with a diagnostic label, reduce or exacerbate stigma and how the child's gender influences peer perceptions. Prior to presenting my study methods, this paper will first provide a general overview of stigmatization and the role of diagnostic labels in ADHD. Following this, I will examine previous empirical findings on how diagnostic labels may contribute to or alleviate stigma. Finally, I will analyze how these effects may vary based on gender. Understanding these dynamics may help identify specific contributors to stigmatization and inform approaches to lessen its impact on individuals with ADHD.

Understanding stigmatization and diagnostic labels in ADHD

Stigmatization is a pervasive barrier to mental health care, with significant implications for individuals with ADHD. The World Health Organization describes the stigma of mental health disorders as "the most important barrier" to accessing mental health care (WHO, 2001, p. 98). In the context of ADHD, stigma is often underestimated as a risk factor but has far-reaching effects, influencing treatment adherence, severity of symptoms, life satisfaction, and overall well-being (Mueller et al., 2012). A recent study highlights how stigmatization deters families from pursuing necessary support for ADHD (Schoeman & Voges, 2022). The barriers outlined include instrumental (practical and logistical), attitudinal (internal beliefs or misconceptions that discourage help-seeking), and stigma-related barriers (external or social pressures; Schoeman & Voges, 2022). Although instrumental barriers had the most significant impact on accessing care, the attitudinal and stigma-related barriers were found to significantly affect the well-being of children with ADHD and their families (Schoeman & Voges, 2022). The most frequently reported stigma-related barrier was the concern that the individual might be seen as weak for the diagnosis of a mental health disorder (Schoeman & Voges, 2022). These findings stress the impact of stigmatization by highlighting how perceptions of ADHD can shape access to care and, ultimately, the well-being of individuals with the disorder.

One critical factor in these perceptions is the role of diagnostic labels. In therapeutic settings, diagnostic labels can facilitate patients' self-understanding and help shape treatment plans by turning complex behavioural issues into defined recognizable traits (Werkhoven et al., 2022).

However, Werkhoven and colleagues pointed out that labels can also be misused or overly reductionistic to the individual by interpreting all aspects of their behaviour through the lens of the diagnosis. Beyond a therapeutic context, diagnostic labels play a role in society by streamlining communication about specific behavioural needs or enabling individuals to access accommodations more readily without repeatedly explaining their condition (Werkhoven et al., 2022). That said, the effectiveness of diagnostic labels depends on a shared societal understanding.

Labels can also contribute to ADHD-related stigmatization by framing certain behaviours as negative social identities or reinforcing stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination. In this way, they may perpetuate harmful assumptions and social exclusion. Alternatively, labels may help reduce stigma by reshaping perceptions (Werkhoven et al., 2022). For instance, knowing a child has an ADHD diagnosis may encourage a more empathetic response. Considering the mixed impact of diagnostic labels, it is crucial to examine whether they ultimately serve a protective function or contribute to stigmatization.

Diagnostic labels as a stigmatizing factor

The existing literature demonstrates these conflicting perspectives on how society perceives and interprets diagnostic labels. For instance, a study by Hoza et al. (2005) investigated how children with ADHD are perceived by their peers and the effects on their social experiences.

The study included 165 children with ADHD (130 boys and 35 girls) and 1,298 classmates who served as peer raters. Peer evaluations involved positive (e.g., best friends) nominations, negative (e.g., avoid as a friend) nominations, and a 5-point "liking" scale to identify key measures such as social preference, social impact, and reciprocated friendships (Hoza et al., 2005). In line with their predictions, children with ADHD were rated lower in social preference, higher in negative social impact, had fewer reciprocated friendships, and were more frequently classified as "rejected" by peers compared to non-ADHD children (Hoza et al., 2005). Due to the heavily skewed gender sample, the study found no significant interaction between ADHD status and gender. However, girls with ADHD tended to receive slightly better peer ratings compared to boys with ADHD, suggesting some influence of gender (Hoza et al., 2005). These findings emphasize the significant social difficulties faced by children with ADHD and the likelihood of experiencing stigma.

Following this study, Ohan et al. (2011) examined whether parents display greater stigmatization toward children with ADHD or depression when a diagnostic label is added.

Parents' attitudes are particularly impactful as they shape their children's social environments, influence friendships, and play a crucial role in forming children's perceptions of mental illness (Ohan et al., 2011). In this study, 225 parents rated their levels of stereotypes, prejudice, and social distance toward vignettes depicting typical behaviour, ADHD or depression symptoms, or the same symptoms with an added diagnostic label. The results were consistent with Hoza et al. (2005) findings in that parents held substantial stigma toward children with ADHD symptoms alone, and more importantly, that adding a label led to slight but statistically significant increase in stigmatization (Ohan et al., 2011).

Diagnostic label as a protective factor

Some studies suggest that diagnostic labels alone may not elicit greater stigmatizing responses. For example, a study conducted by Law et al. (2007) investigated whether labelling a child with ADHD affects peer attitudes and behavioural intentions. In this study, 120 children read one of the three vignettes that describe a gender-neutral peer displaying ADHD symptoms, with variations in the inclusion of an official diagnosis to assess the label's impact (Law et al., 2007).

After reading the respective vignettes, participants completed a self-report questionnaire to gauge attitudes through adjectives and their behavioural intentions in academic, social, and general activities with the character (Law et al., 2007). The findings found that eighty-five percent of participants perceived the vignette character as male and mainly held negative attitudes toward him, regardless of diagnostic labelling. Analyses indicated that including a diagnostic label did not have any additional effects on participants' attitudes toward the peer. This finding suggests that behavioural descriptions alone were sufficient to elicit negative perceptions and behavioural intentions. The perceived male gender implies that boys displaying attentional and externalizing behaviours may be particularly susceptible to negative peer attitudes. However, the study revealed no statistically significant differences in participant attitudes based on their gender, contrasting with findings from Hoza et al. (2005) and other existing studies from the literature such as Cohen et al. (1997), which suggest that males generally report less favourable views.

Similarly, a study by Meza et al. (2019) examined whether children with ADHD face stigmatization due to their behaviour, the presence of a diagnostic label, or both. Participants were assigned randomly to read one of the three vignette descriptions of a boy: (1) no apparent symptoms, (2) behavioural symptoms of ADHD, or (3) behavioural symptoms with a diagnostic label (Meza et al., 2019). Participants evaluated attitudes using adjectives, humanizing and dehumanizing descriptors, and anger levels (Meza et al., 2019). Social distance was assessed on a five-point scale measuring participants' willingness to engage in various social interactions with the character, such as forming friendships or working together. Higher scores indicated a greater desire for social distance (Meza et al., 2019).

While Law et al. (2007) found no significant differences in attitudes between labelled and non-labelled conditions, Meza et al. (2019) revealed that diagnostic labels could act as a protective factor. Participants shown only behavioural descriptions without a diagnostic label exhibited stronger stigmatizing attitudes against the character in the vignette, including a greater desire for social distance and an association of the child with animalistic and negative descriptors (Meza et al., 2019). In contrast, when a diagnostic label accompanied ADHD-related behaviours, participant's stigmatizing attitudes were reduced (Meza et al., 2019). These results offer potential reassurance for parents of children with chronic psychiatric conditions that diagnostic labels may help mitigate social stigma and improve social perceptions. However, because the Meza et al.

study relied solely on a male vignette, it leaves the influence of gender on ADHD-related stigma unexamined. This is a crucial gap, as societal perceptions of ADHD symptoms and diagnostic labels may differ between genders.

The impact of gender on stigmatizing perceptions of ADHD

Although many studies have investigated the psychological and social impact of ADHD, few studies have examined gender as a moderating factor. An empirical research review by Rucklidge (2008) consistently shows that females who are diagnosed with ADHD are more likely than males to receive a predominantly inattentive diagnosis, contributing to the underidentification of ADHD in females. This underdiagnosis not only reflects societal and clinical biases but also suggests that previous literature on ADHD and gender may not have generalizable samples due to a disproportional inclusion of males or an oversight of females who present with less overt symptoms. Rucklidge (2008) found that while girls with ADHD tend to display less physical aggression than boys, they often exhibit higher levels of verbal aggression, such as teasing or taunting through name-calling, compared to girls without ADHD (Rucklidge, 2008). These behaviours likely contribute to increased peer rejection. Additionally, females with ADHD often present lower self-efficacy, more ineffective coping strategies, weaker social support networks, and less confidence within relationships (Rucklidge, 2008). These factors may significantly influence peer perceptions of individuals diagnosed with ADHD. These gender differences in social functioning are compounded by gender-specific experiences of ADHD-related stigma (Visser et al., 2024). Specifically, Visser et al. (2024) conducted a focus group discussion (FGD) study of young adults with ADHD, parents, mental health professionals, and primary school teachers. They found significant gender differences in how stigma is experienced and internalized (Visser et al., 2024). Young women with ADHD reported encountering skepticism from others, delays in receiving a diagnosis, and challenges in developing a stable sense of identity. In contrast, young men reported frequently facing rejection, internalizing negative stereotypes, and exhibiting more limited help-seeking behaviours (Visser et al., 2024). These findings demonstrate the significant role of gender in shaping the experience and impact of ADHD-related stigmatization. Thus, examining gender may reveal distinct effects of diagnostic labelling on social perceptions.

Current Study

The current study investigated the effects of ADHD behaviours, diagnostic labels, and gender on stigmatization. Building on the findings from Meza et al. (2019), it examined whether the influence of stigmatization varies across genders. In this quasi-replication, the primary research question is: Does ADHD behaviours, gender, or labelling an individual with ADHD reduce or exacerbate stigmatization? Specifically, the study investigated whether a diagnostic label influences participants' perceptions of ADHD behaviours and how the gender of a child characterized in a vignette may modify these perceptions.

This study directly tested whether behaviours that are characteristic of ADHD elicit increased or reduced stigmatization when accompanied by a diagnostic label. Social distance, a widely recognized measure of social stigma and prejudice (Wark & Galliher, 2007), was used to assess participants' attitudes. Unlike the design in Meza et al. (2019), which included three conditions: a control, ADHD behaviour, and ADHD behaviour with a diagnostic label, this study incorporates seven vignettes. Participants were randomly assigned to read one of these vignettes that described a child with ADHD behaviours and varied by the presence of an ADHD label (present or absent), and gender (neutral, male, or female), along with a control condition with gender-neutral descriptors and no ADHD behaviours or diagnostic label. This design examines the potential moderating role of a diagnostic label and gender on ADHD-related stigmatization. It is hypothesized that an ADHD diagnostic label may act as a protective factor, reducing negative evaluations and stigma-related responses. The diagnostic label may potentially foster a greater understanding of these behaviours, thereby reducing stigmatization often associated with ADHD. Additionally, gender as a moderator in perceptions of ADHD has not been fully explored in this context. It is hypothesized that the gender of the character in the vignette will influence participant perceptions, with the female vignette condition expected to yield higher stigma-related responses compared to the male and gender-neutral counterparts.

Untreated ADHD can lead to significant adverse outcomes for individuals and those around them. Given this risk, the potential benefits of receiving mental health services that accompany a diagnostic label may outweigh concerns over the potential stigmatizing effects of having a psychiatric diagnosis and may help lower barriers to care.

Methods

Participants

Participants included 717 individuals (560 females, 157 males) with mean age of 18.7 years (SD = 1.98 years) and of mixed racial and ethnic background (57.4% (n = 412) white, 15.6% (n = 112) South Asian, 20.6% (n = 148) East Asian, 4.5% (n = 32) Middle Eastern, and 1.8% (n = 13) other). Participants were excluded from the analysis if they failed both attention checks, asking participants to recall the gender and whether a diagnostic label was stated in their vignette condition, or had more than 50% of their data missing. Additionally, the control vignette condition was removed from the analysis to focus specifically on the effects of Diagnostic Label and Gender. After cleaning, 574 participants (456 females, 118 males) with a mean age of 18.6 years (SD = 1.88 years) remained for the analysis.

Measures and Materials

Demographic Questionnaire

The demographic questionnaire included 11 items assessing participants' age, sex, gender, ethnicity, country of birth, age of immigration to Canada (if applicable), socioeconomic status, education level, political views, and religiosity. Political views were rated on a 7-point scale ranging from very liberal (1) to very conservative (7), and religiosity was also rated on a 7point scale ranging from not at all (1) to extremely (7).

Vignettes

Participants were randomly assigned to read one of seven vignettes adapted from Meza et al. (2019). Six vignettes described a character named Alex with ADHD both hyperactive and inattentive ADHD behaviours. The vignettes varied based the inclusion of an ADHD diagnostic label, and the gender of the vignette character, which was either neutral, male, or female. A seventh vignette described a gender-neutral character with no ADHD behaviours and no diagnostic label and served as a control condition. However, this seventh vignette condition was not included in the analysis. The vignettes were designed to systematically examine how ADHD labels and gender influenced participants' perceptions (See Appendix D).

Attitudes

To measure attitudes toward the vignette character, participants rated how well they thought each of the 23 different adjectives described the character using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from not at all (1) to extremely (7). The adjectives were drawn from Meza et al. (2019) and originally developed by Fiske et al. (2002 Fiske et al. ( , 2007)). Adjectives were categorized into four groups: positive (e.g., intelligent, warm, and reliable); negative adjectives (e.g., clumsy, impulsive, and foolish); human-related (e.g., person, citizen, and human); and non-human or animalistic (e.g., wild, animal, and beast).

Emotional reactions

Emotional reactions toward the vignette character were measured using a single-item question taken from Meza et al. (2019), adapted initially from Angermeyer and Matschinger (2003). Participants were asked to indicate how angry, irritable, or mad they felt about the character after reading the passage. Responses were recorded on 7-point Likert scale, ranging from not at all (1) to extremely (7).

Social Distance

Social distance was assessed using a modified version of the Social Distance Scale drawn from Meza et al. (2019). Participants rated their willingness to engage in five different interactions with the vignette character: (1) move next door to the character; (2) socialize for an evening; (3) form a friendship; (4) work together, and (5) have them become part of the family.

Participants made their ratings using a 5-point scale, ranging from definitely yes (1) to definitely no (5). Thus, lower scores reflected a willingness to interact with the characters, while higher scores reflected a desire to social distance.

ADHD Measures

Participants completed three ADHD-related measures including the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale, the ADHD Knowledge Questionnaire, and the ADHD Attitudes Scale.

Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale

The Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS; Kessler et al., 2005) presents 18 ADHD symptoms (e.g., "How often are you distracted by activity or noise around you?"). For each item, participants rate on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from never (0) to very often (4) on how often they have felt or experienced this symptom over the past six months.

ADHD Knowledge Questionnaire

Participant's knowledge of ADHD was assessed using the ADHD Knowledge Questionnaire (Youssef et al., 2015). Participants were required to determine whether 27 statements were true, false, or if they were uncertain. Example statements included. "There are a greater number of boys than girls with ADHD" and "If medication is prescribed, educational interventions are often unnecessary."

ADHD Attitudes Scale

General attitudes toward ADHD were measured using the ADHD Attitudes Scale (Youssef et al., 2015). This scale included 25 statements assessing participants' agreement with common beliefs about ADHD such as "ADHD is a valid diagnosis" and "All children with ADHD should take medication." Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree (0) to strongly disagree (4).

Procedure

Participants completed the study using the Qualtrics online survey platform of Western University. Participants accessed the survey independently using an internet-enabled device, a computer, laptop, or tablet. Prior to participation, a Letter of Information was provided for review, and a consent form for the study procedures was completed (See Appendix B). The study was described as an investigation into the attitudes and beliefs about certain childhood behaviours and the factors influencing these perceptions. Participants were informed that they would be asked to answer questions pertaining to their thoughts, feelings, and perceptions of others.

Participants began the study by completing a self-report demographic questionnaire. This was followed by brief instructions indicating that they would read a short description of a child and their behaviour, with questions to follow. Participants were randomly assigned to one of seven vignette conditions, which varied based on the child's gender and whether an ADHD diagnosis statement was included. Each condition included approximately 100 participants.

After reading the assigned vignette, participants completed three self-report stigma measures assessing attitudes, emotional reactions, and social distance, adapted from the Meza et al. (2019) study. Following these measures, participants were provided with a two-question attention check to assess whether they recalled key details from their vignette condition, including the gender of the child and whether the paragraph explicitly stated that the child had ADHD. Participants then filled out three additional self-report measures targeting ADHD knowledge and attitudes, including the Adult ADHD Self Report (ASRS; Kessler et al., 2005), ADHD Knowledge Questionnaire (Youssef et al., 2015), and ADHD Attitudes Scale (Youssef et al., 2015), in that order. This was followed by asking participants whether they had received any formal diagnosis. If they indicated that they had received an ADHD diagnosis, they were prompted to specify the ADHD subtype. The study also assessed ADHD exposure by asking participants whether they personally knew someone with ADHD. Upon completing the study, participants viewed an online debriefing form, as detailed in Appendix C. The total duration of the study was approximately 30 minutes.

Data Analysis

Analyses were conducted using RStudio. Following data cleaning procedures, 574 participants remained for the analysis. For detailed information on participant exclusions, including attention checks, missing data, and removing the seventh vignette control condition, please refer to the participant section on page 14.

A series of four 2x3 factorial analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted to examine the effects of diagnostic labels (diagnostic label vs no diagnostic label) and gender (female, male, gender-neutral) on four separate outcome variables: (1) positive/negative adjectives, (2) human/animalistic adjectives, (3) emotional reaction, and (4) social distance. Negative and animalistic adjective responses were reverse-coded to align with positive and human-related responses, respectively. Pearson's product-moment correlation was computed to assess whether the fourth attitude-based outcome variables could be combined into a single composite score.

However, the resulting correlation coefficient (r = .48) indicated a moderate relationship, and therefore, the variables were retained as separate dependent measures.

Each of the four ANOVAs tested for the main effects of a diagnostic label, gender, and their interaction. Assumptions of normality (assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (assessed via Levene's test) were checked for each outcome variable. Tukey's Honest Significant Difference (HSD) tests were conducted as post hoc analyses following significant main or interaction effects for outcome variables that met the assumptions of ANOVA. For emotional reaction and social distance, violations of these assumptions were observed. As a result, nonparametric alternatives were used for pairwise comparisons: the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test and the Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction.

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine how the presence of a diagnostic label influences stigma-related responses towards individuals with ADHD behaviours and how these effects might vary by the individual's gender. Research has shown that ADHD behaviours are stigmatizing (Wilens & Spencer, 2010). This stigmatization is represented in the literature through negative attitudes toward an individual with ADHD, animalistic characteristics, emotional reactions of anger, and desired social distance (Meza et al., 2019; Mueller et al., 2012). These responses may create barriers to individuals receiving proper diagnoses and care due to fear of social stigma (Schoeman & Voges, 2022). However, existing literature is conflicted about whether receiving a diagnostic label reduces or exacerbates stigmatization (Hoza et al., 2005; Law et al., 2007; Meza et al., 2019). A study by Meza et al. (2019) found that a diagnostic label acted as a protective factor, but this study was limited to only male characters with ADHD. Although ADHD diagnosis rates are significantly higher in males, this disparity may be explained by females with ADHD exhibiting predominantly inattentive symptoms, leading to underdiagnosis and misunderstandings about ADHD in females (Rucklidge, 2008). This highlights the need for research that includes both genders

The main goal of this study was to conduct a quasi-replication of the Meza et al. (2019) study, examining the effect of adding a diagnostic label to the description of a child with ADHD behaviours. While extending the study to include female, male, and gender-neutral conditions.

Stigmatization was measured through participants' attitudes, emotional reactions, and desired social distance towards the character with ADHD behaviours.

Discussion of Results

The results confirmed that adding a diagnostic label significantly reduced negative attitudes, emotional reactions of anger, and the desire for social distance. Specifically, participants in the Label condition showed more positive attitudes and lower levels of anger and social distance towards the vignette character, indicating that the presence of a diagnostic label led to more favourable perceptions. While there were no significant effects of gender, the female vignette conditions exhibited the most pronounced effects, suggesting that adding a diagnostic label may have a stronger impact where ADHD behaviours are perceived as more abnormal due to the lower diagnosis rates of females.

The significant reduction in stigma-related responses in the label conditions aligns with the original hypothesis that a diagnostic label may act as a protective factor. The presence of a diagnostic label facilitated understanding of the ADHD behaviours described, likely mitigating negative perceptions by framing these behaviours as part of a recognized disorder rather than as negative personal characteristics. This result is consistent with the Meza et al. (2019) study, which suggests that diagnostic labels can enhance empathy and understanding, thereby reducing stigma.

However, the humanistic/animalistic adjective measure did not show a significant effect on the diagnostic label, contrasting with the other stigma measures and findings from Meza et al. (2019). One possible explanation is social desirability bias. Dehumanizing or animalistic descriptions are extreme and socially undesirable, which may have led participants to avoid rating the character as animalistic, especially when a diagnostic label was present.

The differing results across stigma measures contribute to the conflicting findings in the literature regarding the protective effects of a diagnostic label on stigmatization. This incongruity may, in part, be attributed to cultural differences in how ADHD and clinical diagnoses are perceived. In cultures where ADHD is less pervasive or poorly understood, the protective effect of a diagnostic label may not be as pronounced, as it depends on a shared societal understanding (Werkhoven et al., 2022). This suggests that the impact of diagnostic labelling could vary significantly across different cultural contexts. In the case of Canada, where the study was conducted, the observed protective effect of diagnostic labels aligns with the prevalence of clinical diagnoses.

The effect of gender was statistically non-significant. This may be because, while the gender of the vignette character influenced the strength of the label's effect, gender congruence between the participant and character may have also shaped the results, as the participant group in this study was nearly 80% female. Despite the lack of statistical significance, Figure 1 shows a trend toward stronger labelling effects for the female vignette condition.

While the secondary hypothesis, which predicted that the female vignette conditions would yield higher stigma-related responses compared to the male and gender-neutral counterparts, was not statistically supported, this effect was still observed. ADHD in females is often underdiagnosed or manifests as the inattentive form of ADHD, which is more likely to be misunderstood, ignored, or overlooked (Rucklidge, 2008). As a result, ADHD behaviours are often perceived as more abnormal or difficult to understand, which may explain why the addition of a diagnostic label had a larger impact on the female conditions. This suggests that a diagnostic label could be especially crucial for females in helping others contextualize their ADHD-related behaviours, thereby reducing stigma and enhancing social acceptance.

Implications

This study has significant implications for understanding how ADHD diagnoses and the associated stigmatization influence perceptions of those with the disorder. The findings suggest that a diagnostic label of ADHD can reduce stigma-related responses, which may help lower a barrier to receiving a diagnosis and treatment, particularly for individuals who may feel reluctant to seek it due to fear of social stigma. This is especially important given the impact of ADHD on cognitive, social, and emotional functioning, as well as the long-term negative effects on wellbeing (Godfrey et al., 2021).

In addition to the general reduction of stigma through diagnostic labelling, this study also highlights the gendered nature of ADHD perceptions. Given that ADHD behaviours in females are often perceived as more abnormal or difficult to understand, a diagnostic label may play a crucial role in helping to normalize these behaviours. This could encourage female-specific diagnosis and treatment of ADHD without fear of judgment. This finding further emphasizes the need for more research into ADHD in females, which could lead to more accurate diagnosis and better-tailored interventions, helping to reduce misdiagnosis and underdiagnosis.

Limitations and Future Directions

The following study has offered insight into the effects of diagnostic labels and gender on individuals with ADHD behaviours. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge some methodological limitations that may have potentially impacted the findings. One significant limitation is the absence of a condition where the character had an ADHD label but no ADHD behaviours. Including this condition would allow for a more precise understanding of the effects of the label alone, without the confounding influence of ADHD behaviours. Future research should isolate the labelling effects by including this condition to clarify whether the label alone can reduce stigma. Similarly, this study did not include a no-gender, no-label, and no-behaviour control condition within the analysis to focus exclusively on the effects of gender and label.

However, the inclusion of this condition would have provided a stronger baseline for isolating the effects of diagnosis and ADHD behaviours.

Furthermore, the current study did not account for the potential influence of gender congruence between the participant and the vignette character. As mentioned in the Discussion of Results section, while the gender of the vignette character appeared to affect the extent of the diagnostic label's influence, it is unclear whether the observed gender effects stem from the gender of the character, the gender of the participant, or the congruency between the two. Amplifying this limitation, the overrepresentation of female participants may have influenced the resulting gender effects and impacted the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should aim to include a more gender-balanced sample and investigate where the gender effects truly originate.

Finally, the study sample consisted of undergraduate university students from the University of Western Ontario, while the vignette character was 12 years old. This age gap between participants and the character may have influenced how participants perceived the character's behaviours. The vignette descriptions used in the study were designed to mirror those of Meza et al. (2019), who described a 12-year-old child. Future research could explore how age congruence between the participant and vignette character might impact stigma-related responses, as perceptions of ADHD may differ based on the age of both the individual being diagnosed and the individual making the judgement. Additionally, as the participant sample was primarily Canadian, conducting this study in a different cultural context could affect how ADHD and diagnostic labels are perceived.

Conclusion

In sum, the present study demonstrated that the presence of a diagnostic label significantly reduced stigma-related responses towards individuals with ADHD behaviours, suggesting that labels can play an important role in mitigating negative perceptions and encouraging social acceptance. While no significant gender effects were found, the findings highlight the potential impact of gendered perceptions of ADHD, particularly for females, who are often underdiagnosed. Given the limitations of the current study, future research should continue to explore the role of gender congruence and cultural context in shaping stigma-related responses to ADHD diagnoses. Understanding these dynamics is essential for improving social support and access to treatment for individuals with ADHD.
