Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Stakeholders are concerned that we have not made as much usable progress in autism research as we would want, given that approximately 100,000 papers have been published concerning the condition over the last 100 years. In this chapter, we identify that usable knowledge in the clinical, policy and support realms refers to causal knowledge. In the first half, we diagnose why this causal knowledge is particularly challenging to achieve within the constraints of psychological and cognitive-neuroscientific research, fields in which we typically have to rely on correlation rather than experimental manipulation. The gap between correlation (the information we have access to) and causation (the conclusions we need to make) is related to a large degree to addressing the likelihood of alternative causal paths that are able to explain the observed correlations, and falsifying these alternative causal paths is a primary key to closing the gap and arriving at causal conclusions. In the second half, we prescribe a falsification-centric approach toward approximating causality combined with a quantification of remaining paths. This aggregation will result in an atlas of evidence for and against causal claims in the condition. This approach focuses on empirical opportunities to empirically falsify "unnatural" constructs important to the various autism causal hypotheses as well as falsification of candidate causal relationships between those constructs. Moreover, we are able to accumulate prior results from the 100,000+ papers prior to planning additional data collection in a way that is independent of contemporaneous or even current assumptions in autism research and in a way that circumvents the working memory limitations of any particular researcher, clinician or advocate.

The Diagnosis

Privately and publicly (Neerland, 2023 , Vivanti, 2017), stakeholders in the grand project of autism research have expressed concerns about whether we have derived as much benefit for autistic individuals, their families and communities as we might hope, proportionate to the countless careers, resources and research participant effort invested in the project. With over 100,000 papers including the term "autism" (PubMed search: September, 2026), we seem to be drowning in data, but do we have as much usable knowledge as we should? And what do we mean by "knowledge" in this context?

Let's reverse-engineer the definition by examining what the field seems to want to know. One clinician-investigator (i.e., your humble author) has found the following questions appearing again and again both in 25 years of clinical conversations as well as within the grand conversation that is contained in 100 years of scientific literature (Asperger, 1944 /1991 , Kanner, 1943 , Potter, 1933 , Ssucharewa and Wolff, 1925 /1996): 1.

Why do superficially distinct clinical features co-occur with greater-than-uniform chance? Current diagnostic features include multiple social-communicative features as well as restricted and repetitive behaviors and interests (RRB) (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Not only do social-communicative features share little superficial behavioral similarities with RRBs as a whole, but even within each category, individual behaviors seem as though they are driven by distinct computational or psychological mechanisms: social withdrawal and challenges taking turns in conversation are not selfevidently manifestations of the same computational variation; neither are motor stereotypies and intense fascinations. What underlying mechanism(s) unify these features and also explain the heterogeneity of their presentation? The problem space expands when we also consider non-diagnostic features, like altered motor and executive function, as well as frequent co-occurrence with what are currently considered separate diagnoses: attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety disorders, developmental language disorder (DLD) and intellectual disability (ID). And at what level of analysis does a single mechanism explain and unify these multiple features/diagnoses: psychological, physiological or genetic?

2. What are the most useful and parsimonious clustering/stratification schemes for enhancing research (including clinical trials) and clinical nosologies for optimal outcomes? Which biomarkers best target these mechanisms? Not only is the diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (as we currently conceive of it) heterogenous in terms of feature presentation, so is the whole spectrum of neurodiversity or clinical neurodevelopmental disability. Understanding the landscape of genetic, physiological and psychological/cognitive mechanisms that drive different presentations would allow us to move away from description-based nosologies to causal-mechanism-based stratification that allow us to make the best treatment plans at the individual level (Ewen et al., 2021) mechanistically homogeneous clinical trials, so as not to "wash-out" true-positive effects in responders with negative effects in a mechanistically-different non-responder group (Ewen et al., 2019 , Sahin et al., 2018). And which (bio)markers most effectively tap these mechanisms (Lidstone et al., 2026)? 3. How and why do different feature profiles evolve over time? Which cascades and trajectories are caused by "preprogrammed" biological maturation, and which are causally dependent on experience? When two features are related via experiencedependent mechanisms, how does the expression of one feature cause another? If our goal is to intervene to improve quality of life, features caused by experience-dependent mechanisms would be amenable to environmental modifications or habilitative therapies, whereas those only explainable by biological mechanisms may require biological therapeutics.

4. Which autism features result in the greatest disability? The field has learned in recent decades from the increased involvement of autistic individuals that some autism features, like motor stereotypies, primarily contribute to the wellbeing of the individual (Kapp et al., 2019); social challenges resulting from the expression of these features are best addressed with awareness (Jones et al., 2021) rather than clinically interventions.

However, there is general agreement that ego-dystonic and dangerous features, such as depression and self-injurious behaviors, should be addressed clinically (Baiden et al., 2025 , Dwyer et al., 2025). Understanding which features cause the experience of disability and how these causal paths can be interfered with is key to improving life for autistic individuals. What is latent in each of the questions is the need for causal knowledge, i.e., knowledge that tell us what would happen to all causally downstream features or constructs when we actively intervene on a causally upstream mechanism or construct. This interventionist view of causation aligns well with sense of causation currently dominant among philosophers of science and the statistical methodologists who aim to help us achieve causal conclusions (Pearl, 2009 , Spirtes et al., 2000 , Woodward, 2003).

So what is in our 100,000 autism papers if not causal conclusions? Most of the results in the data papers are descriptive and correlational, of the either the form, "Such-and-such a construct or behavior is systematically different in autistic individuals versus neurotypical individuals," or "Within the population of autistic individuals, such-and-such a construct is statistically associated with this other construct." And we all know that correlation does not equal causation.

In many fields of biomedicine, causal conclusions are de rigueur in individual papers, so why is autism research often limited to descriptive results rather than causal conclusions? The causalknowledge deficit is not due to poor scientific practices on the part of autism researchers; individual studies in our field are simply not well poised to make causal inferences. The source of most causal conclusions in other fields of biomedicine is experimental manipulation. If we wish to know the direct causal impact of construct X on construct Y-say the function of dopaminergic activity on conditioning-then we would selectively activate midbrain dopamine neurons whenever an animal enters an otherwise neutral location. If the animal subsequently develops a preference for that location-given appropriate experimental controls-we have a "positive" causal conclusion that the dopamine neuronal activity is sufficient to produce conditioning (Tsai et al., 2009).

We simply can't do this sort of manipulation in most autism research. Yes, there are preclinical (animal) models used in some autism research (Silverman et al., 2022), but that is not our focus here; our focus is on human-based research. And several realities separate what can be done within human research versus experimental manipulations in the laboratory.

Firstly, there are ethical barriers to many types of intervention. Even clinical trials of novel clinical interventions that are intended to be beneficial to research participants require significant scrutiny because of the demands they put on research participants. Interventions that may be scientifically interesting but would not help individual research participants are more problematic. Interventions that have potential to cause harm are out of the question.

Secondly, there are technical barriers. Laboratory-based experimental manipulations have been designed to cleanly perturb individual biological structures or processes-i.e., to perturb the target entities (the dopaminergic-neuron activity, in our example) and not "adjacent" entities that could confound the causal conclusions (e.g., local GABAergic neurons). The types of interventions we use in human psychology tend to be "dirtier" and affect multiple constructs.

Consider the example of a study of a novel intervention designed to enhance social function by directly targeting imitation ability (Ingersoll, 2012). The participants who received the intervention did indeed show an increase in their social skills…however, an accompanying mediation analysis showed that the social gains were not caused by an enhancement of imitation abilities. Assuming all the constructs were measured appropriately and appropriate power, it must have been some other psychological construct altered by the "imitation-targeting" intervention that actually caused the change in social skills. Now, this intervention may still be useful clinically for enhancing social skills, but it failed to provide the expected experimental information about the causal link between imitation differences and social differences.

Thirdly, even if the other considerations were moot, it is simply not practical to gain all of our causal knowledge through intervention studies. Clinical trials of interventions are costly in terms of financial investment as well as research participants' time. And there are simply too many neuroscientific and psychological constructs implicated as mechanisms for clinical trials of various interventions to be an efficient source of causal knowledge.

The net effect is that most research is limited to the analysis of naturally occurring patterns of human variation, and so we're back to correlation and the limitation that correlation does not equal causation.

Spoiler alert: Correlation actually does entail some causal path between the measured constructs; the "correlation ≠ causation" saw refers to uncertainty about which causal relationship connects the two constructs. The measure of X correlating with the measure of Y does not entail that changing X causes a change in Y; it could alternatively be the case that Y causes X, or that some Z causes both X and Y. This problem is analogous to one in mathematics: any finite set of points can be generated by any of an infinite number of equations, so you cannot derive the unique equation that is working behind the scenes merely knowing sets-however large-of individual "correlational" observations. However, it is possible to converge toward a smaller and smaller subset of "surviving" candidate equations by iteratively collecting more observations; every new set of observations disconfirms some of the candidate equations that entailed results that were not observed in each step. The hope and underlying premise of this approach is that the space of candidate equations will be reduced sufficiently at some point that all remaining equations support the same predictions for whatever one's application may be.

The same approach works in scientific contexts, where causal theories take the role of mathematical equations and individual correlational datasets take the role of mathematical observations. To wit, we start with an observed correlation, we propose sets of theoretical causal relationships that would both generate the observed correlation and also make yet-unobserved predictions. We then run an observational study to collect new data to falsify as many of the plausible causal theories as we can. While we may never converge toward a unique causal theory of autism, our hope is that we at least converge toward a bounded set of remaining theories that all make the same conclusions relevant to our purposes of clinical care and public policy.

This type of theory-driven empirical science based on falsifying explicit sets of candidate theories-what Sherlock Holmes referred to as "eliminat[ing] the impossible"-is precisely the type of science famously advanced by Popper (1963) (see also Lakatos, 1970). Our approach relies on the fact that, while correlation does not identify (one, unique) causation, the absence of correlation can falsify candidate causal relationships (with critical methodological small print: assuming the relevant studies are adequately powered, the constructs are adequately measured, appropriate statistical tests are used, participant ages and measurement time-scales are appropriate, any non-linearities are appropriately considered, there is no masking by heterogeneity, cancellation or compensatory pathways, and the faithfulness assumption is satisfied).

And so the gap between our initial correlational observations and the "true" underlying causal theory that produces the correlation is the comprised of the "yet unfalsified alternative causal theories that could also explain the correlation." Many of these can be characterized as confounds. And as scientists, it is natural for us to think about causal confounds, like when we write a limitations section in a paper, participate in a journal club or serve as Reviewer 2. If the authors contend that a measured association between constructs X and Y demonstrates that X causes Y (e.g., sleep difficulties cause fatigue leading to daytime behavioral dysregulation, implying that improving sleep will improve daytime behavior), we may respond that the measured association could actually be due to a confound: either an alternative mechanisms that actually causes both X and Y (e.g., anxiety causes both sleep difficulties and daytime behavioral dysregulation, but directly modifying sleep would not impact daytime behavior, because the anxiety is still the driver of daytime behavioral dysregulation), a measurement artifact (bias) driving the observed associations (e.g., caregiver global impression causes the caregiver to rate both sleep and daytime behavioral regulation negatively (Levine et al., 2024)), or an alternative causal direction (e.g., daytime dysregulation makes it difficult to calm down for bed, leading to disrupted sleep.) These are precisely the types of possibilities we need to eliminate in order to converge on a causal account.

Think of this as a "massively parallel" version of the scientific method. The "elementary school" version of the scientific method has us generate a single causal hypothesis based on initial observations, and then test it directly, in the manner of the biomedical laboratory experimental manipulation. The results of our experimental manipulation either falsify the hypothesis outright or provide very strong evidence supporting it. However, in the massively parallel case, we generate multiple hypotheses (main hypothesis and alternative hypotheses encompassing confounds and sources of bias) and then we falsify the alternatives one by one (e.g., by control experiments or statistical adjustment). If the initial association disappears when we experimentally control or statistically adjust for the confounder, we have falsified a direct causal effect from X to Y. And once we are down to either a single remaining causal path or a functionally-equivalent set of causal possibilities, our next step is to quantify the remaining relationships between constructs so that we are prepared to predict the effects of various potential intervention for the next patient who walks through the door or the effect of a new policy for all autistic individuals. So this sort of systematic falsification is what the field has been doing for the last century, right? You may guess by the fact that we are only of the way through the chapter that the ⅕ answer is "No." At least this sort of falsification does not occur nearly systematically enough to make the progress we are seeking-that is the diagnosis of the challenge that motivates this chapter. But we should first recognize the instances where empirical, theory-evaluating tests have been made in autism research, both to give due credit and also to serve as an example on how best to proceed more widely.

The clearest and perhaps earliest positive example was the falsification of the strong version of the psychogenic account of the autism phenotype. These hypotheses, for which the archetype is Bettelheim (1967), essentially claim that severely disrupted parenting toward an otherwise typical child causes a psychological "protective" reaction that manifests as social withdrawal.

These theories evolved in an era of psychiatry in which the primacy of Freudian constructs and assumptions was taken for granted by most practitioners and investigators. However, by the 1960s, neurological research became the competitor for the causation of autism, with the problem understood in terms of mutual exclusivity: either autism is caused by highly abnormal parenting in neurobiologically typical children, or it is caused by an inborn neurological difference regardless of the parenting style. The neurological case won out in the literature, supported by observations of motor differences and an increased rate of epilepsy autism (DeMyer, 1975b , Rutter, 1968), neither of which would be predicted nor could be explained by abnormal parenting. The psychogenic account was thereby falsified via the generally accepted criteria of the research community.

Later, the late 1980s and early 1990s saw a grand era of developmental psychological theorizing (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985 , Frith et al., 1991 , Hobson, 1989 , Mundy and Sigman, 1989 , Rogers and Pennington, 1991) accompanied by some empirical efforts to explicitly evaluate the causal claims in those theories. Consider the case of the Weak Central Coherence (WCC) account. Building on observations around the nature of autistic perception, WCC was proposed by Frith in the first edition of her book (Frith, 1989) as an explanation for a wide variety of autism features, including social-communicative features. However, in an era in which performance on Theory of Mind (ToM) tests was taken as a proxy for the broad and heterogeneous social phenotype of autism, a lack of association between WCC and ToM (Frith and Happe, 1994, Happé, 1997) was sufficient for the theory to be substantively reduced in scope, such that became a description of the perceptual features of autism and not an explanation of the core diagnostic features (Frith, 2003 , Happé, 2000).

Other theories were at least partially falsified through research occurring outside of autism populations. An influential set of theories from cognitive neuroscience attributed core intentionunderstanding aspects of autism to atypical function of the so-called mirror neuron system (MNS). The whole concept of the MNS-both relevant to explanations of autism as well as to other scientific applications-was dealt significant blow by sharp attacks from Hickok (2009).

Some of the data supporting these attacks came from research explicitly involving autistic individuals, but other attacks came from the study of different scientific questions. Nevertheless, a blow to the concept of the MNS was also a blow to autism theories that depended on the MNS.

And yet, in your author's anecdotal experience, the vast majority of empirical autism research is not truly hypothesis-evaluating in the sense that data collection was not designed primarily with the intent to evaluate-and potentially falsify-pre-stated causal claims. Most of the autism literature is descriptive: it reports those empirical associations that are, if anything, hypothesisgenerating. Restated, we tend to find theories in the Discussion section rather than the Introduction. The consequence is an explosion of theories rather than a convergence.

And there is further evidence that this is precisely what has happened over the duration of autism research. For example, we have often seen theories or causal claims that fade and reappear decades later, either with or without the explicit acknowledgement by the re-proposer of the theory's earlier career. For example, prediction accounts presented between the 1990s and 2010s (Courchesne and Allen, 1997 , Pellicano and Burr, 2012 , Sinha et al., 2014 , Van de Cruys et al., 2014) directly recapitulate claims made by Hermelin and O'Connor in their 1970 monograph (Hermelin and O'Connor, 1970). The notion of an atypical irreducible affective/social "module," as highlighted by Hobson in the 1980s (Hobson, 1989), was an explicit reanimation of theoretical claims were pioneered by both Kanner (1943) and Asperger (1960). And the notion that early auditory differences lead to language-acquisition challenges and subsequently to social differences-re-invoked by Gage and colleagues (2003) following a novel auditory magnetoencephalography finding-recapitulated claims from 45 years earlier by Anthony (1958). How much more progress would have been made if we had a field-wide shared and running tally of the unfalsified theories and the evidence for/against each, and had continuously directed our empirical efforts throughout the last century at the highest value targets?

But perhaps the greatest drag on the progress of autism research over the last couple of decades has been due to the unjustified persistence of the strongest versions of influential theories that had already been substantially weakened-often called the "Big Three": ToM, WCC and Executive Function (EF). Writing back in 2011, Boucher noted that each of the theories persisted as focal point for research "long after their original proponents had retreated from their early claims" (Boucher, 2011, p. 471), and all three are still invoked to this day (e.g., Burnos and Kopacz, 2025), 15 years after that warning. To the better-read corners of autism research, WCC (and its successor, Enhanced Perceptual Function (Mottron et al., 2006)) had long been restricted to explaining perceptual function in autism, whereas EF and ToM became phenomena to be explained rather than explanatory mechanisms.

But to a large part of the field, the original explanatory theories persisted, largely unmoved by less-widely-socialized disconfirmatory evidence.

Why has autism research suffered from such empirical novelty seeking (in most cases) rather than settling into the more mature pattern of hypothesis-driven research? First, we should note that these issues are endemic to many fields where laboratory-based experimental manipulations are not possible, including broadly throughout the psychological study of many topics (though many sub-fields, like computational motor control, has been highly model-/hypothesis-driven for decades). The Scientific Reform movement over the last several years has focused to a large degree on psychological science for this reason (Open Science Collaboration, 2015 , Popper, 1963 , Smaldino, 2019), and there has been an explosion in meta-theoretical reform generated within psychology as well (Borsboom et al., 2021 , Fried, 2020 , Morton, 2005 , van Rooij and Baggio, 2021). Some have attributed the reproducibility crisis to a lack of truly hypothesisdriven research (Alger, 2019).

Also, within many research programs, it is well described how cognitive biases and academic incentives affect the interpretation of the data-the desire to advance a pet theory rather than contribute "without fear or favor" to a field-wide, evolving model as we propose here (Alger, 2019 , Chamberin, 1890 , Pratt, 1971).

But there are some additional factors that are likely more challenging specifically within autism research than in other scientific programs. One issue is that the 100,000-plus papers of autism can be a curse. No one individual investigator can master all of the literature relevant to The shift likely also had to do with moral disgust at mother-shaming. In this example, moral disgust had a positive role, but it also has the potential to be misleading. Notably, In the aftermath of World War II-era eugenics, the idea of a genetic cause of autism was exceptionally problematic. However, in the current era, heritability research suggests a majority-genetic component (Bai et al., 2019). Ironically, some believe that this restriction on the consideration of genetic causes offered license for Bettelheim's psychogenic or "mother-blaming" hypothesis (Feinstein, 2010, p. 34).

Finally, a challenge that is fairly specific to autism research is that the thing being explained keeps shifting. The malleability of the autism diagnostic criteria has been an area of focus recently (Frith, 2026, Mottron and Bzdok, 2020), but has in fact been discussed for decades (e.g., Wing, 1976). Beyond concerns about the variability over time of feature-magnitude, the autism scientific enterprise has, at different points, subsumed the diverse social features of autism under various proxies: first social withdrawal (Rutter, 1966 , Rutter, 1968 , Wing and Wing, 1971), then ToM (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985 , Leslie, 1987 , Morton, 1986) and more recently joint attention (Mundy et al., 1990). Results defining the causal mechanisms of the "autism social phenotype" in the guise of social withdrawal are unlikely to be directly commensurable with results explaining joint attention differences.

Let's zoom back out to what will move things forward. Our goal is to define a "causal claim space" that includes all clinical features, candidate mechanisms and potential measurement biases derived from the literature, as well as the relationships among them. By iteratively and systematically falsifying as many alternative hypotheses as possible, we can converge toward causal explanation (which we need) primarily from correlational data (which we are able to collect). We then quantify the relationships that survive. We might call this storehouse of falsification and quantification data an "atlas."

Before proceeding, we need to introduce some terminology adopted from graph theory/network science; this is the terminology that is also commonly used in causal inference/structural equation modeling, contemporary network psychometrics and psychological meta-theoretical work. Within this terminology, constructs and observable behaviors are referred to as a nodes (or vertices), and the relationship between two nodes is referred to as an edge. A triple is a node-edge-node combination, and a graph is the whole model taken together (roughly equivalent to a hypothesis or theory, in the sense we are using those terms). Some inference we discuss will be over a triple, and other inference (particularly accounting for confounds) has to occur over the whole graph.

The sections that follow propose a systematic method, first, for definition of the "causal claim space," and, then, for falsification of individual claims. To begin, we have to populate the space of potential graphs, and this is done by considering all of the mechanisms potentially relevant to autism proposed within the fields of psychology and cognitive neuroscience.

The Prescription: Starting with All Possibilities

The initial set of nodes within our candidate claim space consists, in the first place, of the clinical features of autism (McCray et al., 2014 , Wing et al., 2002 , Zhao et al., 2022a) and the mechanistic constructs within the autism theories proposed over the last century (Tables 1 and 2), not to mention sources of selection or measurement bias. At a coarse level of granularity, the number of constructs may be around 150. But we also need to recognize the shifting boundaries between diagnoses on the neurodevelopmental spectrum as well as between what is considered, at any point in time, typical vs. atypical development (Happe and Frith, 2020, Rosen et al., 2021). Today's differential diagnosis may be tomorrow's heterogeneity. A comprehensive model would also need to encompass the theories and constructs associated with differential-diagnostic and co-occurring conditions, including ID (with autistic features), social anxiety disorder, DLD plus anxiety, DLD plus ADHD, social (pragmatic) communication disorder, primary stereotypies, obsessive-compulsive disorder, sensory processing disorder, and developmental coordination disorder (DCD), among others. This need is highlighted by the fact that neurobiological studies carve up the neurodevelopmental spectrum quite differently from (Vandewouw et al., 2023) current nosology.

How to Falsify and Quantify Constructs and Associations (Nodes and Edges)

Constructs: Falsification and Measurement

The nodes in our overall atlas and in the more local empirical/statistical models used to evaluate claims consist of psychological and neurobiological constructs. We have been using this term up to this point without defining it rigorously. In our work, we mean autism features/behaviors and the psychological and neurobiological mechanisms mean to explain them -i.e., that are "causally upstream" of the features.

A key aspect of a construct is that it is an abstraction and not identical to the measures used to assess it. (For the philosophically interested reader, I have to apologize. I recognize that we are using a constructivist term within a realist worldview (see Rychlak, 1968, particularly pp. 12-22) -indeed, the major point of this subsection is falsification through demonstrating that particular "construct" is not a naturally occurring kind. But I simply could not find a clearer term to use).

Constructs as abstractions retain the information from empirical observations that is relevant to a particular use and discard the irrelevant information, so as to avoid distinctions without difference. For example, "age at first word" is predictive of later language outcome, but whether your first word was "dog," "juice" or "ball" is not. Similarly, a certain level of expressivelanguage function encompasses the ability to make a very wide range of novel utterances and not just a particular sentence.

Constructs therefore can be thought of as "mini-theories" that, like all theories, propose a relationship among different variables (e.g., the score on the one expressive-language test will be correlated with the score on another, or results from an autism sample recruited using one test will show similar results to an autism sample recruited using other results), are falsifiable and, if they survive significant attempts at falsification, can be used to predict yet-to-be-observed results. They therefore "breathe" in the sense that they inhale data from multiple measures and populations, and exhale predictions for different measures and populations (Pearl and Bareinboim, 2018).

Constructs also engage other theories to define a relationship with distinct cognitive abilities (e.g., the relationship between expressive and receptive language) (Cronbach and Meehl, 1955).

Implicit in the proposition that constructs are falsifiable is the reality that not every conceivable construct is a natural kind-and Uttal has mourned that new constructs are frequently added in the literature "ad lib and ad hoc" (cited in Poldrack and Yarkoni, 2016) all the time. "Expressive language," in the sense we have been using it above, likely will not be falsified with new data anytime soon, but what about Hippocrates' black bile, Gall's organ of murder or Freud's anal character? These were invented to fit the needs of psychological and medical frameworks that were, as we now know, built on premises that lack credible empirical support (viz., humor theory, phrenology and psychoanalysis).

More modern sub-fields of psychology, however, explicitly require non-trivial evidence to show that a particular construct is a naturally-occurring kind. Cognitive neuroscience may be best thought of a collection of different sub-fields, each of which has a different set of types of things in its "basis set" (Table 2): lobular anatomy, Broadmann areas, classic neuropsychological ("aphasiology") networks, resting-state networks, oscillatory frequencies, neurochemicals, etc., and each with its own method for establishing that they are real and fit-for-purpose entities.

Different types of constructs, from different sub-fields, might each present affordances for different types of interventions (Ewen et al., 2021): TMS-based therapies would likely require us to identify a particular brain region and perhaps an oscillatory frequency (Kopell et al., 2014). A pharmacological intervention for ego-dystonic subjective features may require a particular molecule as the causal construct. A cognitive-training intervention would require a psychological domain. And a policy intervention would require a social determinant to be modified. Singlegene conditions associated with high rates of autism may be amenable to genetic interventions (e.g., NGN-401 in Rett syndrome). And even assessing associations between constructs from two different sub-fields may present challenges (Nagel, 1979 , Shallice and Cooper, 2011 , Sullivan, 2016).

But despite formal procedures in place in each field to substantiate constructs, not every theory certifies up front that all of its constructs are natural kinds. Therefore, our first method of falsification is turned on the constructs; we can then eliminate from our candidate claim space the constructs that are falsified and the causal claims that depend on them.

Consider 1970's-80's notions of hemispheric lateralization. While we currently accept that some cognitive abilities are lateralized in the brain (and have done since Broca), researchers in the 1980's made far stronger (too strong, as it turns out) assumption that individuals varied almost unidimensionally on the strength of abilities located in one brain hemisphere versus the other. Based on that assumption, the field felt it could assume an individual's strength in one "left hemispheric ability" by knowing his or her performance in a different task housed in the same hemisphere (Fein et al., 1984). This type of inference was how the language-primary theories of autism (i.e., social features of autism are caused by lower levels of language function) were falsified to the satisfaction of the field at the time (Boucher, 1976): because autistic individuals did not show strong lateralization in dichotic-listening tasks that disfavored their left hemisphere (where principal language networks reside), altered language could not be responsible for the range of autistic features in the sample. As it happens, there are other reasons to falsify language theories of autism (Tang et al., 2023), but basing the falsification of language-primary theories on a strong construct of a "unidimensional hemispheric interindividual variation," as was done at the time, is not sustainable with subsequent evidence that falsifies the unidimensional hemispheric variation construct (Boles, 1992 , Nielsen et al., 2013).

A second way that we can falsify a theoretical claim is to show empirically that its distribution of values does not differ between the autistic population and the neurotypical population, in cases where a theoretical account demands such a difference. For example, the "broken mirror" theory of autism (Williams et al., 2001) entailed altered performance in automatic imitation; a rigorous meta-analysis disconfirmed this prediction (Cracco et al., 2018).

When conceptualizing constructs in the first place, a non-trivial challenge is that they can be defined at different degrees of granularity. There is not one single degree of granularity that is relevant in all use cases and models. Consider an example from biomedicine. When studying the effect of drugs on muscle, should we lump cardiac and skeletal muscle together, or should we split them as different constructs? It depends on the drug. If we test a drug which binds to similar sodium channels in both cardiac and skeletal muscle on only one muscle type, we would miss an opportunity to generalize the drug's function to the other type of muscle. If we test an agent that is highly selective for calcium channels that exist only in skeletal muscle on an unstratified mixture of both muscle types, we would find that it is only moderately or inconsistently effective (i.e., the error term would be inflated). Appropriate granularity depends on the use case.

Within the autism literature, a classic example of inadequately modeled granularity is that of EF. Both autism and ADHD show an extremely high prevalence of altered (Sadozai et al., 2024).

And despite the high rates of co-occurrence of ADHD in autistic people (Gargaro et al., 2011, Lee and Ousley, 2006), they are in fact distinct conditions under current criteria, since there are many people with autism who do not meet diagnostic criteria for ADHD and vice versa. It is therefore challenging to propose that "altered EF" (at a coarse level of granularity) could be causal for other features of both ADHD and autism. Once we break down the "supra-construct" of EF into multiple sub-constructs, however, the profiles between autism and ADHD are quite distinct. Children with ADHD typically show response-inhibition deficits, as does the subset of autistic children who have a co-occurring ADHD diagnosis (Corbett et al., 2009, Ozonoff and Strayer, 1997). Autistic children, however, show alterations in other EF sub-constructs (set shifting (Ozonoff and Strayer, 1997), cognitive flexibility (Geurts et al., 2009, Hobson and Hobson, 2011) and generativity (Turner, 1997)). Moreover, a close reading of the theoretical literature suggests that there is not, in fact, a single "EF theory of autism," as it is commonly discussed, but rather multiple different theories, each linked to a particular sub-construct of EF (Table 1, entries 3.6, 3.11.1, 3.11.2, 3.11.3). Emerging research using top-down hierarchical unfolding structural analysis (a/k/a the "bass-ackwards" analysis) offers a data-driven approach to select optimal specificity for a particular use-case (Sasia et al., 2026).

Our construct is well-specified and has survived falsification, and now we need to measure it in the context of a dataset. We mentioned earlier that a construct is not identical with the measures intended to quantify it: expressive-language function is not simply whatever number any socalled expressive-language test outputs. No matter what construct is in the title of a psychological instrument, cognitive paradigm or brain physiology measurement, all measures are sensitive to multiple different mechanistic constructs (i.e., there is a risk of confounding), as well as to measurement artifacts (biases). An important example of where a measurement has been misleading by better representing an alternative cognitive construct moreso than the "title" construct has led to some of the most significant attacks against the highly influential ToM theory of autism (Fletcher-Watson and Happé, 2019). Namely, the false-belief tasks intended to assess ToM were (overly) sensitive to language rather than indicating ToM itself in the study samples (Bloom and German, 2000). Some autistic children passed the false-belief test, and language ability predicted a large amount of variation in who passed (Gernsbacher and Yergeau, 2019 , Peterson et al., 2012 , Steele et al., 2003).

An example of how measurement artifact/bias may provide misleading results when quantifying theoretical relationships is the recent finding that a substantial percentage of the variance expressed in parent-report instruments is a function of the reporter perspective (across constructs) rather than individual variation in the construct/trait allegedly being measured (Levine et al., 2024). Within cognitive neuroscience, a single finding wiped out a large portion of fMRI-based altered-connectivity literature. The relevant paper showed that group differences in "connectivity" results could actually be explained by group differences in head movement, rather than a cerebral physiological difference (Adamek et al., 2022 , Power et al., 2012).

Optimally, all measures that inform a model would have a task analysis (Friedrich and Rader, 1997), which endeavors to assess the full range of mechanistic constructs and measurement artifacts to which a given task or instrument is sensitive. Being open-eyed about measurement limitations allows investigators to model these confounds appropriately. One approach to estimating a construct more accurately than a single measure might involves using multiple measures for each construct, where each measure is sensitive to different artifacts or confounds, and the average is a purer assessment of the intended construct (e.g., McWeeny et al., 2025). The classic example of this is the measurement of the full-scale IQ (intelligence quotient) as the intercorrelation of several superficially distinct subtests, each sensitive to different additional cognitive mechanisms and to different measurement artifacts (Spearman, 1904 , Wechsler, 2014).

Community-Endorsed Outcomes and Social Determinants

Two sets of constructs warranting special consideration are community-prioritized outcomes and social determinants. Historically, most autism research has placed either the diagnosis of autism as a whole or particular autism features at the far end of its causal arrows. Autistic selfadvocates and the neurodiversity movement have emphasized the importance of furtherdownstream outcomes, namely those outcomes that matter in everyday life: health/safety, adaptive function, quality of life, autonomy, relationships and community participation (Pellicano and den Houting, 2022, Pellicano et al., 2014). This approach has a precedent in a longer tradition of studying daily functioning, participation and individual/family well-being in other neurodevelopmental conditions, including cerebral palsy (Frisch and Msall, 2013 , Msall, 2009 , Msall and Park, 2008), while actively seeking autistic people's own definitions of valued outcomes. Because priorities differ across cultures, families and individuals, deciding which outcomes matter requires community involvement.

On the causally upstream ("mechanistic") side of the equation, we know that social determinants affect not only outcomes like community participation (World Health Organization, 2001), but also psychological development (e.g., language acquisition (Rakesh et al., 2025)). Just like psychological and neuroscientific entities, social determinants must be broken down into empirically justified constructs in order to be used as mechanisms (Krieger et al., 2018).

Edges: Falsification, Integration and Quantification

In the broadest conceptualization of the candidate causal claim space embraced by our atlas, our approach starts with every empirically valid psychological/cognitive neuroscientific construct imagined to be relevant by autism researchers (nodes), and every one of the 2 • ( n 2 ) directed edges between these nodes.

Estimating ~150 constructs-and ignoring sources of measurement and sampling bias for the moment-this would imply 11,175 possible undirected edges or 22,350 directed edges.

The simplest context for edge falsification is that of the node-edge-node triple. To falsify the edge, we would simply measure two constructs within a sample and show that they are not statistically associated. A crucial technical point is that statistical falsification requires a higher bar than is commonly understood. Even some working scientists believe (mistakenly) that a "p > 0.05" statistical result is evidence that two constructs are not related. In most cases, however, this statistical result is within a statistical grey zone that indicates that, "We didn't learn anything new about whether these constructs are related than we knew before we analyzed this data." No, determining with confidence that two constructs has a high statistical bar (just as p < 0.05 is a high bar for demonstrating the presence of an association) and requires either a specific test of equivalence (Dienes, 2014 , Zhao et al., 2022b) or uncertainty interval excluding effects large enough to support the claim (e.g., Tang et al., 2023). And even these results necessarily depend on a number of valid assumptions, including that the relevant studies are adequately powered, the constructs are adequately measured, any non-linearities are appropriately considered, there is no masking by heterogeneity, cancellation or compensatory pathways, and the faithfulness assumption is satisfied.

But additionally, the way we have presented both the theoretical associations and their measurement has assumed a generic association (i.e., that any measurement of each construct is able to disprove an association between them, without specifically considering age/sensitive periods, time lags and so on). It is rarely the case that theories assume that associations between two constructs are pervasive that this lack of specificity in measurement is sound.

So how do we focus our approach to falsification in order to accommodate these constraints?

In the case of a sensitive period (e.g., Roger's and Pennington's (1991) claims about neonatal imitation), one must measure imitation at the specified developmental period. In the case of a directional claim (as all causal claims should entail), falsifying an undirected association will also falsify both directed associations. When an undirected association is present but the direction is unknown, the approach is trickier. In some special cases, we are able to resolve direction from cross-sectional data (Pearl, 2009 , Spirtes et al., 2000). However, in many cases, it may take longitudinal or even interventional data to assess the direction of influence.

Longitudinal data is also critical for assessing another characteristic of the relationship between two constructs: what Happé (2001) referred to as "online" versus "developmental." This distinction, in our view, encodes a fundamental distinction in a relationship between two constructs. "Online" edges reflect a situation where the causally downstream construct is manifestation of the upstream construct, often indicating that they exist at different levels of analysis, such as when the upstream construct is a computational ability and the downstream construct is a behavior that relies on that computational ability). In such a case, manipulating the upstream construct (computational process) results in an immediate change in the downstream construct (behavioral manifestation of that computational process). The alternative is a "developmental" relationship between two constructs: viz., one in which the change in the downstream construct is an experience-dependent consequence of the manifestation of the upstream construct.

An example from the evolution of Rutter's theorizing illustrates the distinction. Rutter initially proposed such a relationship between altered communication in autism and social approach/withdrawal (Rutter, 1966 , Rutter, 1968 , Wing, 1971), namely that decreased communication abilities lead to frustration in social interactions, and affected children subsequently develop social withdrawal due to inefficacy in social interactions. And indeed such a causal pattern is still believed to be relevant in the developmental auditory aphasia associated with Landau-Kleffner syndrome: an acquired aphasia leads to a learned frustration with social interaction and subsequent withdrawal (Pearl et al., 2001); this withdrawal is at least partially reversible when alternate channels of communication, such as sign language, are made available (Deonna et al., 2009). The relationship between the change in auditory abilities and subsequent learned withdrawal shows a time-lag, as does the recovery.

And then, without explicitly drawing attention to this change, Rutter's theorizing changed: now the communication ability and social skill were not directly causally related but were both viewed as "online" manifestations of another construct, symbolic representation ability (Ricks and Wing, 1976, Rutter, 1978). The interventionist implications of this new account were that a (theoretical) manipulation of an individual's symbolic abilities would cause an immediate change in both social and communicative ability, but, unlike the experience-dependent account, intervening on communication abilities would not effect a change in social engagement.

The above discussion encompasses empirical assessment within the context of the triple, and sometimes that is adequate for purpose. But tempting as it may be, we cannot simply reduce all potential construct-construct interactions to 2• ( n 2 ) triples; to see why, we need to return to the effect of confounding as a primary driver of the correlation-causation gap. Recall that addressing confounding equates to evaluating alternative causal hypotheses, some of which are scientifically interesting and others of which reflect measurement/sampling biases.

Statistically adjusting (or "controlling") for confounders assesses alternative hypotheses: if we have a positive association between autism diagnosis and motor performance (for example) that disappears when we adjust for age, then we know it is age rather than autism-specific mechanisms that directly affects motor performance. But we cannot simply adjust for every alternative hypothesis or potential source of bias in any model; indiscriminately including the wrong covariates can actually create new biases (Greenland et al., 1999 , Tennant et al., 2021).

However, thoughtful considerations of measurement issues and relevant alternative hypotheses can create additional and justified opportunities to falsify an association, even when empirical analysis of the triple does not falsify the claim.

And the consideration of alternative hypotheses in this way also allows us not only to better assess the validity of a single theory but to begin to escape the tyrannical logic of the single theory, and supporting the integration of theoretical claims (and their evidence) beyond their individual theory of origin. Quantifying the associations (rather than merely judging them as present/absent) also helps move us from, "Does X mechanism cause Y feature(s) of autism?" to "How much does X mechanism impact Y features of autism?" The latter conceptualization also allows for the parallel (or even interacting) consideration of alternative mechanisms with the same behavioral consequences (Rothman, 1976). This logic used to be called "multiple deficit" accounts (Goodman, 1989 , Pennington, 2006 , Wing and Wing, 1971), and employing it eliminates the oftentimes artificial and misleading assumption that different candidate causes of a single behavioral feature are mutually exclusive (Alger, 2019 , Pratt, 1971).

This more quantitative approach also eliminates the pernicious consequences of the binarized framework that is implicit in the question, "Does an 'impairment' in mechanism X cause Y feature(s) of autism?" This logic prompts the questions, "What threshold of function in X qualifies as an 'impairment'?" and "How much of behavior Y is deemed 'clinically relevant'?" It also leads to an unnecessary appeal to theoretical virtues such as uniqueness (Does a particular mechanistic "impairment" lead consistently to the autism phenotype and not to other outcomes?) and universality (Is a single mechanistic "impairment" a necessary cause of autism?) (Rajendran and Mitchell, 2007).

Instead of placing such high inferential burdens on the data, we simply ask to what degree the variation in a particular mechanistic construct causes linear or non-linear variation in autism-relevant behavioral function.

And when we consider multiple mechanisms, not all effects need be simply additive. Some interactions between multiple mechanistic contributors may be supra-additive (Valla and Belmonte, 2013); others may represent compensatory pathways. For example, an early autism theory that was proposed as a forme fruste of deafblindness, in which sufficient function in either modality would have allowed the individual to compensate for the other and gain the minimal developmentally necessary social input via the typically functioning sensory modality (Wing, 1971). And some historical theories may be combined together in serial chains (e.g., the activity of so-called Mirror Neuron System regions causing altered intention-understanding (Williams et al., 2001), and altered intention-understanding causing challenges in social interaction (Frith et al., 1991)).

Accumulating Prior Results

Now where do we start? Conceptually, we have begun by accruing behavioral and mechanistic constructs and causal claims from the literature (Tables 1 and 2). Empirically, we already have data from something like 100,000 "autism" papers. The first step toward an atlas would be to merge the two, prior to planning an additional data collection (which, of course, is continuing anyway). By accurately integrating existing data around the validity of individual constructs, how they differ between groups, whether edges exist between constructs and what their effect sizes are, we can begin to sparsify the candidate claim space and identify the highest-yield targets for future data collection (Fig. 2).

But I do not believe we can do so simply by querying an LLM to summarize the evidence for and against individual constructs and edges, which is likely to be rooted in an uncritical acceptance of the authors' interpretation of their findings. And even beyond the need to reinterpret data critically, we believe that there are additional challenges in integrating literature over decades and different sub-fields of psychology and cognitive neuroscience.

For one thing, the use of terminology has changed over time, in several different ways. Firstly, different labels have been used for the same construct in different historical periods. Consider the case of EF. While EF research in autism under that label commenced in the 1980s and 1990s (Posner and Petersen, 1990), both psychological research broadly and autism research had, as early as the 1940's, examined what was then called the "abstract attitude"; it was even linked to frontal-lobe damage (Goldstein, 1941), which was, half a century later, the inciting case for the development of the EF construct. Evidence collected under the label "abstract attitude" would have to be integrated into the more recent research under the label of EF.

Secondly, terminology has been used loosely in such a way that different constructs have been collapsed under the same label. The term "local connectivity" has been applied to measures that report on very different spatial scales, from minicolumns (~50 μm) (Buxhoeveden and Casanova, 2002) up to neighboring voxels in MRI tractography (up to 35 mm) (Belmonte et al., 2004a , Shukla et al., 2010 , Vasa et al., 2016), a nearly three-order-of-magnitude difference. The underlying neurobiology of these spatial scales is very different, and it is hard to imagine that data relevant to one spatial scale would be directly informative about a hypothesis around the other. Any extraction of data from papers will need to make sure that distinct constructs are not accidentally lumped under the same label.

For another thing, contemporaneous inferences from data were made under assumptions that have been revised. The falsification of language-primary theories of autism using a unidimensional hemispheric function construct, as discussed earlier, needs to be reconsidered in light of the evidence that has, in turn, falsified the unidimensional hemispheric function construct. Similarly, the major work that falsified the broadest causal claims around WCC (Happé and Frith, 2006) did so by showing that it does not explain ToM, which at the time was accepted as a proxy for the whole autism social phenotype. That assumption has changed (Fletcher-Watson and Happé, 2019), and it is conceivable that additional research would be needed to determine whether WCC explains current-day conceptualizations of the autism social phenotype.

Finally, the conceptualization of autism itself has changed over time, in a way that will impact contemporary reinterpretation and integration of prior results. Different diagnostic criteria or thresholds mean that integrating results from two different time periods will almost certainly introduce biases (Frith, 2026 , Happe and Frith, 2020 , Mottron and Bzdok, 2020 , Rosen et al., 2021). This challenge is amplified when integrating over different era-specific proxies for the social phenotype (social withdrawal, ToM, joint attention).

Fortunately, recent research tends to honor the heterogeneity of the phenotype to a greater degree. The old maxim that, "if you've met one person with autism, you've met one person with autism," (S. Shore) means that any statistical model trying to explain two people with nonoverlapping features is going to contain a lot of noise (though see also Constantino et al., 2021, but then see McWeeny and Ewen, 2026, Waterhouse et al., 2016). How to lump vs. split the heterogeneous autism construct is the construct-specificity problem all over again. And it explains why the atlas would promote modeling individual features of autism rather than the heterogenous and evolving autism diagnosis as a whole. New, empirically-derived tools, including the Stanford Social Dimensions Scale (SSDS) (Phillips et al., 2019 , Uljarevic et al., 2020) and Dimensional Assessment of Repetitive Behavior (DARB) (Uljarevic et al., 2023) extend a decades-old tradition of finding subgroups within the autistic population (DeMyer et al., 1971 , Lotter, 1966 , Wing and Gould, 1979) and provide empirically distinct social and RRB constructs to be explained by future efforts.

However, even when we work to explain individual features rather than the diagnosis as a whole, the literature from different eras will be differentially impacted by sampling (e.g., recruitment) based on whatever diagnostic criteria were current at the time of each study. A separate but related issue is that commonly-used purposive sampling on the syndrome of autism has the potential to generate false-positive associations (edges) between constructs that would be falsified when transdiagnostic, random or population-based (i.e., random) sampling.

We should also note the possibility of literature-wide bases in evidence accumulation. Two prominent ones include selection bias (Nebel et al., 2022), based on who is able to participate in research and leading to issues of generalizability, and publication bias (Sandbank et al., 2024), which may bias toward positive results and undercount disconfirmatory (or at least within-thebounds-of-uncertainty) results.

However, once we integrate the results of the existing of 100,000 autism papers and whoknows-how-many "non-autism" papers that nevertheless have a bearing on this program, we can both log these methodological limitations as subjects for further empirical investigation. And the clean results that we do achieve can be used in a variety of different applications, including guiding novel mechanistic work.

Using the Causal Knowledge Embedded in the Atlas

Guiding Novel Data Collection

There is a sense in which our proposed atlas does not do anything beyond what a perfectly informed experimentalist does not already do: evaluate the prior literature comprehensively and critically, find the most field-important gaps in that literature, and design experiments that will incontrovertibly fill those gaps. However, this atlas is conceived of to synthesize a literature that is immense, spans many different sub-fields and requires considerable nuanced integration over a century of work. It would also record measurement and sampling bias considerations. And it would do so in a way that would enhance both grant and manuscript reviews, by transparently giving reviewers and investigators access to the same prior literature and therefore placing the focus on an established rather than fanciful set of confounds. Existing efforts in related fields (Landreth and Silva, 2013, Silva, 2017) have done just this.

The causal structure that emerges from such an atlas will also allow investigators and funders to place the best-informed bets on (costly) novel intervention development.

The proposed atlas would also inform the design of large registries. One imperative that emerges from the outline of the atlas is the need for either population-based or at least transdiagnostic sampling. As noted in prior sections, the drift in diagnosis creates challenges for applying yesterday's results to today's-and tomorrow's-ASD diagnostic group. The neurodevelopmental diagnostic spectrum is likely to have a significant overhaul due, in part, to emerging evidence that neurobiology (potentially relevant to novel interventions) may not carve up in the same way that past clinical description-based nosologies have (Choi et al., 2020 , Vandewouw et al., 2023). And broader sampling is needed to avoid the false-positive edges that appear to exist only because of limited, autism-based sampling.

Applying to Clinical Decision-Making and Policy

An atlas will never be complete, but it will be progressively more functional and accurate for application in the clinic and for advancing useful policies. Specifically, causal knowledge predicts the response of system (human-society interactions) in ways that have not been directly observed-applied to humans with profiles that are similar to others but have not themselves been directly studied, interventions similar to others but have not been directly studied, etc.

Because of the ability of theoretical causal knowledge to transport to novel situations, the information contained in the atlas goes beyond the results of individual RCTs in its explanatory power.

Refining Theories

Having an atlas that contains causal claims-those that are falsified, those that are strongly supported and quantified, and those that are as yet uncertain-will also lead to improved theories. Iteratively improving theories in evidence-based (and not ego-driven) ways has been a challenging issue particularly in psychology, with a lot of ink spilled over the topic for generations (Chamberin, 1890 , Krugman, 2013 , Kukla, 2001 , Lakatos, 1970 , Pratt, 1971). Our goal is, conceptually, to build up something like "one grand model to rule them all" and explain all of features of autism-and their heterogeneity-using multiple causal pathways. We do so, in part, out of the unfalsified pieces (triples) of historical theories (Tables 1 and 2), even if we are somewhat reliant at times on the original theories, for example, to identify the locally relevant confounds. A hope is that by reducing individual theories to granular units and contrasting claims in a way that is visible to the entire research community, we can reduce the tendency of investigators to interpret findings to the advantage of pet theories (Pratt, 1971) and clear the way for more transparent refinements of theory.

One way that theories have traditionally and unfairly escaped falsification is by blaming their empirical failures on the weakness of auxiliary hypotheses (It was the measure! It was the sample! My theory is still correct though, despite the evidence.) (Kukla, 2001). These patches have frequently been offered as a defensive way to create uncertainty in the disconfirmatory evidence rather than as a guide to further research to intentionally test those auxiliary hypotheses. An atlas would make these auxiliary measurement considerations transparent in a way that can-and would have to be-tested empirically.

Conversely, an atlas that breaks down individual claims from theories could impartially save key theoretical elements from undue falsification. The conventional approach to evaluating theories-i.e., there should be multiple claims, and every claim should survive tough empirical testing (Peirce, 1960)-was designed to set a high bar to support a theory in order to avoid bias whereby an investigator would protect his/her theory from falsification. This broad-brushstroke however approach carries the risk of throwing out all of the claims of a particular theory, even the individual claims that were not specifically falsified by evidence. In the case of the atlas, each claim would be assessed separately, and yet-unfalsified claims would not be unduly killed because they exist in the same original theoretical treatment as falsified claims. This chapter would be incomplete without recognizing emerging work in more formal psychological theory construction (Borsboom et al., 2021, van Rooij and Blokpoel, 2020); more rigorously propose theories will certainly make it easier to be explicit about the content of individual causal claims.

One specific area ripe for advancement under an atlas is in the construction of multilevel models. A scientifically compelling model might include constructs from behavioral genetics, cognitive neuroscience, psychology and community-endorsed outcomes. A major challenge to date for such models has been the fact that each level of analysis represents a high-dimensional space. Accumulated knowledge from the atlas would allow for dimensional reduction (and therefore also an increase in statistical power for any particular dataset). Moreover, a construct that has the broadest and largest impact on causally-downstream constructs within one level of analysis may be the most informative target for study at a causally upstream levels of analysis.

Post Script

When I started writing versions of this chapter almost exactly 10 years ago, my focus was on promoting truly hypothesis-driven science in autism. It seemed to me at the time that that too much autism research was descriptive, hypothesis-generating and novelty focused-often constructed under the hope that the newest framework or newest neuroscience machine would offer a simple solution ex machina, as it were, that had been denied to prior generations of researchers. I contrasted this with what I would consider the more-mature science I witnessed in cognitive psychology, where rival labs would iterate over versions of the same model for decades. This mature science was rarely headline-worthy in a way that would seem to befit a scientific and clinical question with the importance of autism, but it did create durable knowledge of how certain human faculties worked-knowledge that has influenced technologyhuman interfaces; knowledge that may well be refined over time but will not be fundamentally overturned or forgotten with the next trend. It seems to me that this is precisely the sort of knowledge that the loyal opposition to the current autism research program has in mind.

Two things happened in parallel over the last decade to move from promoting traditional hypothesis-testing to proposing the construction of this sort of atlas of evidence to support a shrinking/converging causal claim space. The first was significant interest from supporters, and in particular M. Belmonte (co-editor of this volume) around the notion of how to stitch together theories rather than just testing them independently. The second was the conjunction of many developments during the last decade that supports an atlas approach-in particular, our own work using on causal statistical approaches over large datasets (e.g., Simons Powering Autism Research; SPARK) (Tang et al., 2023) and on using LLMs to integrate current knowledge within a scaffolding provided by theory (McWeeny et al., under review).

I personally have no misapprehensions that this all will be straight forward. Simply look at how and why the psychoanalytic theory of autism actually disappeared from discourse; it was not as simple as the demonstration of evidence for the mutually-exclusive neurological theory. In addition to attention being drawn toward alternative frameworks as much as away from empirically-disproven psychogenic accounts, it is also the case that the task was never quite so clear cut as mutual exclusivity: Goldfarb, in the early 1960s (Goldfarb, 1961), had held out the possibility that some autistic individuals had a psychosocial cause and others had a neurological cause. Simply demonstrating that some (or even) many children diagnosed with autism had neurological signs that could not be explained by a psychosocial cause was not actually adequate proof at the time it was reported that there was no role for a psychosocial cause. (This is not a claim on the author's part that subsequent research has proven a psychosocial cause in any case.) And even the disconfirmatory evidence that did exist did not prevent recrudescence of the primary claims of the psychogenic theory (Tinbergen and Tinbergen, 1983). Sociological factors apart from a sober weighting of the evidence are clearly evident in scientists', clinicians' and advocates' understanding of the data, but the benefit of an atlas program is that it makes transparent all of these assumptions.

Another foreseeable challenge is the fact that taxonomies (and therefore constructs) will-and should-continue to expand. This would seem to be counterintuitive to the logic of iteratively reducing the causal claim space toward a single (or at least a set of functionally equivalent) causal account(s) through falsification. However, "paradigm shifting" scientific progress is often driven by an abductive transition in the "basis set" of constructs (Zahavy, 2026). Even so, the causal knowledge contained efficiently within an atlas will make it easier for human innovators to wrap their heads around the evidentiary state of the field in order to generate these paradigm shifts.

The path will be non-linear, but at its core, the accumulation of information as exhaustively as possible and transparent to the field should be uncontroversial. And integrating it in the framework of causal knowledge is critical to answering fundamental questions about how features diagnostic for autism (and other neurodevelopmental conditions) come to be and evolve over the lifespan, why heterogeneity presents as it does, how to group individuals for specific clinical purposes, and how to intervene on features that cause distress while preserving that variation that does not is critical to being effective as advocates and clinicians.
