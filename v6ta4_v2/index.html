Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Text-based chat (TBC) services are among the most accessible mental health support for youth, yet more is known about whether they help than about how they help, that is, what workers do in sessions. Without an account of these worker skills, TBC services cannot be reliably trained for or evaluated. This study described the techniques chat workers use, drawing on the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy (BCTTv1), and linked them to userreported outcomes.

We analyzed 1,439 real chat sessions (44,916 worker turns) from 73 workers at QuickChat, an anonymous, free, synchronous TBC service for youths aged 12 to 25 in Singapore. Each worker turn was coded for the presence of 32 behavior change techniques (BCTs). We examined how often, densely, and broadly techniques were used; how far use varied across workers; and associations with three post-session outcomes: understanding, coping, and perspective.

Results: All 32 techniques appeared, but practice was concentrated in a few: social support (emotional, unspecified, and practical), problem solving, re-attribution, and framing/reframing accounted for three quarters of all instances. Use varied mainly by session, not by worker, indicating skills shared across the workforce, not individual styles. Sessions in which workers used more techniques per worker turn received slightly higher ratings on all three outcomes. Three techniques were each positively associated with all three outcomes: social support (unspecified), problem solving, and pros and cons, though effects were small. Conclusions: Chat workers deliver change through a small set of core skills, centered on social support and problem solving, and shared across the workforce. These findings offer a first step toward a program theory of TBC to guide training and evaluation.

Introduction

Many young people who struggle with mental and emotional issues do not reach professional help. Stigma, fear of being dismissed, distrust of adults, cost, and limited autonomy in accessing services are well documented barriers to help seeking among youths (Chia et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025). Text-based chat (TBC) services have grown as a response to these barriers. These services let young people reach a trained chat worker through synchronous text messaging, usually anonymously, free of charge, and without referral. They deliver support in a single session rather than a course of treatment (Hoermann et al., 2017). Text messaging is a medium young people already use daily, and studies report that chat users find these services acceptable and helpful (Gould et al., 2021). Such services now operate in many countries and handle a large volume of youth contacts each year, including the Lifeline Crisis Chat (United States; Gould et al., 2021), Open Up (Hong Kong; Yip et al., 2021), krisenchat (Germany; Eckert et al., 2022), and 113Online (Netherlands; Mokkenstorm et al., 2017), with comparable services in Canada, Denmark, and Finland (Côté & Mishara, 2022; Helfer et al., 2025; Sindahl et al., 2019).

Yet we know far more about whether TBC helps than about how it helps (Dowling & Rickwood, 2013; Tibbs et al., 2026). Outcome evaluation cannot be robust without a clear account of what the intervention involves. Not knowing what workers do leaves evaluators unable to select outcome measures sensitive to its effects or build fidelity measures against a defined model (Tibbs et al., 2026), so the resulting measurement is inappropriate and inconsistent (Curll et al., 2024). TBC cannot remain a black box; it needs a program theory that specifies what workers do to bring about change and how those activities connect to the outcomes chat users report (Carey et al., 2018). Tibbs et al. (2026) produced one of the first such theories for a single session chat service, but it was co-produced with staff and describes what the service intends to do, not what workers actually do in real chat sessions. Building that account from actual practice is missing.

The stakes are practical. For many young people, TBC is among the most accessible routes into mental health support, reaching those who avoid or cannot access face-to-face care (Gatti et al., 2016; Navarro et al., 2019). But accessibility only brings a young person to the service, and what they meet once they arrive is a separate question, one that young people raise themselves. When young adults were asked why they had not used a crisis text service they knew about, the most common answer was that they did not think it would help. When users were asked how such a service could be improved, they asked for warmer and more personal replies, and some said the chat felt scripted (LaForge et al., 2026). Read To describe what workers do in a standardized and replicable way, we draw on the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy (BCTTv1; Michie et al., 2013). A behavior change technique (BCT) is an observable, replicable component of an intervention designed to alter or redirect the causal processes that regulate behavior (Michie et al., 2013), such as providing social support, goal setting, or reframing a problem. The taxonomy gives intervention research a shared vocabulary for the active ingredients of care, and coding intervention content against it is an established method (Jäger et al., 2024). This vocabulary transfers to TBC. In this context, the target behavior is how the chat user copes with and manages the problem after the session, in school, work, and daily life, including taking positive next steps such as further help seeking (Curll et al., 2024). Techniques work by altering the processes that regulate this behavior (Michie et al., 2013): how the chat user understands the problem, how they view it, and their belief in their own ability to cope. The behavior change literature calls these processes mechanisms of action (Carey et al., 2018). Tibbs et al. (2026) mapped ten BCTs onto their chat service, showing that BCTs are present and codable in this service form. Patterns of BCT use can also point back to a service's implicit theory, even when none is stated (Bohlen et al., 2020). Coding BCTs in actual transcripts therefore offers a way to reconstruct, from the bottom up, the program theory of a TBC service (Olsson et al., 2023).

Observational research on real TBC sessions, including our own studies of the service examined here, has begun to identify what makes sessions helpful (e.g., Boonstra et al., 2026; Chung, Yee, et al., 2026; Chung & Lim, 2026; Imel et al., 2024). But this work borrows context specific typologies rather than a framework designed to capture intervention content. Chung & Lim (2026), for example, coded worker support with the Social Support Behavior Code (Cutrona & Suhr, 1992), a typology developed to classify how spouses support one another; such bespoke schemes make findings hard to compare across services or connect to the wider behavior change evidence base. This is the problem the BCT taxonomy was built to solve: without a common vocabulary, the same technique is reported under different labels, and the same label can cover different techniques (Michie et al., 2013). The taxonomy also separates the change content of a message, the technique intended to alter how a person thinks, feels, or acts, from its mode of delivery, the tone and phrasing that carry it (Michie et al., 2013). Seen this way, prior work on this service has mostly captured the mode of delivery, the tone, phrasing, and emoticons that carry a worker's message, and shown that these features predict outcomes.

What those messages do, their change content, the techniques a worker uses to help a chat user cope, reframe, or understand, has not been described with a standard taxonomy, even though it is this content, not its packaging, that a theory of change must specify.

To reconstruct the program theory, we assemble three kinds of evidence. First, what is delivered and in what dose: which BCTs appear, how often (prevalence and intensity), how much in total (volume), and how many distinct techniques a session draws on (breadth). Second, whether techniques belong to the service or to individuals. A BCT used by most workers is plausibly a shared ingredient of the service model, whereas one that varies widely across workers is closer to an individual preference, a distinction that matters for training and quality assurance. Third, whether techniques are associated with the outcomes chat users report.

Consistent with recommendations that single session chat services be judged on proximal, user reported outcomes rather than symptom scales (Tibbs et al., 2026), we examine three post session outcomes: improved coping, changed perspective, and increased understanding of one's problems. Together, these three kinds of evidence, what techniques are used, how consistently, and which track outcomes, produce a first, empirically grounded model of how the service works.

In this study, we analyzed 1,439 real chat sessions from QuickChat, an anonymous, free, synchronous TBC service for youths aged 12 to 25 years in Singapore. QuickChat does not prescribe a counseling approach for workers to follow, and it does not specify what workers do to bring about change. To reconstruct one focusing on what workers do in the single session, each worker turn was coded against a shortlist of 32 BCTs from BCTTv1. We asked three questions. First, what are the prevalence, intensity, volume, and breadth of BCTs used across sessions? Second, how much does BCT use vary across chat workers? Third, are BCTs associated with three chat user reported outcomes at post session: improved coping, changed perspective, and increased understanding? The findings provide building blocks toward a program theory of TBC. Knowing what is delivered in a real service, and what appears to work, is a first step toward explaining how this service form brings change, and toward developing TBC into a dependable part of youth mental health care.

Methods

Data and Sample

The data come from eC2.sg, the digital mental health platform of Fei Yue Community Services in Singapore, which operates QuickChat, an anonymous text-based chatline for youths aged 12 to 25 years. QuickChat provides free, immediate, synchronous chat: users register with only an email address and are assigned to an available chat worker on duty. The service operates as a one-session brief intervention and triage platform rather than long term therapy, with the primary objectives of immediate emotional support, situation assessment, and referral (Chung, Lim, et al., 2026; Chung & Lim, 2026). Chat workers are clinical professionals (social workers and counsellors) or trained volunteers.

The corpus comprises 1,854 chat sessions from 2016 to 2020. Within each session, consecutive messages by the same speaker were combined into a single turn, so turns alternate between the chat user and the chat worker; worker turns were the focal unit of coding. We retained sessions whose chat users completed the post session evaluation questionnaire, giving 44,916 worker turns across 1,439 sessions (mean 31.2 per session), delivered by 73 chat workers (median 8 sessions per worker). Because service use is anonymous, demographic characteristics of chat users are not available.

Approval for use of the data was obtained from the agency and research ethics approval was obtained from [Blinded for Review] University.

Measures

Behavior change techniques.

We coded chat workers' use of BCTs using a shortlist of 32 techniques drawn from the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy v1 (BCTTv1; Michie et al., 2013). The shortlist began with the techniques that Tibbs et al. (2026) identified in a comparable single session youth chat service, where intervention components described by service staff in co-design workshops were mapped onto BCTTv1. A senior chat worker who supervises the service then selected additional BCTTv1 techniques assessed to be relevant to the type of change work done by chat workers. Technique definitions followed the BCTTv1 wording. The full list of the 32 techniques and their definitions is provided in the Supplementary File.

The unit of coding was the worker turn. Given 44,916 turns and 32 techniques, automated coding was necessary. Following the approach validated in our previous study (Chung & Lim, 2026), we used a privacy preserving large language model deployed locally (OpenAI's open weight gpt-oss 20b). Each worker turn was presented with the chat user turns immediately before and after it as context, with instructions to label the focal turn only. For every technique, the prompt gave the BCTTv1 definition and a chat context example, and required a presence judgment ("yes", "no", "not sure") made independently for each technique, so a turn could carry multiple techniques. "Not sure" responses were conservatively recoded as absent. Full documentation of the labelling pipeline is provided in the Supplementary Material.

To validate the LLM labels, two human coders independently coded a random sample of 200 worker turns against the same technique definitions. Inter-coder agreement, pooled across the 32 techniques, was almost perfect (Cohen's kappa = 0.90); disagreements were resolved through discussion to produce a gold standard set of labels. Agreement between the LLM labels and this gold standard was substantial (kappa = 0.80).

From the turn level codes we built session level measures. For each technique, the primary session metric was its rate per worker turn (count of turns using it divided by the number of worker turns), which accounts for the number of worker turns. We summarized overall BCT use with three metrics: breadth (distinct BCTs used, range 0 to 32), volume (total BCT events), and intensity (volume divided by length, the number of worker turns). While breadth and volume both scale with session length, intensity is independent of session length.

Chat user reported outcomes.

At the end of every session, the QuickChat platform directs chat users to a post session evaluation questionnaire. We used three items, each rated on a 5 point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to5 = Strongly Agree): • Understanding: "I understand my issues better"

• Coping: "I am able to cope with my issues better"

• Perspective: "I am able to face my issues with a different perspective"

We analyzed the three outcomes separately and did not combine them into a composite.

These items focus on change in the chat user, in how they understand, cope with, and view their problems, consistent with recent work on outcome measurement for helpline services, which distinguishes such help-seeker change from service experience and satisfaction and finds the latter to be prioritised by researchers more than by help-seekers themselves (Curll et al., 2024).

Analytical Method

Analyses followed the three study aims. All data cleaning and analyses were conducted using R (Version 4.5.3; R Core Team, 2026) and RStudio (Version 2026.4.0.526; Posit team, 2026).

Aim 1: Prevalence, intensity, volume, and breadth.

We described each technique's prevalence as the share of worker turns containing it and the share of sessions using it at least once. We then estimated the correlations between the three session metrics (intensity, volume, and breadth) and the three outcomes using Spearman rank correlations, chosen because the metrics are right skewed and the ratings are bounded with a strong ceiling. Because session length can inflate volume and breadth, we controlled for session length and computed partial Spearman rank correlations for these two metrics.

Aim 2: Variation across chat workers.

This aim asks whether a technique is a shared ingredient of the service model, used at a similar level across the workforce, or reflects a worker's characteristic engagement style, drawn on more by some workers than by others. The distinction matters for training and quality assurance: a technique that varies mainly from session to session is one the workforce applies in common and can anchor training and fidelity standards, whereas a technique that depends heavily on the worker reflects differences in engagement style across the workforce.

To make this distinction, we computed intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) from null random intercept multilevel models across the 73 chat workers, with sessions nested within workers. These are "null" models in that they contain no predictors; they only partition the variation in a technique's use into a between worker component and a within worker (session to session) component. The ICC is the proportion of the total variation that lies between workers: a low ICC means workers use the technique to a similar degree, so its use varies mainly with the session, whereas a high ICC means use depends on the worker, reflecting a difference in engagement style. We estimated two ICCs per technique, which capture different aspects of use: an intensity ICC, from a linear model of the technique's per turn rate, for how much the technique is used; and an occurrence ICC, from a logistic model of whether the technique was used in the session at all, for whether the worker draws on it at all.

Aim 3: Associations between BCTs and outcomes.

We fitted one linear mixed model per technique per outcome, regressing the 1 to 5 rating on the technique's per turn rate for the session. As defined for Aim 1, this rate is the count of worker turns using the technique divided by the total number of worker turns in the session, so it expresses how often a worker drew on the technique relative to how much they said, rather than a raw count that would simply grow with session length. The rate was standardized (z scored across sessions) before entry, so its coefficient reflects a one standard deviation difference in how heavily the technique was used. We fitted a separate model for each technique rather than entering all techniques together because the aim is descriptive, to characterize each technique's association with outcomes one at a time, and because the techniques are correlated in use, which would make coefficients from a single joint model difficult to interpret. Session length (the number of worker turns), also standardized, was included as a covariate so that any association reflects how heavily the technique was used rather than how long the session ran. The random intercept for the chat worker accounts for the nesting of sessions within workers. Coefficients (b) are the change in the rating, in rating points, per one standard deviation increase in the technique's rate, adjusted for session length and for clustering within worker. Because we fitted many models (32 techniques by three outcomes), p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

Results

Aim 1: Prevalence, Intensity, Volume, and Breadth

The 1,439 sessions contained 44,916 worker turns and 22,519 coded BCT instances, where one instance is one technique coded as present in one worker turn. Every one of the 32 techniques we coded for appeared in at least one worker turn; none went unused. Even so, use was concentrated in a small set of techniques (Table 1). Social support (emotional) appeared in 12.9% of worker turns and in 82.1% of sessions, social support (unspecified) in 6.3% of turns (65.6% of sessions), and social support (practical) in 5.8% of turns (62.7% of sessions). Together these three social support related techniques made up half of all BCT instances (49.9%). Problem solving (4.8% of turns), re-attribution (4.3%), and framing and reframing (4.2%) came next.

These six techniques made up three quarters (76.4%) of all BCT instances. Most techniques saw little use: 25 of the 32 each appeared in fewer than 2% of worker turns. Grouped into BCTTv1 clusters, the social support cluster accounted for 49.9% of all BCT instances, followed by shaping knowledge (14.9%) and goals and planning (13.6%; Supplementary Table S2).

At the session level, we summarized technique use with three metrics. Breadth is the number of distinct techniques a session used at least once. Volume is the total number of BCT instances in the session. Intensity is volume divided by the number of worker turns, so it measures how densely techniques were packed into a session; unlike breadth and volume, it does not rise simply because a session is longer. Sessions drew on several techniques: the average session used 6.1 distinct BCTs (SD = 3.7) with a volume of 15.6 BCT instances (SD = 14.4), giving an intensity of 0.47 BCT instances per worker turn (SD = 0.27). In other words, techniques appeared at a rate of about one for every two worker turns.

Table 2 shows the Spearman correlations between the three session metrics and the three chat user reported outcomes: understanding, coping, and perspective, each rated 1 to 5 with higher meaning better. Intensity correlated positively with all three outcomes (rho = .068 to .090, all p < .05). Breadth and volume, adjusted for session length, were also positively correlated with all three outcomes (breadth: partial rho = .053 to .070; volume: partial rho = .061 to .075; all p < .05). In other words, sessions in which workers used more techniques, and used them more densely, tended to receive slightly higher ratings. All the correlations were small (rho below .10), so technique use explains only a small part of how chat users rated their sessions.

Aim 2: Variation Across Chat Workers

The 1,439 sessions were delivered by 73 chat workers (median 8 sessions per worker).

Many techniques were widespread across the workers (Supplementary Table S3): 17 of the 32 techniques were used by more than half of workers, and 7 by more than 80%. The technique with most widespread use were social support (emotional; 96% of workers), social support (unspecified; 93%), and problem solving (90%). The least widespread, mental rehearsal of successful performance, was used by 12% of workers. This percentage, however, only shows how many workers have ever used a technique. A worker who used a technique once counts the same as a worker who uses it in every session, so the percentage cannot tell us how far technique use depends on the individual worker.

The ICC (intra-class coefficients) addresses this because ICC is the share of the variation in a technique's use that lies between workers: a high ICC means use depends on who the worker is, while a low ICC means use varies with the session rather than the worker. We report two ICCs per technique (Table 3): the occurrence ICC, for how much the worker determines whether a technique is used at all in a session, and the intensity ICC, for how much the worker determines how heavily it is used. The final column of Table 3 flags which of the two is larger, that is, whether the worker's influence shows more in whether the technique appears or in how much it is used.

Both ICCs were low. The median occurrence ICC was 10.6% and the median intensity ICC was 4.0%: for the typical technique, only about 11% of the variation in whether it appeared in a session, and about 4% of the variation in how heavily it was used, could be traced to who the worker was. The rest of the variation, roughly 89% and 96%, lay between the sessions of the same worker, who would draw on a technique in one session but not the next, and use it more in one and less in another, depending on the session in front of them. Read together, the occurrence ICC (whether a technique is used at all) and the intensity ICC (how much it is used) point the same way: which techniques are used, and how much, depends far more on the session than on the individual worker.

Ratings averaged 3.81 (SD = 1.20) for understanding, 3.70 (SD = 1.23) for coping, and 3.72 (SD = 1.23) for perspective.

Thirteen of the 96 associations were significant, involving six techniques, all positive. The coefficient b is the change in the outcome rating when a technique's rate per worker turn increases by one standard deviation. Three techniques were associated with all three outcomes: social support unspecified (b = 0.099 to 0.118, adjusted p = .015 to .027), problem solving (b = 0.096 to 0.114, adjusted p = .015 to .030), and pros and cons (b = 0.089 to 0.102, adjusted p = .025 to .042). Social support unspecified and problem solving were among the most prevalent techniques, whereas pros and cons was rarely used (0.3% of worker turns, 6.3% of sessions).

Re-attribution was associated with understanding (b = 0.094, adjusted p = .030) and perspective (b = 0.105, adjusted p = .025). Instruction on how to perform a behavior (b = 0.100, adjusted p = .025) and information about antecedents (b = 0.093, adjusted p = .028) were associated with understanding only. Social support emotional, the most prevalent technique, was positively associated with understanding before adjustment (b = 0.084, p = .008), but this association fell just short of significance after adjustment (adjusted p = .053). Its associations with coping and perspective were weaker (adjusted p = .084 and .198). Framing and reframing and social support practical were used often but were not associated with any outcome (all adjusted p > .22). No technique was negatively associated with any outcome. unspecified, and practical), problem solving, re-attribution, and framing and reframing together made up three quarters of all BCT instances. These six techniques work on three processes: social support gives the chat user care, encouragement, and practical help, re-attribution and framing and reframing change how the user understands and views their problem, and problem solving helps the user work out what to do next and how to cope (Carey et al., 2018). In a chat these are concrete moves: suggesting that a problem may have a different cause than the one the user assumes (re-attribution), offering another way to see the situation (framing and reframing), or working through the options for a next step (problem solving). The outcome results partly support this account. Re-attribution was associated with chat users' increased understanding and changes in perspective while problem solving was associated with all three outcomes. In summary, QuickChat is therefore a service centered on social support, which made up half of all BCT instances, supplemented by techniques that change how users think about their problems and techniques that help them plan. The combination of BCTs a service uses reflects its underlying theory (Bohlen et al., 2020). This profile therefore provides a preliminary account of QuickChat's program theory.

Discussion

The second finding matters because a program theory describes the service, not Even so, sessions with more instances per turn, more instances in total, and a wider range of techniques had slightly better outcomes, although these associations were small. One explanation is that each instance gives the session another chance to move one of the processes described above, and a wider range of techniques raises the chance that one of them fits the user's problem. A program theory for TBC should therefore consider not only which techniques workers use but also how much technique use a session carries. Future work can test whether training workers to use techniques more often per turn (intensity) and to draw on a wider range of them (breadth) improves outcomes.

The second direction concerns social support and problem solving. Both were among the most used techniques, and each was associated with all three outcomes. In a chat, social support (unspecified) is the worker encouraging the user as they work toward coping with the problem, and problem solving is the worker helping the user analyze what stands in the way and choose a way forward. Together they point to a candidate active ingredient, supportive problem solving: encouraging the user while helping them work out what to do next. Each technique was tested on its own, however, so whether the two work best in combination remains to be tested.

The problem solving result is consistent with reviews of health behavior interventions, which have linked better outcomes to self-regulatory techniques such as goal setting, from the same BCTTv1 cluster (Dombrowski et al., 2012; Webb et al., 2010). The social support result is not, and the setting may explain why. Those interventions aim to sustain a behavior over weeks or months, such as exercise or diet, whereas a single session chat supports the user through a present problem. In that setting, the worker's encouragement may itself help the user cope.

Social support may strengthen the user's belief in their ability to cope (Schwarzer & Knoll, 2007) and help them reframe the problem as more manageable (Feeney & Collins, 2015a , 2015b).

Social support (emotional), the most used technique, showed a weaker pattern: its association with understanding fell just short of significance after adjustment (adjusted p = .053), and its associations with coping and perspective were weaker. Because it appeared in 82% of sessions, there was little variation in its use from which to detect an association. It may also act as the base on which a session's change work is built, rather than as a driver of change on its own.

The third direction concerns pros and cons. In a chat, this is the worker helping the user weigh the reasons for and against a step they are considering, a technique known in motivational interviewing as decisional balance. It appeared in only 6.3% of sessions and 0.3% of worker turns, yet it was associated with all three outcomes (b = 0.089 to 0.102). This makes it a candidate for a larger role in the service. Future work could examine when workers use it, how it combines with problem solving, and why it is used so rarely.

The fourth direction concerns the core techniques that were used often but were not associated with outcomes after adjustment: framing and reframing, social support (practical), and social support (emotional). This does not mean they have no value. We tested their rate of use, not how well they were used or how well they fitted the chat user's problem. These techniques likely depend on that fit: a reframe or a piece of practical help is useful when it matches the user's situation, not when it is frequent. The open question for these techniques is therefore quality and fit, not amount. Testing this would mean rating how well each use of a technique matches the problem the chat user describes and how well it is carried out, rather than counting how often it appears.

Limitations

The effects of the techniques were not tested with a rigorous controlled design.

Techniques were observed as workers used them rather than assigned to sessions, so sessions in which a technique appeared may differ from other sessions in ways we did not measure. The data also came from one service for youths in Singapore, and worker practices may differ in other services and countries (Higuera-Lozano et al., 2026). The analysis included only sessions whose chat users completed the post session evaluation, and these users may differ from those who did not complete it. In addition, we measured how often techniques were used, not how well. Two sessions with the same count of a technique can differ in how skillfully the technique was delivered, and our counts do not capture this difference. We also examined each technique on its own and did not test how techniques work in combination. Techniques are typically used in groups rather than alone (Bohlen et al., 2020), and effectiveness often depends on the combination rather than any single technique (Michie et al., 2015), which may be one reason the associations we found were small.

Recommendations for Practice

Our findings begin to open the black box of TBC and give services a program theory to work from (Figure 1). In it, change is delivered through a small set of core techniques that every and build fidelity measures to check that the techniques are delivered (Tibbs et al., 2026).

These recommendations concern what a worker's messages do, their change technique.

A message also has a second layer, its mode of delivery, which is how it is written, including the tone, the phrasing, and features such as emoticons. The taxonomy keeps these two layers apart. Michie et al. (2013) defined techniques by their mechanism of change rather than their mode of delivery, and removed "tailored personal message" from the taxonomy because it was a mode of delivery, not a technique. Both layers matter. The same technique can be written warmly or flatly, and our earlier work on this service found that the mode of delivery also predicts how chat users rate the session (Chung & Lim, 2026). Training and supervision should therefore develop both, the techniques a worker uses and the way the worker writes them.

Finally, we recommend that TBC services treat each session as a single session intervention. Single session interventions are structured programs that intentionally involve only one encounter with a provider, designed so that a single session can produce meaningful change (Schleider et al., 2023). Our findings suggest TBC already works in this form. A single session intervention requires that its one encounter be intentionally structured to do change work, not an unplanned or incidental contact. Our finding that the core techniques were shared across the workforce, rather than left to individual workers, shows that this change work is deliberate rather than incidental. Each session therefore carries structured technique use within one contact. That literature offers concrete resources for practice, including a training stance in which workers treat every session as potentially the last while assuming it can still help, session designs in which the chat user practices a coping skill and leaves with a concrete next step, and outcome expectations matched to what one contact can achieve (Schleider et al., 2023).

Knowing what produces change within a session is what would make an accessible entry point dependable: a specified account of what the service delivers, which workers can be trained in and evaluators or funders can hold it to.

This study used real transcripts to describe what chat workers do in a TBC service. The findings speak to the program theory of TBC in two ways. One set of findings shows what the service delivers now: which techniques workers use, in what amounts, and whether the techniques belong to the service or to individual workers. The other set shows how the program theory can develop: which techniques, and how much of them, were associated with the outcomes chat users reported, and what still needs testing. We take each in turn. The first set of findings shows what QuickChat chat service delivers. Workers drew on a wide range of techniques, but six formed the core of practice: social support (emotional, individual workers.

If each worker had their own preferred techniques, what QuickChat delivers would depend on who happens to answer the chat, and there would be no single service model to describe. Our ICCs show the opposite: technique use varied mainly from session to session, not from worker to worker. The core techniques are therefore shared across the workforce rather than tied to particular workers. Because they are shared, these techniques can be specified as the service model, taught to every worker, and used as the standard for fidelity checks. Some worker variation remained, and for the core techniques it lay more in how heavily a technique was used (intensity) than in whether it was used (occurrence). This was clearest for social support. For the three social support techniques, the worker accounted for 6.2% to 10.2% of the variation in whether the technique was used in a session (occurrence ICC), but for 8.7% to 19.4% of the variation in how heavily it was used (intensity ICC). In other words, what the service delivers is largely shared, and what varies by worker is the dose. A program theory for QuickChat can therefore specify the core skills as part of the service model, while treating how heavily each is used as a point on which workers differ. The findings so far describe what QuickChat delivers now. The next question is how the program theory can develop, and the outcome findings point to four directions.
