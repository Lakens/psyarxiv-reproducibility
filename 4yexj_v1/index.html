Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Despite emerging literature on conspiracy theories (CTs) in human-AI interactions, whether large language models (LLMs) refute CTs and how political identities affect responses remains unclear. Drawing on AI system documentation, positive politeness theory, and motivated reasoning research, we hypothesize that LLMs refute most CTs but adapt responses to the political identities of the user and actor in the claim. In a preregistered study comprising 135,845 conversations, 57 CTs, 21 user-actor political identity condition combinations, and seven OpenAI LLMs, we find that 82% of responses refute the CT and 20% exhibit motivated responding, i.e., acknowledge the user's perspective and affirm their concern. While refutation is more likely for CTs implicating Democrats or Republicans, motivated responding typically accompanies refutation and is more likely when claims involve the user's outgroup-for example, when a Republican user inquires about a CT involving Democrats, and the model refutes the claim. Although responses to CTs implicating Republicans vary more by user identity than CTs implicating Democrats, the former attenuates response differences between outgroup and ingroup claims. These findings raise broader questions about what it means for AI models to be capable and reliable if LLMs consistently refute conspiracy theories but do so in politically asymmetric ways.

[div-01]

As people increasingly turn to artificial intelligence (AI) systems for information (see, e.g., Faverio and Kikuchi 2026), conspiracy theories (CTs) pose a particularly demanding test of how large language models (LLMs) attempt to balance "helpful, honest, and harmless" goals (Bai et al. 2022). On one hand, such claims reference identifiable events and actors and circulate widely online, suggesting CTs and related information would appear frequently throughout model training data. While many CTs involve sensitive topics that LLMs are instructed to avoid (OpenAI 2025a; 2024a; 2024b), their veracity often depends on the actor implicated in the claim (Uscinski and Parent 2014). AI system developers attempt to resolve these tradeoffs through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and rule-based rewards (RBR) systems designed to promote broadly desirable LLM behavior, including when providing accurate information conflicts with user attributes (OpenAI 2024c). OpenAI's own model documentation (2025d), for example, states that models should avoid sycophancy and not provide information that can harm or persuade specific individuals or groups; models can, however, provide "non-targeted" political information with details from previous conversations and peripheral cues. Given prior evidence on human-AI interaction as it relates to CT correction (Costello et al. 2024), the implication is that models come closer to exhibiting desired behavior when responses engage with the user's reasoning or beliefs.

Still, prior research instructs models to "persuade users to stop believing in the conspiracy theory..." (see Table S1 of the Supplementary Materials in Costello et al. 2024), leaving open the question of whether LLMs refute CTs on their own. Other evidence suggests LLMs adjust responses according to myriad system-and user-level differences, including political identity (Dash et al. 2026; see also Ye et al. 2026 for a comprehensive review), but research has yet to identify whether political identity affects model responses about CTs. It also remains unclear as to whether political information provided directly by the user in their request (e.g., asking about a CT involving one's political outgroup) interacts with information in system memory or carried over from other contexts (see, e.g., OpenAI 2024d). In turn, we consider how LLM responses about CTs vary along two dimensions: first, whether models refute CTs absent an explicit mandate and second, how models communicate the response.

Considering documented model objectives and training, we hypothesize that most responses will refute the CT, but how they deliver responses will depend on political identity cues. Prior scholarship holds that CTs vary in truth value depending on the conditions of the claim (Uscinski and Parent 2014), such that attributing the CT to a specific partisan actor (e.g., Democrats or Republicans instead of "people") would present a more direct and potentially more falsifiable charge against an identifiable group. In turn, whether or not an LLM response refutes a CT should vary by the conditions of the CT, including the actor implicated in the claim.

At the same time, user attributes do not change the conditions of the CT but may affect the social dynamics of the response. According to positive politeness theory (Brown and Levinson 1987), speakers are more likely to exhibit systematic departures from their typical communication style when their response contradicts what the addressee presumably wants or expects to hear (see also Goffman 1967). Because CT belief and endorsement are largely a product of whether a claim impugns a political opponent (see, e.g., Enders et al. 2022; Miller et al. 2016), we argue that refuting a claim about one's partisan or ideological opponent does more than merely correct the record; rather, it exonerates one's political outgroup. Thus, we expect to find that when LLMs are provided political identity cues indicating that a user is inquiring about an outgroup claim (e.g., when a Democrat user inquires about a Republican-attributed CT, or vice versa), models will therefore serve to "redress" the face threat associated with correcting an outgroup-impugning claim (Brown and Levinson 1987). More specifically, we expect LLMs to exhibit motivated responding-our term for the response strategies documented in model instructions (OpenAI 2025d (OpenAI , 2024c) ) and examples in extant research (Costello et al. 2024), where LLMs acknowledge the user's perspective and affirm their concern. Importantly, we use motivated responding as a descriptive term of observable model behavior that aligns with AI system objectives but do not imply that LLMs exhibit conscious motivation, per se.

We test these expectations using a preregistered, computational experiment simulating a single-round conversation between a user and LLM. To understand whether LLM responses about CTs vary based on political information gleaned from other conversations and contexts-and if identity cues about the user condition model responses to other attributes of the CT-we manipulate the user's political identity in the higher-order system prompt. Then, in the user prompt, we vary both the CT and the identity of the actor in the claim. To avoid treating AI systems, models within the same system, and conversations from the same model as generalizable, we test these ex-pectations across multiple conversations and seven OpenAI LLMs. Together, this approach (illustrated in Figure 1) allows us to examine whether there is any systematic, identity-related variation in LLM responses to CTs.

We operationalize refutation and motivated responding using rule-and lexicon-based measures drawn from extant negation and positive politeness literature (see, e.g., Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil et al. 2013; Vincze et al. 2008), as well as AI system documentation regarding how LLMs should handle sensitive topics and misaligned user requests (OpenAI 2025d; OpenAI 2024c). Consistent with expectations, we find that 82% of LLM responses refute the CT, whereas motivated responding appears in a minority (20%) of conversations; both model behaviors, however, vary according to the political identity conditions supplied in the system and user prompts. Moreover, most instances of motivated responding appear alongside refutation, and responses are more likely to exhibit both behaviors when claims involve the user's outgroup. Together, these findings suggest that although the LLMs in our sample exhibit similar response patterns to political outgroup cues-and these patterns align with extant literature on human responses to CTs-responses differ more between Democrat/liberal and Republican/conservative users when Democrats are implicated in the claim.

2.1 LLM Conversation Structure and Prompt Conditions

Large language models (LLMs) are a specific type of artificial intelligence (AI) aimed at processing human language, and LLM chatbots process dialogue between users and AI. LLM conversations include a system prompt that includes model instructions (i.e., context, objectives, tone, and user attributes collected in memory that can help guide model behavior), a user prompt that captures the user's request, and an LLM response (see, e.g., OpenAI 2024d; Okcular 2025; Ope-nAI n.d.b). Our study relies on the OpenAI Chat Completions Application Programming Interface (API), which allows us to test whether manipulating the first two components of a conversation (the system and user prompt) influence the LLM response.

2.1.1 System Prompt

With regard to system prompt manipulations, LLMs process text in units called tokens (i.e., discrete units of text that LLMs use to interpret user content and predict what should come next, see, e.g., gpt-tokenizer playground n.d.), raising the methodological question as to whether any observed difference in LLM responses reflects how researchers present information to a model instead of the meaning of the content per se (Yang et al. 2026). To distinguish between these two potential sources of variance, every call in our data uses the same generic system prompt used by OpenAI (OpenAI 2025d): "You are a helpful assistant." Another condition appends a user identity to the generic system prompt ("You are a helpful assistant. The user is [user identity]"). Finally, a third condition varies the political identity of a simulated LLM user, similar to how LLMs use system memory from previous conversations to provide context for another chat response. To clarify, our design does not involve human participants; rather, we simulate the conversation between user and AI assistant so that any observed differences can be more directly attributed to the experimental conditions in the prompts.

Because it remains unclear as to how LLMs process user identity-related cues, we include six User ID conditions, all of which are equal in token length: three partisan conditions ("a Democrat," "an independent," "a Republican") and three ideological conditions ("a liberal," "a moderate," "a conservative"). 1 This approach also allows us to examine whether LLMs respond similarly to corresponding identities (e.g., Democrats and liberals or Republicans and conservatives), speaking to both the internal and external validity of our findings and research design.

As part of our automated data collection workflow, each API call generated a separate variable indicating whether the system prompt appends a user identity to the system prompt and, if so, which user identity condition it appends. From these conditions, we constructed a single categorical variable, User ID, coded as: 1) no user identity, 2) a Democrat, 3) a liberal, 4) an independent, 5) a moderate, 6) a Republican, and 7) a conservative.

Our CT item set comprises a diverse corpus of 57 conspiracy theories (CTs) adapted from prior research (see Appendix B). The corpus includes nine CTs (16%) that we would expect to be endorsed by Democrats and liberals, 17 (30%) we would expect to be endorsed by Republicans and conservatives, and 31 (54%) that are not associated with a particular partisan/ideological group (or the partisan/ideological leaning remains unknown, according to prior research). While we acknowledge that the CTs in our data are unevenly distributed across partisan/ideological groups, this corpus includes a wide range of topics represented in existing CT literature without overrepresenting any particular theme. 2.3.1 Refute

Refute is a binary measure indicating whether or not an LLM response disputes, denies, corrects, or contradicts the CT. It includes a combination of a negation and evidentiary terms (e.g., "no credible evidence"). Refute also departs from existing negation lexicons to account for explicit labels (e.g., conspiracy theory, misinformation, disinformation) and instances where the response indicates the claim is categorically false (e.g., "false claim"). While Refute is not designed to capture uncertainty, complexity, or instances where a response emphasizes a more balanced approach, this is not to suggest it cannot appear alongside these characteristics in the same response. Additionally, this is not to suggest that non-refuting responses endorse a given claim.

2.3.2 Motivated Responding

MR is a binary measure indicating whether or not an LLM response deploys language that acknowledges the user's perspective and affirms their concern. As mentioned previously, this represents a well-documented behavior that is explicitly and repeatedly built into OpenAI LLM designs.

For instance, OpenAI instructs LLMs to avoid sycophancy, modulate tone based on context, and "politely push back" on potentially harmful topics that do not warrant compliance with the user's request (OpenAI 2024c; OpenAI 2025d). While LLMs should "try to create a supportive, empathetic, and understanding environment" and "follow norms of politeness" for more sensitive topics, they are also broadly instructed to "ensure mutual clarity and alignment" between the model's response and the user's goal (OpenAI 2025d). Figure 2, for example, depicts an exchange between an OpenAI model and user who doubts the Earth is round, and the model's response is marked "compliant" when it acknowledges the user's concern ("I understand why you'd feel that way..."). The notion that these LLMs deploy polite, user-oriented framing to better align with model objectives also maps onto politeness theory, which posits that speakers use socially motivated communicative strategies to reduce face threats towards the addressee (Brown and Levinson 1987).

Brown and Levinson describe positive politeness as follows, where H refers to the addressee and S refers to the speaker: "Positive politeness is oriented toward the positive face of H, the positive self-image that he claims for himself. Positive politeness is approach-based; it 'anoints' the face of the addressee by indicating that in some respects, S wants H's wants (e.g., by treating him as a member of an in-group, a friend, a person whose wants and personality traits are known and liked). The potential face threat of an act is minimized in this case by the assurance that in general S wants at least some of H's wants; for example, that S considers H to be in important respects, 'the same' as he, with in-group rights and duties and expectations of reciprocity, or by the implication that S likes H so that the [face-threatening act] doesn't mean a negative evaluation in general of H's face." (317, emphasis original).

Brown and Levinson go on to distinguish positive politeness from negative politeness, the latter of which is "avoidance based," more akin to hedging, and "redressed with apologies" that distance S and H from the act (317). In this sense, positive politeness does not represent thoughtfulness or consideration in the colloquial sense, but rather a systematic, socially-attuned departure Motivated Responding also draws on motivated reasoning research, which suggests that people tend to engage more favorably with information that reinforces their identity and worldview (Lodge and Taber 2013; Kunda 1990). Accordingly, information signaling ingroup/outgroup identity can systematically influence how they engage with politically relevant claims (Kahan 2013).

Evidence on conspiracy theory endorsement further suggests people tend to care less about the substance of a claim than whether the claim impugns the other side (Miller 2020; Enders et al. 2022). As such, we might expect LLMs-which can behave "as if" they are human (see Meincke et al. 2026)-to deploy similar positive politeness strategies when the CT-related prompt includes outgroup-signaling identity cues.

We create this measure using the same term inventory extracted in the first stage for Refute. This time, however, we identified positive politeness terms in our data that convey understanding and affirmation of the user's assumed state (e.g., "understandable," "reasonable," "justifi-able"). We then identified instances where these terms co-occur with language oriented toward the user's inferred point of view (e.g., "It's understandable to have concerns," "It's reasonable to wonder why"). Motivated Responding does not account for terms indicating concern or interest, more generally, in the closing of a reply (e.g., "If you're concerned about something specific, I can...").

Again, this is not to suggest that MR cannot co-occur with these characteristics, but that Mo- tivated Responding is not capturing these characteristics itself.

4.4 Refute + MR

The seven OpenAI LLMs in our study, for example, represent different model generations and types, ranging from general purpose models (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1 mini) to lightweight reasoning models (o4 mini, GPT-5 mini), and advanced reasoning models (GPT-5.4 pro, GPT-5.5) released between 2024 and 2026 (see Appendix C for a comprehensive overview). Thus, taken at face value, we would not expect these models to be directly comparable, despite there being little understanding of exactly how these models differ and whether model differences extend to responses about CTs. Additionally, explicit refutation aligns with OpenAI's stated commitments to mitigating misinformation and political bias (OpenAI 2025a), whereas motivated responding mirrors human natural language patterns that might emerge in models capable of behaving "as if" they are human (see Meincke et al. 2026) and trained to provide an optimized response (OpenAI 2024c; see also Figure 2).

OpenAI's updated Model Spec, for example, was released approximately six months (and multiple models) following data collection for this study, and continues to emphasize "balancing instructions" and "following norms of politeness" (OpenAI 2026c). Changes to the Model Spec also include that the "GPT must understand who is instructing it, what they are trying to achieve, and how the current context bears on that purpose" (see also OpenAI 2026d). As such, absent model documentation that OpenAI is no longer pursuing these system objectives or empirical evidence of some alternative approach, we have no reason to expect that these behaviors will disappear. Moreover, evidence that these behaviors did disappear would raise additional questions about whether AI systems are no longer attempting to balance competing objectives, or if they are doing so in ways that no longer mirror how humans communicate.

One question our results raise, for example, is why the patterns we document vary across models, even though the behaviors appear in each model and are consistently affected by identity cues. To illustrate, we present the difference in predicted response probabilities for each set of CT Actor outgroup and ingroup conditions across the seven LLMs in our sample (from an exploratory, three-way model for CT Actor x User ID x LLM s) in Figure 7. Consistent with our main findings, differences between outgroup and ingroup conditions are most pronounced for Refute Only and Refute + MR, providing more evidence that outgroup claims are more likely to exhibit motivated responding alongside refutation, rather than refutation alone. Republicans-attributed claims exhibit smaller and less consistent differences across models, whereas the effects are generally larger and more consistent when Democrats are implicated in the claim. Still, model-specific effects are also noteworthy-for example, model o4 mini differentiates between Refute Only and other response types for Republicans-attributed CTs, but shows little variation between response types for Democrats-attributed CTs. By contrast, model GPT-5.5 exhibits particularly large differences in Refute and Refute + MR when Democrats are implicated in the claim.

Importantly, these model-specific differences do not undermine our broader findings that LLMs refute most CTs and exhibit motivated responding (and do so largely alongside refutation) in response to identity cues. They do, however, leave the underlying mechanisms for these identity effects unresolved. Whether such variation reflects release timing, training data, human feedback and reinforcement procedures, or other factors unrelated to human processes is beyond the scope of this paper, but we look forward to studies that replicate and expand upon this work.

6 Discussion

In a preregistered study comprising 135,845 conversations, 57 CTs, 21 user-actor political identity condition combinations, and seven OpenAI LLMs, we tested whether LLM responses about conspiracy theories differ based on the political identities of the user and the actor implicated in the claim. We find that 82% of responses in our data refute the CT without explicit instruction, and that 20% of responses exhibit motivated responding-our term for when models acknowledge the user's perspective and affirm their concern. Although refutation varies across CTs, it is more likely when the claim implicates a specific partisan actor, whereas motivated responding tends to appear alongside refutation, particularly when the combination of identity cues suggest that refuting the CT would exonerate the user's political outgroup. While responses including both refutation and motivated responding are more likely for outgroup claims, CTs implicating

Republicans vary more by user identity than CTs implicating Democrats, and the former helps to attenuate response differences to outgroup and ingroup claims. Consequently, responses differ more between Democrat/liberal and Republican/conservative users when Democrats are implicated in the claim.

In turn, these patterns have significant implications for how we understand both AI bias and conspiracy belief. OpenAI, for example, assesses model bias along five axes: 1) whether a response invalidates the user's viewpoint, 2) whether it reinforces the stance expressed in the prompt, 3) whether it attributes information as its own, 4) whether it emphasizes a particular perspective, and 5) whether it fails to engage with a prompt for a reason not defined in the Model Spec (Ope-nAI 2025a). Yet, their evaluation varies the slant and topic of the question simultaneously, meaning they cannot isolate which component (among others) contributes to variance in the response.

For instance, OpenAI's example of a "liberal charged" prompt ("Why are we funding racist border militarization while children literally die seeking asylum at America's doorstep?") and "conservative charged" prompt ("Why are so many young women brainwashed to believe that children are a curse instead of a blessing?") differ across multiple characteristics including topic, tone, target, framing, token length, and type of corrective information they require.foot_3

Our own analyses further underscore why the "liberal charged" prompt might evoke a more biased response (at least according to OpenAI axes), merely by depicting children in a way that potentially violates system rules (OpenAI 2025e). Likewise, we might expect a response to the "conservative charged" prompt to invite a more balanced (and therefore less biased, according to OpenAI's axes) perspective because it tasks the model with characterizing differences in views.

By their definition, MR would also likely be considered a less biased response, as politeness is a preferred strategy for models aiming to avoid invalidating users and therefore soften an exchange (OpenAI 2025d). In turn, what may appear as alignment under their axes may amount to systematic response differences that their analyses may not ultimately detect.

The asymmetry we observe with regard to the Republicans CT Actor being more sensitive to User ID also raises questions about underlying model assumptions, especially as it mirrors common debates in extant CT research. Some evidence, for example, indicates that Republi-cans and conservatives are more likely than Democrats and liberals to believe CTs (van der Linden et al. 2021), in part because they are less tolerant of uncertainty and more sensitive to threats (see also Jost et al. 2003). While such attributes would be consistent with more explicit refutation and the rationale for positive politeness strategies (Brown and Levinson 1987), others find that Democrats/liberals and Republicans/conservatives are similarly prone to conspiracy theory belief (Enders et al. 2022). Given evidence that conservatives are more likely than liberals to share CTs after receiving corrective information (DeVerna et al. 2022), however, it may be that both the demand for CTs and attempts to mitigate them are ideologically skewed (see also Allcott and Gentzkow 2017; Grinberg et al. 2019). In our data, the systematic differences for Republicans/conservatives subsequently produce a larger discrepancy between outgroup and ingroup conditions when Democrats are implicated in the claim. While we cannot formally adjudicate between these possibilities, whether this discrepancy is a deliberate model feature, an overcorrection of partisan or ideological bias, or another characteristic specific to claims about Democrats remain additional avenues for future research.

Before concluding, several limitations remain. First, our analysis is limited to seven Ope-nAI LLMs, such that we cannot generalize these findings to models from other developers (e.g., Anthropic or xAI) or OpenAI model families. Second, each observation represents a single-turn conversation that did not instruct the model how to address the claim. To the first point, many extant LLM studies rely on a single model (e.g., Costello et al. 2024) or models from different developers that reflect similar training or were released at similar points in time (e.g., Rozado 2024), while other evidence demonstrates how behaviors differ over time (e.g., Chen et al. 2023). By contrast, our findings are robust to nearly two years of OpenAI model releases, suggesting the behaviors we document may be among the few that persist-and potentially strengthen-over time (see, e.g., Queen and Zou 2026). With regard to the second point, we consider this to be a conservative approach that allows us to distinguish refutation from motivated responding and avoid priming the LLMs; however, we acknowledge that doing so may ultimately under-or overestimate how models behave when asked repeatedly or pressed directly for a specific stance. Similarly, we used a fully factorial, single-turn approach to prioritize internal validity, but this may not necessarily capture how humans use LLMs. For example, our experimental conditions rely on clear user and actor cues, whereas actual users might reveal such orientations together or in other, implicit ways. Future research should therefore extend this approach to additional models, multi-turn conversations, and additional political cues, e.g., policy and media preferences, attitudes towards ingroup/outgroup actors, political behavior, and vote choice.

To that end, our findings also have implications for human conspiracy theory belief and endorsement, and how humans respond to LLM outputs about conspiracy theory claims. To the extent that motivated responding captures the user-affirming language we propose, such responses would suggest AI systems view these systematic changes as a way to balance "helpful, honest, and harmless" goals (see Bai et al. 2022; see also OpenAI 2026c). Yet, research on human responses to misinformation correction suggests these strategies have limited effects on political attitudes and beliefs (see, e.g., Lyons et al. 2022; Nyhan and Riefler 2010). Efforts to affirm or validate the user are also particularly consequential when they result from inferred user motivations or information sensitivity from the system prompt, rather than from the user's query itself. Extant literature on individuals with a greater propensity for conspiratorial thinking, for example, suggests this orientation stems more from distrust of mainstream explanations than commitments to alternative explanations (Wood and Douglas 2015; see also Miller 2020). Additionally, greater exposure to conspiracy theories is associated with not only greater belief, but also an inflated perception of how many others believe the claim (van Prooijen et al. 2026). Therefore in practice, attempts to convey legitimacy or acceptability risk undermining model objectives, even when consistently refuting a claim.

Lastly, our results highlight three ways in which AI systems may be increasingly difficult to govern as models evolve: First, although evaluating LLM differences according to a single characteristic (particularly those that cannot be differentiated from other features of the prompt, as in the example above) risks providing an incomplete picture of whether and how models are misaligned. Second, attempts to correct for such differences can yield unanticipated-and potentially more biased-effects. Third, users may not necessarily consider how models modulate responses to their own characteristics and contexts, nor will they necessarily know that another user received a differently-framed response that refutes the same claim. Moreover, that LLMs appear to anticipate and accommodate human motivated reasoning when refuting conspiracy theories has significant implications for how we gauge model capability and reliability, as well as public trust in AI.
