Files that do not belong in the repository

We left out 1 file that software creates by itself, not the authors: operating system (1). They hold nothing a reader can use, but they clutter the repository, and software can mistake them for real files (for example a ._ copy of a .csv for data).

Remove these: Operating system files: macOS adds a __MACOSX folder and a ._ copy of every file it puts in a zip, and .DS_Store files to folders; Windows adds Thumbs.db and desktop.ini. On a Mac, create zips from the command line with zip -r -X, or remove them afterwards with zip -d archive.zip "__MACOSX/*" "*.DS_Store". RStudio session files (.Rproj.user/) hold open tabs and unsaved edits; R history (.Rhistory) lists the commands typed in a session and can hold file paths and passwords; Jupyter checkpoints (.ipynb_checkpoints/) are automatic copies of notebooks; compiled Python files (__pycache__/, .pyc) are recreated when the code runs; open-document lock files (~$...docx, .~lock...#) are left while a document is open; editor backups (file~, .bak, .swp) and temporary files (.tmp, .temp) are copies a program made by itself.

Keep only on purpose: version control folders (.git, .svn, .hg) hold the project’s history, which can include earlier versions of files and data that was later removed; editor settings (.idea, .vscode) can hold paths on your own computer. A .gitignore based on GitHub’s templates (https://github.com/github/gitignore) keeps them out of a repository.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

1. Introduction

Internet addiction has become a prominent mental health and behavioral addiction risk in the digital age. Although using the Internet itself-for social media, shopping, gaming, and work etc.

-is not inherently problematic, and users can generally obtain immediate pleasure and emotional relief, they are also prone to developing patterns of uncontrolled, repetitive, and continued use despite known harm (Shi et al., 2022). Existing research has consistently shown that Internet addiction is closely associated with negative emotions such as depression and deficits in cognitive control (A et al., 2026; Ioannidis et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2026). The Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution model (I-PACE) proposes that negative emotions, as distal predisposing factors, can drive Internet addiction through interactions among the cognitive-affective-executive systems, while cognitive control may serve as a "brake" that buffers this pathway (Brand et al., 2019; 2025). Survey studies have found that cognitive control significantly moderates the relationship between Internet addiction and negative emotional states (Bai et al., 2026), but the neural mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear.

Brain functional changes provide an important window for understanding how cognitive control moderates the depression-Internet addiction link. Internet addiction can be conceptualized as an imbalance between the dual pathways of "feels better" and "must do" and the "stop now" cognitive control process (Brand, 2022a). Depressive mood may drive Internet addiction through the "feels better" pathway and temporarily alleviate depressive mood; after repeated use, the behavior gradually becomes habitualized and progressively shifts toward the "must do" pathway, manifesting as continued use despite known harm. "Stop now" involves cognitive control and subserves functions such as inhibiting impulses and maintaining goal-directed behavior (Brand, 2022a). At the neural level, this imbalance mainly involves the prefrontal-striatal circuit, with regions such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex participating in cognitive control and regions such as the dorsal and ventral striatum participating in reward processing and habit formation (Brand et al., 2016).

In recent years, advances in resting-state fMRI and brain functional connectivity network research have provided testable network-level evidence for the aforementioned "dual-pathway-cognitive control" imbalance. An activation likelihood estimation (ALE) meta-analysis of cognitive control tasks showed consistent hyperactivation in the left middle frontal gyrus and right superior parietal lobule in individuals with Internet addiction, suggesting that "stop now" is not simply hypoactivation of the control network but may instead reflect compensatory over-recruitment when the frontoparietal control network is inefficient (Li et al., 2026). Resting-state independent component analysis (ICA) further found that higher smartphone addiction scores were associated with weaker within-network functional connectivity in the executive control network, sensorimotor network, and left frontoparietal network (Áfra et al., 2023), providing within-network evidence for "weakened control networks." A study using the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) longitudinal sample and connectome-based predictive modeling found that baseline resting-state functional connectivity predicted problematic social media use 4 years later, involving the frontoparietal network, medial frontal cortex, and sensorimotor network, suggesting that weakened connectivity in the cognitive control system and enhanced connectivity in the sensorimotor integration system jointly constitute susceptibility to problematic use (Park et al., 2026). A meta-analysis of resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) showed that both substance use disorders and behavioral addictions exhibit hyperconnectivity in reward and control networks, including the striatum and medial frontal cortex, supporting the role of the prefrontal-striatal circuit in "must do" habitualization (Tolomeo & Yu, 2022). In summary, from a network perspective, the imbalance is not caused by a single brain region but may result from dynamic interactions among the frontoparietal control network, reward network, and other networks.

Cognitive control is manifested during tasks, and task-based fMRI has also provided activation-level evidence for studies of inhibitory control in Internet addiction. Some studies have found that the smartphone addiction group showed stronger right DLPFC activation during Stroop processing, and this activation was positively correlated with smartphone addiction scores, suggesting a compensatory effect in key brain regions involved in inhibitory control (Shen et al., 2023). Other studies using the Go/No-Go task have found that impaired function of the prefrontal inhibitory circuit in individuals with Internet gaming disorder may be associated with high impulsivity (Ding et al., 2014). Dong et al. (2012) found that individuals with Internet addiction showed stronger ACC and PCC activation during the Stroop task, suggesting decreased inhibitory control efficiency. Recent methodological advances have enabled task-modulated functional connectivity, like resting-state fMRI, to characterize cognitive control-related brain regions from a network perspective in the relationship between depression and Internet addiction. Masharipov et al. (2024) developed a toolbox specifically for task-modulated functional connectivity. Their research indicates that task designs should take into account both the number of events and the events themselves, and that event-specific methods such as Beta-series correlation using the least squares separate (BSC-LSS; for trial-by-trial covariation between brain regions) analyses should be prioritized to more reliably identify cognitive control related network dynamics (Masharipov et al., 2024); these approaches have already been applied in task-based fMRI studies (Zheltyakova, Kireev, Knyazeva, et al., 2026; Zheltyakova, Kireev, Myznikov, et al., 2026).

Using a publicly available fMRI dataset employing a cognitive control task (Liu et al., 2026), this study intends to characterize the roles of cognitive control-related brain regions and networks in the depression-Internet addiction relationship, employing region-of-interest selection and task-modulated functional connectivity methods to examine individual brain regions and brain networks, respectively. We propose the following hypothesis: task-modulated functional connectivity between regions involved in the frontoparietal control network and the subcortical reward network carries the moderating effect of cognitive control on the link between depression and Internet addiction; specifically, the stronger the functional connectivity, the stronger the moderating effect of cognitive control on the link, manifesting as a weaker or even nonsignificant depression-Internet addiction association; the weaker the functional connectivity, the weaker the moderating effect of cognitive control, manifesting as a stronger depression-Internet addiction association. This study's findings will facilitate a better understanding of the neurodynamic mechanisms underlying Internet addiction and inform the development of targeted brain-based interventions.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Dataset and participants

This study used behavioral and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data from the publicly available database (Liu et al., 2026). No new data were collected and no identifiable information was accessed; therefore, no additional ethical approval was required. The original data collection obtained ethics approval and informed consent, as described in the original dataset publication. The original study initially enrolled 38 participants (10 males; mean age = 19.88 years, SD = 1.67, age range = 17-24 years). None of the participants had been previously diagnosed with psychiatric or neurological disorders. After data quality screening, four participants were excluded due to excessive head motion (translation > 3 mm or rotation > 3°) or poor MRI data quality, leaving a final sample of 34 participants for analysis.

2.2. Materials

The present study aimed to investigate the potential mechanisms by which cognitive control contributes to the association between Internet addiction and depressive states. Therefore, only the experimental materials directly relevant to the present research question were selected from the public database and included in subsequent analyses. Specifically, these comprised the runs under the no-social-distraction condition of the global-local attention allocation task, the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI).

2.2.1. Global-local attention allocation task

The global-local attention allocation task is commonly used to examine individual differences in attentional resource allocation and has been applied in previous studies (Medaglia et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2024). This paradigm includes both congruent and incongruent trials and can therefore be used to assess cognitive control processes. The stimuli were hierarchical compound figures consisting of a larger global shape and a smaller local shape, with shape types including circle, X, triangle, and square. In congruent trials, the global and local shapes were identical; in incongruent trials, they differed. The two trial types each accounted for 50% of trials and were presented randomly. Participants were required to make judgments based on stimulus color: if the figure was green, they reported the shape of the larger figure; if it was white, they reported the shape of the smaller figure. The response mapping was as follows:1 = square,2 = circle,3 = triangle, and4 = X. Each stimulus was presented for 2000 ms on a black background, with an inter-trial interval (ISI) of 1500-4500 ms (mean = 3000 ms).

Because the present analysis focused on general cognitive control rather than social information interference effects, not all three conditions from the original study-online social distraction, online non-social distraction, and baseline-were included. The present study selected only the global and local attentional levels under the baseline condition, as well as the congruent and incongruent trial types, to exclude interference from irrelevant factors such as social information. Conditions of social distraction, non-social distraction, and baseline corresponded to different runs, and the run order was counterbalanced across participants. In each run, a working memory control task and the global-local attention task were administered alternately to control for potential confounding variables. Therefore, only the fMRI run corresponding to the baseline condition was analyzed in this study. The baseline run comprised 48 trials in an event-related design, with 24 trials for the global condition and 24 trials for the local condition. Visual stimuli were presented using E-Prime 3.0 software (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) on a HP computer with a screen resolution of 1920 × 1080 and a refresh rate of 60 Hz.

2.2.2. Internet Addiction Test (IAT)

The Internet Addiction Test was used to measure the severity of individuals' internet addiction (Young, 1998). The scale consists of 20 items (Sample item: "How often do you find that you stay online longer than you intended?"). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = rarely,5 = always). A higher total score indicates a stronger tendency toward internet addiction.

2.2.3. Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)

The Beck Depression Inventory was used to assess the presence and severity of depressive symptoms in adolescents and adults (Beck, 1961). The scale consists of 21 items rated on a 4-point scale. A higher total score indicates more severe depressive symptoms.

2.3. Behavioral data analysis

Behavioral data were analyzed using general linear models (GLMs). Specifically, the total score of IAT was treated as the dependent variable, with age and gender as covariates, while the total score of BDI and the mean reaction times and accuracy rates under the six conditions of the global-local attention allocation task (global congruent, global incongruent, local congruent, local incongruent, global incongruent -global congruent, and local incongruent -local congruent) were treated as independent variables. The main effects and interaction effects of each predictor were examined. For conditions with significant interaction effects, simple effects analyses were further conducted to clarify the specific patterns of effects across conditions.

2.4. Neuroimaging data collection

Whole-brain images were acquired in an MRI environment using a 3T Prisma MRI scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head-neck coil. Task-related functional images were obtained using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo sequence, with the following main scanning parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, field of view (FOV) = 224 × 224 mm, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2.3 mm, 62 slices, slice thickness = 2 mm, flip angle = 90°, and multi-band factor = 2. To exclude participants with obvious brain lesions and to improve the standardization of functional imaging data, T1-weighted high-resolution anatomical images were acquired using a spoiled gradient-echo pulse sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2530 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, matrix size = 224 × 256, voxel size = 0.5 × 0.5 × 1 mm, 192 slices, slice thickness = 1 mm, flip angle = 7°, and multi-band factor = 2.

2.6. Neuroimaging data analysis

First-level GLM analyses were performed using SPM12. The model included regressors for global congruent, global incongruent, local congruent, and local incongruent trials. To improve motion control, six head motion parameters were included as covariates.

2.6.1. Region of interest (ROI) selection

Based on previous studies (Beckes et al., 2021; Ford et al., 2011; Ito et al., 2008; Kahn & Shohamy, 2013; Krienen & Buckner, 2009), MNI coordinates for the DLPFC, VMPFC, DMPFC, caudate nucleus, putamen, nucleus accumbens, substantia nigra, and ventral tegmental area were selected to define ROIs with a radius of 6 mm (Figure 1). The corresponding MNI coordinates are shown in Table 1. For each ROI, the mean activation strength under each condition (global congruent, global incongruent, local congruent, and local incongruent) was calculated and entered separately as a moderator in GLM analyses, with the BDI total score as the independent variable and the IAT total score as the dependent variable. For conditions with significant interaction effects, simple effects analyses were conducted.

2.6.2. Task-Modulated Functional Connectivity

To further examine the moderating effects of functional connectivity across different brain regions on the relationship between negative affect and internet addiction, we performed task-modulated functional connectivity analyses using the Task-Modulated Functional Connectivity (TMFC) toolbox (https://github.com/Masharipov/TMFC_toolbox) (Masharipov et al., 2024). The Brainnetome Atlas (BNA; 246 regions) was adopted as the ROI parcellation scheme.

This atlas comprises 210 cortical regions and 36 subcortical regions, each classified according to the Yeo 17-network system (including visual peripheral/central, somatomotor A/B, dorsal attention A/B, salience/ventral attention A/B, limbic A/B, control A/B/C, default A/B/C/D, and subcortical) (Yeo et al., 2011). Regions not assigned to any Yeo network (36 subcortical regions in total) were collectively assigned to the "subcortical network" (Subcort), yielding 18 networks in total: 17 Yeo cortical networks plus 1 subcortical network. The 246 ROIs were imported into the TMFC toolbox to construct the ROI set. Task-based functional connectivity was estimated using beta-series correlation based on BSC-LSS approach. (1) least-squares separate (LSS) GLMs: this method estimates a beta value separately for each trial via LSS regression, thereby yielding a trial-wise neural activity time series. For each participant, each condition, and each trial, a separate GLM was fitted. The target trial was represented by its own regressor, all remaining trials of the same condition were collapsed into a single regressor, and other conditions were left unchanged. In this way, one beta image was generated for each trial. (2) BSC-LSS functional connectivity estimation: for each ROI, the first eigenvariate was extracted from the beta values of all voxels within that region to form the ROI's beta series.

Pearson correlation coefficients were then computed between the beta series of any two ROIs and converted to Fisher's Z values, yielding a 246 × 246 ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity matrix. Linear contrasts were used to generate between-condition differences in functional connectivity, including (i) global incongruent -global congruent and (ii) local incongruent -local congruent conditions. To improve the biological interpretability of the functional networks, the 246 × 246 ROI-level connectivity matrix was reduced to the network level based on the 17 Yeo cortical networks plus 1 subcortical region.

Because the present study primarily focused on functional connectivity patterns between cortical regions and subcortical regions, only connections between cortical networks and the subcortical region were analyzed. For each pair of networks, the connectivity strength was averaged across all ROI pairs, yielding 17 network-level connectivity variables that served as moderators in subsequent moderation analyses.

GLMs were used to test whether network-level functional connectivity moderated the relationship between IAT total scores and BDI total scores. The dependent variable (Y) was the IAT total score; the independent variable (X) was the BDI total score; the moderator (W) was functional connectivity under each condition contrast; and covariates included age and sex.

All first-level GLM analyses were performed in SPM12; BSC-LSS functional connectivity analyses were conducted using the TMFC toolbox; ROI-level beta extraction and moderation analyses were implemented in MATLAB R2025b using custom scripts. Because this study involved a focused, hypothesis-driven analysis that reduced the number of multiple comparisons, an uncorrected significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted, and effect sizes (partial η² and R²) were also reported to evaluate the robustness of the results.

Discussion This study aimed to investigate the behavioral and neural evidence for underlying mechanisms by which cognitive control modulates Internet addiction and depression. At the behavioral level, individuals' accuracy under conflict conditions significantly moderated the relationship between Internet addiction and depression; enhanced cognitive control attenuated the positive association between Internet addiction and depression. At the neural level, some prefrontal regions and some subcortical regions showed antagonistic moderating effects on the association between Internet addiction and depression. Specifically, increased activation in some prefrontal regions attenuated the positive association between Internet addiction and depression, whereas increased activation in some subcortical regions strengthened this positive association. Brain network results provided convergent evidence, showing that increased functional connectivity between the control network and the subcortical network attenuated the positive association between Internet addiction and depression.

The present study found that enhanced cognitive control attenuates the positive association between Internet addiction and depression, as reflected in the finding that accuracy under conflict conditions significantly moderated the relationship between Internet addiction and depression. This is consistent with previous findings (Bai et al., 2026). Compensatory Internet use theory suggests that individuals tend to turn to online activities to alleviate stress and negative emotions in real life (Kardefelt-Winther, 2014). For individuals with weaker cognitive control, however, it is more difficult to inhibit the affect-driven drive to engage in online activities when such use gets more habitual via repeated Internet use (Brand et al., 2025). It may also result in weaker actual and perceived competence of emotional regulation, further prompting individuals to engage in compensatory Internet use, thereby strengthening the link between depression and Internet addiction.

At the neural level, our results of activation in different brain areas also corroborated the behavioral findings. Specifically, some prefrontal regions, including the left DLPFC and left VMPFC, negatively moderated the positive association between Internet addiction and depression, such that the positive association was weaker in individuals with stronger activation in these regions. Previous studies have found that the DLPFC and VMPFC are responsible for cognitive control functions (He et al., 2021; MacDonald et al., 2000), integrating somatic markers or value signals related to emotional experience into decision-making and exerting self-control over tempting behaviors (Bechara, 2005). Therefore, individuals with stronger DLPFC and VMPFC activation are better able to integrate value signals and implement control behaviors. In subcortical regions, including the substantia nigra, nucleus accumbens, and caudate nucleus, their activation positively moderated the positive association between Internet addiction and depression, such that the positive association was stronger in individuals with stronger activation in these regions. This helps to explain the "feels better" and "must do" pathways in individuals with Internet addiction, which may indirectly attenuate the role of cognitive control. The substantia nigra amplifies craving and activates both the "feels better" and "must do" pathways (Zhang et al., 2016); the nucleus accumbens enhances reward-seeking and use motivation (Cai et al., 2016); and the caudate nucleus promotes behavioral automatization, gradually decoupling use behavior from reward-provided motivation (Niu et al., 2024). Collectively, these processes contribute to dysregulated cognitive control and strengthen the association between depressive mood and Internet addiction.

Based on the brain atlas and the TMFC method, this study further found that the functional connectivity pattern between a subregion of the frontoparietal control network and the subcortical brain network negatively moderated the positive association between depression and Internet addiction; when the functional connectivity between the two was stronger, the positive association between Internet addiction and depression was weaker. This supports the argument at the level of task-based dynamic brain networks, namely that the cognitive control "stop now" module located in the frontoparietal region regulates the subcortical "feels better" and "must do" pathways (Brand, 2022b). The frontoparietal control network includes the lateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and inferior parietal lobule (Yeo et al., 2011), and supports processes such as flexible and adaptive cognitive control (Vincent et al., 2008), whereas subcortical regions are responsible for functions such as reward, motivation, and craving (Cai et al., 2016; Niu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2016). The observed moderating effects of frontoparietal-subcortical functional connectivity on the depression-Internet addiction association provide additional neural evidence that stronger connectivity reflects stronger cognitive control inhibition of the bidirectional depression-Internet addiction link, whereas weaker connectivity reflects weaker inhibition. This supports an interactive rather than purely unidirectional top-down regulatory account (Cruz et al., 2023), which corresponds to the early-and late-stage model of I-PACE (Brand et al., 2019; 2025). In the early stage, Internet use is more goal-directed (e.g., for emotional relief) because of reward sensitivity; at this time, top-down regulation dominates and cortical regions such as the frontoparietal control network mainly assume decision-making roles, determining when to start and stop Internet use. In the late stage, individuals' behavioral patterns have become more automatized; subcortical structures are simultaneously positioned at both early (sensory input) and late (motor output) stages in whole-brain circuits, meaning that they have a major influence on originally goal-directed behavior. This processing mode for Internet stimuli and the final output of use behavior can bias decision-making in cortical regions such as the frontoparietal control network in a bottom-up manner, thereby preventing fully effective top-down decisions and leading to habitual behavior. This view is also supported by relevant literature (Zhu et al., 2025).

This study has the following limitations. First, it did not use longitudinal data, making it difficult to directly verify the causal relationships of functional connectivity patterns. Second, the study was conducted in a laboratory environment, and brain functional patterns in real-world Internet use contexts remain to be further explored. Third, depression and Internet addiction were assessed using self-report scales; future studies should incorporate more objective behavioral measures to verify the robustness of the findings.

Overall, at the behavioral level, this study found that cognitive control negatively moderates the positive relationship between Internet addiction and depression. Based on neuroimaging data, it further found that functional connectivity patterns between regions involved in the frontoparietal control network and subcortical regions such as the striatum negatively moderate the positive relationship between Internet addiction and depression. The present study thereby verifies that the cognitive control "stop now" module regulates the imbalance in the subcortical "feels better" and "must do" pathways, and further validated the I-PACE theoretical model at the level of brain imaging. These findings may help inform future neurofeedback treatment for individuals with Internet addiction and the design of specific therapeutic targets.

fMRI data were preprocessed using SPM12 (Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). The preprocessing pipeline was as follows: (1) the first three volumes of each scan were discarded; (2) slice-timing correction was performed; (3) head motion correction was conducted using a six-parameter rigid-body transformation with reslicing; (4) functional images were coregistered to the structural image; (5) the high-resolution structural image was segmented into tissue classes, with skull stripping and bias field correction; (6) images were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space and resampled to 3 × 3 × 3 mm; and (7) spatial smoothing was applied using a Gaussian kernel with a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 8 mm.
