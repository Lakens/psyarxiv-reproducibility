No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

We proposed knowing sufficiency as a psychological construct comprising knowing what and how much is sufficient for one's well-being, not seeking beyond that point, and feeling content with what is already present in one's life. Existing psychological measures related to sufficiency primarily assess affective aspects such as dispositional contentment, but not the cognitive and behavioral aspects of recognizing what is enough and refraining from further pursuit. We therefore developed and validated the Knowing Sufficiency Scale (KSS) to assess individual differences across these cognitive, behavioral, and affective dimensions among Japanese adults.

Following item generation and evaluation by experts and the target population, we collected two independent online survey samples, with final analytic sample sizes of n = 443 and n = 455. We conducted exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses and examined internal consistency, construct validity through correlations with related measures, and incremental validity. A further assessment was conducted approximately 2 weeks later, with 294 participants included in the test-retest analysis.

The resulting KSS comprised 19 items across three subscales: Knowing What Is Enough, Stopping at Enough, and Felt Sufficiency. A higher-order three-factor model showed acceptable fit on CFI, TLI, and SRMR, but not RMSEA, and fitted better than a one-factor model. Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω in both samples and test-retest ICCs were all at least .70, indicating acceptable to good reliability. The KSS overall and subscale scores generally showed positive associations with contentment, life satisfaction, savoring, gratitude, and psychological well-being, and negative associations with materialism, depressive symptoms, and ability-related social comparison orientation. Adding Knowing What Is Enough and Stopping at Enough to a model including age, gender, savoring, and gratitude increased the explained variance in contentment by 7.55 percentage points.

The findings provide initial evidence for the reliability and validity of the KSS. The scale offers a means of studying knowing sufficiency in terms of its cognitive, behavioral, and affective dimensions. Further research is needed to establish generalizability across languages and populations and to examine validity through associations with behavioral measures.

Background

Materialism, which places material possessions and acquisition at the center of one's values and goals in life [1], is not necessarily associated with greater well-being. A meta-analysis of the association between materialism and personal well-being found that stronger endorsement of materialistic values and goals was associated with lower well-being [2]. By contrast, Princen [3] described sufficiency at the individual level as a "sense of enoughness and toomuchness." The idea of knowing what is enough and stopping at that point has a long intellectual history. In Chapter 44, the Dao De Jing, a foundational Daoist text, presents knowing contentment (知足) and knowing when to stop (知止) in parallel [4]. It frames them as practically important because excessive attachment and accumulation can lead to loss and harm, an emphasis on limits that resonates with contemporary sufficiency approaches to sustainability. The Lotus Sutra, a major Mahayana Buddhist scripture, likewise identifies having few desires and being content with what one has (少欲知足) as a desirable quality of those who practice its teachings [5]. Taken together, these traditions motivated our focus on individual differences in how people recognize and respond to sufficiency in their lives.

Sufficiency at the individual level has been operationalized in terms of attitudes toward resource consumption and frugal living [6], as well as subjective sufficiency in income and material living conditions [7, 8]. However, these operationalizations focus on consumption and economic domains. They therefore do not capture sufficiency as a broader psychological construct across life domains.

In psychology, contentment is a concept related to sufficiency. Cordaro et al. [9] conceptualized contentment as an emotion that arises when one's present circumstances are appraised as whole and enough, a conceptualization subsequently examined empirically by Cordaro et al. [10]. Dispositional contentment has been measured using instruments such as the Contentment subscale of the Dispositional Positive Emotion Scales (DPES-C) and the Positive Emotion Assessment of Contentment Experience (PEACE) Scale [11, 12]. Cordaro et al. [12] showed that dispositional contentment measured using the PEACE Scale was positively associated with unconditional self-acceptance and negatively associated with psychological distress and materialism. These existing conceptualizations of dispositional contentment capture the affective experience of having enough, but do not directly assess the cognitive and behavioral aspects of sufficiency.

We therefore propose knowing sufficiency as a comprehensive psychological construct encompassing these cognitive, behavioral, and affective elements. We define this construct as knowing what and how much is sufficient for one's well-being, not seeking beyond that point, and feeling content with what is already present in one's life. In this definition, well-being is used broadly, without commitment to a specific theoretical model, to cover physical and psychological states, activities, relationships, and material conditions. The term knowing refers not only to an explicit understanding of what is sufficient, but also to an experiential or practical sense of when enough has been reached.

We distinguish three interrelated dimensions of knowing sufficiency. The first, Knowing What Is Enough, is the cognitive dimension of understanding what one needs for well-being and what and how much is enough for oneself. The second, Stopping at Enough, is the behavioral dimension of not going beyond what one judges to be enough, whether by seeking more or by continuing an activity after reaching that point. The third, Felt Sufficiency, is the affective dimension of experiencing a sense of sufficiency or contentment with what is already present in one's life. Measuring these three dimensions separately may allow a more nuanced understanding of individual differences in knowing sufficiency.

This study aimed to develop and validate the Knowing Sufficiency Scale (KSS) as a measure of individual differences in this construct among Japanese adults. Following item generation, we examined the relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility of the items through evaluations by experts and non-expert adults from the target population. We first examined the scale's factor structure using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) in Sample 1, and then tested the three-factor structure of knowing sufficiency using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in an independent Sample 2. We also tested hypotheses concerning the construct validity of the KSS in each sample. In Sample 1, incremental validity was examined by testing whether the KSS provided information about dispositional contentment beyond closely related measures of savoring and gratitude. Finally, test-retest reliability over a 2week interval was assessed in Sample 2.

The hypotheses concerning construct validity are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

First, we expected knowing sufficiency to show moderate-to-large positive correlations with dispositional contentment, dispositional joy, life satisfaction, savoring, and dispositional gratitude. In particular, because Felt Sufficiency represents the affective dimension, we expected it to correlate more strongly with dispositional contentment than the other subscales.

We also expected a moderate negative correlation with the tendency to compare one's abilities with those of others, on the premise that people higher in knowing sufficiency are satisfied according to their own standards. Self-acceptance, a dimension of psychological well-being, was expected to show a large positive correlation because it is conceptually similar. In contrast, small-to-moderate correlations were expected with Personal Growth, Purpose in Life, Autonomy, and related dimensions, because achievement and growth were expected to be less important to people higher in knowing sufficiency. Materialism was considered to contrast conceptually with knowing sufficiency and was therefore expected to show moderate-to-large negative correlations.

The cognitive dimension, Knowing What Is Enough, includes individual differences in how clearly people understand what would make them feel content. Because this aspect partly overlaps with the emphasis on values clarification in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy [13], a moderate positive association with values was expected. The behavioral dimension, Stopping at Enough, measures individual differences in the tendency to stop seeking more than is sufficient. Because this involves refraining from further pursuit out of contentment rather than depression, we expected a small negative correlation with depressive symptoms. We also expected a small negative correlation with the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS), because Stopping at Enough does not entail refraining from pursuit out of fear of punishment. Finally, because Stopping at Enough involves not seeking rewards or excitement excessively, small negative correlations with Behavioral Activation System (BAS) Drive and Fun Seeking were expected. These hypotheses were tested to evaluate the construct validity of the KSS.

Methods

Conceptual development and item generation

Items were generated based on our definition of knowing sufficiency while examining its similarities to and differences from related constructs. Initial item generation was conducted by the authors. A researcher with expertise in psychometrics then provided informal feedback on the initial construct definition, candidate items, and scale-development approach. Based on this feedback, we subsequently incorporated generative artificial intelligence (AI) into item generation [14, 15], using ChatGPT via the web interface (OpenAI). The generative AI was provided with the definition of knowing sufficiency and instructed to generate candidate items. The specific model, prompts, and outputs are available in the Zenodo record for this study (see Availability of data and materials; prompt and output file 1); the supplementary table S2 identifies which items originated from the generative AI and which were generated by the authors. After further candidate items had been generated, two researchers-one with expertise in well-being and one in Buddhist meditation-provided informal feedback on the construct definition and candidate items. Additional items were generated and existing items revised in response to this feedback, resulting in a pool of 52 candidate items, which are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Item refinement and content validity assessment: Phase 1

The relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility of the 52 items were examined in stages with reference to the content validity framework of the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) [16]. First, seven experts conducting research in human sciences rated how well each item described their usual selves on a 7-point scale (1 = Very untrue of me to7 = Very true of me). They were then shown the definition of knowing sufficiency and asked to rate the relevance of each item to the definition on a 4-point scale:1 = not relevant,2 = slightly relevant and needs revision,3 = relevant but needs minor revision, and4 = very relevant [17]. We also invited open-ended feedback on the clarity and comprehensibility of each item's wording. Experts assessed whether the item pool comprehensively covered the construct using three response options: "yes," "no," and "difficult to judge." They were additionally asked to describe any missing or unnecessary elements and suggest other revisions.

The ratings were summarized, and item-level content validity indices (I-CVIs) were calculated [17] (see Supplementary Table S2). Regarding comprehensiveness, one expert answered "yes," six answered "difficult to judge," and none answered "no." Because the intended meaning of the comprehensiveness question itself might have been difficult to understand, during a subsequent meeting with the experts, we clarified the definition of knowing sufficiency and the purpose of the question and obtained further feedback. Based on the relevance ratings, 13 of the 52 items, which had I-CVIs below .70, were removed [17].

In preparation for the pilot survey in the target population, we asked ChatGPT to suggest item revisions based on these findings; the prompts and outputs are available in the Zenodo record for this study (see Availability of data and materials; prompt and output file 2).

Two non-experts also completed the items and took part in online interviews about items that were difficult to understand, the comprehensiveness of the item pool, and possible revisions. Drawing on the seven experts' open-ended responses and feedback, ChatGPT's suggestions, and the interviews with non-experts, we revised the items to improve content validity and comprehensibility. This yielded 39 items for the subsequent pilot survey (see Supplementary Table S2 for the revised items).

Pilot survey and content validity assessment: Phase 2

To examine whether the 39 items were understood as intended by the target population Participants first rated how well each of the 39 items described their usual selves on a 7-point scale. To screen for inattentive responding (survey satisficing), an instructed-response item, "Please select 2 for this item," was inserted midway through the items [18]. In this pilot survey, the order of the knowing sufficiency items was fixed across participants rather than randomized. Participants then rated the comprehensibility of the 39 items on a 4-point scale: 1 = could not understand,2 = could not fully understand (revision needed),3 = generally understood (minor revision needed),4 = understood well.

On the next page, they were shown the definition of the tendency toward knowing sufficiency and asked to describe difficulties and suggest revisions for items they had rated as difficult to understand (ratings of 1 or 2).

On the final page, which asked for demographic information (age and gender), a bogus item, "Have you ever been to Jupiter?", was placed at the end as a further check for survey satisficing [18]. To identify and screen responses generated by AI agents, the instruction "Please write 'Done'" was displayed below the Jupiter question in white text on a white background, together with a free-text field that was likewise not visible to ordinary participants.

All 100 participants answered the instructed-response item correctly, but one answered "yes" to the bogus Jupiter item and was excluded from the analyses. No responses were detected by the AI-agent screening procedure. The final pilot sample of 99 participants had a mean age of 45.9 years (SD = 11.2; range = 20-75 years) and included 78 male and 21 female participants. For each item, we calculated the mean, standard deviation, and percentages of responses in the lowest (1) and highest (7) categories (see Supplementary Table S3). No item showed a pronounced concentration of responses at either extreme.

We reviewed content validity and item redundancy on the basis of the comprehensibility ratings and feedback, and removed or revised items with reference to ChatGPT's suggestions for redundant items and wording revisions (see prompt and output files 3 and 4 in the Zenodo record for this study; see Availability of data and materials).

Subsequently, one human sciences expert and one non-expert who had not participated in the preceding phase completed the items and provided in-person feedback on difficulties in understanding and related issues. Further removal and revision yielded the 27 items used in the Sample 1 survey (see Supplementary Table S3 for reasons for item removal and details of revisions).

Measures in Sample 1

Dispositional contentment and joy

Dispositional contentment and joy were assessed using the Japanese version of the Dispositional Positive Emotion Scales (DPES-J [20]), originally developed by Shiota et al. [11]. We used the five-item Contentment and six-item Joy subscales; items were rated on a 7point scale and summed within each subscale, with higher scores indicating stronger dispositional tendencies to experience the respective emotion.

Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction was assessed using the Japanese version [21] of the Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS [22]). Each of the five items was rated on a 7-point scale, and item scores were summed, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction.

Savoring beliefs

Beliefs about savoring positive experiences were assessed using the Japanese version of the Savoring Beliefs Inventory (SBI-J [23]), based on Bryant [24], which comprises Anticipating, Savoring the Moment, and Reminiscing subscales. Each of the 24 items was rated on a 7point scale and summed within each subscale after reverse-scoring, with higher scores indicating greater perceived savoring ability.

Dispositional gratitude

Dispositional gratitude was assessed using Shiraki and Igarashi's [25] five-item Japanese adaptation of the Gratitude Questionnaire-6 (GQ-6 [26]). Items were rated on a 7-point scale, and the mean score was calculated after reverse-scoring, with higher scores indicating greater dispositional gratitude.

Social comparison orientation

Social comparison orientation was assessed using the Japanese Social Comparison Orientation Scale (SCOS [27]), adapted from the Iowa-Netherlands Comparison Orientation Measure [28]. The scale comprises Ability and Opinion dimensions; following Toyama [27], Item 9 was excluded from scoring, and mean scores were calculated for the remaining seven Ability and three Opinion items after reverse-scoring. Items were rated on a 5-point scale.

Higher scores indicate a stronger social comparison orientation in the respective dimension.

Sample 2 survey

Sample 2 was recruited to test the three-factor structure of the KSS using CFA and to examine construct validity through associations with other psychological constructs. An independent sample of 498 participants completed the survey through Yahoo! Crowdsourcing on a weekday in June 2026. Individuals who had participated in the pilot survey or Sample 1 were prevented from participating. Participants completed the final 19-item KSS, which had been reduced from 27 items following the Sample 1 survey and analyses. The survey conditions and procedures were identical to those used for Sample 1, except that different measures were administered to assess construct validity (see "Measures in Sample 2"). They received 50 PayPay Points (equivalent to JPY 50) for completing the initial survey.

To examine test-retest reliability, participants were asked to complete the KSS again approximately 2 weeks later, without the other psychological measures. Responses from 360 participants could be matched to their initial responses. Participants received 10 PayPay Points (equivalent to JPY 10) for completing the second survey.

Measures in Sample 2

Psychological well-being

Psychological well-being was assessed using the 24-item short form of the Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWBS-SF [29]), based on Ryff's six-dimensional model [30]. It comprises four-item subscales for Personal Growth, Purpose in Life, Autonomy, Self-Acceptance, Environmental Mastery, and Positive Relations with Others; items were rated on a 6-point scale and summed within each subscale after reverse-scoring. Higher scores indicate greater psychological well-being in the respective dimension.

Materialism

Materialism was assessed using the six-item Japanese Material Values Scale (J-MVS-P6 [31]), comprising Success/Centrality and Happiness subscales. Items were rated on a 5-point scale, and mean scores were calculated for each three-item subscale.

Values-consistent behavior

Values-consistent behavior was assessed using the Japanese Valuing Questionnaire (VQ [32]), developed by Smout et al. [13]. The 10-item VQ comprises two five-item subscales: Progress, reflecting progress toward valued living, and Obstruction, reflecting interference with valued living from psychological experiences and other obstacles. Items were rated with reference to the preceding week on a 0-6 scale and summed within each subscale.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-8 [33], which omits the death/self-harm item from the PHQ-9; the Japanese PHQ-9 has been validated by Muramatsu et al. [34]. The eight items were rated from 0 to 3 for the preceding 2 weeks and summed, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

Behavioral inhibition and activation

Behavioral inhibition and activation were assessed using the Japanese BIS/BAS Scales [35], adapted from Carver and White [36]. The BIS (seven items) assesses sensitivity to punishment and potential threats, whereas the BAS assesses reward-oriented approach tendencies through three subscales: Drive (four items), reflecting persistent goal pursuit; Reward Responsiveness (five items), reflecting positive responses to actual or anticipated rewards; and Fun Seeking (four items), reflecting spontaneous approach toward novel or rewarding experiences. Items were rated on a 4-point scale and summed for the BIS and each BAS subscale after reverse-scoring the two reverse-keyed BIS items, with higher scores indicating stronger tendencies in the respective domain.

A total of 39 participants were excluded on these grounds. We then excluded 12 participants whose initial responses had not passed the standard data-cleaning criteria for Sample 2 at the initial assessment. A further 15 were excluded because gender differed between the two assessments, or because reported age differed and could not be accounted for by a 1-year increase at the second assessment. Thus, 66 participants were excluded in total, leaving 294 for the test-retest analysis. The interval between assessments was M = 15.29 days (SD = 0.67; range = 14.95-18.78 days). Mean age was 53.1 years (SD = 12.5; range = 20-91 years); 154 participants reported their gender as man and 140 as woman.

Exploratory factor analysis

EFA was conducted on the 27 candidate items in Sample 1. Items were treated as ordinal variables with seven response categories, and a polychoric correlation matrix was calculated after reverse-scoring the relevant items. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) index was calculated from the polychoric correlation matrix of the 27 items to assess suitability for factor analysis. The number of factors was examined using parallel analysis, with 500 independent permutations within each item and a 95th-percentile criterion, and Velicer's minimum average partial (MAP) criterion [38, 39]. Factors were extracted using the minimum residual method (minres) with oblimin rotation. We examined a three-factor solution in light of both the factor-retention results and the theoretically proposed cognitive, behavioral, and affective dimensions. Item selection considered the pattern of factor loadings, consistency with theory, and factor interpretability. Parallel analysis, MAP, and three-factor EFA were repeated after item removal.

Confirmatory factor analysis

CFA was conducted in Sample 2, which was independent of Sample 1. For the 19 items selected in Sample 1, we examined a one-factor model in which all items loaded on a single factor and a higher-order three-factor model in which the cognitive, behavioral, and affective first-order factors loaded on a common higher-order factor. Because the higher-order portion of a model with three first-order factors is just-identified and yields the same overall fit as a correlated three-factor model, the latter was not examined separately. Items were treated as ordered categorical variables, and estimation used weighted least squares with mean and variance adjustment (WLSMV). Model fit was evaluated using the mean-and varianceadjusted χ² statistic, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and its 95% confidence interval, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). The scaled versions of CFI, TLI, and RMSEA were obtained from lavaan [40]. Following Weston and Gore [41], CFI and TLI values of at least .90 and RMSEA and SRMR values of .10 or lower were used as guidelines for acceptable fit, with the indices evaluated jointly [42]. Relative fit was compared using Satorra's [43] adjusted χ² difference test. Because the one-factor model lies on the boundary of the higher-order model's parameter space, the adjusted χ² difference test was treated as an approximate supplementary comparison and interpreted cautiously.

Internal consistency

Internal consistency of the final 19-item KSS overall score and its three subscales was assessed separately in Samples 1 and 2 using Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω total. Alpha was calculated from the covariance matrix of the observed item scores. Omega total was calculated from the higher-order CFA model estimated using WLSMV with the 19 items treated as ordinal variables. For the overall scale and each subscale, the calculation accounted for both variance attributable to the higher-order factor and common variance remaining in the corresponding first-order factors. The Green-Yang correction was applied to obtain estimates corresponding to composite scores based on the observed item responses, with observed-data variances used in the denominator [44]. Values of α and ω of at least .70 were used as guidelines for acceptable internal consistency [45].

Hypothesis testing for construct validity

To test hypotheses concerning construct validity, we calculated Pearson's product-moment correlations between the KSS and the other measures, together with their 95% confidence intervals. The individual hypotheses are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Incremental validity

To examine incremental validity in Sample 1, we conducted a hierarchical multiple regression analysis with DPES Contentment as the dependent variable. Step 1 included gender, age, the total savoring score, and gratitude as predictors. Savoring and gratitude were selected because they were expected to be related to knowing sufficiency and to explain substantial variation in contentment. Felt Sufficiency had considerable content overlap with DPES Contentment and was therefore considered unsuitable for examining the distinctive contribution of the KSS through incremental validity. Accordingly, only the other two subscales, Knowing What Is Enough and Stopping at Enough, were entered at Step 2. Regression coefficients were estimated using ordinary least squares. Because the residuals suggested heteroskedasticity, the type 3 heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC3) covariance matrix estimator was used to obtain coefficient standard errors and 95% confidence intervals. An HC3-robust Wald F test was used to test the null hypothesis that the coefficients for the two added subscales were jointly zero. Additional explained variance was evaluated using ΔR² and Cohen's f².

Test-retest reliability

Test-retest reliability of the KSS overall score and its three subscales was examined using the initial assessment (T1) and the follow-up assessment approximately 2 weeks later (T2) in Sample 2. Reliability was assessed using a two-way, absolute-agreement, singlemeasurement intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC(A,1), with its 95% confidence interval [46]. Following the COSMIN criteria for evaluating measurement properties, ICC values of at least .70 were interpreted as indicating sufficient test-retest reliability [47].

Discussion In this study, we proposed knowing sufficiency as a psychological construct comprising knowing what and how much is sufficient for one's well-being, not seeking beyond that point, and feeling content with what is already present in one's life, and developed the KSS in Japanese adults. Content validity assessment, addressing item relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility, and factor analyses in two independent samples yielded a 19-item scale comprising Knowing What Is Enough, Stopping at Enough, and Felt Sufficiency.

Internal consistency and approximately 2-week test-retest reliability coefficients met the specified criteria. Correlations with related constructs were consistent with several hypotheses, and the cognitive and behavioral dimensions were associated with dispositional contentment after accounting for savoring and gratitude. However, RMSEA did not meet the specified criterion for acceptable fit, and some correlations with related constructs did not meet our initial expectations. These findings provide initial evidence concerning the measurement properties of the KSS while also indicating a need to refine the conceptual positioning of knowing sufficiency.

Factor structure and reliability

For the final 19 items, both parallel analysis and MAP in the EFA suggested three factors.

Each item loaded most strongly on its intended factor, and all loadings on other factors were below .30 in absolute value. CFA in the independent Sample 2 indicated that the higher-order three-factor model fitted better than the one-factor model. Although CFI, TLI, and SRMR met the guidelines specified in this study, RMSEA was .103 [95% CI: .096-.111], so not all indices indicated acceptable fit. We cannot conclude that the current simple-structure model adequately accounts for the covariances among items. Taken together, however, the findings provide some support for the structural validity of the KSS in distinguishing its three dimensions.

Regarding internal consistency, α and ω were at least .70 for the KSS overall score and each subscale in both samples. Nevertheless, coefficients for Stopping at Enough were lower than those for the other two subscales. This may partly reflect its smaller number of items and its coverage of behaviors across different contexts, including acquisition, continuation of activities, and eating. For approximately 2-week test-retest reliability, ICC point estimates ranged from .720 to .791, indicating short-term stability of scores. These findings are consistent with the purpose of the KSS as a measure of dispositional tendencies, although stability over longer intervals and the precision with which change can be measured require separate investigation.

Construct validity

The KSS was associated with dispositional contentment in the expected direction. In particular, Felt Sufficiency showed a large correlation with DPES-J Contentment; its point estimate (r = .792) was larger than those for Knowing What Is Enough (r = .538) and Stopping at Enough (r = .423). A similar pattern was observed for life satisfaction. These findings are consistent with the interpretation of Felt Sufficiency as measuring the affective dimension of experiencing sufficiency in what one already has and in one's current life. They also accord with the conceptualization of contentment as arising from appraising one's present circumstances as sufficient and complete [9, 10].

Associations with savoring were also consistent with the focus of knowing sufficiency on one's present life. The KSS overall score showed a large positive correlation with Savoring the Moment and moderate positive correlations with Anticipating and Reminiscing.

Its positive association with gratitude further suggests that knowing sufficiency is related to savoring positive everyday experiences and positively appreciating what one already has.

However, the strength of these associations was not uniform: the correlation between Stopping at Enough and gratitude was smaller than the expected moderate association.

Conversely, correlations with Joy were larger than expected for the KSS overall score and Felt Sufficiency. Thus, these associations may reflect not only a connection with contentment but also commonality with a broader range of positive emotions.

Associations with social comparison differed according to what was being compared.

As expected, the KSS overall score showed a moderate negative correlation with abilityrelated comparison (r = -.300). However, it showed a moderate positive correlation with opinion-related comparison (r = .314), for which no hypothesis had been specified. A broad interpretation that people higher in knowing sufficiency do not compare themselves with others therefore appears inappropriate. Evaluating one's abilities in comparison with others needs to be distinguished from referring to others' opinions. This distinction is consistent with the separation of ability and opinion comparison in the INCOM and its Japanese adaptation [26, 27]. Knowing what is enough for oneself and being satisfied with one's present life may not imply a lack of interest in other people's perspectives.

Associations with psychological well-being revealed differences among the subscales in their relationships with Self-Acceptance. Felt Sufficiency showed the expected large positive correlation with Self-Acceptance (r = .554), whereas the correlation for Stopping at Enough was small (r = .201), leaving some hypotheses unsupported. An association between contentment and self-acceptance has also been reported previously [10, 12]. Our findings suggest that this association may correspond relatively closely to the affective dimension of The associations with materialism were negative, as expected, but smaller in magnitude than anticipated. Correlations of the KSS overall score with J-MVS-P6 Success/Centrality and Happiness were -.218 and -.342, respectively, and the expected moderate or large negative associations were generally not observed for the subscales either.

These findings do not support positioning knowing sufficiency as simply the opposite of materialism. One possible explanation is that the KSS assesses not only material possessions but also physical and mental states, activities, and relationships, which may attenuate these associations. Future research should therefore examine associations with actual ownership and acquisition behavior.

Regarding values, Knowing What Is Enough showed the expected moderate positive correlation with VQ Progress (r = .484). VQ Progress assesses progress in valued living [13], suggesting an association between knowing what is enough for oneself and living in personally important directions. However, the positive correlation between Felt Sufficiency and VQ Progress was larger than expected (r = .574). Associations with valued living are therefore not limited to the cognitive dimension of knowing sufficiency. The sense that one is living in accordance with what matters personally may be closely related to the affective experience of contentment with one's present life.

Associations with BAS Drive differed from expectations for the KSS overall score and all three subscales. We had expected no association or small negative associations with the KSS overall score, Knowing What Is Enough, and Felt Sufficiency, but instead observed small or moderate positive correlations. In particular, the correlation with Knowing What Is Enough was r = .349. The correlation with Stopping at Enough was close to zero (r = .033, 95% CI [-.059, .124]), so the expected negative association was not observed for this subscale either. These findings suggest that higher knowing sufficiency should not be understood as weaker motivation to pursue goals. Understanding what is enough for oneself may be compatible with actively pursuing what one considers necessary.

In contrast, Stopping at Enough showed a small negative correlation with BAS Fun Seeking (r = -.198), supporting the corresponding hypothesis. It also showed the expected small negative correlations with depressive symptoms and BIS (r = -.189 and -.195, respectively). This pattern supports the possibility that being able to stop at sufficiency differs from refraining from pursuit simply because one is depressed or because of sensitivity to punishment.

Incremental validity and theoretical implications

In the hierarchical multiple regression analysis, adding Knowing What Is Enough and Stopping at Enough to a model containing age, gender, savoring, and gratitude increased the explained variance in DPES-J Contentment by 7.55 percentage points. Both added subscales were positively associated with contentment after accounting for the other variables. Beyond savoring positive experiences and feeling grateful, knowing what is enough for oneself and tending to stop further pursuit once sufficiency is reached therefore provided additional information for understanding individual differences in dispositional contentment. These findings suggest that Knowing What Is Enough and Stopping at Enough have distinctive contributions to make as additional traits to consider in contentment research.

Taken together, this study highlights the value of examining knowing sufficiency in terms of recognizing what is enough, not seeking beyond that point, and experiencing contentment, rather than simply as low materialism or weak motivation to pursue things. In particular, its positive associations with goal pursuit and valued living suggest that being content with what is already present may be compatible with engaging in meaningful activities. Future research could measure the point at which individuals judge that they have enough and the point at which they actually stop an activity, for example in shopping or leisure contexts, and examine how these points relate to each KSS dimension. Repeated assessments in daily life could also examine whether recognizing sufficiency precedes a reduction in further pursuit and feelings of contentment, or whether contentment curbs subsequent pursuit. Such research could extend our understanding beyond correlations among individual differences in the three dimensions to the psychological processes linking them.

Limitations

First, participants were recruited through an online crowdsourcing service and did not constitute a probability sample representative of Japanese adults. Replication incorporating Fourth, the KSS was developed in Japanese, and its measurement properties were examined in Japanese adults; its properties in other languages and populations remain unknown. The English translations presented in this article are intended to communicate the item content, and their comprehensibility, factor structure, reliability, and validity have not been examined in English-speaking populations. Use in other languages and cultures requires not only translation and content validity assessment but also further validation, including examination of measurement invariance.

Conclusions

This study developed the 19-item KSS to measure the cognitive, behavioral, and affective dimensions of knowing sufficiency and provided initial evidence for its factor structure, internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and construct validity in Japanese adults. In particular, the incremental validity of Knowing What Is Enough and Stopping at Enough for dispositional contentment suggests the value of measuring the cognitive and behavioral dimensions of knowing sufficiency to understand individual differences in contentment.

Nevertheless, questions remain regarding generalizability and some of the hypotheses.

We also did not test whether the association between knowing sufficiency and well-being remained after accounting for income and living conditions. Including living conditions in future research will be important for distinguishing an actual lack of necessary resources from difficulty experiencing sufficiency despite having resources.

Second, the examinations of construct and incremental validity were based on associations among self-report measures administered at the same time. Shared response tendencies [50] and overlapping item content may therefore have contributed to the associations, and neither causality nor its direction can be determined. Although Stopping at Enough was designed to assess a behavioral dimension, it measured self-perceptions of behavior rather than actual behavior. Further clarification of KSS validity requires the integration of behavioral indicators and assessments in daily life.

Third, the factor structure and item composition warrant further examination. Fixing the small cross-loadings observed for several items in EFA to zero may have contributed to the inadequate RMSEA in CFA [51]. In Sample 2, we examined only conventional CFA models with cross-loadings fixed to zero and did not evaluate more flexible approaches such as exploratory structural equation modeling. Future research using an independent sample may therefore need to refine the item composition or factor model. In addition, content evaluations by experts and members of the target population were conducted mainly before item selection; the extent to which the final 19 items adequately cover the construct should be reassessed. Item 12, "I can stop eating when I feel about 80% full," had a relatively low loading and communality in the final EFA. Further examination of the behavioral items should consider both the benefit of including concrete everyday situations and the possibility that such items reflect domain-specific individual differences.
