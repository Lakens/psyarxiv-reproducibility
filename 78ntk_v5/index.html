Ethics Approval Check

Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, and it was present:

However, exploratory mixed-effects modelling revealed that participants were sensitive to increases in relative target distance, and additional load, but less sensitive to increases in target speed.

This demonstrates that participants were sensitive to social affordances, specifically opponents’ interception potential, shaped by postural information through facing direction when judging the minimum gap width they could successfully kick through.

Participants were recruited through university football clubs, social media and posters.

All participants were male Tier 2 athletes (Mckay et al., 2021) training at least three times per week to compete locally, with no history of motor, learning-related or neurological conditions, and no moderate to severe injury in the last three months, ensuring a coherent sample of comparable ability.

Participants were given an inconvenience allowance for their time.

For each testing session, participants were paired with another individual from the participant pool who acted as the opponent during the session.

Prior to testing, participants were given 10 minutes to conduct a self-led dynamic warm up, reducing the risk of muscular injury (Perez-Gomez, 2020).

Prior to the first trial, participants were verbally instructed by the experimenter to only attempt an interception if they believed they would get to the ball ahead of their opponent, and informed that movements of more than two steps towards the ball would be recorded as an interception attempt.

In study 1, participants completed 3 trials against their opponent at each target marker, with the order counterbalanced for each participant using a Latin Square and a Mirrored Latin Square, accounting for order effects.

In study 2, participants completed 6 trials per position.

In study 3 participants completed 10 trials per position.

Participants completed each study during separate testing sessions.

The order in which participants completed each study was counterbalanced by repeating the six possible study orders in a fixed sequence across participants, then alternating study orders across participants only completing studies 2 and 3. Nevertheless, this pattern suggests that participants were successful only up to the point that neither player held a spatial advantage, indicating that the proactive action strategy adopted by participants did not correspond with their interceptive success.

As shown in Figure 2c and Figure 2d, participants were more likely to attempt interceptive action, and succeed, when the oncoming ball was closer to them than their opponent, (i.e., when the distance ratio decreased).

This indicates that participants were sensitive to the spatial constraints of their environment when making interceptive decisions.

However, study 1 does not examine whether participants are sensitive to the temporal constraints of their environment.

If participants are sensitive to the temporal constraints of the environment, then they should perceive that they have less time to execute a successful interceptive action when the ball is moving more quickly and therefore be less likely to attempt interceptive action.

As shown in Figure 3e and Figure 3f, participants were more likely to attempt interceptive action and succeed when they had a distance advantage over their opponent at each ball speed.

As ball speed increased, participants were less likely to attempt an interception, highlighting that they were sensitive to the reduced time available when making interceptive decisions at higher ball speeds.

If participants are sensitive to the changes in their own, and their opponents, movement velocity brought on by additional load (Macadam et al., 2019), then they should attempt less interceptions when the load reduces their ability to reach the ball and attempt more when the load impacts their opponents’ abilities.

Accordingly, we hypothesised that mean PSE would be significantly different at each additional load condition, with higher PSEs when opponents are under load and lower PSEs when participants are under load.

As shown in Figure 4b, outcome-based PSEs were significantly greater than 1.0 across conditions (V = 2227, p = .028, r = 0.24), and when opponents were under load (-5kg: V = 120, p = .042, r = 0.50; -10kg: V = 114, p = .002, r = 0.79) indicating that participants were more successful when opponents were under load.

This highlights that participants were sensitive to the changes in their own and their opponents movement capabilities, when making interceptive decisions.

However, as participants had no explicit awareness of the specific load condition on each trial, and no opportunity to explore their action capabilities before or between trials, any influence of visual and proprioceptive cues was likely minimal and non-specific.

Exploratory evidence suggests that participants are sensitive to increases in target distance and additional load, even on a trial-by-trial basis, but less sensitive to changes in target speed.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Full text

Abstract

To perform well, athletes must perceive both individual and social affordances within sporting environments. Interceptive action is often crucial to sporting success, yet research investigating interceptive affordance perception in open-play competitive sporting environments is extremely limited. This novel research investigates how factors such as target distance, target speed, and additional load jointly influence affordance perception for interceptive actions against opponents. Across three pre-registered studies, university level footballers (n = 26) made action-based judgments on whether they could successfully intercept a target moving perpendicularly towards the space between themselves and an opponent. The position of the target systematically varied in its proximity to the participant versus the opponent. Participants consistently displayed proactive interceptive behaviour, persisting with manipulations to target speed, and additional load applied to players and opponents. However, exploratory mixed-effects modelling revealed that participants were sensitive to increases in relative target distance, and additional load, but less sensitive to increases in target speed. These findings have wide ranging implications across many sporting and non-sporting contexts, providing new insights to understanding affordance perception in competitive sporting situations and other fast-paced environments. Additionally, this work has practical applications, highlighting opportunities to explore coaching interventions to enhance interceptive decision-making during matches.

Introduction

Interceptive actions have been widely investigated in many everyday domains from collision avoidance in traffic situations (Chihak et al., 2014; Louveton et al., 2012), or navigating busy crowds (Taylor & Cinelli, 2021; Meerhoff et al., 2018), to catching, striking, tackling and blocking in sports such as baseball (Gray et al., 2025, Ranganathan and Carlton, 2007), cricket (Mann et al., 2010 , Runswick et al., 2018), tennis (Rosker & Majcen Rosker, 2021; Sáenz-Moncaleano et al., 2018), football (Travassos et al., 2012 , Zheng et al., 2021), and rugby (Brault et al., 2012; Passos et al., 2008). With their prevalence in everyday life, interceptive actions present a perceptual-motor challenge, requiring observers to estimate the trajectory of objects within the environment, and determine the most appropriate point of interception (Brenner & Smeets, 2015). For any perceptual-motor control process to perform efficiently, the perceiver must have access to information that informs: 1) when they should attempt to perform an action, and arguably more importantly, 2) when they should not.

Visual information processing such as object processing and spatial processing provides perceivers with cues about the properties of their environment and the objects within it (Adaval et al., 2018), informing the actions that the perceiver could potentially undertake (Cisek, 2007; Tucker & Ellis, 1998). This information is relayed to the motor system which facilitates the appropriate action(s), an important step in the visual cognitive process (Tucker & Ellis, 2001; Türkan et al., 2025). In his theory of direct perception, Gibson (1979) argues that vision provides information to specify opportunities for action, or affordances, which emerge from the relationship between an organism's action capabilities and the properties of their environment (Stoffregen, 2004). The environment is perceived relative to one's ability to act on it, a principle known as action-scaled perception (Fajen et al., 2011). The perception of affordances is important for making adaptive action decisions, but also for the ongoing guidance of action (Fajen et al., 2005). For example, In the case of football interception players perceive information within the environment-such as ball speed, trajectory and distance-and use that information to guide action. Here the key question is not simply "How fast is the ball?" but "Can I successfully act on this ball?". As informational variables in the environment change during ball flight, players must prospectively adjust their movements to ensure the interception remains possible-"Can I still act on this ball?". According to affordance theory, the ball presents an affordance for interception if the player perceives that it is possible to reach it. The interception affordance continues to exist up until the point that the ball is no longer possible to reach, at which point the player should pursue a different course of action, an example of affordance-based control (Fajen, 2005). Crucially, as action capabilties change, for example through fatigue or injury, the perception-action link must be rescaled to relevant informational variables, a process known as recalibration (Brand & de Oliveira, 2017; Withagen & Michaels, 2007). Individuals accurately and reliably perceive and update their affordances for many everyday actions including stair climbing, aperture passing, reaching, grasping, jumping, and catching in isolated environments (Barsingerhorn et al., 2012). However, sporting environments include other individuals, both teammates and opponents, and the potential to interact with these individuals gives rise to social affordances (Rietveld et al., 2013). Social affordances refer to the actions that other individuals afford the self, the actions that are afforded to others, and the actions that are afforded to a group within the environment (Valenti & Gold, 1991).

Performers may use kinematic cues from postural information (Abernethy & Zawi, 2007; Huesmann et al., 2023) or draw on contextual cues such as opponent preferences or behavioural patterns generated over the course of a match (Gray, 2002; Cañal-Bruland & Williams, 2010; Loffing et al., 2015) to anticipate the affordances, or potential actions, an opponent may undertake. These social affordances shape how players perceive interception opportunities, for example whether an opponent can reach a pass, or whether a teammate can receive one. Peker et al., (2023) found that perceived minimum kickable gap width between two footballers was influenced by kick distance and team affiliation, as well as their interaction. Perceptions of minimum kickable gap width increased as the distance of the gap from the kicker increased, as well as when the gap was formed by opponents rather than teammates. Crucially, the effect of kick distance was stronger when the gap was formed by opponents. In a second study, Peker et al. (2023) found a three-way interaction when additionally manipulating facing direction (whether players were facing inward towards the gap or outwards away from the gap). Specifically, minimum kickable gap widths were largest when the gap was formed by opponents facing inwards at higher kick distances. This demonstrates that participants were sensitive to social affordances, specifically opponents' interception potential, shaped by postural information through facing direction when judging the minimum gap width they could successfully kick through.

Sporting environments are highly dynamic and unpredictable, requiring athletes to perform highly technical and complex actions, adhering to sport specific rules, while their personal and social affordances are constantly changing (Fajen et al., 2008). A ubiquitous, yet vital action in many sports is interception: the act of preventing an object from continuing its trajectory. A substantial body of research examining the informational basis of interceptive behaviour suggests that interceptive actions are primarily guided by early ball flight information. Work in tennis (Triolet et al., 2013; Shim et al., 2005), cricket (Runswick et al., 2018), and baseball (Ranganathan & Carlton, 2007) shows that postural cues support the preparation of movement, primarily through stepping patterns, while the initiation and accuracy of interceptive action is based predominantly on early ball flight information.

Similarly, North et al., (2009) found that footballers anticipate pass outcomes more accurately in the absence of postural cues. Although, postural cues become crucial under severe temporal constraints such as penalty kicks (Dicks et al., 2010; Zheng et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, this research underscores the importance of early-ball flight information for interceptive actions in the absence of severe temporal constraints.

Successful interceptive action requires athletes to continuously perceive personal and social affordances, as well as regulate the initiation or withholding of actions based on whether they are afforded to the individual (Marinovic et al., 2009). Motor-response inhibition refers to the ability to cancel unwanted or incorrect motor actions (Diamond, 2013), allowing individuals to abandon pre-planned actions that may no longer be afforded (Gokeler et al., 2023). For example, a ball that was perceived as hittable for a baseball player when pitched, may no longer be hittable during flight, depending on the type of pitch used (fastball, curveball, changeup, slider etc). It is crucial that the baseball player can perceive this changed affordance and suppress their original motor response to prevent a strikeout or a mishit (Gray, 2025). Misperception of one's interceptive affordances, as well as an inability to suppress inappropriate actions can lead to negative sporting and personal outcomes, such as a loss of points, or injury (Fajen et al., 2008; Leventer et al., 2014; Gokeler et al., 2023).

In football specifically, interceptive action allows teams to foil opposition attacks, regain possession from the opposing team, and subsequently launch their own attacks to score points. However, unsuccessful interception attempts leave space for opposing teams to attack and exploit. Players must perceive informational variables such as ball speed, distance, and trajectory and use that information to guide action. Travassos et al. (2012) conducted time series analyses on intercepted and non-intercepted passes in futsal (five a side indoor football) and found that spatial constraints such as defenders' distances to the ball trajectory determined their interceptive success. However, it is still unknown how these constraints affect interceptive decisions , rather than outcomes. Barte et al. (2018) and Quammen et al. (2025) show that target speed shapes both perceived and actual interceptive ability during interceptive tasks against imaginary opponents. Although, it remains unclear how target speed impacts interception decisions in the presence of a real opponent, who possesses the real threat of acting on their affordances. Previous work on interceptive decision-making in football has typically focused on single informational constraints (e.g target speed), and as such has yet to capture how the combined manipulation of multiple informational variables and action capabilities shapes both the perceived and actual boundary at which interception is no longer possible against a live opponent.

The present research aims to address these gaps by using the Point of Subjective Equality (PSE: the point at which performers judge interceptive success and failure as equally likely) (Liuna & Dongjun, 2024) to capture the perceptual boundary at which interception is no longer possible, under manipulations to target distance, target speed, and additional load against a live opponent. Greater understanding of how footballers perceive their interceptive affordances could have implications for training and coaching interventions, leading to improved athletic performance (de Pinho et al., 2020). Beyond football, the present research addresses a broader psychological question concerning how performers perceive the boundaries for action opportunities, under changing informational constraints and disturbances to action capabilities, providing valuable insights into the processes underlying affordance perception in fast-paced interpersonal environments (Azam et al., 2024). This research examines how the combined manipulation of informational variables and individual action capabilities shape the perception of interceptive affordances across three separate studies. Study 1 isolates the effect of relative target distance on interceptive decision-making when presented with an oncoming ball against an opponent. Study 2 investigates the joint effects of relative target distance and target speed, while study 3 examines the joint effects of relative target distance and additional load. Across the three studies, we hypothesised that PSE would reflect the actor with the theoretical interception advantage. Specifically, in study 1 we hypothesised that the PSE would occur when the participant and their opponent were equidistant from the oncoming ball trajectory. In study 2 we hypothesised that the PSE would shift closer to the participant at slower ball speeds, and closer to the opponent at higher ball speeds, while we hypothesised in study 3 that the PSE would shift closer to whichever actor (participant or opponent) was wearing additional load.

Together, these studies map how the perceptual boundary for interceptive affordance perception shifts under differing constraints.

Methods

Participants

Sample size was calculated a-priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Faul et al., 2009). For all studies, the required power (1-β) was set at .9 and the significance level (α) set to .05. For study 1, a large effect size was used while being conservative (Cohen's D = 0.8) based on findings from Travassos et al. (2012) who found a large effect of defender distance to ball trajectory when observing interceptive actions in futsal. For studies 2 and 3, a moderate to large effect size of target speed, and additional load was used (Cohen's D = 0.65) to be conservative while reflecting previous research finding strong effects of target speed on interceptive affordances (Barte et al., 2018), and additional load on affordances for the self and others (Walter et al., 2019). For the parameters defined, a sample size of n = 15 was required for study 1, and n = 22 for studies 2 and 3. However, the stopping rule was set at n = 20 for study 1, and n = 26 for studies 2 and 3, to allow for potential outliers, participant drop out and/or experimenter error. Participants were recruited through university football clubs, social media and posters. Of the 85 participants who expressed interest, 26 met the eligibility criteria and took part in the studies (Supplementary Figure S1) (age: 19.4 ± 1.5 years, height: 180.7 ± 6.2 cm, body mass: 74.44 ± 10.05 kg, competitive experience: 12.7 ± 3.1 years). All participants were male Tier 2 athletes (Mckay et al., 2021) training at least three times per week to compete locally, with no history of motor, learning-related or neurological conditions, and no moderate to severe injury in the last three months, ensuring a coherent sample of comparable ability. Participants were given an inconvenience allowance for their time. Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Science and Technology Research Ethics Committee at the lead researcher's institution (FST-2024-5023-RECR-1) and all testing conformed to the standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This research was pre-registered before recruitment of the first participant (https://osf.io/r8j2v/overview?view_only=569577502c324d4197289c1b074c03df).

Materials

A Professional Football Delivery Machine (Pro Ball Launcher -Football Delivery

Machine, Net World Sports, Wrexham, UK) was used to ensure that the speed and trajectory of the ball remained consistent across trials. Size 5 official FIFA Quality Professional Match Balls were used (Puma Orbita 2 TB Pro Match Footballs, Pro:Direct Sport, Newton Abbot, United Kingdom) and inflated to 12.5 PSI (The Football Association, 2022), ensuring flight consistency across trials. Study 3 also used an adjustable weighted vest (20kg Adjustable Weight Vest, Bulldog Gear, Staffordshire, UK) to ensure consistency in the additional load applied to participants and their opponents across trials.

Experimental set up

The study was conducted on a standard sized 3G football pitch at the lead researcher's institution. Two flat markers (Flat Disc Markers, Vertent, Amazon, London, UK) were placed horizontally along the edge of the 18-yard box, equidistant from the centre of the box, separated by 9.14 metres (10 yards) adapting previous research designs (Quammen et al., 2025). These marked the starting point for the participant and opponent during each trial. In front of these starting points, five flat target markers were placed horizontally along the pitch (see Figure 1a), each separated by 2.29 metres (2.5 yards) to provide consistent spatial constraints for the task. They were placed such that there were markers positioned 9.14 metres (10 yards) directly in front of the participant and opponent, with three markers placed between them, the middle marker being equidistant from the outermost markers. These markers acted as targets for the ball to be aimed, and subsequently intercepted, during each trial. The Pro Football Delivery Machine (Pro Ball Launcher -Football Delivery Machine, Net World Sports, Wrexham, UK) was aligned with each of the five target markers before release, then used to launch oncoming balls to each marker in a straight-line trajectory from 18.3 metres (20 yards) away (27.4 metres (30 yards) away from the participant and opponent start positions). This set up aimed to mimic competitive match situations (Cordon-Carmona et al. 2023; Coito et al., 2023) as medium length passes are used more often around the 18yard box. For each testing session, participants were paired with another individual from the participant pool who acted as the opponent during the session.

Procedure

Prior to testing, participants were given 10 minutes to conduct a self-led dynamic warm up, reducing the risk of muscular injury (Perez-Gomez, 2020). Each trial began with the participant and opponent at their starting points (Figure 1a). Prior to the first trial, participants were verbally instructed by the experimenter to only attempt an interception if they believed they would get to the ball ahead of their opponent, and informed that movements of more than two steps towards the ball would be recorded as an interception attempt. Opponents were instructed to attempt an interception on every trial. Upon a signal from the experimenter, the ball was launched in a straight-line trajectory at 30mph, reflecting average passing velocities (Pepping et al., 2011; Russell et al., 2010), towards one of the five target markers using the Pro Football Delivery Machine. Consistent with best practice in sports decision-making research, participants performed a perception-action coupling level 6 (Huesmann & Loffing, 2024) forced choice decision-making task (Raab & Johnson, 2004; Savelsbergh et al., 2002) in which they could either a) attempt to intercept the oncoming ball, or b) retreat towards the penalty spot (defend), depending on their perceived best course of action. In study 1, participants completed 3 trials against their opponent at each target marker, with the order counterbalanced for each participant using a Latin Square and a Mirrored Latin Square, accounting for order effects. There were 10 possible position orders, with 2 participants being randomly allocated to each using an online randomisation tool (12345, 23451, 34512, 45123, 51234, 54321, 15432, 21543, 32154, 43215) (Research Randomiser: https://www.randomizer.org). The participant and opponent were given a 5-minute break halfway through the study, to minimise fatigue. In study 2, participants completed 6 trials per position. Within those trials, oncoming balls were launched at 20mph, 30mph, and 40mph, with two trials at each ball speed. The position order was counterbalanced using the same method as study 1. We also counterbalanced the order in which the ball speed conditions were presented to participants. There were six different condition orders: 20mph, 30mph, 40mph; 20mph, 40mph, 30mph; 30mph, 20mph, 40mph; 30mph, 40mph, 20mph; 40mph, 20mph, 30mph; 40mph, 30mph, 20mph. We repeated the six possible orders in a fixed sequence across successive participants. In study 3 participants completed 10 trials per position. Within those trials, two were completed under five different additional load conditions: -10kg (opponent under load), -5kg, 0kg, +5kg, (participant under load) and +10kg. The position order was counterbalanced using the same method as study 1. The additional load condition order was counterbalanced using a Latin Square and a Mirrored Latin Square. There were 10 possible condition orders, with participants being randomly allocated to each using an online randomisation tool (Research Randomiser: https://www.randomizer.org). In studies 2 and 3, the participant and opponent were given short breaks every 10 trials, to minimise fatigue. Immediately following each trial, participant action decisions (intercept vs defend) and trial outcomes (successful vs unsuccessful interception) were agreed upon by two researchers then recorded. In the case of disagreement, a third researcher provided the final opinion. Participants completed each study during separate testing sessions. The order in which participants completed each study was counterbalanced by repeating the six possible study orders in a fixed sequence across participants, then alternating study orders across participants only completing studies 2 and 3. Analysis

Player vs opponent distance-to-ball ratios were calculated for the five target markers by dividing the distance between the participant and the target marker, by the distance between the opponent and the target marker. For target markers directly in front of the participant or opponent, the straight-line distance from their starting position to the target marker was used (9.14 metres). Pythagorean theorem was then used to calculate the diagonal distances between players and target markers, when necessary, to ascertain the shortest path for each player to the interception point (See Figure 1b). Therefore, the distance ratio provides a relational measure of the spatial advantages or disadvantages at ball release. To determine how participants' action decisions were influenced by distance to ball ratio, we calculated the point of subjective equality (PSE) for each participant, representing the distance ratio at which the probability of the participant attempting an interception was 0.5.

Decision-based PSE for each participant was calculated by performing a binomial linear model predicting participants' action decision (intercept vs defend) against distance ratio. A PSE <1.0 indicated more cautious interceptive behaviour, as the oncoming ball was closer to the participant than the opponent when their probability of attempting an interception was 0.5. Likewise, a PSE >1.0 indicated more proactive interceptive behaviour, as the oncoming ball was closer to the opponent when participant probability of interceptive behaviour was at 0.5. A PSE of 1.0 indicated that the oncoming ball was equidistant between the participant and the opponent when the probability of participants attempting an interception was at 0.5.

To determine how trial outcomes were influenced by distance ratio, we removed trials in which participants did not attempt an interception and then performed another binomial linear model for each participant predicting participant trial outcomes (successful vs unsuccessful interceptions) against distance ratio. The distance ratio at which the probability was 0.5 was taken as the outcome-based PSE. Due to the structural properties of the outcome-based data, specifically the reduced and asymmetrical number of observations per participant leading to instances of non-estimable psychometric functions (PSEs could not be reliably estimated) and unstable model fits, interpretations based on outcome-based PSEs are made cautiously.

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 4.5.2). Prior to analysis, outliers were removed using median absolute deviation, which involved removing participants with PSE scores ± 3 absolute deviations from the median. This approach ensured that the outlier identification process was less influenced by extremely large or non-estimable PSEs (Leys et al., 2013). For study 1, a one-sample t-test was conducted to investigate whether PSEs across participants differed significantly from 1.0 (the point at which the oncoming ball is equidistant between the participant and opponent). For studies 2 and 3, Holm corrected one-sample t-tests were conducted investigating whether PSEs for each condition, and across conditions, differed significantly from 1.0. To determine whether PSEs varied significantly between conditions, one-way repeated measures ANOVAs were conducted comparing PSEs across conditions. Holm-Bonferroni corrections were also applied to these analyses to control for multiple comparisons. The above analyses were conducted for both decision-based and outcome-based PSEs. These analyses were supported by Bayes Factors (BF) (Dienes et al., 2017) given the limitations of frequentist statistics, specifically their inability to quantify the strength of evidence supporting the null hypothesis. As the reliability of prior specifications based upon a single study can be limited by the methodological quality of that study, default priors were used reflecting the frequency of observing effect sizes across the psychological field (Rouder et al., 2012). Strength of evidence was judged according to Kass & Rafferty (1995) criteria in which BF <0.33 provides evidence in favour of the null hypothesis, while BF>3.0 indicate support for the alternative hypothesis.

Exploratory Analysis

To complement the primary analyses, we conducted two generalised binomial linear mixed effects models for each study. These exploratory models enabled the investigation of interception probabilities across distance ratio, ball speed, and additional load at the population level, as well as the examination of potential interactions while retaining participant-level random effects. Full models were constructed from the outset (Meteyard & Davies, 2020) with action decisions (1 = intercept,0 = defend), or trial outcomes (1 = successful interception,0 = unsuccessful interception) as the dependant variable and fixed effects of distance ratio, trial number, ball speed/additional load, and the interaction between distance ratio and ball speed/additional load. Distance ratio, ball speed and additional load were standardised before being entered into the models. A 'maximal approach', was taken, fitting the models with increasingly complex random effects while maintaining the same fixed effects (Barr et al., 2013). The most complex models that did not encounter convergence or singularity issues were chosen (Barr et al., 2013; Meteyard & Davies 2020).

Fixed effects were evaluated for significance using Wald z tests while confidence intervals were estimated using the Wald method from the lmerTest package (version 3.1-3; Kuznetsova et al., 2017). Pre-registered analyses were followed in full. However, the Study 1 analytical approach was adjusted to align with studies 2 and 3, and trial outcomes were modelled in the same way as action decisions, enhancing comparability across studies.

Study 2 Results

Proactive interceptive behaviour persists across changes to ball speed

Study 2 replicated the pattern of proactive interceptive behaviour from study 1, yet interceptive action decisions were systematically influenced by the temporal constraints of the environment. As ball speed increased, participants were less likely to attempt an interception, highlighting that they were sensitive to the reduced time available when making interceptive decisions at higher ball speeds. These findings build on study 1, showing that participants jointly use spatial and temporal information to make interception decisions.

Nevertheless, study 2 leaves open the question of how interception decisions are shaped by disturbances to one's action capabilities. To address this, study 3 aimed to investigate how additional load impacts interceptive decision-making. If participants are sensitive to the changes in their own, and their opponents, movement velocity brought on by additional load (Macadam et al., 2019), then they should attempt less interceptions when the load reduces their ability to reach the ball and attempt more when the load impacts their opponents' abilities. Accordingly, we hypothesised that mean PSE would be significantly different at each additional load condition, with higher PSEs when opponents are under load and lower PSEs when participants are under load.

General Discussion

Across three separate studies, this research aimed to investigate the effects of target distance, target speed, and additional load on interceptive actions using a novel interceptive decision-making task. Study 1 explored the hypothesis that participants would be sensitive to the relative advantages/disadvantages arising from differences in target distance and therefore the mean PSE would occur when neither the participant nor the opponent had a distance advantage. Study 2 explored the hypothesis that participants would be sensitive to the tighter temporal constraints of the environment at higher ball speeds and therefore PSEs would be greater at lower ball speeds than higher ball speeds. Lastly, in study 3, we explored the hypothesis that participants would be sensitive to the changes in their action capabilities brought on by additional load and therefore PSEs would increase as the additional load shifted from the participants to their opponents.

The findings from all three studies demonstrate that participants consistently used a proactive action strategy, readily initiating interception attempts even when at a distance disadvantage relative to their opponents. These attempts were often unsuccessful indicating that participants may have been prone to overestimating their interceptive ability. These findings corroborate research by Quammen et al. (2025) who found initial overestimations in interceptive ability across footballers, which decreased over time during an interceptive decision-making task. This behaviour may reflect limited motor-response inhibition (Diamond, 2013), preventing the suppression of interceptive action once emerging information dictates that it is no longer afforded. Given that expert athletes typically display enhanced motor inhibitory control compared to less expert athletes (Fledderman et al., 2023; Simonet et al., 2023) one may expect experts to produce fewer overestimation errors.

However, this contradicts the findings reported by Quammen et al. (2025) that expert athletes produce more overestimation errors than less experts.

Instead, this behaviour may reflect a strategy used to identify individual maximal action capabilities within a novel task through error-based feedback (Truong et al., 2023). Indeed, interceptive errors provide opportunities to explore new movement solutions and evaluate how each affects the achievement of task goals (Silva et al., 2019), thereby facilitating the perception of new affordances leading to more accurate action decisions (Franchak et al., 2010). In the present task, where the opponent is a live actor, error-based feedback is especially important to evaluate not only task constraints, but to evaluate how the opponents' action capabilities relate to their own. The low-risk nature of the task also provides a conducive environment for the use of error-based feedback, due to the lack of negative consequences for interception errors, allowing participants to fully explore the affordance landscape at no detriment to themselves or any teammates.

Alternatively, these overestimations may serve as a strategic attempt to intimidate or pressure the opponent into producing an error, consistent with principles of high pressing football (Forcher et al., 2022; Low et al., 2021). Considering the nature of the outcome-based PSE data, further research is needed firstly to validate the findings before strong comparative claims can be made, as well as examine whether they reflect perceptual error or serve a strategic function related to opponent information gathering (potentially mediated by contextual factors relating to risk) or intimidation. Exploratory findings also indicated that interception decisions and outcomes consistently varied with distance ratio, echoing previous research showing that athletes use spatial information relating to their opponents when making competitive action decisions in football (Vilar et al., 2012) and other sports (Correia et al., 2012). While distance ratio captures the relative spatial advantages between players at ball release, more dynamic measures such as time to arrival (Travassos et al., 2012) or required running velocity derived through video analysis may further shed light on interceptive affordance perception within football. It is also important to note that throughout the studies, on some trials participants may have made interception decisions based on the visibility of the ball machine and which marker it was aligned to prior to ball release, in line with classic affordance-perception paradigms where information is available for decisionmaking prior to action (Warren, 1984; Mark et al., 1990). Further research is needed to examine whether the behavioural patterns observed across all three studies persists when spatial information within the environment is less readily available.

The findings from Study 2 demonstrate that participants adapted their action decisions to the different target speeds presented. These findings are in line with previous research highlighting the use of early ball flight information when making interceptive action decisions in football (Barte et al., 2018; Quammen et al., 2025) and striking sports (Tresilian et al., 2003; Ranganthan & Carlton, 2007; Runswick et al., 2018). However, interceptive outcomes appeared to be unaffected by target speed. Moreover, exploratory findings suggest that interceptive outcomes, but not decisions, were partially determined by an interaction between the spatial and temporal constraints in the environment. These patterns echo previous research showcasing that spatial-temporal variables significantly impact interceptive actions (Travassos et al., 2012), passing (Corrêa et al., 2012), shooting, (Vilar at al., 2012), and penalty kicks (van der Kamp et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2021) in football.

Though, the present study appears to uncover a mismatch between the perceptual information used to make interceptive decisions, and the information that determines interceptive success. A potential factor in this result may be the use of the football delivery machine, removing kinematic cues that would ordinarily convey ball speed information prior to release. Pinder et al. (2009) found that cricket players were able to successfully intercept balls from projection machines, even at high velocities, by relying on early-ball flight information. However, they also found significant differences in the backswing initiation and peak height, front foot placement and movement, downswing, and step length when batters action to perceptual information following a disturbance to either perception or action (Brand & De Oliveira, 2017). Previous research in both athletes and non-athletes demonstrates perceptual-motor recalibration with load-based changes to action capabilities (Nakamoto et al., 2012; Scott & Gray, 2010). Although Wilson et al. (2023) found that footballers struggled to recalibrate to loads of 450g applied to the ankles during a 1 v 1 anticipation task, only being able to recalibrate towards their maximal action capability when the load was removed.

Despite the pattern of results, it is difficult to conclusively determine whether recalibration occurred as there was no explicitly measured perceptual-motor baseline and only a short rearrangement period after the application of additional load, with no removal stage or post-rearrangement period to assess a return to baseline (Brand & De Oliveira, 2017). Additionally, while loads of 5-10kg applied to the torso have been known to significantly reduce running capabilities (Macadam et al., 2019), without baseline sprint measures, the observed changes in decision-making cannot be explicitly attributed or scaled to any load-induced changes in action capabilities.

Nevertheless, the results indicate that participants altered their action decisions on the second trial, potentially reflecting an attempt to recalibrate their perceptions following the disturbance to their movement. This builds on previous work showing that individuals may use relevant perceptual-motor information to update perceptions of their own abilities to achieve task goals (van Andel et al., 2017). However, as recalibration is driven through active exploration (Brand & De Oliveira, 2017), further research with action capability measures before and after load application is needed to confirm this implication. Also, further research using a greater number of trials is needed to strengthen the reliability of PSE estimates. It is possible that visual or proprioceptive cues from the weighted vest being applied or adjusted to participants and their opponents may have partially influenced decision-making prior to ball release. However, as participants had no explicit awareness of the specific load condition on each trial, and no opportunity to explore their action capabilities before or between trials, any influence of visual and proprioceptive cues was likely minimal and non-specific. While athletes and their opponents are not subject to additional load during matches, these findings are relevant to other action capability disturbances such as fatigue over the course of a match (Balagué et al., 2019), or injuries over a longer time span (Van Andel et al., 2017).

The present work contributes to affordance theory by demonstrating how perceptual boundaries shift under manipulation to specifying informational variables and individual action capabilities during fast-paced interceptive tasks. Our findings align with research on interceptive actions, as well as broader work demonstrating altered behaviour when informational variables change (Quammen et al., 2025; Travassos et al., 2012; Azam et al., 2024), or with disturbances to individual action capabilities (Barte et al., 2018; Gotardi et al., 2024). However, the present work also reveals a more complex relationship, whereby perceptual boundaries emerge from the integration of multiple informational and task constraints, and shift in relation to changes in these constraints. The findings support ecological accounts of behaviour, supporting the notion that affordances are relational and adaptive, continuously shifting in response to changing actor-environment relations (Stoffregen, 2004).

From an applied perspective, these findings support the use of constraints-based coaching interventions, highlighting that decision-making within football is heavily influenced by environmental and task constraints. Further empirical evidence documents the positive effects that constraints-based manipulations have on decision-making behaviour (Corrêa et al., 2012; Davids et al., 2013; Ometto et al., 2018; de Pinho et al., 2020). Coaching interventions such as small-sided games may provide the spatio-temporal constraints necessary to improve interceptive decision-making, while providing a sufficient fatigue stimulus to alter individual action capabilities and elicit real time perceptual-motor recalibration (Wilson et al., 2023).

Though, it is important to note limitations of this research. The use of a forced choice decision-making task (Raab & Johnson, 2004) does not capture the richness of competitive environments with the wide array of actions available to athletes. Further consideration of interceptive decision making while incorporating the continuous interpersonal interplay between attackers and defenders will help to clarify the actions made in competitive environments (Müller et al., 2026; Ramsey et al., 2025). Additionally, the present task does not fully capture the multitude of match contexts in which these interceptive actions may occur. Future research should investigate how contextual factors, such as game score or time remaining, impact perceptual boundaries in open-play football (Loffing & Cañal-Bruland, 2017).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research demonstrates that participants display proactive behaviour during an interceptive decision-making task across varying spatial and temporal constraints and under disturbances to action capabilities. Exploratory evidence suggests that participants are sensitive to increases in target distance and additional load, even on a trial-by-trial basis, but less sensitive to changes in target speed. These findings suggest that footballers behave in a way that enables continuous evaluation of their maximal action capabilities, while being highly sensitive to spatial constraints but less sensitive to spatio-temporal constraints.

Together, the studies provide a foundation for understanding how athletes perceive their interceptive affordances in competitive contexts. Future work should explore the role of kinematic and contextual information on perceptual boundaries and explore coaching interventions to enhance real-time decision-making during matches.
