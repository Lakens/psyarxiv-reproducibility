Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Abstract

Satisfying romantic relationships are important for well-being yet are difficult to maintain, as sexual satisfaction and relationship quality often decline across early relationship development.

Sexual growth and destiny beliefs reflect whether sexual satisfaction is viewed as something that develops through effort or depends on natural, inherent compatibility. Such beliefs may be associated with how couples navigate changes in their relationship. Using longitudinal data from 161 couples (N= 322) early in their relationship, followed over 18 months, we examined whether individuals' and their partners' sexual beliefs predicted trajectories of sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment. Relationship satisfaction and commitment showed small declines over time, whereas sexual satisfaction and sexual beliefs remained relatively stable. Higher actor and partner sexual growth beliefs were associated with higher levels of all outcomes in general, and individuals with partners' higher in sexual growth beliefs were buffered from steeper declines in relationship satisfaction and commitment over time. In contrast, higher actor, but not partner, sexual destiny beliefs were associated with lower relationship satisfaction and commitment in general. Overall, the findings suggest that sexual beliefs function as relatively stable cognitive frameworks, with partner sexual growth beliefs playing a role in protecting relationship quality and commitment during early relationship development.

Do Sexual Beliefs Shape Relationship Quality Over Time? Evidence From Early

Romantic Relationships

Early romantic relationships are often characterized by high levels of passion, sexual satisfaction, and relationship quality, which tend to change as couples transition out of the "honeymoon phase" and into a more stable relational period (Carswell et al., 2019; Freeman et al., 2023; Schmiedeberg & Schröder, 2016). During this transition, maintaining sexual satisfaction may be particularly important, given that sexual satisfaction is a central component of romantic relationships and a key indicator of overall relationship quality (Joel et al., 2020; Park et al., 2023). Individuals who report higher sexual satisfaction also tend to report greater relationship satisfaction and commitment (Sprecher, 2002; Sprecher & Cate, 2004), and longitudinal work suggests that changes in sexual satisfaction predict later changes in relationship satisfaction, but not the reverse (Park et al., 2023). Given these close links, changes in sexual satisfaction as relationships develop may also be accompanied by changes in broader relationship quality. Indeed, sexual satisfaction has been shown to increase during the first year of dating and subsequently decline (Schmiedeberg & Schröder, 2016). Relationship satisfaction also tends to decline across the early years of relationships, with meta-analytic evidence suggesting that the steepest declines occur relatively early in relationship development (Bühler et al., 2021; Bühler & Orth, 2024). Commitment may follow a somewhat different pattern, with cross-sectional data suggesting progressively higher commitment over the first three years of dating, but lower commitment at longer durations thereafter (Freeman et al., 2023). Together, these findings suggest that early relationship development is a consequential period of change in sexual and relational experiences, highlighting the importance of identifying factors that are associated with how these trajectories unfold over time.

One such factor may be individuals' lay beliefs about how sexual satisfaction is maintained. Individuals who endorse sexual growth beliefs view sexual satisfaction as something that can be maintained through effort, communication, compromise, and the resolution of differences. In contrast, those higher in sexual destiny beliefs view sexual satisfaction as something dependent on inherent compatibility and "natural" sexual chemistry (Maxwell et al., 2017). In general, sexual growth beliefs are associated with higher sexual and relationship satisfaction for both partners (Wu & Zheng, 2022), even in the face of sexual disagreements (e.g., Maxwell et al., 2017). In contrast, sexual destiny beliefs have been linked to lower sexual and relationship satisfaction, particularly when couples face sexual difficulties or differences, as these challenges are more likely to be interpreted as signs of incompatibility (e.g., Maxwell et al., 2017; Raposo et al., 2021; Rossi et al., 2022).

However, we know relatively little about how sexual beliefs change over time as a relationship develops, or how they are associated with trajectories of sexual satisfaction and relationship quality. To date, sexual growth and destiny beliefs have primarily been studied among individuals in established relationships (Cultice et al., 2021; Maxwell et al., 2017; Raposo et al., 2021; Rossi et al., 2022; Uppot et al., 2024; Wu & Zheng, 2022). Across early relationship development, changes in sexual desire or frequency may shift these beliefs, which may be linked to sexual and relationship satisfaction and commitment over time. In the current study, we examine trajectories of sexual growth and destiny beliefs in couples early in their relationship, followed over 18 months, and test whether sexual growth and destiny beliefs are associated with sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment over time.

Trajectories of Sexual Satisfaction and Relationship Quality in Early Relationships

Although sexual and relationship satisfaction often decline as relationships progress, the timing and pattern of these changes differ across aspects of relationship quality. Sexual satisfaction tends to be particularly high early in relationships: in a large longitudinal German panel study, sexual satisfaction increased during the first year of a relationship and then declined (Schmiedeberg & Schröder, 2016). Relationship satisfaction also tends to decline over time, with the largest declines occurring during the early years (Bühler et al., 2021) and particularly pronounced declines among relationships that eventually dissolve (Bühler & Orth, 2024).

Commitment may follow a different pattern, with cross-sectional evidence suggesting higher commitment across the first several years of dating (Freeman et al., 2023).

These patterns point to a potentially paradoxical feature of early relationship development: partners may become increasingly invested in and motivated to maintain their relationship (Rusbult, 1980 (Rusbult, , 1983)), even as they begin to encounter declines or changes in sexual desire and satisfaction (Frederick et al., 2017; Schmiedeberg & Schröder, 2016). How partners interpret these changes may therefore be particularly important. Declines in sexual desire or satisfaction may be understood as normative challenges that can be navigated together or, alternatively, as signs of deeper incompatibility between partners. Consistent with this possibility, among women who perceived declines in sexual desire from earlier in their relationships, some described maintaining high relationship quality despite this, whereas others questioned their relationships and reported greater conflict (Moor et al., 2021). Given that couples may experience sexual changes such as declines in desire during early relationship development, beliefs about how sexual satisfaction is maintained, rather than beliefs about relationship maintenance more generally, might play a particularly important role in sexual and relationship quality during this time. That is, sexual growth and destiny beliefs may guide interpretation of sexual changes during this time, shaping how couples respond to such changes and potentially buffering against or exacerbating declines in sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment.

Trajectories of Sexual Growth and Destiny Beliefs

Relatively little is known about how sexual growth and destiny beliefs themselves change over time. Research on implicit beliefs about relationships more broadly suggests that these beliefs show low to moderate stability over time, with test-retest reliabilities for relationship beliefs ranging from approximately .40 to .74 (Franiuk et al., 2004; Knee, 1998). One study found that, across two time points, five months apart, general growth beliefs increased, whereas general destiny beliefs decreased slightly over the same period, showing that implicit relationship beliefs may fluctuate over time (Gander et al., 2025).

To our knowledge, no study has tracked sexual growth and destiny beliefs over the early stages of a romantic relationship. Previous research has demonstrated that relationship and sexual beliefs can be shaped temporarily by experience and intervention (Maxwell et al., 2017; Sutherland & Rehman, 2018), suggesting they might change over time, especially during relationship transition periods. Relational perceptions and idealized assumptions tend to recalibrate as couples transition out of early-stage romantic intensity (e.g., initially viewing a partner as "perfect" or "always understanding," and later recognizing realistic flaws and recurring disagreements; Murray et al., 2011; Niehuis et al., 2016). Given that sexual growth and destiny beliefs guide interpretations of sexual changes (Maxwell et al., 2017), early relationship development may represent a developmental window in which discrepancies between sexual expectations and reality reinforce or reshape these beliefs.

Associations Between Lay Sexual Beliefs and Sexual Satisfaction and Relationship Quality

A growing body of research demonstrates that implicit beliefs about sexuality are associated with sexual and relational well-being. Sexual growth beliefs have been associated with higher sexual and relationship satisfaction for individuals and greater satisfaction among their partners (Maxwell et al., 2017), as well as lower sexual rejection sensitivity and greater responsiveness during sexual challenges (Cultice et al., 2022; Uppot et al., 2024). In contrast, sexual destiny beliefs have been linked to poorer sexual and relationship well-being, particularly when couples experience sexual incompatibilities or challenges (Maxwell et al., 2017; Raposo et al., 2021). However, these associations may depend on perceived sexual incompatibility. Wu and Zheng (2022) found that both sexual growth and destiny beliefs were positively associated with satisfaction with sexual communication, and subsequent work has suggested that the positive association with destiny beliefs may be more likely among people who perceive high sexual compatibility (Uppot et al., 2024). Most past research on sexual beliefs has been conducted with couples facing sexual challenges, such as those with low sexual desire, couples transitioning to parenthood, or couples in established relationships (Cultice et al., 2022; Maxwell et al., 2017; Raposo et al., 2021; Rossi et al., 2022; Uppot et al., 2024; Wu & Zheng, 2022). As a result, we know relatively little about how these beliefs function during normative changes in early relationships.

The early years of a relationship are characterized by shifts in satisfaction and commitment (Bühler et al., 2021; Bühler & Orth, 2024; Freeman et al., 2023; Schmiedeberg & Schröder, 2016). During this period, partners must determine whether emerging declines in desire and satisfaction reflect normative changes that can be navigated or deeper incompatibilities. Sexual growth and destiny beliefs are especially relevant to this interpretive process because they shape whether sexual changes are understood as requiring effort and problem-solving or whether they reflect inherent incompatibilities between partners (Maxwell et al., 2017). However, we do not yet know whether these beliefs change during the early stages of a relationship or whether they are associated with trajectories of sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment during this period. Examining these patterns can help clarify whether sexual beliefs function as relatively stable cognitive frameworks that are associated with how couples interpret and respond to sexual and relational changes as more enduring relationship patterns are established.

As sexual growth and destiny beliefs shape how sexual differences and declines are interpreted, they may also be relevant to how partners respond to one another, not only to how each person experiences their own relationship. Partners higher in sexual growth beliefs may be more likely to view emerging sexual differences as challenges that can be addressed rather than signs of incompatibility, and prior work suggests that growth beliefs are associated with greater responsiveness during sexual challenges (Cultice et al., 2022; Uppot et al., 2024). A partners' responses to sexual differences can shape one another's relationship experiences; therefore, a partner's sexual beliefs may be associated with the trajectory of one's own relationship satisfaction and commitment, above and beyond one's own beliefs. Prior dyadic research has shown that partners' sexual beliefs are associated with sexual and relational experiences (Maxwell et al., 2017; Uppot et al., 2024), but it remains unclear whether a partner's sexual beliefs are associated with how relationship quality unfolds over longer periods of relationship development.

Current Study

In the current study, we examine trajectories of sexual growth and destiny beliefs during early relationship development and test how sexual beliefs are associated with sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment. We recruited couples in the first year of their relationship and followed them over 18 months, with reports every three months. First, we explored how sexual growth and destiny beliefs change over time. Next, we examined trajectories of our key outcomes, sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment over time. We then examined whether sexual beliefs were associated with these outcomes and their trajectories over time. Finally, we conducted exploratory analyses to test whether one partner's sexual beliefs were associated with changes in relationship quality over time, as well as covariate analyses accounting for general relationship beliefs to examine whether associations with sexual beliefs remained after accounting for general relationship beliefs. This study was pre-registered, and the data and code are on OSF https://osf.io/f3gw8/overview?view_only=9f1ab0ce98014c27831ed5e75540aa66.

Method Participants

The current data are from the Experiences in Relationships Overtime Study (EROS), which consisted of seven online surveys: a 60-minute baseline survey and six 45-minute followup surveys administered every 3 months over 18 months. Recruitment occurred from November 2020 to January 2022. Eligible couples were those in which both partners agreed to participate, were 18 years or older, in a committed relationship for at least 3 months and less than 15 months, sexually active, and living in Canada or the United States. We aimed to recruit 200 couples, with recruitment continuing for the duration of the planned recruitment period. In total, 198 couples (n = 396) completed the baseline survey. For the current analyses, we included participants who completed at least two time points. Measures

Sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment were assessed at all time points. Sexual growth and destiny beliefs, as well as general relationship growth and destiny beliefs, were assessed at baseline, 6 months, 12 months, and 18 months. Unless otherwise noted, items were averaged so that higher scores reflected higher levels of each construct. Descriptive statistics and reliability estimates at each time point are reported in Table 2, correlations among key variables are reported in Table 3, and full versions of all measures are provided in the supplementary materials.

Sexual Growth and Destiny Beliefs

Sexual growth and destiny beliefs were assessed using the shortened 10-item Sexual Growth and Destiny Scale (Maxwell et al., 2017). Participants rated items on a 7-point scale from1 = strongly disagree to7 = strongly agree. Five items assessed sexual growth beliefs, or the belief that sexual satisfaction can be developed and maintained through effort and communication (M = 5.75, SD = 1.01, ω = .95), and five items assessed sexual destiny beliefs, or the belief that sexual satisfaction depends on natural compatibility (e.g., "A couple is either destined to have a satisfying sex life, or they are not"; M = 3.58, SD = 1.62, ω = .98).

Relationship Growth and Destiny Beliefs

Relationship growth and destiny beliefs were assessed using the 22-item Implicit Theories of Relationships Scale (Maxwell et al., 2017). Eleven items assessed relationship growth beliefs (M = 5.32, SD = 0.86, ω = .96), and eleven items assessed relationship destiny beliefs (M = 4.07, SD = 1.31, ω = .98), rated on the same 7-point scale.

Sexual Satisfaction

Sexual satisfaction was assessed with the 5-item Global Measure of Sexual Satisfaction (GMSEX; Lawrance & Byers, 1998). Participants rated their sexual relationship on 7-point semantic differential items, including "bad-good," "unpleasant-pleasant," "negative-positive," "unsatisfying-satisfying," and "worthless-valuable" (M = 6.07, SD = 1.12, ω = .98).

Relationship Satisfaction

Relationship satisfaction was assessed with three items from the Perceived Relationship Quality Components Inventory (Fletcher et al., 2000), including "How satisfied are you with your relationship?" Items were rated on a 7-point scale from1 = not at all to7 = extremely (M = 6.06, SD = 1.03, ω = .98).

Commitment

Commitment was assessed with three items from the Investment Model Scale (Rusbult et al., 1998), including "I want our relationship to last for a very long time." Items were rated on a 9-point scale from0 = do not agree at all to8 = agree completely (M = 6.77, SD = 1.38, ω = .94).

Data Analytic Approach

Data screening and cleaning were conducted in RStudio before analysis. Composite and aggregated scores were created for each variable and then grand-mean centred. Time was coded so that the baseline data was set to zero (T1 = 0), with subsequent timepoints spaced three units apart, corresponding to three-month intervals (e.g., T1 = 0 months; T2 = 3 months).

All analyses were conducted in SPSS using two-level cross-classified indistinguishable dyadic growth curve models (Kashy et al., 2008). Graphs were created by running multi-level models in R version 4.6.0 (RStudio Version 2026.05.1+225) using the ggplot2 (version 4.0.3) package. Since participants contributed up to seven time points, depending on the variable, repeated observations were nested within individuals, with partners nested within each couple. First, we assessed the trajectories of each key variable and separate models were estimated for each outcome; each model included linear and quadratic effects of time predicting the outcome. We initially tested models with only linear effects; results are reported in the supplement tables (S1, S3, S5, and S7-S9). Next, to test whether sexual beliefs were associated with the trajectories of sexual and relationship satisfaction and commitment over time, we included time, sexual growth and destiny beliefs, and their interactions as predictors of each outcome. In these models, sexual growth and destiny beliefs were grand-mean centred. Baseline beliefs were treated as the primary prospective predictors of each outcome trajectory, with beliefs averaged across the study examined in secondary models. We began by testing the simplest model and incrementally added complexity, ensuring each model ran successfully before proceeding. Specifically, we tested models with: (1) linear time moderated by actor sexual growth and destiny beliefs (entered simultaneously as predictors in all models), (2) linear and quadratic time moderated by actor sexual beliefs, (3) linear time moderated by actor and partner sexual beliefs, and (4) linear and quadratic time moderated by both actor and partner sexual beliefs. As results were largely similar across models, we report findings from the most comprehensive model-linear and quadratic time moderated by both actor and partner sexual beliefs-in the main text. Results for all other models are available in the supplement (Tables S1-11). When interactions were significant, simple slope analyses examined the effect of time on the outcome at high (+1 SD) and low (-1 SD) levels of sexual beliefs. All models with significant interactions were re-estimated while controlling for relationship growth and destiny beliefs.

To examine exploratory partner effects, all models testing associations with outcomes were guided by the Actor-Partner Interdependence Model (APIM; Kenny et al., 2006). The data were structured so that each couple was represented by two rows, allowing each person to serve as both an actor and a partner (Campbell & Kashy, 2002). That is, both actor and partner reports of sexual beliefs were included as predictors, allowing us to test whether partners' beliefs were associated with sexual and relationship satisfaction and commitment beyond individuals' own beliefs, and whether partner beliefs were associated with the trajectory of outcomes over time.

Participant retention was assessed across the longitudinal study period. Attrition analyses examined whether study variables were associated with the total number of surveys completed and study completion status (see Tables S24-S25 in the Supplemental Materials). Overall, there was little evidence of systematic attrition. The few significant differences indicated that greater retention was associated with higher general growth beliefs and sexual growth beliefs across the study, and that participants who dropped out reported lower relationship satisfaction at the 12month assessment than those who remained in the study. No other significant associations emerged.

Discussion

Couples' sexual relationships often change with increasing relationship duration (e.g., Schmiedeberg & Schröder, 2016), yet little was known about how partners' sexual beliefs are associated with these early relationship trajectories. In the current study, we followed couples over 18 months as they moved beyond the early stages of their relationship and examined trajectories of sexual beliefs, sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment. To assess whether these patterns extended beyond beliefs specific to sexuality, we also examined associations with sexual beliefs after accounting for general relationship beliefs. Sexual growth and destiny beliefs were largely stable over time, with sexual growth beliefs showing significant but very small declines, suggesting that these beliefs may function as relatively stable cognitive frameworks during early relationship development. At the same time, relationship satisfaction and commitment declined modestly, with small nonlinear trends, whereas sexual satisfaction was stable.

In line with past research (e.g., Maxwell et al., 2017), sexual growth beliefs were consistently associated with more positive sexual and relational outcomes. Individuals who held stronger sexual growth beliefs-and those whose partners held stronger sexual growth beliefsreported higher sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment. Partner sexual growth beliefs also moderated changes in relationship satisfaction and commitment over time: individuals with partners lower in sexual growth beliefs showed steeper declines in these outcomes, whereas those with partners higher in sexual growth beliefs showed relatively stable levels.

In contrast, sexual destiny beliefs were associated with lower relationship satisfaction and commitment but were not associated with changes in outcomes over time.

Trajectory of Sexual Beliefs in Early Romantic Relationships

Sexual destiny beliefs remained stable across the study, whereas sexual growth beliefs showed a small decline over time. Overall, however, both beliefs demonstrated substantial stability across 18 months, consistent with theoretical perspectives that conceptualize relationship and sexual beliefs as relatively enduring cognitive frameworks that guide how individuals interpret relational experiences (Franiuk et al., 2004; Gander et al., 2025; Knee, 1998; Maxwell et al., 2017). This stability is notable given that participants were followed during a period of early relationship development in which sexual and relational experiences are likely in flux.

We also found stability in general relationship growth and destiny beliefs, a finding that diverges from prior research documenting modest increases in relationship growth beliefs and decreases in destiny beliefs over five months among individuals in longer-term relationships (Gander et al., 2025). Rather than changing routinely as relationships develop, beliefs may therefore be more likely to shift under particular relational conditions or experiences. Consistent with this possibility, experimental and intervention research suggests that sexual beliefs can be temporarily shifted (Maxwell et al., 2017; Sutherland & Rehman, 2018), although whether such changes persist is less clear. Future research should examine when and under what conditions sexual and relationship beliefs change, including whether more substantial sexual challenges or major relationship transitions are sufficient to reshape these belief systems.

Trajectories of Sexual Satisfaction and Relationship Quality in Early Relationships

Relationship satisfaction and commitment declined modestly over time, with small quadratic effects indicating that declines were more pronounced earlier in the study and leveling off over time. The decline in relationship satisfaction is consistent with prior longitudinal work showing particularly pronounced declines during the early years of relationships (Bühler et al., 2021; Bühler & Orth, 2024). The decline in commitment differs somewhat from prior crosssectional evidence suggesting higher commitment across the first several years of dating (Freeman et al., 2023) and may reflect some recalibration of relationship evaluations as couples move beyond the highly idealized early stages of relationships and encounter more routine relational experiences (Niehuis et al., 2016).

Sexual satisfaction, in contrast, remained relatively high and stable across the 18-month study, differing from prior work showing declines with increasing relationship duration (Schiedeberg & Shröder, 2016). Because couples in the current study had been together for approximately nine months on average at baseline, we might have expected sexual satisfaction to decline across the follow-up period. Instead, it remained relatively stable. Other longitudinal work indicates that sexual satisfaction increases when people enter romantic relationships (Qin et al., 2026), and that sexual activity tends to be more frequent earlier in relationships, when sexual satisfaction is also higher (Schröder & Schmiedeberg, 2015; Yucel & Gassanov, 2010). These factors may help explain the relatively high levels of sexual satisfaction observed early in the study, although they do not fully account for its stability across the follow-up period.

Taken together, these findings suggest that sexual and broader relationship outcomes may follow somewhat distinct trajectories during early relationship development. Relationship satisfaction and commitment declined even while sexual satisfaction remained relatively stable, underscoring the value of examining these dimensions separately rather than assuming that changes in sexual and relationship quality necessarily occur in parallel.

Associations and Trajectories Between Sexual Beliefs and Sexual and Relationship Quality

The current findings extend prior research on sexual beliefs by examining how sexual growth and destiny beliefs are associated with sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment over time in early relationships. Consistent with prior work, higher actor sexual destiny beliefs were associated with lower relationship satisfaction and commitment (Maxwell et al., 2017; Raposo et al., 2021; Rossi et al., 2022). However, sexual destiny beliefs were not associated with sexual satisfaction and did not predict changes in sexual or relationship outcomes over time. One possible explanation for these findings is that destiny beliefs become particularly consequential when couples encounter salient sexual challenges. The absence of an average decline in sexual satisfaction in the present sample may indicate that the couples in our study were not undergoing sexual challenges. Prior work with couples navigating sexual difficulties or major transitions similarly suggests that sexual destiny beliefs may be especially relevant in the face of sexual issues or incompatibility (Maxwell et al., 2017; Raposo et al., 2021; Rossi et al., 2022). Future research should examine whether destiny beliefs become more consequential over longer periods of relationship development or during periods of sustained sexual challenge.

Sexual growth beliefs showed a more consistent pattern. Stronger sexual growth beliefs-both individuals' own and their partners'-were associated with higher sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment, consistent with prior work (Cultice et al., 2022; Maxwell et al., 2017; Raposo et al., 2021). Sexual growth beliefs did not, however, predict trajectories of sexual satisfaction, perhaps because sexual satisfaction remained high and stable.

Growth beliefs may instead operate through more proximal processes such as sexual communication, responsiveness to sexual challenges, and motivation to meet a partner's needs (Busby et al., 2024; Uppot et al., 2024; Wu & Zheng, 2022), which could help explain why their dyadic implications extend beyond changes in global sexual satisfaction.

The clearest evidence that sexual beliefs were associated with change over time emerged for partners' sexual growth beliefs. Individuals whose partners reported lower sexual growth beliefs showed steeper declines in relationship satisfaction and commitment, whereas those whose partners reported higher sexual growth beliefs showed more stable trajectories. These findings extend prior work linking sexual growth beliefs to relationship quality (Maxwell et al., 2017) by indicating that a partner's beliefs may be associated with how relationship quality unfolds as couples move through early relationship development. Partners higher in sexual growth beliefs may be more likely to view sexual differences as manageable and to respond to them with communication, responsiveness, or continued investment in addressing the problem (Cultice et al., 2022; Uppot et al., 2024; Wu & Zheng, 2022). Future research should directly test these interpersonal mechanisms to clarify how one partner's sexual beliefs are associated with the other partner's relationship trajectory.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite the strengths of the current longitudinal dyadic design for testing how sexual beliefs are associated with early relationship trajectories, the study was limited in its ability to capture relationship development from the onset of the relationship. Couples had been together for between three months and just over one year at baseline, and many had likely already established important relational and sexual patterns by the start of the study.

In addition, some couples were already cohabiting, engaged, or married, suggesting that the sample may not fully capture the earliest changes that occur as romantic relationships first form. To better understand early relationship development, future research could follow individuals before or at the very beginning of relationship formation, although this type of design is difficult to implement (Eastwick & Joel, 2025).

A second limitation concerns the generalizability of the findings. The sample included couples with diverse sexual orientations, but most participants were in mixed-gender relationships, and the sample was not large enough to test whether trajectories or associations differed across groups. Prior research suggests that many sexual and relational processes are similar across mixed-sex and same-sex couples, although some differences have been observed in sexual desire, communication, and satisfaction (Holmberg & Blair, 2009).

Future research with larger and more diverse samples is needed to test whether the current findings generalize across different relationship structures and orientations. In addition, all participants were living in Canada or the United States, and beliefs about sexuality, relationship development, and relationship maintenance may vary across cultural contexts. Future work should examine whether sexual growth and destiny beliefs show similar trajectories and associations with relationship quality in non-Western contexts and in cultures with different norms surrounding sexuality and romantic relationships. Attrition analyses provided relatively little evidence of systematic dropout, although some selective retention was evident. Participants retained longer tended to report higher growth beliefs, and those who dropped out reported lower relationship satisfaction at 12 months. Later waves may therefore somewhat overrepresent more growth-oriented and satisfied participants, potentially contributing to the apparent levelling off in relationship satisfaction at later assessments. However, the attrition analyses did not directly test whether selective retention influenced the associations between sexual beliefs and changes in relationship quality, including the partner sexual growth belief effects. Thus, the direction and magnitude of any bias in these longitudinal associations remain unclear. Future longitudinal research should assess relationship dissolution and reasons for attrition to better distinguish true stabilization in relationship quality from selective retention of more satisfied couples. The current findings also point to potential directions for interventions. Prior work suggests that sexual beliefs can be temporarily shifted through brief interventions (Maxwell et al., 2017), raising the possibility that helping couples adopt stronger sexual growth beliefs could support sexual and relational well-being. However, given the correlational nature of the present findings, future research is needed to test whether fostering sexual growth beliefs leads to improvements in relationship quality over time. Such work would benefit from dyadic designs that target both partners' beliefs and examine whether changes in sexual growth beliefs promote more adaptive interpersonal processes, such as sexual communication, mindfulness, or motivation to meet a partner's needs (Cultice et al., 2022; Wu & Zheng, 2022; Uppot et al., 2024). Such studies could clarify whether sexual growth beliefs play a causal role in maintaining relationship satisfaction and commitment during early relationship development.

Conclusion

The present study provides one of the first longitudinal dyadic tests of sexual growth and destiny beliefs across early relationship development. Across 18 months, sexual beliefs were relatively stable (showed significant, but very small declines), suggesting that beliefs about how sexual satisfaction is maintained may shape how partners understand and navigate their sexual relationships. These beliefs were also meaningfully linked to both partners' experiences. Sexual growth beliefs-both individuals' own and their partners'-were associated with higher sexual satisfaction, relationship satisfaction, and commitment, whereas sexual destiny beliefs were associated with lower relationship satisfaction and commitment. Most notably, partners' sexual growth beliefs were associated with trajectories of relationship quality: individuals with partners higher in sexual growth beliefs showed more stable relationship satisfaction and commitment over time. Thus, sexual beliefs may matter not only for how individuals experience their relationships at a given point in time, but also for how relationship quality unfolds between partners. As couples move beyond the earliest stages of their relationships, having a partner who views a satisfying sexual relationship as something that can be cultivated may be particularly relevant for sustaining relationship quality over time.
