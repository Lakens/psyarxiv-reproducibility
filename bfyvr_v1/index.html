Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Departing from traditional information-maximization accounts, recent work proposes that saccade selection arbitrates economically between the expected benefits and costs of shifting attention. We tested this economic account by manipulating the cognitive resources available during selection, arguing that reduced resources should increase the weight of costs, thereby altering selection. Across two experiments, participants selected between saccade targets while performing a non-spatial memory task that manipulated cognitive load. Targets appeared in directions with previously and independently established intrinsic (effort) costs, quantified through pupillometry. In Experiment 1, monetary rewards were staircased against participants' choices, replicating that money can compensate for intrinsic costs. This yielded an informative reward range from which Experiment 2 sampled randomly. Experiment 2 revealed that higher memory load biased selection toward cost-efficient saccades, while reward sensitivity remained unaffected. Load also selectively shortened the long latency tail, consistent with reduced deliberation under increased load and therefore an increased weight on deliberation cost. Saccade selection thus depends on available cognitive resources, supporting an economic account of visual selection over information maximization accounts.

Main

Humans redirect their eyes several times per second, rapidly moving the eyes (socalled saccades) to bring task-relevant information onto the fovea, where it can be processed in detail. Saccade selection is traditionally understood as reflecting the physical salience of visual stimuli, the history of previous selections, and the observer's current goals [1]. Therefore, saccade selection can be thought of as a process that maximizes expected information gain [2] [3] [4] [5]. Such models rest on the sometimes explicit, but more often implicit assumption that making saccades is effectively free [6, 7]. However, the deployment of gaze is arguably also constrained by the costs of accessing visual information [8]. Saccades require motor control, complex remapping of visual information, can delay the acquisition of other information, and may impose opportunity costs when alternative locations could have been sampled [7]. Recent work therefore suggests that saccade selection may be better understood as an economic extension of priority-map accounts in which the expected benefits of fixating a location are weighed against the costs of shifting and maintaining gaze. This perspective is consistent with a broader view of natural visually guided behavior (including but not limited to saccades) as a sequence of decisions, in which the costs and benefits of alternative actions jointly determine behavior [9].

Although the idea of intrinsic saccade costs has long been theoretically attractive [2, 8, 10, 11], empirical tests were limited by the absence of independent physiological measures. A first step in breaking this gridlock lies in reverse-engineering costs from the systematic behavioral residuals left unexplained by other factors [12] [13] [14], albeit at the risk of circularity. More recent work demonstrates that saccade costs can be physiologically measured using effort-linked pupil dilation: More complex saccades (e.g., diagonal) are preceded by increased pupil dilation during planning [15, 16]. These costs in turn predict selection preferences in separate tasks [16] [17] [18]. Most recently, we showed that rewards can be titrated to participants' saccade preferences until those preferences disappear [18]. The hereby inferred amount of reward needed to balance out a direction matched its physiological cost, as independently inferred from pupil dilation.

Together, this evidence motivates the hypothesis that saccade selection depends on available cognitive resources. Saccade costs can be understood as resource expenditure, consequently, resource scarcity should increase the weight of costs in selection. Moreover, because deliberation is itself costly, the depth to which competing saccades are evaluated should also track the available resources. Indeed, many aspects of gaze behavior are affected by load. This includes lower saccade rates [16, 19] or more gaze tunneling under higher load [20, 21], smooth pursuits deviating more strongly from a moving target [22], alterations in microsaccade rates [23], and peak saccade velocity [24] The economic model thus predicts a recalibration of selection (costs gain weight), whereas non-specific interference predicts degradation, i.e., less predictive power of any factor [see also 25, 26].

Across two experiments, participants selected between saccade targets as in [16] [17] [18], with concurrent memory load being manipulated. In Experiment 1, we used reward titration to replicate cost-reward compensation following Strauch et al. [18] and establish an informative reward range. Experiment 2 then used the obtained fixed reward distribution to test how memory load changes the effects of cost and reward on selection. Latencies were examined across both experiments to assess whether load selectively reduces prolonged decisions.

Load could reduce resources for deliberation during selection nonspecifically and thus may make selection noisier, weakening the link between costs and rewards with selection. We found no evidence for such general interference under load. C Economic prediction: Load could reduce investment in deliberative cost-benefit weighing, selectively reducing prolonged choices in the upper tail of the latency distribution. Methods

Experiment 1 Participants, inclusion, and ethics

Twenty-eight participants (M age = 24.00, SD age = 3.30; 16 women, 12 men) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision took part in Experiment 1. Sample size was based on sample sizes from Strauch et al. [18] and Koevoet et al. [16]. Participants received a monetary compensation of €9, with the possibility of earning up to €4 in addition, depending on how many points were accumulated during the task. The experimental procedure was approved by Utrecht University's Faculty of Social Sciences ethical review board (24-0376, 24-0541).

Apparatus

Gaze positions were recorded at 1000 Hz with an EyeLink 1000 Plus desktop mount (SR Research, Ontario, Canada) in a brightness-and sound-attenuated laboratory.

Head movements were limited by using a chin-and forehead-rest. Stimuli were presented using PsychoPy [v2024.2.3 27] on an ASUS ROG PQ278Q monitor (2560 x 1440, 100 Hz) positioned 67.5 cm away from eye position. The eye-tracker was calibrated (9 points) at the beginning of each session and during breaks whenever participants wished to move their head away from the desktop mount. The central fixation dot was a black ring (1° diameter) presented on a screen-centered circle (12° radius, gray ≈42.4 cd/m 2 ). The remaining screen was black (≈0.18 cd/m 2 ) to ensure equal brightness and contrast adjacent to all target locations.

Procedure, task, and stimuli

The present study combined a saccade preference task [as in [16] [17] [18] with a dualtask paradigm, manipulating cognitive load by means of a color memory task. Every block began with the presentation of two colors and was followed by the saccade preference task (ten trials). Blocks were concluded with a color report (Figure 2A).

Saccade preference task

Saccade targets were dotted rings (1° diameter) containing up to six equally spaced and sized shapes (or none in case of zero points).

Rewards were signaled by two visual properties of the target: shape type and the number of shapes (one to six). Shape type determined the reward category, with circles worth less than triangles, which were worth less than squares (not counterbalanced). Within each category, the number of shapes scaled the reward value, with more shapes indicating either a higher or a lower reward (counterbalanced across participants).

Participants started a trial on the saccade preference task by looking at the central fixation point (within 1.5°) for a random duration between 100-500 ms. Thereafter, two saccade targets were presented at two of the 36 predetermined locations arranged in a circular array (eccentricity of 10° from central fixation). Participants then selected a target by making an eye movement toward it. Choices were registered once gaze fell within 1.5° of the target's center for at least 100 ms. After successful selection, decisions were fed-back with the corresponding reward value via centered black text. Participants completed a total of 500 trials each.

All directions initially rewarded participants with 9 points. Subsequently, a staircase procedure dynamically titrated rewards for each direction based on participants' choices (Figure 2B). Whenever participants made a saccade into a specific direction, the following instance in which that same direction was presented, its reward amount was lowered by one point. Concurrently, the unselected target's direction gained one point in its next appearance.

Color recall task

At the start of each block, a centered cue (2000 ms) indicated whether participants had to memorize (+) or not memorize (×) the following two colors. Note that, due to a coding error, the cue presentation sequence was randomized only once, resulting in the same sequence being used for all participants.

This sequence was therefore counterbalanced by inverting the conditions for half of the participants. Memory items consisted of two colored disks (4° radius) which were presented centrally during the encoding phase for 1500 ms. Each color was independently followed by a visual mask (4° radius, 500 ms) to eliminate the occurrence of afterimages [28]. After a short delay (1000 ms), the second color was presented. Saturation and lightness were kept constant, with hue being randomized. The second color was always separated from the first by at least 45°. This constraint was implemented to facilitate separate encoding.

After ten successful trials in the saccade preference task, the color recall phase followed and participants were asked to select the memorized colors on a color ring with a mouse click (self-paced). Participants received feedback by a centered black text based on the angular distance between target and response color ("perfect!", "good", "could be better", "very poor"). In blocks where colors did not need to be memorized, participants were asked to click the inside of the color ring twice.

Orientation of the color ring was randomized for each individual response. Participants completed a total of 50 blocks; half of which involved memorization of the presented colors.

Data processing

All data were processed using custom Python (v3.10) and R (v4.4.3) scripts. Eyetracking data were downsampled to 250 Hz, and saccades were detected using velocity thresholds. Choices were determined based on the Euclidean distance of gaze position relative to the two presented targets. Trials with ambiguous selections (i.e., where gaze fell outside 1.5° of the target) or very fast or very slow saccade latencies (<85 ms or >1250 ms) were excluded. Saccade cost per direction was taken from an independent study wherein participants' pupil size was tracked as a cost index when planning saccades to the same locations [16]. The first 80 trials (8 blocks) were excluded from analysis to account for additional consequences of the rewardupdating algorithm (i.e., the staircase procedure causes rewards to be roughly equal across directions at the start of the experiment).

Blocks of trials were excluded from analysis when participants either reported colors during the low-load block or failed to report the correct colors during the high-load block (937 trials, 6.69%; exceeding 30°, set based on visual inspection).

Data analysis

All statistical tests were two-sided with α = .05. Features of shapes signaling reward (numerosity and shape) were considered during analysis through their interactions with reward sensitivity. Models that predicted saccade latency were analyzed through a generalized mixed-effects model with a gamma distribution using a log link, following Lo & Andrews [29].

Experiment 2

Experiment 2 was a direct replication of Experiment 1 except for the following changes, and was tested on 30 participants (M age = 22.57, SD age = 3.22; 18 women, 12 men).

In contrast to Experiment 1, rewards were not staircased against participants' choices. Instead, reward values were randomly and independently drawn for each target combination from the rewards observed in Experiment 1. This allowed us to independently investigate the role of costs and rewards on saccade selection. As a result, all trials could be taken into account for the analysis, except for trials during which color reports were incorrectly provided (exclusion as before, 903 trials, 6.02%).

Additionally, the mapping of shape type on the reward values was counterbalanced among participants to control for potential salience effects of shapes (in addition to numerosity of shapes). Different from Experiment 1, memory/no memory blocks were in full random sequence.

Saccade latency as a marker of decision-making

We next turned to saccade latencies as a measure of decision depth [30, 31]. Deliberating longer is more in-depth but also more costly [32]. If saccade selection depends on resource availability, this investment should become less attractive under higher load, just as we found for the effort of saccades themselves. We addressed this in two steps. First, we asked whether saccade latencies indeed reflect deliberation: if the utility gap between targets (i.e., the net difference between their costs and rewards) is what makes deliberation worthwhile, latencies should increase with the utility gap [6].

Second, we asked how load affects latencies, testing whether load selectively cuts the prolonged decisions that are consistent with deeper deliberation in particular.

Differences in utility gain predict saccade latency

First, we looked into whether the degree of elaboration can reflect the available utility gain during saccade choices. Effectively, higher rewards in Experiment 1 were usually coupled also to higher costs and vice versa, while this was not the case in Experiment 2. We reasoned that this should make it more attractive to invest in deliberate decision-making in Experiment 2 than in Experiment 1, given that deliberation allows observers to maximize utility more effectively. Indeed, we found If load makes deeper deliberation less worthwhile (see also Figure 1C), its effect should be largest where deliberation is most extensive: in the long tail of the latency distribution. Longer latencies arguably reflect more elaborate decisions. Hence, load should shorten prolonged decisions in particular, while leaving fast decisions largely unaffected.

Naturally, it is impossible to compare the same decision under low and under high load, as the counterfactual is not available. However, the skew of the latency distributions can be compared across load levels. To do so, we divided trials into 25 equal trial number bins based on each participant's latency distribution, split between memory loads, i.e., following a quantile-quantile logic. Per participant, we then subtracted the low-load bin from the high-load bin at identical bin number, resulting in an average latency difference per bin and participant. A selective shortening through load on the longer tail would thus show in a more negative latency difference with increasing bin number. Indeed, we found such an effect in both Experiment 1 (Figure 4C, β = -1.20, t = -10.45, p < 0.001) and Experiment 2 (Figure 4D, β = -1.05, t = -7.54, p < 0.001). This supports the exploratory notion that resource-scarcity causes in-depth decisions to be cut short in particular, arguably allowing one to reduce expenses from one's own resource budget (Wilkinson notation: latency difference ∼ latency bin + (1 | participant)).

Discussion

Recent work has proposed that saccade selection follows an economic principle, weighing the costs of shifting gaze against the rewards available at alternative locations. We reasoned that if saccade selection draws on a limited cognitive budget, concurrent memory load should alter how costs and rewards are weighed. We found evidence for two out of three predictions. First, non-spatial load made saccade choices more cost driven, consistent with these costs being computed relative to the available resources. Second, load selectively curtailed the prolonged decisions that reflect deeper deliberation, shortening the upper tail of the latency distribution. In contrast, we found no evidence for non-specific interference of load with optimal selection. Together, this suggests that load does not degrade selection in-discriminately, but recalibrates how strongly saccade costs are weighed and how much deliberation each choice is worth.

In Experiment 1, we replicated that physiologically inferred costs [16] and necessary rewards to compensate for said costs are traded-off and are therefore correlated [18].

The economic model of visual selection assumes that intrinsic attentional costs are defined relative to the available resources [6]. Reduced resources should therefore raise the weight of intrinsic costs during selection. This central assumption remained untested. Existing work shows that gaze changes under load, among which reduced saccade rates [16, 19] and gaze tunneling [20, 21]. Such effects are compatible with several accounts, because they do not separate costs from other determinants of selection. We here demonstrate that load selectively reshaped selection: the weight of previously established, physiologically-inferred intrinsic saccade costs increased under load, while reward sensitivity was unaffected.

This pattern is predicted by the economic model and indicates that load does not degrade selection, but changes the relative weighting of its determinants. In other words, selection remains optimal, but input variables change. Likely, many global load effects on gaze can thus be understood parsimoniously as reweighting of costs within visual selection.

The economic model further holds that decision-making is itself costly. Elaborating longer means higher costs [32], and should thus only be recruited when its expected utility gains exceed that cost [6]. This idea has a long history in economics, where decision costs are thought to limit how much information is processed [33, 34], and work which describes how simple heuristics can match or outperform exhaustive deliberation [35, 36]. The empirical basis for this account to apply to visual selection as suggested in Strauch et al. [6] has been limited thus far, however. Our latency data speak to this in two ways: Wider utility gaps should come with more elaborate (and thus longer) decisions. Indeed, the larger utility gaps in Experiment 2 than in Experiment 1, where they were minimized by design, were associated with longer latencies. Second, load selectively shortened the prolonged decisions of Ex-periment 2 while leaving short decisions unaffected. This is to be expected when the value of deliberation drops under reduced resources due to an increased weight of deliberation costs. Both findings thus support the assumption that decision depth during saccade selection is itself decided economically.

In principle, shorter latencies under load could also reflect memory-induced urgency to reach recall sooner. Such hurrying, however, should make choices noisier, weakening the mapping of both costs and rewards onto selection. Instead, reward mapping was unaffected and cost mapping became steeper rather than flatter under load. Both findings are thus incompatible with an urgency account, but in line with economic adjustment of decision depth. Additionally, were memory-induced urgency truly present, we assume its effect would have been present in both experiments. Still, latencies are not uniquely determined by decision depth. The findings are therefore consistent with reduced deliberation under higher load, but do not conclusively establish that interpretation.

Task load induced in the present experiments was arguably relatively low, as expressed in the overall good performance in the color reports. Future work could thus extend the present data by inducing stronger load, which should increase effect sizes on visual selection in the same directions as observed here. Load should affect visual selection as a whole [6] and not just saccades (which are easiest to study). Indeed, load likewise affects eye movements ranging from smooth pursuit deviations [22] to microsaccade rates and beyond [23, 37]. Costs further constrain fixation selection in natural tasks [38], suggesting that the load-induced cost reweighting observed here generalizes to everyday viewing. Consequently, establishing cost differences among such attention shift types should allow for specific predictions about the alterations that occur under increased load.

Lastly, effects such as induced here through additional load, should equally occur when resources become more limited for different reasons. For instance, beginning cognitive decline may keep visual selection optimal, but change the weight of costs on the side of input variables. Similar effects may be producible due to exhaustion or tiredness, all effects that remain to be tested as of today. Understanding eye movement behavior can thus, in turn, provide insights into the amount of available resources and reward of the observer as an instantiation of high throughput decision-making. Ideally, such understanding would allow for models that incorporate the observers' individual resource budget and cost representations. Intrinsic cost estimates already improve computational gaze prediction [14], and the present findings show that the resource budget and weighing could help individualize such models beyond one-size-fits-all approaches [see also 39].

Conclusion

Together, we tested a central difference between information maximization and economic accounts of selection that factor in costs: only an economic account predicts changes with available resources. Under load, saccade costs gained weight while reward sensitivity was unaffected. Furthermore, prolonged decisions were particularly cut short under load, suggesting that arbitration depth is itself decided economically. Selection thus does not merely maximize information gain, but does so relative to costs in a variable budget. This opens the door to better predictions of selection across situations and individuals.
