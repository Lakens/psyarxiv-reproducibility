Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

1. Self-reported affect (PANAS). The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule – Short Form (Thompson, 2007) was administered immediately before and after the TSST. Ten items rated on a 5-point Likert scale yield two 5-item subscales: Positive Affect and Negative Affect (each scored 5–25).

2. Cardiac vagal tone (lnRMSSD). Root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) of inter-beat intervals, derived from continuous ECG (Polar H10) and processed in Kubios HRV Scientific 4.2.0 with the Lipponen and Tarvainen (2019) artefact-correction algorithm and smoothness-priors detrending (λ = 500). RMSSD will be extracted for three TSST phases — baseline (5 min rest), anticipation (two 2-min pre-speech periods averaged), and recovery (5 min rest), and natural-log-transformed. Phases with greater than 5% corrected beats or less than 3.5 min of valid recording will be excluded.

Main predictors
Group. Binary between-subjects classification: long-term mindfulness meditator (1) vs. meditation-naïve control (0).

Mediator and exploratory predictors
DERS Total (Gratz & Roemer, 2004). 36-item self-report measure of trait emotion regulation difficulties; total score (range 36–180) is the primary mediator.

Meditation practice (meditator subsample). MonthsPractice (total months of consistent practice; square-root-transformed) and MinsLastMonth (minutes practised in the month before the session; natural-log-transformed).
indices PANAS subscale scores. Each PANAS subscale (Positive Affect, Negative Affect) is the unweighted sum of its five constituent items at each timepoint.

lnRMSSD per phase. For each TSST phase (baseline, anticipation, recovery), lnRMSSD = ln(RMSSD), where RMSSD is the root mean square of successive differences of inter-beat intervals computed by Kubios HRV Scientific 4.2.0 over the relevant phase window. The anticipation value is the arithmetic mean of lnRMSSD computed from the two 2-minute pre-speech preparation windows (lnRMSSD_S1a and lnRMSSD_S1b averaged).

lnRMSSD reactivity and recovery indices. Two within-subject difference scores on the log scale:
lnRMSSD_reactivity = lnRMSSD_anticipation − lnRMSSD_baseline
lnRMSSD_recovery = lnRMSSD_recovery_phase − lnRMSSD_anticipation
Negative reactivity values indicate vagal withdrawal under anticipation; positive recovery values indicate vagal rebound after the stressor.

DERS Total. Unweighted sum of all 36 DERS items (range 36–180), with reverse-keyed items recoded prior to summation per Gratz and Roemer (2004).
data_exclusion_criteria Inclusion criteria (participant level): All participants who completed the laboratory protocol and provided at least the primary affective outcome data (pre-stress and post-stress PANAS) are included in the analytic sample (N = 149). No further exclusions are applied at the participant level for the affective analyses.

Exclusion criteria (observation level, HRV only). Heart-rate variability data are subject to two phase-level quality criteria, applied independently to each TSST phase per participant:

Artefact correction threshold. A phase is excluded if the Kubios artefact-correction algorithm (Lipponen & Tarvainen, 2019) flagged more than 5% of beats for correction.
Recording duration threshold. A phase is excluded if less than 3.5 minutes of valid recording remain after artefact rejection.
missing_data_handling Models are conducted on all available data using case-wise deletion within each analysis.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

• We tested affective and autonomic stressor responses in long-term meditators.

• Meditators reported lower negative and higher positive affect after the stressor.

• Meditators and controls showed comparable autonomic responses to the stressor.

• Emotion regulation accounted for baseline affect differences, not affective change.

• Meditators reported fewer and less severe stressors over the preceding year.

1. Introduction

Mindfulness meditation and similar contemplative practices have been associated with improved mental and physical health (Black & Slavich, 2016; Creswell et al., 2019; Galante et al., 2023; Goldberg et al., 2018), but why these benefits occur remains unclear. One influential theoretical account is that mindfulness changes how people respond to stress. Building on the original stress-buffering hypothesis (Cohen & Wills, 1985), Creswell and colleagues (2014; 2019) proposed that mindfulness alters stress appraisals so that stressors are perceived as less threatening, with corresponding reduction in both affective and physiological stress responses.

These attenuated responses are, in turn, thought to explain the downstream effects of mindfulness on health. Because repeated and prolonged activation of the stress response systems contributes to the onset and exacerbation of stress-related disease (Evans et al., 2025; Juster et al., 2010; McEwen, 2008; McEwen & Stellar, 1993; Parker et al., 2022; Shields & Slavich, 2017), reduced stress reactivity is indeed a plausible mechanism through which mindfulness could be beneficial. Although the mindfulness stress-buffering account was developed primarily in the context of trait mindfulness and standardised, typically eight-week mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs), these effects should also be observable in long-term meditators. In particular, years of sustained practice may cultivate greater acceptance-based emotion regulation capacities, which are associated with reduced affective reactivity (Raugh et al., 2025).

The biological basis of the mindfulness stress-buffering account includes complementary topdown and bottom-up pathways (Creswell & Lindsay, 2014). In the brain, mindfulness is thought to strengthen recruitment of prefrontal regulatory regions and reduce reactivity in stress-related regions such as the amygdala (Way et al., 2010). These central changes are proposed to be expressed peripherally in the stress-response systems: the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, whose activity is reflected in cortisol, and the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) axis, reflected in circulating catecholamines and sympathetically mediated cardiovascular responses such as blood pressure (Chrousos, 2009; Ulrich-Lai & Herman, 2009). The heart is also under parasympathetic (vagal) control, which is rapidly withdrawn during acute stress and restored during recovery (Berntson et al., 1997; Laborde et al., 2017). Vagally mediated heart rate variability (HRV) provides a non-invasive measure of this parasympathetic regulation (Laborde et al., 2017), and can be recorded continuously across different phases of the stress response. It can therefore be used to test the extent to which long-term mindfulness meditation is associated with altered autonomic stress responding, whereas self-reported affect provides a measure of affective response to stress that the stress-buffering account also predicts.

Despite these possibilities, empirical support for autonomic and affective stress-buffering comes almost entirely from MBIs (typically eight weeks of training in previously inexperienced participants) rather than long-term practice. For example, a meta-analysis of 19 randomised controlled trials found that MBIs do not increase HRV during rest (Brown et al., 2021). When looking specifically at HRV in response to stress, the evidence is less conclusive. For example, a recent scoping review found more adaptive HRV reactivity following MBIs, but this effect was apparent only in more rigorously designed studies of non-clinical populations (Christodoulou et al., 2020). Additionally, a systematic review found buffering of the response to acute stress more reliably for self-reported outcomes (7 of 10 studies) than for physiological outcomes (6 of 13; Morton et al., 2020). Although MBIs thus appear to attenuate the stress response, particularly for self-reported affect, their typical dose and duration, and the fact that follow-ups beyond six months are extremely rare (Galante et al., 2023), make it difficult to investigate how the stress response changes with sustained practice. Long-term meditators, who accumulate years of regular practice, therefore offer a stronger test of whether sustained practice is associated with altered affective and autonomic responses to acute stress.

Direct tests in long-term meditators are necessarily cross-sectional and remain scarce, in part due to the difficulty of recruiting this rare population into in-person laboratory stress paradigms, such as the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST; Kirschbaum et al., 1993). Indeed, to our knowledge, only 6 two such studies exist. First, Gamaiunova et al. (2019) found that long-term meditators showed faster cortisol recovery from TSST but did not differ from controls in heart rate, HRV, or selfreported levels of stress. Second, Rosenkranz et al. (2016) found lower cortisol, reduced capsaicin-induced inflammation, and lower self-reported stress in long-term meditators, yet no differences in salivary alpha-amylase, a marker of sympathetic activity. The broader autonomic literature also remains inconclusive. A recent review synthesised the autonomic correlates of long-term meditation across sixteen studies that did not use laboratory stress paradigms (Sezer & Sacchet, 2025). It found resting and meditative-state literature to be heterogeneous, reporting sympathetic activation, parasympathetic activation, both concurrently, or temporally varying patterns depending on practice type and amount of meditation expertise. It thus remains inconclusive whether the differences implied by the stress-buffering account extend to autonomic and affective responses in long-term mindfulness meditators.

That experienced meditators "stress less" is therefore among the most widely assumed claims about contemplative practice, but among the least directly tested. Less often considered is whether attenuated physiological reactivity would indicate better regulation at all. Vagal withdrawal during acute challenge is the normative mobilising response, and a growing literature indicates that blunted physiological responses to acute stress are associated with adverse rather than favourable outcomes, including depression, obesity, addiction, and impaired motivated behaviour (Carroll et al., 2017; Lovallo, 2011). From this perspective, an adaptive response to acute stress is not damped responding, but appropriate mobilisation followed by efficient recovery. Whether long-term practice should reduce stress reactivity, preserve it, or alter only parts of it needs to be investigated further.

1.1. Present study

To address this critical gap, we compared long-term meditators with meditation-naïve, matched controls on affective and autonomic responses to an acute psychosocial stressor (i.e., the TSST).

Our primary aim was to test whether meditators differ from controls in their affective and autonomic responses to the acute psychosocial stressor. Following the mindfulness stressbuffering account (Creswell & Lindsay, 2014; Creswell et al., 2019), we hypothesised that, relative to controls, meditators would report lower negative affect and higher positive affect following the stressor, after accounting for pre-stress affect. In the subsample with available autonomic data, we also examined whether long-term meditators and controls differed in heart rate variability across the baseline, anticipation, and recovery phases of the TSST. Here we did not specify a directional hypothesis as the stress-buffering account suggests attenuated physiological responding, whereas broader stress research suggests that autonomic withdrawal is the normative physiological response to acute stress and blunted reactivity has been related to adverse, rather than beneficial, outcomes (Carroll et al., 2017; Lovallo, 2011).

In addition to testing whether affective and autonomic differences exist, we explored processes that might explain such differences. First, because differences in stressor exposure could provide an alternative explanation for any group difference in stress responses, we compared meditators and controls on lifetime stressor exposure. Second, the stress-buffering account attributes the benefits of sustained practice partly to greater acceptance-based emotion regulation (Lindsay & Creswell, 2017; Raugh et al., 2025), and difficulties in emotion regulation are themselves linked to both affective and autonomic functioning (Williams et al., 2015). We thus tested whether group differences in affect were statistically mediated by trait emotion-regulation difficulties, hypothesising that lower emotion-regulation difficulties in meditators would account for group differences in affective responding.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Study design

This was a cross-sectional study combining a naturalistic lifetime stressor exposure assessment with a laboratory-based experimental stressor paradigm in which we compared long-term mindfulness meditators with meditation-naive controls on affective and autonomic responses to the acute psychosocial stressor. Data were collected as a part of a larger research project that also included functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), but the present report concerns the acute-stress protocol only. All participants were tested in a single laboratory session at the University of Amsterdam between June 2022 and February 2026. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Faculty of Social and Behavioural Sciences, University of Amsterdam (project number 2022-CP-14705), and all participants provided written informed consent.

2.2. Participants

Both groups were recruited through the participant recruitment platform of the University of Amsterdam, flyers, social media, and snowball sampling. Long-term meditators were additionally recruited through direct contact with meditation centres across the Netherlands.

Participants received €30 as compensation. The two groups were matched on age and gender at the group level, by recruiting controls to approximate the age and gender distribution of the meditator sample. The final sample consisted of 148 adults aged 19-68 years, with 80 long-term mindfulness meditators and 68 control participants. Autonomic (HRV) data were available for a subsample of 70 participants (37 meditators, 33 controls). Further details on the demographic characteristics of the sample can be found in Table 1.

The inclusion criteria for the meditator group were at least three years of consistent mindfulness meditation practice with a minimum weekly practice of three 15-minute sessions. This corresponded to a minimum of 7020 minutes of lifetime practice. On average, meditators in our sample far exceeded this threshold, with a mean of 8.4 years of practice (M = 100.8 months, Mdn = 72 months, range = 3-30 years). 37.5% of meditators practiced primarily traditional forms (e.g., Zen, Vipassana), 25.0% primarily secular forms (e.g., Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction), and 37.5% reported regularly practicing both. Controls reported no history of consistent mindfulness practice. Since this study was conducted within a larger fMRI project, exclusion criteria for both groups were largely based on MRI safety requirements: pregnancy, claustrophobia, metal or electronic implants (e.g., pacemakers or aneurysm clips), a history of head trauma, fainting, or neurological disorders (e.g., stroke or brain tumours). Further criteria were applied to limit confounding on neural and autonomic measures: treatment for a psychiatric disorder within the previous 12 months, use of medications or substances known to affect brain functioning, heavy alcohol consumption (more than 10 drinks per week), and heavy smoking (more than 140 cigarettes per week).

2.3. Power calculation

The sample size was determined by the number of participants who could be recruited within the parent project. For transparency, a sensitivity power analysis was conducted in G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) to identify the minimum detectable effect at α = .05 and 1 -β = .80, two-tailed. For the affective outcomes, modelled as analysis of covariance with three covariates (df = 1, N = 139-140), the minimum detectable effect was f = 0.24, approximately a medium effect. For the autonomic outcomes, modelled as a repeated-measures analysis of variance with two groups and three measurements and a correlation among repeated measures of .72 (n = 70), the study could detect a small Group × Phase interaction (f = 0.12) but only a medium-to-large between-group main effect (f = 0.31).

2.4. Procedure

Participants were instructed to refrain from consuming caffeine, alcohol and heavy meals on the day of the testing until the laboratory session had been completed. All sessions were conducted between 11:00 and 16:00. Upon arrival, the participants first provided written informed consent and then completed approximately 30 minutes of computer-administered questionnaires. This period also served as an acclimatisation phase, allowing participants to settle before physiological recording began. Immediately after completing the baseline assessment of affect, a Polar H10 chest-strap sensor was fitted to record electrocardiographic activity throughout the acute stress protocol.

Participants first completed a five-minute seated resting baseline in a quiet room. They were alone and instructed to keep their eyes open, remain still and not to use their phone. The acute psychosocial stressor was a modified version of the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST; Kirschbaum et al., 1993) adopted from Bosch et al. (2003). The task consisted of delivering two consecutive speeches in front of two judges who maintained neutral facial expressions, refrained from interacting with participants, and took sham notes on a standardized social-performance rating scale (Fydrich et al., 1998). Each speech was preceded by a two-minute silent preparation period and followed by four minutes of speech delivery. All task instructions were presented automatically on a screen. Participants remained standing in front of a camera during the entire task period, and they spoke into a microphone. They were led to believe that their performance was being recorded for later evaluation. If they stopped speaking before the four-minute period had elapsed, the judges instructed them to continue.

Immediately after the second speech, participants again completed the brief affect assessment and proceeded to a five-minute seated recovery period under the same conditions as at baseline.

Participants then had the chest-strap removed, and they completed the Stress and Adversity Inventory for Adults (STRAIN; Slavich & Shields, 2018). All participants were debriefed about the deceptive recording equipment and speech evaluation procedures. The protocol was administered identically in both groups. Note. 2.5. Measures

>Measures

2.5.1. Positive and negative affect

Affect was measured using the International Positive and Negative Affect Schedule Short Form (I-PANAS-SF; Thompson, 2007), which consists of two five-item subscales assessing positive and negative affect. Participants rated the extent to which each item described how they felt at the moment on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely).

Positive and negative affect subscale scores were calculated separately for each assessment by summing the relevant items. Possible scores range from 5 to 25, with higher scores indicating greater levels of the corresponding affect. In the original validation sample, I-PANAS-SF showed acceptable psychometric properties, including internal consistency of α = .78 for positive affect and α = .76 for negative affect (Thompson, 2007).

2.5.2. Heart rate variability

Cardiac activity was recorded continuously using a Polar H10 chest strap (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele Finland), which has demonstrated a high agreement with laboratory-grade electrocardiography for RR intervals and time-domain HRV indices (Blalock et al., 2026; Schaffarczyk et al., 2022). The sensor transmitted the data via Bluetooth to the Kubios HRV mobile application running on a smartphone.

HRV was indexed by the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) between adjacent RR intervals. RR interval recordings were preprocessed in Kubios HRV Scientific 4.2.0. In accordance with the preregistered protocol, RMSSD was calculated for three phases: a fiveminute resting baseline, two two-minute silent anticipation periods immediately preceding the respective speeches (which were combined into a single four-minute segment representing the stress phase), and a five-minute post-stress recovery phase. The speech periods were not included in the stress phase because vocalisation involves movement and irregular breathing that may affect HRV estimates (Druschky et al., 2020; Laborde et al., 2017). Artefacts and abnormal beats were identified and corrected using the automatic beat-correction algorithm developed by Lipponen and Tarvainen (2019). All recordings were subsequently visually inspected, and remaining ectopic beats, missed beat detection and misaligned beats were corrected using cubicspline interpolation. Low-frequency trends were removed using the smoothness detrending (λ = 500). Segments shorter than 3.5 minutes of valid recording or containing more than 5% of corrected beats were excluded.

2.5.3. Emotion regulation

The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004) & Roemer, 2004, and α = .94; Bardeen et al., 2012), as well as good 4-to 8-week test-retest reliability (ρ = .88; Gratz & Roemer, 2004).

2.5.4. Lifetime stressor exposure

We used the Stress and Adversity Inventory for Adults (STRAIN; Slavich & Shields, 2018) to assess participants' lifetime stressor exposure. It includes 55 stressors (26 acute events and 29 chronic difficulties; see http://www.strainsetup.com). Participants indicated for each stressor whether they had experienced it (yes / no). If they answered 'yes' to a specific item/stressor, they were asked follow-up questions about severity, frequency, exposure timing, and duration. Based on participants' responses, we derived the STRAIN's two main outcome scores: (1) lifetime stressor count (i.e., objective stressor exposure) and (2) lifetime stressor severity (i.e., the perceived severity of the stressors experienced). In addition to these totals, the STRAIN produces subscale scores related to stressor timing (early adversity occurring before age 18, adulthood, and the past year), stressor type (acute life events, chronic difficulties), 12 life domains (housing, education, work, treatment/health, marital/partner, reproduction, financial, legal/crime, other relationships, death, life-threatening situations, and possessions), and five core socialpsychological characteristics (interpersonal loss, physical danger, humiliation, entrapment, and role change/disruption). Each subscale is available as both a count and a severity score, which results in a total of 46 subscales. In its initial validation with community adults, the STRAIN demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability over a median interval of 13 days (rs ≥ .87 for all main indices; r = .919 for total lifetime stressor count and r = .904 for total lifetime stressor severity; Slavich & Shields, 2018); test-retest reliability was not assessed in the present sample.

The STRAIN has since shown good reliability and predictive utility across a wide variety of cultures (e.g., Cazassa et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2025; Sturmbauer et al., 2019), and psychological, clinical, and biological outcomes (Lam et al., 2019; Mengelkoch et al., 2025; Moseley et al., 2025).

2.6. Data analysis

Analyses were performed using R (version 4.6.0; R Core Team, 2026) with tidyverse packages for data preparation (Wickham et al., 2019), lme4 for linear mixed-effects models (Bates et al., 2015), car for Type III tests (Fox & Weisberg, 2019), emmeans for estimated marginal means and contrasts (Lenth, 2023), effectsize for effect size estimation (Ben-Shachar et al., 2020), influence.ME for influence diagnostics in mixed models (Nieuwenhuis et al., 2012), and ggplot2 for figures (Wickham, 2016).

Bootstrap resampling was implemented in base R. All tests were two-tailed with α = .05. The hypotheses, measures, primary analyses of affective and autonomic responses, and mediation analyses were preregistered (OSF: https://osf.io/wy3rn). Exploratory analyses involving lifetime stressor exposure were added post-hoc. Group comparisons on sample characteristics (age, gender, education, and total lifetime stressor exposure) were conducted using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and χ 2 tests for categorical variables.

Because RMSSD values are usually positively skewed (Laborde et al., 2017), they were naturallog transformed prior to analysis. To verify that the protocol elicited the intended stress response, two preliminary checks were conducted. Paired-samples t-tests examined pre-to post-stress changes in positive and negative affect. In parallel, the Phase-only mixed-effects model described below was used to test the omnibus effect of Phase on lnRMSSD, followed by pairwise comparisons between baseline, anticipation, and recovery phases using Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom with Tukey correction. Next, to determine whether Group predicted poststress affect (H1), separate linear regression models were fitted for negative and positive affect (ANCOVA, as preregistered). Each model included Group as the predictor and the corresponding pre-stress affect score, age and gender. The latter two were included because of the age-and gender-related differences in affective reactivity (Bagley et al., 2011; Kelly et al., 2008). Gender was entered as a binary indicator (female vs. not female; the five participants who selected "other" were retained in the reference category).

Group differences in phase-related changes in lnRMSSD were examined by fitting linear mixedeffects models in the HRV subsample, comprising participants with at least one valid HRV measurement across the three phases (resulting in n = 70; one participant was excluded because all three phases had more than 5% of corrected beats). There were a total of 203 of 210 possible phase-specific observations. Phase was treated as a within-participant factor and Group as a between-participant factor. Age and gender were included as covariates, as previous studies have shown they have been differently associated with HRV (Adjei et al., 2018; Hamidovic et al., 2020; Hernández-Vicente et al., 2020 Koenig & Thayer, 2016), and a random intercept for participant accounted for the dependence among repeated observations. Three nested linearmixed effects models estimated using maximum likelihood were compared: the full model (Group, Phase, Group x Phase), the no-interaction model (Group and Phase), and the no-Group model (Phase only). First, we used the no-Group model to conduct a manipulation check to determine whether the stress protocol elicited the expected pattern of physiological stress response. We examined the omnibus effect of Phase, followed by pairwise comparisons between the baseline, anticipation, and recovery phases, with p values adjusted for multiple comparisons with Tukey method. Specifically, we expected lnRMSSD to decrease from baseline to anticipation and to recover thereafter. The primary hypothesis was tested by comparing the full and no-interaction models to assess whether changes in HRV across phases differed between the groups. The no-interaction and no-Group models were then compared to assess whether the groups differed in overall HRV levels. Models were compared using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with differences converted into approximate Bayes Factors to quantify the strength of evidence for competing models using the threshold of Lee and Wagenmakers (2013).

Likelihood-ratio chi-square tests of the same nested model comparisons are reported as a frequentist robustness check.

Furthermore, meditators and controls were compared on all STRAIN subscales using independent-samples t-tests, with group differences expressed as standardised mean differences (Cohen's d) and 95% confidence intervals. In sensitivity analyses, each primary affect and HRV model was re-estimated with each STRAIN subscale entered separately as an additional covariate (subscales were never entered jointly because they overlap, with each stressor contributing to several subscales), to establish whether the group effects on affect and HRV depended on differences in lifetime stressor exposure. As a further robustness check, we tested 16 whether pre-to-post changes in affect differed between groups. Because non-randomised groups may differ at baseline, baseline-adjusted and change-score estimates can diverge (Lord, 1967; Miller & Chapman, 2001). We therefore compared pre-stress affect between groups (Welch's ttests) and fitted change-score models with the same covariates as the primary models, equivalent to testing a Group × Time interaction.

Finally, exploratory mediation analyses examined whether emotion regulation difficulties represented a potential mechanism underlying group differences in affective responses, with separate analyses for positive and negative affect. For each outcome, an ordinary least-squares apath model examined whether groups differed in DERS scores while controlling for age and gender. Before fitting the mixed effects models for the b and c' paths, DERS was grand-mean centered to improve interpretability of coefficients. Affect was predicted from Group x Time, DERS, DERS x Time, and the same covariates as previous models, as well as the random intercept for participant to account for dependency. The model was used to estimate whether DERS partially accounted for group differences in pre-stress affect and in the pre-to-post change in affect. We additionally calculated the dissociation between these indirect effects to determine whether the indirect effect through DERS differed between pre-stress affect and the pre-to-post change in affect. Inference was based on 95% bootstrap confidence intervals derived from 5000 participant-level resamples with replacement, with an effect supported when its interval excluded zero.

In further exploratory analyses, we examined associations between each STRAIN subscale and participants' affective and autonomic responses using partial correlations controlling for age, gender, group, and the corresponding pre-stress (baseline) value. Lastly, within the meditator group, we examined whether practice amount was associated with the affective and autonomic outcomes, entering lifetime practice (months, square-root-transformed) and recent practice (minutes in the previous month, natural-log-transformed), each grand-mean-centred, into the corresponding models. There were no directional hypotheses for these exploratory analyses, and these results are considered hypothesis-generating.

Missing values were not imputed. As preregistered, each model was estimated by case-wise deletion on participants with complete data for that model, thus the analytic n varies across analyses (negative affect n = 139, positive affect n = 140, heart rate variability n = 70). Model assumptions were evaluated through visual inspection of residual distributions and residualversus-fitted plots, of random-effect distributions for the mixed-effects models, and through influence diagnostics. Homogeneity of regression slopes was satisfied for both analyses of covariance, and all variance inflation factors were below 1.15. Group variances differed only for post-stress negative affect (Levene's test, p = .011), but given that groups were of similar size, analysis of covariance is robust to heterogeneity of this magnitude. Influence diagnostics identified no case whose removal altered the significance or direction of any preregistered effect.

3.4. Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the group effects to lifetime stressor exposure, each primary model was re-estimated with each STRAIN subscale entered individually as an additional covariate.

The effect of group remained significant across all subscales for negative affect (all ps ≤ .011).

For positive affect the effect was less robust: it remained significant for 44 of 46 subscales (p range .019-.069), falling just above the conventional threshold when adjusting for possessionrelated severity (p = .069) or reproduction-related count (p = .056). For heart rate variability, the main effect of group (all ps ≥ .41) and the Group × Phase interaction (all ps ≥ .76) remained nonsignificant in all models, and the Group × Phase interaction was also non-significant when untransformed RMSSD was analysed (χ 2 (2) = 0.86, p = .65). Within the meditator group, neither lifetime nor recent practice was associated with post-stress affect (all ps ≥ .13) or with lnRMSSD across phases (all ps ≥ .30, all BF01 ≥ 9.0).

Finally, because the groups also differed in pre-stress affect, with meditators reporting lower negative affect (p = .042, d = -0.36) and higher positive affect (p = .006, d = 0.47) than controls (Table 2), we examined as a robustness check whether pre-to-post change differed between groups. Negative affect increased less in meditators than in controls (+0.60 vs. +1.45), but the group difference in change was not statistically significant, b = -0.87, 95% CI [-1.79, 0.05], F(1, 135) = 3.48, p = .064, ηp² = .03; leave-one-out p-values ranged from .020 to .106. Positive affect increased more in meditators than in controls (+1.48 vs. +0.88), and this difference was also not significant, b = 0.61, 95% CI [-0.51, 1.72], F(1, 136) = 1.15, p = .286, ηp² = .01.

3.5. Emotion regulation difficulties as a mediator

Exploratory mediation analyses tested whether trait emotion regulation difficulties accounted for group differences in affective response to the TSST. Controlling for age and gender, long-term meditators showed lower emotion regulation difficulties than controls (b = -20.45, SE = 2.51, p < .001). Greater difficulties predicted higher baseline negative affect (b = 0.045, SE = 0.013, p < .001) and lower baseline positive affect (b = -0.047, SE = 0.020, p = .018), but did not predict the pre-to-post change in either (both ps ≥ .77). Indirect effects followed the same pattern: significant on baseline affect (negative: ab = -0.93, 95% CI [-1.48, -0.42]; positive: ab= 0.97 [0.11, 1.85]) but not on pre-to-post change (negative: ab = 0.06 [-0.74, 0.84]; positive: ab = -0.11 [-0.87, 0.63]). The baseline and change indirect effects did not, however, differ significantly from each other (negative: ab = -0.98 [-2.23, 0.20]; positive: ab = 1.07 [-0.26, 2.40]).

3.6. Associations between lifetime stressor exposure and responses to the TSST

Exploratory associations between each STRAIN subscale and affective and autonomic responses were examined for the full sample, controlling for group, age, gender and corresponding prestress value. Of 230 associations tested, only 10 reached p < .05, close to the 11.5 expected by chance alone (see Supplementary Figure S1 for stressor severity associations and S2 for stressor count associations). Where associations did emerge, their direction was consistent within outcomes: greater lifetime stressor exposure was associated with lower post-stress positive affect (five associations, all negative), with higher heart rate variability during the stress phase (two associations, both work-related), with lower post-stress negative affect (two associations, both count indices), and with higher resting heart rate variability (one association).

4. Discussion

"As a mountain of rock is unshaken by wind, so also, the wise are unperturbed by blame or by praise." -Dhammapada (Verse 81) Both classical and contemporary portrayals often depict the experienced meditator as remaining unperturbed under stress. Buddhist scriptures use the image of a mountain "unshaken by wind" (Fronsdal, 2023), popular accounts of mindfulness promise comparable composure (Van Dam et al., 2018), and the mindfulness stress-buffering account proposes psychological and physiological pathways that may explain this phenomenon (Creswell & Lindsay, 2014; Creswell et al., 2019). When we put these assumptions to a direct empirical test, they were supported at the psychological but not physiological level. Specifically, long-term meditators reported lower post-stress negative affect and higher positive affect than meditation-naïve controls after accounting for pre-stress affect, consistent with the stress-buffering account. However, their autonomic responses, measured by vagally mediated HRV, were not different from those of controls, both overall and in their trajectories across phases of the TSST (both BF01 > 14). Longterm meditation practice was thus associated with lower negative and higher positive affect following the acute psychosocial stressor, but not with a different autonomic response to it. These group differences were not explained by lifetime stressor exposure and were largely robust to adjustment for the various lifetime stressor domains assessed by the STRAIN. Furthermore, differences in emotion-regulation difficulties accounted for group differences in affect before the stressor occurred but did not account for group differences in affective change following the stressor.

Our findings are consistent with the wider mindfulness-based intervention literature, in which stress buffering has also been observed more reliably for self-reported than for physiological outcomes (Morton et al., 2020) and extend that pattern to long-term meditators with more than three years of regular practice. To our knowledge, however, only two directly comparable studies exist, and their affective findings were mixed. Rosenkranz et al. (2016) found that long-term meditators rated the TSST as less stressful than controls, whereas Gamaiunova et al. (2019) found no group differences in momentary stress ratings or post-stress negative affect, although meditators did report less shame and higher self-esteem. These earlier studies, however, had smaller samples that could detect only large effects, and affect was assessed with either a single stressfulness rating or a post-stressor affect questionnaire alone. The effects observed in our preregistered affect analyses were small-to-moderate (ηp² = .04-.05). The groups also differed in affect before the stressor, and a robustness analysis of pre-to-post change showed smaller effects in the same direction that did not reach statistical significance. Both groups showed a clear affective response to the TSST, and the advantage associated with long-term meditation therefore appears modest rather than a state of being unaffected by stressful events. Such modest effects may nevertheless become clinically relevant if they recur across many stressors.

Consistent with this possibility, meditators reported fewer and less severe stressors over the preceding year.

Similarly, our finding of no difference in autonomic responses to the TSST between long-term meditators and controls is consistent with both prior long-term meditator studies, which found no differences in HRV (Gamaiunova et al., 2019) or salivary alpha-amylase (Rosenkranz et al., 2016). It also aligns with meta-analytic evidence that mindfulness-based interventions do not reliably alter vagally mediated HRV (Brown et al., 2021; Christodoulou et al., 2020). In the present study, there was a large decrease in HRV during stress anticipation and full recovery within five minutes in both groups. Bayesian analyses provided strong to extreme evidence for the absence of group differences. These findings suggest that any stress-buffering effects of longterm meditation may not operate through reduced autonomic reactivity to acute stress. In fact, physiological reactivity to acute stress is a necessary human capacity that prepares the organism for challenge and maximises the chances of survival when demands are real (Sapolsky, 2004).

This system is engaged by acute stressors of any kind, including the social-evaluative demands that characterise modern life and laboratory paradigms. Blunted physiological responses to acute stress predict adverse rather than favourable outcomes (Carroll et al., 2017; Lovallo, 2011). From this perspective, an adaptive profile is not a damped physiological response but appropriate mobilisation followed by efficient recovery, and both groups showed this pattern.

The dissociation between affective and autonomic responding may provide clues about the processes underlying these group differences. Subjective and physiological responses to acute stress do not necessarily covary (Campbell & Ehlert, 2012), and emotion-regulation strategies can alter subjective emotional experience more strongly than physiological responding (Webb et al., 2012; Zaehringer et al., 2020). Two strategies associated with mindfulness practice are plausible candidates for the pattern observed here. Acceptance has been linked to stress buffering in both theory and experimental research (Lindsay & Creswell, 2017; Lindsay et al., 2018), positive reappraisal has been associated with mindfulness training (Garland et al., 2011; 2015), and long-term practitioners report greater use of both strategies than controls (Gamaiunova et al., 2019). Because strategy use was not assessed here, either during the stressor or as a disposition, these mechanisms remain to be tested directly.

The content of our affect measure provides an additional reason to consider appraisal in interpreting the present findings. All five positive-affect items of the I-PANAS-SF assess relatively activated states. Thus, meditators reported not only lower negative affect following the stressor, but also greater alertness, attentiveness, and determination, despite showing autonomic responses comparable to those of controls. The combination of preserved physiological mobilisation, lower negative affect, and greater positive engagement is compatible with a more challenge-oriented rather than threat-oriented experience of evaluated performance (Seery, 2011). A related interpretation comes from contemplative accounts of equanimity, which describe a more even-minded relationship to experience regardless of its valence (Desbordes et al., 2015; Hadash et al., 2016). Early Buddhist texts express a similar distinction through the metaphor of the "second arrow", which separates the initial impact of an adverse event from the additional suffering generated in response to it (Bodhi, 2000). Our findings suggest a modestly weaker second arrow in long-term meditators, although a part of the affective difference was already present before the stressor.

More broadly, any advantage associated with long-term meditation may operate across a wider timescale than the laboratory acute response captured here. Before a stressor occurs, practice may influence how situations are selected or appraised. Situation selection is among the earliestacting forms of emotion regulation (Gross, 2015), and differences in how avoidable stressors are managed could contribute to differences in stressor exposure over time. After a stressor, differences may instead emerge during recovery. Prolonged activation sustained by processes such as rumination and worry is considered an important pathway linking acute stress to poorer health (Brosschot et al., 2005), while reductions in rumination and worry are one of the mediators of mindfulness-based interventions (Gu et al., 2015). One possibility, therefore, is that group differences are more pronounced during longer-term recovery and in the periods between stressors than during the acute response itself. Several observations in the present data are consistent with this possibility. First, affective differences during the acute episode were smallto-moderate, while autonomic responses did not differ, suggesting that the acute response may not be where group differences are most pronounced. Second, emotion-regulation difficulties accounted for differences in baseline affect but not for affective change following the stressor.

Although the indirect effects did not differ significantly, this pattern is consistent with emotion regulation shaping the affective context in which stress occurs more than the acute response itself. Third, meditators reported fewer and less severe stressors over the preceding year despite no significant difference in lifetime stressor exposure. Although exploratory, this finding raises the possibility that processes such as situation selection (Gross, 2015) or reduced stress generation (Hammen, 1991) operate over longer timescales.

Alternative explanations cannot be excluded. For example, affect was assessed by self-report, and long-term meditators may have inferred the study hypotheses and responded in ways consistent with their identity as meditation practitioners (Grossman, 2008; Van Dam et al., 2018). No questionnaire-based design can fully rule out this possibility. Two observations, however, argue against a simple favourable-response account. First, a general tendency toward favourable self-presentation would be expected to influence self-report more broadly, yet the groups did not differ in total lifetime stressor exposure. Second, the affect measure did not ask participants directly how stressful they found the task, but instead assessed specific positive and negative affective states, making the expected direction of the study hypothesis less obvious than it would be with a direct stressfulness rating. It is also possible that autonomic differences existed but were not captured by our measurement approach. For instance, long-term meditators may differ in the trajectory of recovery rather than in average levels across broad phases, and such differences could be obscured by phase-averaged measures.

4.1. Strengths and limitations

The present study has several strengths. Of note, direct laboratory tests of stress reactivity in long-term meditators are rare, and the two previous studies were based on smaller samples. We compared 80 practitioners averaging 8.4 years of practice with 68 age-and gender-matched controls, with hypotheses, measures, and primary analyses preregistered. In addition, lifetime stressor exposure was measured directly, and group differences in affect remained robust after adjustment for nearly all STRAIN subscales, making differential stress history an unlikely explanation. Finally, vagally mediated HRV was assessed in speech-free windows to avoid vocalisation artefacts, modelled across baseline, anticipation, and recovery rather than reduced to a difference score, and evaluated using Bayesian model comparison.

The main design limitation is inherent to research on experienced practitioners: long-term meditators are self-selected. In principle, prospective longitudinal designs beginning before meditation practice could separate selection effects from changes associated with sustained practice. In practice, however, such studies are difficult to conduct because only a small proportion of individuals who begin meditating are likely to sustain regular practice over many years. Cross-sectional studies therefore remain a feasible approach for studying highly experienced meditators. The study also had several measurement limitations. HRV was recorded seated at baseline and recovery but standing during anticipation, and respiration was not measured. The decline in HRV during anticipation therefore cannot be attributed solely to stressrelated vagal withdrawal. Because the protocol was identical across groups, however, these factors are unlikely to explain the absence of group differences, and baseline and recovery remain directly comparable. Moreover, HRV captures only one component of autonomic responding and does not provide a comprehensive measure of physiological stress responding.

Pre-session behaviours that can influence HRV, including nicotine use and physical exercise, were not restricted or recorded and may have increased measurement variability. Finally, meditation history was self-reported and heterogeneous across traditions, limiting interpretation of dose-response effects, while the predominantly Dutch, highly educated sample constrains generalisability.

4.2. Future directions

These limitations and the possibilities raised above suggest several priorities for future research.

Directly measuring challenge and threat appraisals, acceptance, and reappraisal during acute stress would allow the proposed mechanisms to be tested rather than inferred. Laboratory studies could extend the recovery period to 30-60 minutes, repeatedly assessing affect and rumination alongside continuous HRV. Concurrent measurement of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal activity could determine whether the faster cortisol recovery previously observed in long-term meditators (Gamaiunova et al., 2019) coexists with comparable HRV responses. Experience-sampling studies could extend this work into daily life, tracking stress anticipation, acute response, recovery and emotion-regulation strategies as they unfold.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, the present findings are among the first that we know of to suggest that long-term meditation practice is associated with lower negative and higher positive affect following an acute psychosocial stressor, but not with different autonomic responses to it. At the same time, meditators reported fewer and less-severe stressors over the preceding year, suggesting that group differences may also extend beyond the acute stress response to processes operating over longer timescales.
