Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Cognitive control is traditionally conceptualized as a set of top-down mechanisms that select and regulate stimulus-response mappings to support goal-directed and flexible behaviour. Traditional accounts have provided fundamental insights into the neural architecture of cognitive control and identified a set of core control processes, including goal maintenance, task switching, control allocation, conflict monitoring, and cognitive effort. However, they rarely address how these functions can be integrated within a common computational architecture. Furthermore, most theories tend to assume that the core control functions use specialized mechanisms that are fundamentally distinct from those supporting action-perception loops. Here, we review an alternative perspective grounded in predictive coding and active inference, according to which cognitive control emerges from hierarchical elaborations of the same generative inference supporting perception and action. Within predictive coding and active inference, the brain continuously generates predictions about sensory inputs, actions, and their expected consequences, and updates these predictions on the basis of precision-weighted prediction errors. We argue that the same precision-weighting mechanism, when operating beyond sensory processing-to optimize the precision of higher-level beliefs and of courses of action (policies)-provides the foundations of cognitive control, allowing a cognitive system to balance the stability of current goals and task representations against the flexibility required to respond to unexpected information and environmental changes. From this perspective, the core control processes emerge as different facets of precision regulation within a hierarchical generative model: the precision of higher-level beliefs supports goal maintenance and task switching; policy precision supports control allocation, setting the balance between habitual and goal-directed control and prioritizing the latter when required by task demands; conflict monitoring provides information about whether the selected policy continues to produce expected and preferred outcomes; and cognitive effort reflects the cost of increasing precision to change the resulting control configuration. Finally, we link the precision-based computations to putative neural processes in frontoparietal networks and neuromodulatory processes spanning dopaminergic, noradrenergic, and cholinergic systems, and derive empirical predictions that can test the proposed framework.

1 Introduction

Cognitive control refers to the set of processes that allow thoughts and actions to be organized in accordance with internal goals, particularly when habitual or immediately available responses are inadequate. In traditional models, the prefrontal cortex maintains task goals and provides top-down biasing signals that enhance the processing of task-relevant representations across posterior cortical and subcortical systems (Miller and Cohen, 2001). Related conflict-monitoring theories propose that the anterior cingulate cortex detects competition between incompatible response tendencies and recruits additional control when conflict increases (Botvinick et al., 2001; Botvinick, 2007a; Botvinick and Cohen, 2014). Other influential approaches distinguish between proactive control, based on the sustained maintenance of goal information, and reactive control, which is transiently recruited following the detection of interference or an unexpected event (Braver, 2012). Together, these theories have provided a comprehensive account of core control functions, such as goal maintenance, task switching, control allocation, conflict monitoring, and cognitive effort (Egner, 2023; Friedman and Robbins, 2022; Badre, 2008; Diamond, 2013). Neuropsychological and neuroimaging studies suggest that such functions depend on a distributed frontoparietal system capable of flexibly representing task rules, contextual information, stimulusresponse mappings, and behavioural goals (Duncan, 2010; Niendam et al., 2012; Cole et al., 2013; Koechlin et al., 2003; Miyake et al., 2000).

From a computational perspective, cognitive control corresponds to a challenging problem, since it has to simultaneously ensure adaptive behaviour and save computational resources -hence, decrease control when automatic (or habitual) processes are sufficient and rapidly increase it when task demands require overriding such automatic responses through controlled processes (Norman and Shallice, 1986). Moreover, control must operate across multiple spatial and temporal scales. Abstract goals may remain relevant for minutes or hours, whereas individual decisions and motor responses unfold over milliseconds. The brain must therefore maintain stable representations of current goals while remaining sufficiently sensitive to unexpected information that signals the need for behavioural change (Goschke, 2003; Dreisbach, 2012; Hommel, 2015; Pezzulo et al., 2018; Verschure et al., 2014). Traditional control theories describe many of the core control functions required to achieve this balance, such as goal maintenance, task switching, control allocation, conflict monitoring, and cognitive effort (see below for a review), but they do not always specify how these functions can be integrated within a common computational architecture. Furthermore, most theories tend to assume that the core control functions are not only anatomically related to distinct brain networks, but also use specialized mechanisms that are fundamentally distinct from those supporting action-perception loops. An alternative possibility is that these core control functions are not fundamentally distinct from, but rather hierarchical elaborations of, the same mechanisms steering action-perception loops (Fuster, 2001 (Fuster, , 2004;; Koechlin et al., 2003; Koechlin and Summerfield, 2007; Pezzulo, 2012; Pezzulo et al., 2018).

If this is the case, a starting point for better understanding the architecture of cognitive control is offered by predictive processing, which is increasingly recognized as the architecture of action-perception. The theory of predictive coding proposes that the brain functions as a hierarchical generative model that continuously predicts the causes of its sensory inputs (Rao and Ballard, 1999; Friston, 2005). At each level of the hierarchy, neuronal populations encode hypotheses about the states of the world and generate predictions about activity at subordinate levels. These descending predictions are compared with incoming signals, and the resulting mismatches are encoded as prediction errors that propagate upwards to update higher-level representations. Because higher levels represent progressively more abstract and temporally extended regularities, the same computational principle can in principle explain inference across sensory, cognitive, and motor domains (Kiebel et al., 2008; Friston, 2010; Phillips et al., 2015; Zada et al., 2025; Goldstein et al., 2022; Aizenbud et al., 2025; Keller and Mrsic-Flogel, 2018; Kok and de Lange, 2015). The influence of prediction errors on belief updating depends on their precision, the inverse of their expected variance, which determines how strongly prior predictions and incoming evidence shape inference (Feldman and Friston, 2010). Active inference extends predictive coding from perception to action (Friston et al., 2017; Parr et al., 2022). Beliefs about hidden states are updated to explain current observations, and actions are selected by inferring which policy, that is, which sequence of actions, is expected to lead to preferred outcomes and to reduce uncertainty. Precision applies to this inference as well: policy precision determines how strongly these expectations guide action selection relative to habits acquired through repetition (Friston et al., 2016; Parr and Friston, 2017; FitzGerald et al., 2015).

Predictive coding and active inference have been widely used to explain perception and simple forms of action control, but their application to higher cognitive functions and cognitive control is still in its early stages. Here, building on these two frameworks, we develop the proposal that the architecture of cognitive control is a hierarchical elaboration of the architecture supporting actionperception loops and shares its predictive coding and active inference mechanisms. Specifically, we propose that the architecture of cognitive control is responsible for the context-sensitive regulation of precision across the levels of the hierarchical generative model supporting cognitive control (at higher levels) and movement control (at lower levels) (Pezzulo, 2012; Pezzulo et al., 2018; Proietti et al., 2025). Precision regulation operates at least at two levels. First, at the level of goal and task rule representations required for cognitive control, it allows these representations to remain precise enough to guide lower levels, while at the same time allowing reliable prediction errors to revise them to maintain flexibility; this level underlies goal maintenance and task switching. Second, at the level of action control -seen as inference over policies (Parr and Friston, 2017; Liu et al., 2025; Donnarumma et al., 2026; Botvinick and Toussaint, 2012) -policy precision sets the balance between habitual (automatic) and goal-directed (deliberative) control; this level underlies control allocation, conflict monitoring, and cognitive effort. This proposal contextualizes traditional models by identifying the regulation of precision as the common principle behind the core cognitive operations they describe.

Here, we review this proposal, highlighting its relations with traditional accounts of cognitive control as well as with predictive coding and active inference accounts of perception and action. We first revisit traditional theories of cognitive control and the questions they leave open, and then introduce predictive coding and active inference together with their proposed neural implementation. We next develop an account of cognitive control grounded in predictive coding and active inference mechanisms, showing how precision control can explain the core control functions. Finally, we discuss neuronal and neuromodulatory processes putatively related to precision-modulation computations and outline distinctive empirical predictions.

2 Traditional theories of cognitive control: a brief review

Traditional theories have generally described cognitive (or executive) control as a set of mechanisms that configure stimulus-response mappings, maintain task-relevant information, resolve competition among alternative actions, and adjust behaviour following changes in environmental demands (Baddeley, 1996). Yet different theoretical accounts have focused primarily on distinct aspects of cognitive control. Below, we briefly summarize key traditional accounts -supervisory attention, goal maintenance and top-down biasing, hierarchical prefrontal organization, conflict monitoring, the expected value of control, and proactive and reactive control -and identify the questions they leave open.

2.1 Supervisory attention and the control of action

One of the earliest influential accounts was the supervisory attentional system proposed by Norman and Shallice (1986). According to this framework, well-learned behaviour is ordinarily governed by relatively automatic action schemas. When multiple schemas are activated simultaneously, they compete for control of behaviour through a contention-scheduling mechanism (Cooper and Shallice, 2000). A supervisory attentional system becomes necessary when routine schemas are insufficient or inappropriate. The supervisory system biases competition among schemas so that behaviour remains aligned with current intentions and is not captured by habitual tendencies.

The distinction between automatic schema selection and supervisory intervention, which parallels the earlier distinction between automatic and controlled processing (Schneider and Shiffrin, 1977; Shiffrin and Schneider, 1977), established a central theme of subsequent control theories: goal-directed behaviour requires higher-order information capable of modifying otherwise dominant stimulus-response mappings. However, the supervisory attentional framework was primarily functional. It specified the conditions under which executive regulation was required but provided a comparatively limited account of the computational and neural mechanisms through which supervisory signals were generated, represented, and implemented.

2.2 Prefrontal maintenance and top-down bias

The guided-activation theory of prefrontal function provided a more explicit neural and computational formulation of this idea. Miller and Cohen (2001) proposed that the prefrontal cortex maintains representations of goals, task rules, and contextual information in an active state. Through reciprocal connections with posterior cortical and subcortical systems, these representations produce top-down biasing signals that modify the flow of activity along task-relevant processing pathways.

When a stimulus is compatible with several possible responses, maintained contextual representations increase the activation of pathways associated with the currently appropriate response and reduce the relative influence of competing alternatives. For example, during an incongruent Stroop trial, the stimulus activates both a highly practised word-reading response and the task-relevant colour-naming response (Stroop, 1935; MacLeod, 1991; Cohen et al., 1990). Maintaining the instruction to name the ink colour allows prefrontal representations to bias processing towards the relevant perceptual dimension and corresponding response pathway.

The theory also provides a mechanistic bridge between working memory and cognitive control: control depends on the stability of task-relevant representations and on their capacity to bias distributed processing systems (Miller and Cohen, 2001; O'Reilly and Frank, 2006). A complementary perspective comes from inhibitory control, which enables behaviour to remain aligned with current goals despite competing stimuli, thoughts, or prepotent responses (Diamond, 2013). Inhibitory control encompasses response inhibition, which supports the restraint of dominant or impulsive actions, and interference control, which supports selective attention and resistance to distracting thoughts and memories (Logan, 1994; Mayr and Keele, 2000). These functions interact closely with working memory: maintaining the current goal helps determine which responses or distractions should be inhibited, while interference control helps protect goal-relevant information.

The adaptive-coding hypothesis further extends this perspective by arguing that frontoparietal control regions flexibly represent the information currently required by a task (Duncan, 2001 (Duncan, , 2010)). Neuronal populations within the multiple-demand network are not restricted to a single category of stimuli, rules, or responses: they adapt their coding properties to represent taskrelevant distinctions (Wallis et al., 2001; Stokes et al., 2013). This flexibility allows the same distributed network to participate in multiple cognitive operations, including attention, working memory, problem solving, response selection, and task switching (Duncan, 2013; Cole et al., 2013). Nevertheless, adaptive-coding accounts primarily describe how task-relevant information is represented. They provide less detail about how the brain estimates the reliability of those representations or decides when an established task model should be revised.

2.3 Hierarchical organization of cognitive control

Traditional theories of cognitive control have also emphasized the hierarchical organization of action and control in the cortex (Fuster, 2004 (Fuster, , 2001)). Complex tasks often involve nested dependencies in which concrete motor responses are selected according to immediate stimuli, while more abstract rules determine which stimulus-response mapping is currently relevant.

Neuroimaging evidence suggests that the lateral frontal cortex exhibits a functional gradient along its rostro-caudal axis. Premotor and caudal prefrontal regions tend to be recruited when control depends on relatively concrete stimulus and response information, whereas progressively more rostral regions are recruited when behaviour depends on increasingly abstract, contextual, or temporally extended information (Koechlin et al., 2003; Badre and D'Esposito, 2009; Badre, 2008; Badre and Nee, 2018; Collins and Koechlin, 2012; Kiebel et al., 2008). Although the precise computational dimension underlying this gradient remains debated (Botvinick, 2007b (Botvinick, , 2008;; Koechlin and Summerfield, 2007; Pezzulo et al., 2018), converging evidence suggests that anterior frontal regions represent information capable of constraining processing at more posterior levels.

In the cascade model proposed by Koechlin et al. (2003), cognitive control is organized through a succession of nested levels extending from premotor to anterior prefrontal cortex. Sensory control, associated primarily with premotor regions, selects responses according to currently available stimuli. Contextual control, associated with caudal lateral prefrontal regions, uses contextual signals to determine the appropriate stimulus-response mapping. Episodic control, associated with more rostral prefrontal regions, uses information about the current temporal episode to determine which contextual representation should guide behaviour. Lesion evidence supports this organization: the level of abstraction at which action selection is impaired depends on the location of frontal damage along the rostro-caudal axis (Badre et al., 2009).

Hierarchical control models therefore move beyond the idea of a single, undifferentiated central executive. Nevertheless, these accounts have primarily characterized hierarchy in terms of representational abstraction, temporal extension, and informational demands. Although they explain how control can be distributed across hierarchical levels, they leave open what mechanisms support hierarchical control from higher to lower stages.

2.4 Conflict monitoring and control adjustment

A complementary line of research has focused on the mechanisms that detect the need for cognitive control. Conflict-monitoring theory proposes that the anterior cingulate cortex, particularly its dorsal portion, monitors conflict arising from the simultaneous activation of incompatible processing or response pathways (Botvinick et al., 2001; Botvinick and Cohen, 2014). In the original computational formulation, response conflict increases when mutually incompatible response units are strongly co-activated, as commonly occurs during incongruent trials in Stroop, flanker, and Simon tasks. According to this model, the conflict signal is used to strengthen prefrontal control, so that on subsequent trials increased top-down bias towards the task-relevant pathway reduces the influence of competing information (Botvinick et al., 2001; Kerns et al., 2004). This mechanism accounts for the reduced congruency effect that follows high-conflict trials, known as the congruency sequence effect or conflict-adaptation effect (Gratton et al., 1992; Egner, 2007).

Conflict-monitoring theory introduced an influential functional distinction between the evaluation and implementation of control. Medial frontal systems, particularly the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, were proposed to detect conditions indicating an increased demand for control, whereas lateral prefrontal systems implemented compensatory adjustments in task processing (Botvinick et al., 2001 (Botvinick et al., , 2004;; MacDonald et al., 2000; Carter et al., 1998). This distinction provided a computational account of how medial and lateral frontal regions could make coordinated contributions to adaptive behaviour.

Subsequent theories broadened the proposed functions of the medial frontal cortex beyond the monitoring of response conflict. Reinforcement-learning accounts related medial frontal activity to worse-than-expected action outcomes and error processing (Holroyd and Coles, 2002), whereas predicted-response-outcome models proposed that the medial prefrontal cortex learns expectations about possible action outcomes and signals deviations from those predictions (Alexander and Brown, 2011). More recent formulations have proposed that the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex contributes to determining whether control is worth allocating by integrating its expected benefits and costs (Shenhav et al., 2013). Conflict can therefore be considered one among several performance-related signals relevant to the adaptive regulation of behaviour.

2.5 The Expected Value of Control

Conflict monitoring explains how competing responses can signal a need for control. The Expected Value of Control (EVC) theory addresses a related question: whether, and how much, control is worth allocating (Shenhav et al., 2013). According to this framework, the cognitive system evaluates candidate control signals defined along two dimensions: their identity and their intensity. The identity of a control signal specifies which process, representation, or processing pathway should be prioritized, whereas its intensity specifies how strongly that control signal should be applied. The expected value assigned to a candidate control signal depends on the value of its anticipated outcomes, the probability of those outcomes given the selected signal and the current state, and the (subjective) cognitive cost of implementing control at the specified intensity. The optimal control configuration is the combination of identity and intensity expected to result in the greatest net value. This formulation also explains why individuals may not engage maximal control even when doing so would improve accuracy. Increasing control intensity may improve the probability of a correct response while also increasing the subjective cost of mental effort. These costs may reflect several complementary factors, including computational constraints, interference with other processes, and opportunity costs associated with allocating limited cognitive resources (Shenhav et al., 2017; Musslick and Cohen, 2021; Zénon et al., 2019). Control allocation is therefore optimized rather than maximized: the system selects the type and intensity of control expected to provide the most favourable balance between anticipated benefits and costs.

The EVC framework treats control allocation as a value-based decision (Shenhav et al., 2013). At the neural level, the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex is proposed to evaluate candidate control signals and identify the configuration with the highest expected value, while lateral prefrontal and associated systems implement the selected control signal. Complementary accounts address how control is learned and adjusted through experience. The Reinforcement Meta-Learner model proposes that reciprocal interactions between the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and catecholaminergic brainstem nuclei regulate learning rates and effort allocation (Silvetti et al., 2018). Subsequent work connects this adaptive regulation to Bayesian surprise, integrating performance monitoring, value estimation, and cognitive control within a meta-reinforcement learning framework (Vriens et al., 2025).

2.6 Proactive and reactive modes of control

Another relevant distinction in the cognitive control literature, linked to the Dual Mechanisms of Control framework, is between two temporal modes through which goal information can influence behaviour (Braver, 2012; Braver et al., 2021). Proactive control involves the anticipatory and sustained maintenance of goal-relevant information before a cognitively demanding event occurs. By maintaining task rules or contextual cues in advance, proactive control allows upcoming stimuli to be processed according to the appropriate task model and can prevent interference before it develops.

Reactive control, in contrast, is recruited transiently after the occurrence of conflict, interference, an error, or another event signalling that goal-relevant information must be retrieved. Reactive control may be less metabolically or computationally demanding than sustained proactive maintenance, but it also leaves behaviour more vulnerable to interference from prepotent responses. The relative reliance on proactive and reactive control varies across tasks, individuals, motivational states, and clinical populations (Braver et al., 2021).

2.7 Open questions in traditional accounts of cognitive control

Together, the accounts reviewed above have helped identify, despite their differences, a coherent set of core control functions: goal maintenance, task switching, control allocation, conflict monitoring, and cognitive effort. Yet, key questions remain open.

First, although these core control functions are commonly grouped under the general label of executive (or cognitive) control, it is still unclear whether they can be understood under a common computational principle or whether they require an appeal to distinct mechanisms following different principles (e.g., each optimizing a distinct objective) -and, in the latter case, how they are connected. Relatedly, control signals -which are arguably the central component of the cognitive control architecture -are variously described as top-down biases, maintained goals, changes in processing gain, or selected combinations of control identity and intensity, without any single computational principle shared across these accounts.

Second, some aspects of the cognitive control architecture that relate to lower-level processes (perception and action) remain unresolved. For example, established models focus primarily on how task representations are maintained and used, and less on how the relevant representation is inferred and learned from uncertain or ambiguous evidence. A contextual cue may be assumed to activate an appropriate task rule, but the confidence assigned to that interpretation, the influence of prior experience, and the revision of the rule when contingencies change are not always formally represented. It is also unclear how the influence of a maintained representation should be balanced against uncertain sensory evidence, competing contextual interpretations, and alternative task policies.

These problems, such as learning and inferring representations and integrating prior knowledge with uncertain evidence, also arise at lower levels that deal with perception and action control. This points to a third problem: the fact that cognitive control theories tend not to connect well with theories of perception and action. Explanations of core cognitive functions tend to appeal to a different set of mechanisms and representational vocabularies compared with those used in explanations of perception-action loops. While not necessarily wrong, this move is difficult to reconcile with influential theoretical accounts emphasizing that cognitive control is better seen as an "upper stage" of perception and action (Fuster, 2001 (Fuster, , 2004)).

A different approach would be to start from computational architectures for perception and action and study whether their mechanisms could be elaborated to also explain the "upper stage" -namely, the core cognitive functions identified by the aforementioned theories, which allow living organisms to solve cognitive control problems. Predictive processing theoriesencompassing predictive coding, active inference, and related accounts -provide influential accounts of perception and action and are therefore well suited to address this challenge.

3 Hierarchical predictive coding and active inference

Predictive processing frameworks, of which predictive coding is the best-known example, propose that the brain can be modelled as a hierarchical generative system that infers the latent causes of sensory inputs (Srinivasan et al., 1982; Rao and Ballard, 1999; Friston, 2005; Clark et al., 2013; Hohwy, 2013; Parr et al., 2022). The system generates predictions about the sensory consequences of hidden environmental states and uses them to constrain the interpretation of lower-level signals. At each level of the cortical hierarchy, predictions are propagated downwards and compared with signals conveyed from subordinate levels. Discrepancies between predicted and observed activity generate prediction error signals, which are propagated upwards and used to update beliefs represented at higher levels. This recurrent exchange supports at least two forms of model adjustment operating over different timescales. During perceptual inference, prediction errors update posterior beliefs about the latent states currently generating sensory input. Over longer timescales, accumulated prediction errors drive learning by modifying the parameters, contingencies, or structure of the generative model. Predictive coding therefore distinguishes the rapid updating of beliefs from the slower learning of the model through which those beliefs are formed, and explains both in terms of the minimization of prediction error or, more generally, of variational free energy, a quantity that measures how poorly the current beliefs explain the observations (Rao and Ballard, 1999; Friston, 2005 Friston, , 2010)).

3.1 Putative neural implementation of hierarchical predictive coding

There are multiple possible biological implementations of predictive coding (or its variants), which are under empirical scrutiny (Aizenbud et al., 2025; Hodson et al., 2024). One influential proposal links predictive coding to the laminar organization of the cerebral cortex. In canonical microcircuit models, superficial pyramidal cells send prediction errors forward to higher areas, while deep pyramidal cells send predictions back to lower areas (Bastos et al., 2012).

A related line of work emphasizes that feedforward and feedback signals also travel in partly distinct frequency bands. In the visual systems of monkeys and humans, feedforward influences are carried mainly by gamma activity, with theta contributions in some studies, while feedback influences are carried by alpha and beta activity (Bastos et al., 2015; Michalareas et al., 2016). Predictive processing accounts interpret the first channel as carrying prediction errors and the second as carrying predictions and contextual modulation.

Human fMRI provides converging evidence: regions of the early visual cortex that receive no direct stimulation still carry information about the surrounding scene (Smith and Muckli, 2010), and this contextual feedback is represented mainly in the superficial layers of V1 (Muckli et al., 2015), in line with broader accounts of how context modulates cortical processing (Phillips et al., 2015). Contextual feedback is compatible with predictive coding but could also arise from other mechanisms, and the frequency-specific organization found in the visual cortex may differ in other cortical systems.

The predictive routing framework provides an alternative to standard predictive coding, suggesting that predictions also control which information travels through the cortical hierarchy (Bastos et al., 2020; Collado et al., 2026). Alpha/beta activity in deep layers inhibits the pathways that process predicted inputs, reducing superficial-layer spiking, gamma activity, and feedforward output, whereas unpredicted inputs are less inhibited and produce stronger feedforward signals. Like predictive coding, predictive routing relies on recurrent exchanges between ascending input and descending expectations. However, in predictive routing, predicted inputs are selectively inhibited, without the explicit subtraction between separate neuronal representations of predictions and sensory inputs found in standard predictive coding.

Predictive signals are also found beyond the early sensory cortex, in higher-order representations and in prefrontal activity (Gabhart et al., 2025; Miller et al., 2018a). Working-memory contents, attentional priorities, abstract rules, learned sequences, and task representations may therefore take part in the same predictive interactions, a possibility that standard predictive coding allowed but that has rarely been tested. In this case, alpha/beta feedback would stabilize the representations relevant to the current task and suppress irrelevant processing, while gamma feedforward activity would signal information that the current task model does not explain (Lundqvist et al., 2018).

3.2 Precision and precision-weighting in predictive coding

In predictive coding formulations, the impact of prediction errors on belief updating depends on both their magnitude and their uncertainty. This uncertainty is expressed through the concept of precision, which, under commonly used Gaussian assumptions, is formally defined as the inverse variance (Friston, 2010; Feldman and Friston, 2010). A precision-weighting mechanism ensures that a prediction error assigned high precision has a relatively strong influence on beliefs, whereas an error assigned low precision has less influence and may be treated as noise or irrelevant variation. More generally, posterior inference depends on the balance between the precision of prior beliefs and that of incoming evidence.

At the neuronal level, precision has been associated with neuromodulation and the postsynaptic gain of populations encoding prediction errors (Feldman and Friston, 2010). Increasing the gain of an error-encoding population amplifies the effect of its activity on belief updating, providing a candidate mechanism through which attention, contextual relevance, and estimated sensory reliability influence cortical processing.

3.3 Active inference: from beliefs to policies

Active inference extends predictive coding from perception to action (Parr et al., 2022; Friston et al., 2017); a related perspective is offered by active predictive coding (Rao et al., 2023). Active inference emphasizes that there are two complementary ways to suppress prediction errors (or, more broadly, variational free energy) and reduce the discrepancy between prior predictions and sensations. One way corresponds to updating beliefs, as described by predictive coding. Another way consists of selecting courses of action that realize prior predictions. For example, if one expects to see an apple that is not present in their visual field (hence generating a prediction error), they may either change their mind or make a saccade towards an apple in order to make their prior predictions come true. This second way of minimizing free energy through action is the "active" part of active inference.

Active inference also extends to planning and the selection of sequences of actions (or policies). For this, each candidate policy is scored according to its expected free energy -a quantity that is distinct from variational free energy and intuitively corresponds to the free energy expected from the outcomes the policy would produce. Expected free energy considers two terms: a pragmatic term that prioritizes policies expected to reach preferred outcomes (encoded as priors over observations) and an epistemic term that prioritizes policies expected to reduce uncertainty. Policies with lower expected free energy are more likely to be selected, so that actions can serve to reach preferred outcomes (pragmatic behaviour), gather information (epistemic behaviour), or combine both. During policy selection, the expected free energy component, which represents deliberation, is balanced with other (e.g., habitual) components, and the relative priority of each of these components depends on a precision-weighting mechanism (see below).

Active inference can also be hierarchical. In deep temporal models, higher levels infer slowly changing states, such as the current task, and these inferences set the prior beliefs and preferences under which lower levels evaluate policies over shorter timescales (Friston et al., 2018; Pezzulo et al., 2018; Van de Maele et al., 2024). A higher level can thus change how a lower level selects actions without specifying the lower-level actions themselves. The account of cognitive control developed below is based on this property.

4 Cognitive control as precision optimization within hierarchical active inference

Can predictive coding and active inference theories provide a starting point for addressing cognitive control? Here, we develop the proposal that in active inference, cognitive control can be described as part of the inference performed by a hierarchical generative model (Pezzulo, 2012; Pezzulo et al., 2018; Proietti et al., 2025; Mittenbühler et al., 2026). From this perspective, cognitive control emerges within an active inference hierarchy in which abstract and temporally extended representations constrain lower-level sensory and motor processing (Kiebel et al., 2008; Pezzulo et al., 2018; Friston et al., 2018). Higher levels may represent contextual states, task rules, distal goals, and extended action sequences, whereas lower levels represent more immediate perceptual and motor states -but crucially, the mechanisms that govern inference across both levels are the same, placing cognitive control mechanisms in continuity with predictive processing theories of perception and action. Key to our proposal is the fact that cognitive control involves the regulation of two precisions. The former is the precision of higher-level beliefs about the current context and task, corresponding to goals and task sets that need to be prioritized. The latter is the precision of policies, which in active inference sets the strength of the deliberative component of action selection (related to expected free energy) (Friston et al., 2017; Parr and Friston, 2017). The core control functions identified by theories of cognitive control -goal maintenance, task switching, control allocation, conflict monitoring, and cognitive effort -emerge from the regulation of these two precisions: the first two from the precision of higher-level beliefs, the other three from the precision of policies.

4.1 Precision of higher-level beliefs, goal maintenance, and task switching

Figure 1 shows how the two kinds of precision interact within a hierarchical active inference scheme (Proietti et al., 2025). Actions produce outcomes that can be compared with their predicted consequences, and deviations between expected and observed outcomes indicate that the current beliefs or policy may be inaccurate. The higher level receives as input the belief updating of the level below (not sensory observations); it estimates the reliability and behavioural relevance of this evidence and sets the precision of lower-level beliefs and policies accordingly. A reliable and goal-relevant error would then promote substantial belief or policy revision, whereas a discrepancy attributed to noise would have little influence.

Within this framework, goal maintenance may be interpreted as the stabilization of high-level task beliefs by increasing their precision. When a task belief is assigned sufficiently high precision, it strongly constrains expectations about relevant stimuli, appropriate courses of action, and likely action outcomes. These expectations bias lower-level processing towards information consistent with the current task. This can be achieved by linking the precision of higher-level goals to the precision or gain of task-relevant sensory information. This would produce selective attention, allowing selected (task-and goal-related) evidence to exert a greater influence on inference (Feldman and Friston, 2010; Corbetta and Shulman, 2002; Kaiser et al., 2023). While in this formulation selective attention and goal maintenance are distinct -the former regulates the influence of incoming evidence, while the latter stabilizes the higher-order context within which that evidence is interpreted -they can act synergistically to increase the focus on the current task or goals.

Excessive precision of higher-level, task-and goal-related beliefs makes behaviour inflexible. Cognitive flexibility sometimes requires the opposite: lowering the precision of the current task belief when reliable observations contradict it. From this perspective, task switching involves two steps, lowering the precision of the current task-and goal-related beliefs and revising the belief about which rule is in force, and switch costs may partly reflect the time needed to complete both (Monsell, 2003; Kiesel et al., 2010; Vandierendonck et al., 2010). Because each task context specifies its own preferred outcomes and stimulus-response mappings, revising the belief about the context also changes which policies are evaluated and with what precision (see below) (Proietti et al., 2025; Pezzulo et al., 2018; Friston et al., 2018).

Balancing the stability and flexibility of higher-level task-and goal-related beliefs is a central problem for cognitive control. Excessive confidence in a current task model or habitual policy may reduce the influence of contradictory evidence, contributing to behavioural rigidity or perseveration. Conversely, insufficient confidence in task-relevant beliefs may allow irrelevant sensory information or competing policies to exert excessive influence, producing distractibility and unstable performance. Adaptive control requires task representations to remain stable enough to organize behaviour while remaining revisable when reliable evidence indicates that the current model is no longer appropriate (Altmann and Gray, 2002; Dreisbach, 2012; Hommel, 2015). Adaptive behaviour therefore depends on estimating which representations should be trusted, at which hierarchical level, and over which timescale. In this proposal, solving this problem requires only the precision-weighting that regulates belief updating in hierarchical predictive coding architectures: estimating the precision of prior beliefs relative to incoming evidence is the same problem whether the beliefs concern the current percept (in perceptual inference) or the current task and goals (in cognitive control).

4.2 Precision of policies, control allocation, and conflict monitoring

As explained above, in active inference, action selection is itself a form of inference over policies, which are scored by their expected free energy (Friston et al., 2017). In this formulation, increasing the policy precision γ increases the influence of the deliberative component (based on expected free energy) on policy selection, producing greater confidence in policies favoured by current goals and predicted outcomes. Conversely, decreasing γ reduces this goal-sensitive influence and allows the habit prior to play a comparatively greater role (Proietti et al., 2025; Friston et al., 2016; FitzGerald et al., 2015).

Control allocation can therefore be described as the capacity to reassign the relative weight of these components (e.g., deliberative versus habitual) depending on task demands. Well-learned habits, represented by a habit prior E that strongly favours frequently selected policies, allow behaviour to unfold rapidly and with limited dependence on current contextual evaluation (Friston et al., 2016; Proietti et al., 2025; Maisto et al., 2019; Dolan and Dayan, 2013; Keramati et al., 2011; Hommel, 2019; Miller et al., 2018b; Dezfouli and Balleine, 2013; Daw et al., 2005; Schwöbel et al., 2021). Deliberative control is more context-sensitive and becomes important when habitual policies conflict with current goals, when environmental contingencies are uncertain, or when multiple courses of action remain plausible. Under these conditions, the expected free energy (G) component of the goal-directed policy, weighted by γ, must exert sufficient influence to overcome the habit prior E. The exertion of cognitive control therefore corresponds to increasing policy precision γ to promote deliberation over habits (Proietti et al., 2025).

Conflict monitoring can be formulated within the same framework. Traditional conflict monitoring theory defines response conflict in terms of the simultaneous activation of incompatible response tendencies (Botvinick et al., 2001; Botvinick, 2007a). Within an inferential account, such competition may correspond to uncertainty in the posterior distribution over policies or to different policies predicting incompatible actions. Conflict monitoring then corresponds to registering this uncertainty, which signals that the balance between habitual and deliberative components may need to be adjusted. The Stroop task provides a useful example of this competition. Reading a word is a highly practised mental action supported by a strong habit prior E, whereas naming its ink colour is a deliberative, context-dependent policy established by the current task instruction, which gives it a lower expected free energy G (Cohen et al., 1990; Parr et al., 2023; MacLeod and Dunbar, 1988; Butz, 2022). On incongruent trials, word meaning and ink colour support different responses. Successful performance requires context-sensitive beliefs to favour the colour-naming policy strongly enough to overcome the habitual tendency to read the word. Failure can occur when the habitual policy prior dominates posterior policy inference or when current goals and preferences exert insufficient influence on policy selection.

As illustrated in Figure 2, the same incongruent stimulus can produce different behavioural outcomes depending on the relative influence of habitual versus deliberative components of policy evaluation (weighted by the policy precision). In Figure 2B, the habitual word-reading policy dominates posterior inference, leading to an incorrect response. If internal performance monitoring or external feedback provides evidence that the selected response fails to satisfy the task requirements, this evidence can update beliefs about the current policy and the control configuration responsible for its selection. In Figure 2C, a subsequent adjustment of control increases policy precision γ, which strengthens the influence of the expected free energy G relative to the habit prior. This controlled adjustment increases the posterior probability of the colour-naming policy and makes the task-appropriate response more likely. The sequence depicted in Figure 2 resembles the loop proposed by conflict monitoring theory, in which detected conflict recruits additional control on subsequent trials (Botvinick et al., 2001; Kerns et al., 2004). The precision account nevertheless differs from it in at least two respects. First, the evidence that drives adjustment is weighted by its estimated reliability: the same error should produce a substantial adjustment when it is attributed to an inadequate policy, but little adjustment when it is attributed to noise. Second, the cost of control need not be postulated as a separate quantity weighed against its benefits: it is the cost of raising policy precision γ, which follows from the same inference (Proietti et al., 2025; Zénon et al., 2019; Ortega and Braun, 2013).

The adjustment in Figure 2C is reactive, since it follows an error. Hierarchical active inference also accommodates the distinction between proactive and reactive control (Proietti et al., 2025; Aarts et al., 2008; Sohn et al., 2007). Proactive control may be modelled as the anticipatory configuration of high-level beliefs and policy precision before the relevant stimulus is encountered. Informative task cues can establish the current rule, prepare relevant perceptual dimensions, and bias policy selection towards task-appropriate actions. In the Stroop task, proactive control would correspond to establishing the colour-naming context before presentation of the incongruent word. Reactive control may instead be modelled as belief and policy updating initiated when the target, conflict, surprising evidence, or feedback indicates that the current configuration is inadequate (Braver, 2012; Proietti et al., 2025; Forster et al., 2011; King et al., 2010). On this hypothesis, the two modes correspond to different temporal configurations of hierarchical inference (Mäki-Marttunen et al., 2019; Barceló, 2021). Reactive updating can alter high-level beliefs that support proactive preparation on subsequent trials, while proactive beliefs influence which observations are treated as relevant and sufficiently reliable to trigger further updating. In both modes, favouring deliberation over habits requires raising policy precision, and this adjustment has a cost, which we consider next.

4.3 Cognitive effort as precision-dependent policy reconfiguration

Overriding a habit is experienced as effortful. Effort, however, is not simply task difficulty or a generic expenditure of neural energy: it depends on the anticipated benefits and costs of control (Shenhav et al., 2013 (Shenhav et al., , 2017;; Westbrook and Braver, 2015; Kool and Botvinick, 2018), including the opportunity cost of alternative activities (Kurzban et al., 2013), and people avoid tasks that require more control when given a choice (Kool et al., 2010). In the long run, exerting cognitive control during tasks can lead to mental fatigue (Pessiglione et al., 2025).

Information-theoretic accounts relate the costs of cognition to the information that must be processed to move beliefs away from their default state (Zénon et al., 2019; Ortega and Braun, 2013). In the proposal advanced here, cognitive effort stems from the increase in policy precision γ (Proietti et al., 2025). Policy selection depends on the habit prior E and on the expected free energy G, weighted by γ. When G strongly favours the goal-directed policy, for example because its outcomes are highly preferred, that policy can be selected with a low γ and little effort, even if it departs markedly from habit. Effort arises when G alone is not sufficient to overcome E, and γ must be increased so that the expected consequences of the goal-directed policy outweigh the habit. This account also links effort to motivation. Incentives that increase the value of the goal-directed outcome increase the differences in expected free energy between policies and reduce the precision needed to override the habit, consistent with evidence that reward reduces interference and the subjective cost of control (Padmala and Pessoa, 2011; Westbrook and Braver, 2015; Kool and Botvinick, 2018; Botvinick and Braver, 2015). When preferences are weak, the same behaviour requires a larger increase in γ and is experienced as more effortful. Effort therefore depends on how much precision must be supplied, and not only on how far behaviour departs from habit (Shenhav et al., 2017).

Another way to appreciate the same idea is by describing habitual and goal-directed policies as alternative "attractor basins" within an inferential landscape (Figure 3). The habitual policy occupies a readily accessible basin because it is supported by well-established policy priors and can be selected with relatively little context-sensitive evaluation. Although this policy may provide a stable and computationally economical solution, it may be inconsistent with the agent's current preferences or task goals. A goal-directed policy is represented as an alternative basin that is more consistent with the current context but less immediately accessible because its selection requires a departure from the established habitual prior. The separation between these states is represented by a schematic barrier. In Figure 3, the barrier is the cost of moving from one basin to the other, that is, the increase in policy precision needed to leave the habitual basin. The barrier is higher when the habit prior E is strong. Once the goal-directed policy has been selected, precision must be maintained until the action is completed; otherwise, behaviour can fall back into the habitual basin (Shenhav et al., 2017; Proietti et al., 2025). The inferential landscape of Figure 3 is not fixed: the accessibility of the competing policies changes with learning, motivation, uncertainty, fatigue, and task context (Iodice et al., 2017; Pessiglione et al., 2025). Repeated practice can strengthen the prior probability of a policy, making its subsequent selection less dependent on prospective evaluation (Anderson, 1982). Conversely, distraction or reduced capacity to maintain goal information may increase the relative influence of habitual responding. The same cognitive operation may therefore be experienced as more or less effortful in different contexts.

5 Putative neuronal underpinnings of the proposed architecture

So far, we have discussed an active inference perspective on cognitive control in computational terms, showing that it can be described as an extension of the same inferential processes supporting perception and action in predictive coding and active inference. This proposal needs to be validated empirically, and one way to do so is by designing experiments that investigate the possible neural signatures of the proposed mechanisms, such as precision modulation, prediction error processing, and belief updating. Below, we first discuss the putative neural architecture supporting our account of cognitive control, with a focus on the frontoparietal control hierarchy. Then we discuss electrophysiological and neuromodulatory signatures that would be expected if the precision control central to our account is in play.

5.1 Precision regulation across the frontoparietal control hierarchy

Here we return to the core control functions reviewed in Section 2 and maps each of them onto a form of precision and onto the regions that could regulate it. The effect of precision depends on the quantity it weights. Precise cue evidence can support a change of rule, whereas a precise belief about the current context can protect the same rule against distraction. Policy precision, in turn, sets the influence of expected free energy on policy selection relative to the habit prior (Feldman and Friston, 2010; Pezzulo et al., 2018; Proietti et al., 2025). The account therefore explains executive functions through the interaction of a few forms of precision within the same task.

Consider a task in which a participant applies one response rule until a cue signals that another rule may apply. Before the cue, beliefs about the current rule and about the probability of a rule change determine the expected task context. The cue then provides evidence whose impact depends on its estimated reliability. The updated belief about the context constrains which actions are evaluated, and the habit prior and policy precision determine which policy is selected. Maintaining a rule, revising it, and selecting an action are therefore related but distinct operations. Confidence about the current context and expectations about its change are also distinct: a participant can be certain which rule applies now while expecting it to change soon.

Table 1 places these operations alongside the traditional constructs used in the cognitive control literature. Goal maintenance concerns the precision of contextual beliefs; task switching concerns the revision of the inferred context; control allocation concerns the balance between habitual and deliberative policies set by policy precision; conflict monitoring provides the evidence used for subsequent adjustment; and cognitive effort corresponds to the increase in policy precision needed to override the habit prior. The table also includes selective attention, which concerns the weight of incoming evidence, proactive and reactive control, which describe when these operations take place, and updating after surprise.

In the proposed anatomical mapping, the operations involved in cognitive control form a recurrent circuit. Lateral prefrontal regions represent task information at different levels of abstraction, and the frontoparietal multiple-demand network represents the information relevant to the current task (Badre and Nee, 2018; Duncan, 2010; Cole et al., 2013), maintaining contextual priors and assigning precision to lower-level information. Medial frontal regions instead monitor actions and outcomes and inform the subsequent allocation of control (Alexander and Brown, 2011; Shenhav et al., 2013); as also suggested by reinforcement meta-learning models, this monitoring might be linked to adjustments mediated by neuromodulatory systems (Silvetti et al., 2018; Vriens et al., 2025). Monitoring and implementation thus form a feedback loop: the task context determines which discrepancies are relevant, and relevant discrepancies change the context or the policy used on subsequent trials. Corticostriatal circuits contribute the habitual priors against which this context-sensitive evaluation competes (Yin and Knowlton, 2006).

5.2 Possible electrophysiological signatures of precision regulation

One way to test the proposal advanced here empirically is by considering event-related potentials (ERPs) and oscillatory activity recorded with electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG), which provide temporally resolved markers of prediction-error processing, context updating, and the coordination of control (Gómez et al., 2026; Polich, 2007; Cavanagh and Frank, 2014; Engel and Fries, 2010; Gratton et al., 2018); these markers can be related to precision weighting and model updating.

As summarized in Table 1, the core control functions may have distinct neural signatures, which would allow their contributions to be dissociated empirically. In the rule-maintenance example introduced above, the electrophysiological question is whether the detection of evidence, the revision of the context, and the adjustment of action can be separated in time. Because precision is a latent quantity, the critical test is whether manipulating the reliability of evidence changes a neural response together with the influence of that evidence on subsequent beliefs or actions (Nassar et al., 2012; Jepma et al., 2016).

The first operation is the detection of a discrepancy, and early auditory responses show that it can be separated from the revision of a task model. The mismatch negativity (MMN) reflects violations of sensory regularities, and predictive-coding models explain it through recurrent auditory-frontal interactions (Garrido et al., 2008 (Garrido et al., , 2009)); repetition and expectation have partly separable effects on these responses (Todorovic et al., 2011). In process theories of active inference, belief updating at successive levels of a hierarchical model produces MMN-like responses at lower levels and later, P3-like responses at higher levels, with amplitudes that depend on precision (Friston et al., 2017 (Friston et al., , 2018;; Da Costa et al., 2020 , 2021). An irrelevant deviation can therefore elicit an MMN while the task rule remains unchanged.

The second operation, the incorporation of evidence into the task context, is reflected in later activity. The P3 varies with event probability and task relevance and has been interpreted as contextual updating (Donchin and Coles, 1988; Polich, 2007). Computational studies relate it to probabilistic inference (Kopp and Lange, 2013; Kolossa et al., 2015), and task-switching studies relate cue-locked ERPs to preparation and reconfiguration (Karayanidis et al., 2011; Barceló, 2021). In the present account, an informative cue should revise contextual beliefs more than an unreliable one, given the same prior belief. The prediction is therefore a relation between cue-locked activity and independently estimated belief revision, with P3 amplitude following the size of the revision. Decision formation and response requirements also contribute to the P3 and need to be controlled (Twomey et al., 2015; Verleger, 2020).

The third operation, the evaluation of the selected action, is reflected in monitoring signals. The error-related negativity (ERN) and frontal midline theta are associated with errors, conflict, and behavioural adjustment (Holroyd and Coles, 2002; Yeung et al., 2004; Cavanagh and Frank, 2014; Ullsperger et al., 2014) and could signal whether the current rule or policy is still appropriate. An error can have different causes: the participant may have selected the wrong rule, applied the correct rule incorrectly, or received misleading feedback, and the present account predicts a different adjustment for each cause. Frontal activity would support precision-dependent control if it tracked this attribution and predicted the corresponding change in behaviour over and above error occurrence and response conflict.

Between these events, oscillatory activity provides measures of how the task representation is maintained and of how the regions involved communicate. Alpha/beta activity has been associated with attentional gating, maintenance, and the endogenous reactivation of task representations (Jensen and Mazaheri, 2010; Engel and Fries, 2010; Spitzer and Haegens, 2017), and theta and beta dynamics have been related to the adjustment of control within and across trials (Zavala et al., 2018; Prochnow et al., 2026). The association of feedforward signals with gamma and of feedback signals with alpha/beta comes from the visual system (Bastos et al., 2015; Michalareas et al., 2016), but more recent work within the predictive routing framework has linked this idea to frontoparietal control tasks (Bastos et al., 2020).

Together, these measures can test a sequence of operations: preparation of the task representation, cue-dependent revision of the context, and outcome-dependent adjustment. The sequence is functional, and each operation can be expressed in different components or frequency bands depending on the task (Collado et al., 2026). Scalp EEG does not resolve cortical layers, and oscillatory power also reflects factors unrelated to precision (Donoghue et al., 2020). The most informative studies will therefore combine neural measures with model-based estimates of beliefs and choices obtained under experimentally controlled uncertainty.

5.3 Neuromodulatory control of precision

Precision modulation has been extensively linked to neuromodulation, too. Within active inference, it is common to associate different forms of precision with distinct ascending neuromodulatory systems (Parr and Friston, 2017). This is in line with earlier proposals that neuromodulators have a broad effect on the brain's adaptive dynamics (Shine, 2023; Aston-Jones and Cohen, 2005; Silvetti et al., 2018) and set the meta-parameters of learning and choice (Doya, 2002).

Policy precision has usually been linked to dopamine: dopaminergic signals would report the confidence with which a policy can be selected, so that the balance between the habit prior and expected free energy depends on the dopaminergic state (Friston et al., 2012 (Friston et al., , 2014;; FitzGerald et al., 2015; Schwartenbeck et al., 2015). This proposal is consistent with the role of dopamine in balancing the stability and flexibility of task representations (Cools and D'Esposito, 2011; Cools, 2016; Iodice et al., 2017) and in the willingness to invest cognitive effort (Westbrook and Braver, 2016; Westbrook et al., 2020), which in the present account corresponds to the cost of increasing policy precision (Proietti et al., 2025).

Noradrenaline has been related to volatility or "unexpected uncertainty", which arises when the current model of the environment may no longer be valid (Yu and Dayan, 2005). Locus coeruleus activity regulates the balance between exploiting the current task and exploring alternatives (Aston-Jones and Cohen, 2005; Bouret and Sara, 2005), and the rate at which beliefs are revised increases with environmental volatility and covaries with pupil-linked arousal and locus coeruleus signals (Behrens et al., 2007; Nassar et al., 2012; Jepma et al., 2016; Jordan and Keller, 2023). In active inference, these effects correspond to the precision of beliefs about state transitions, and locus coeruleus responses during cognitive flexibility have been modelled in these terms (Sales et al., 2019; Proietti and Pezzulo, 2026). Phasic locus coeruleus activity has also been related to the P3 (Nieuwenhuis et al., 2005), which links this system to the electrophysiological markers discussed in Section 5.2.

Acetylcholine has instead been associated with "expected uncertainty", that is, the known unreliability of cues within a stable context, and with the precision of sensory evidence (Yu and Dayan, 2005; Moran et al., 2013; Vossel et al., 2014). It is therefore a candidate substrate for the attentional gain described above, and pharmacological studies indicate that its contribution to belief updating can be separated from that of noradrenaline (Marshall et al., 2016).

In this view, part of what traditional theories call a control signal could correspond to neuromodulatory changes in precision parameters: dopamine shifts the balance between the habit prior and goal-directed evaluation, noradrenaline sets how readily the current task model is revised, and acetylcholine sets the weight given to sensory evidence. Because prefrontal regions project to the locus coeruleus and to the dopaminergic midbrain, these systems also offer a route through which the higher level in Figure 1 could adjust the precision used at lower levels. Although this mapping is simplified, since each neuromodulator acts on several targets and not on a single model parameter, it can be tested with pharmacological manipulations.

6 Discussion

Cognitive control is a hallmark of higher cognition and has been studied since the beginnings of cognitive science. Traditional theories helped identify core control functions, including goal maintenance, task switching, control allocation, conflict monitoring, and cognitive effort (and more). A central question is whether and how these functions can be understood within a common neural and computational framework, and whether the underlying principle is shared with the processes supporting action-perception loops.

We have proposed that this question can be addressed by treating control as the regulation of precision within a hierarchical generative model, which can be seen as an extension of predictive coding and active inference architectures supporting action-perception loops (Pezzulo, 2012; Pezzulo et al., 2018; Proietti et al., 2025). In this proposal, setting the precision of higher-level, task-and goal-related beliefs determines the relative influence of contextual expectations and incoming evidence. This addresses a central challenge of cognitive control: balancing the stability and flexibility of behaviour. An established task rule must be protected against irrelevant interference while remaining open to revision when circumstances change. Within the framework proposed here, precision regulation provides a candidate computational mechanism for adjusting the relative influence of goal-related representations and competing evidence. We therefore propose that estimates of reliability contribute to determining whether incoming information should be suppressed as distraction or allowed to modify the current task model -providing a candidate explanation of core control functions like goal maintenance and task switching (and possibly more, like inhibitory control).

In turn, setting policy precision determines how strongly prospective policy evaluation influences action selection relative to habitual priors. This addresses another challenge of cognitive control: ensuring an appropriate balance of deliberative and habitual control. Habitual control can be prioritized when the context is stable, but behaviour has to become deliberative again when circumstances change. Within the framework proposed here, conflict monitoring ensures that novel circumstances are not ignored and, if needed, can trigger control allocationvia the increased precision of policies that permit overriding strong habits; in turn, this exertion of control is experienced as effortful. This perspective therefore links core control functions, like control allocation, conflict monitoring, and cognitive effort, to the optimization of the precision of the policies. These operations -precision modulation at the level of beliefs and policies -are related without being interchangeable: a participant can be confident about the active rule yet select an inappropriate habitual response, or revise a rule belief while remaining uncertain about which action will best satisfy it. This distinction provides the basis for experiments that separate failures of contextual inference from failures of policy selection.

The contribution of our proposal is not to replace traditional cognitive theories, but rather to contextualize the core cognitive functions that they have identified within a common inferential architecture that is in continuity with theories of predictive coding and active inference. In our proposal, the core cognitive functions stem from a common inferential architecture, which minimizes free energy across hierarchical levels, from lower levels more related to action-perception loops (as described by theories like predictive coding and active inference) to higher levels more related to cognitive control (here conceptualized as the regulation of the predictions and precision estimates that guide internal processing towards adaptive overt behaviour). Within a hierarchical generative model, sensory causes, contextual states, task rules, and policies can all be treated as uncertain hypotheses whose influence and selection depend on both their posterior probability and estimated precision. This provides a first-principles perspective to address the neural and computational cognitive control mechanisms in continuity with those supporting action-perception loops.

While predictive processing theories have a long history in the study of perception and action, their application to understanding cognitive control is still in its infancy, and much remains to be understood at the theoretical, conceptual, and empirical levels. We consider the ideas advanced here as a possible starting point, which would need to be improved and refined in many ways -and above all, validated empirically. Because the framework relies on formal quantities such as prediction error and precision, it lends itself to quantitative tests, and the candidate neural signatures discussed above, at the functional, neurophysiological, and neuromodulatory levels, can guide this work and help refine the proposal.

To conclude, we have reviewed an account that extends principles of predictive coding and active inference, which have mostly been applied to perception and action, to also address cognitive control and executive functions. From this perspective, cognitive control emerges from the regulation of precision within a hierarchical generative model that balances habitual and goal-directed courses of action. Goal maintenance corresponds to keeping a contextual belief precise; task switching, to lowering that precision when reliable evidence signals a change of context; control allocation, to setting policy precision so that deliberation can override habits; conflict monitoring, to evaluating the reliability of evidence that the current policy is failing; and cognitive effort, to the increase in policy precision needed to override habitual priors. This reformulation preserves the main insights of traditional theories, places them within a single inferential framework, and generates empirically testable predictions about how these computations could be realized in the brain.
