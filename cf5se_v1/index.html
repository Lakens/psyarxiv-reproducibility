To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 13 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Honesty plays a vital role in children's moral development, social trust, and cooperation, making it important to identify effective ways to promote honest behavior. Although numerous strategies have been tested, findings have varied considerably across studies. It therefore remains unclear which strategies reliably reduce dishonesty, which are comparatively more effective, and what accounts for this heterogeneity. We systematically reviewed 82 experimental studies involving 11,283 children and conducted separate network meta-analyses of cheating and lying. To clarify heterogeneity across diverse strategies, we organized them by their primary proximal target into social-influence, physical-context, and internal-standard activation approaches. Six strategies reduced cheating: promises, environmental nudges, negative moral stories, moral reputation, approval-based strategies, and self-awareness. Nine reduced lying: direct observational learning, moral emotions, combined strategies, approval-based strategies, positive moral stories, selfawareness, injunctive norms, promises, and positive descriptive norms. Promise and environmental nudges ranked highest for cheating, whereas direct observational learning and moral emotions ranked highest for lying. Among five strategies examined in both networks, promises, approval-based strategies, and self-awareness reduced both forms of dishonesty, and positive moral stories were less effective for cheating than for lying. In contrast, negative moral stories significantly reduced cheating but not lying. Moderator analyses found no robust moderators for cheating, but lying-reduction effects were weaker in Western than Eastern samples.

Overall, these findings suggest that children's honesty can be shaped by targeted psychological strategies, and offers an integrative basis for understanding and refining honesty-promotion strategies in childhood.

Public Significance Statement

Promoting honesty in childhood supports moral development, social trust, and cooperation and is a priority for parents, educators, and researchers. Developmental psychologists have tested ways to encourage children to tell the truth and avoid cheating, but findings have been mixed, leaving it unclear which approaches work best. Synthesizing 82 studies, we found that asking children to promises to be honest, expressing approval of honesty, and prompting children to reflect on their behavior can reduce both lying and cheating. However, the most effective approaches differed between the two behaviors. These findings suggest that honesty promotion should be tailored to the behavior and context.

Honesty supports social trust and cooperation, helps sustain interpersonal relationships, and is widely regarded as a core moral value in children's socialization (Ettinger & Jehiel, 2021; Szekely et al., 2023). As part of efforts to promote honesty, children are commonly taught that truth-telling is morally valued. Yet dishonest behavior remains common, suggesting that knowing honesty is valued is not sufficient to ensure honest action. One reason is that honesty often arises in situations involving competing motives: individuals may recognize that lying is wrong, but dishonesty can bring immediate benefits, help avoid punishment, or conceal transgressions. Promoting honesty therefore requires more than communicating the value of truth-telling; it requires understanding how to support honest behavior at the moment moral decisions are made. Consistent with this view, research in behavioral ethics and social-contextual approaches to moral behavior suggests that moral behavior is not merely the expression of stable traits or internalized values, but is also shaped by various social-situational factors. This perspective is particularly relevant during childhood, when children are developing the cognitive and social capacities to navigate moral expectations and engage in dishonest behavior (Talwar & Crossman, 2011; Talwar & Lee, 2008). Over the past several decades, researchers have identified a range of strategies that may influence children's honest decision-making, but findings vary across studies, making it difficult to determine which strategies reliably reduce dishonest behavior, how their effects compare, and under what conditions they are most effective. The present meta-analytic review integrates this literature to evaluate the overall and relative effectiveness of honesty-promoting strategies and to clarify the conditions under which these strategies are more or less effective.

Dishonesty encompasses a range of behaviors that violate expectations of truthfulness or fairness. Among these, cheating and lying have received particular attention in prior research.

Building on Skowronek's (2025) distinction between the performance and reporting stages of unethical decision-making, cheating refers to rule violations or the pursuit of unfair advantage during task performance, whereas lying involves false reporting or denial during the reporting stage. Developmental research has examined the emergence of these behaviors and their associated factors, showing that children begin to lie or cheat as early as 2-3 years of age, with dishonest behavior increasing across the preschool years (Evans & Lee, 2013; Fu et al., 2018; Guo & Rochat, 2025). Cognitive abilities such as executive function and theory of mind have also been implicated in children's ability to engage in dishonest behavior (Sai et al., 2021; Talwar & Lee, 2008). Building on this understanding of the development and correlates of dishonesty, more recent research has increasingly focused on experimentally testing strategies designed to promote children's honesty (Evans & Talwar, 2024; Fu et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2014; Sai et al., 2020). These strategies draw on theoretical accounts emphasizing the roles of social and physical contexts and children's internal moral standards in shaping moral behavior.

Accordingly, we organize honesty-promotion strategies into three broad categories based on their primary proximal target: social-evaluative information surrounding children's decisions, the physical choice architecture of the task, and the salience of children's internal moral standards.

Importantly, these categories reflect the primary proximal target of each manipulation rather than mutually exclusive psychological mechanisms, as individual strategies may operate through multiple pathways.

Social-influence strategies aim to promote honesty by altering the social information, normative expectations, and evaluative consequences surrounding children's decisions. These strategies draw on theoretical perspectives emphasizing that children's moral behavior is shaped by what they observe others doing, what they perceive others expect, and how they anticipate being evaluated by others. Social learning theory (Bandura, 1991), for example, proposes that children acquire moral standards through observational learning, whereby they infer which behaviors are socially approved by attending to others' actions and their consequences. Within this framework, social reinforcement can further strengthen such learning by providing positive social feedback for moral responses, thereby increasing the likelihood that children adopt and maintain socially approved behaviors (Bandura & McDonald, 1963). Whereas social learning emphasizes learning from others' behavior and its consequences, social norms theory (Cialdini et al., 1990 (Cialdini et al., , 1991) ) emphasizes the behavioral guidance provided by perceived norms. Descriptive norms convey what others typically do, whereas injunctive norms convey what others approve or disapprove of. Complementing these perspectives, reputation-based accounts highlight the role of anticipated social evaluation: children may be more likely to act honestly when they believe that their reputation is at stake or that others are evaluating their behavior (Engelmann & Rapp, 2018; Fu et al., 2016). Commitment and consistency theories (Cialdini, 1984; Kiesler, 1971) provide a further pathway through which social expectations may promote honesty. Publicly pledging to act honestly may increase motivation to behave consistently with one's prior commitment and to maintain social trustworthiness; when pledges are made to another person, they may also create interpersonal expectations of trustworthiness, thereby increasing the likelihood of truth-telling (Zickfeld et al., 2024).

Building on these theoretical perspectives, empirical studies have operationalized social influence through several types of manipulations. For example, modeling-based approaches have examined both direct and indirect forms of social learning. Some studies expose children to peers' or adults' honest or dishonest behavior and its consequences (Engarhos et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2018), whereas others use moral stories, such as the story of George Washington and the cherry tree, to provide indirect models of honesty and communicate the consequences of truthful or untruthful behavior (Lee et al., 2014; Sai et al., 2025; Talwar et al., 2016). Reinforcementbased approaches have emphasized the positive emotional consequences of truth-telling, using such anticipated outcomes to strengthen honest behavior (Sai et al., 2020; Talwar et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2025). Norm-based approaches have examined whether explicit information about descriptive and injunctive norms influences children's honesty (Alempaki et al., 2021; Bucciol & Piovesan, 2011; Liu et al., 2022). Finally, reputation-focused and commitment-based manipulations seek to promote honesty by making social evaluation salient-for example, by reminding children that others may evaluate their moral character or abilities (Fu et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2017 Zhao et al., , 2018 Zhao et al., , 2024))-or by asking them to promises to be honest before the task (Evans & Lee, 2010; Heyman et al., 2021; Kanngiesser et al., 2021). Collectively, these approaches seek to promote honesty by altering the social information, normative cues, and evaluative contexts surrounding children's honesty-related decisions.

A second line of research promotes honesty by targeting features of the immediate physical environment in which decisions occur, referred to here as physical-context strategies.

These approaches are grounded in nudge theory, which proposes that subtle changes to choice architecture can systematically influence behavior without eliminating available options or relying on explicit moral instruction (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). One relevant mechanism is accessibility, or the ease with which a behavior can be enacted within a given environment, which can influence which behavioral options are most readily pursued (Cole et al., 2021; Rozin et al., 2011; Wansink et al., 2006). Building on this framework, existing studies have manipulated features of the physical environment-for example, by introducing physical barriers, increasing distance, or reducing visual access to dishonest options (Zhao et al., 2020 (Zhao et al., , 2021 (Zhao et al., , 2022)). By changing the physical affordances of the decision environment, these manipulations can make dishonest behavior less convenient, less salient, or more effortful at the moment of choice, thereby nudging children toward honesty.

In contrast to strategies that alter the social or physical context, internal-standard activation strategies target children's self-evaluation and the salience of their internal moral standards. These strategies promote honesty by increasing self-focus or otherwise making internal moral standards salient at the moment of choice, thereby encouraging children to evaluate whether their behavior is consistent with those standards. For example, self-awareness theory (Duval & Wicklund, 1972) proposes that directing attention toward the self prompts individuals to compare their current behavior with relevant standards, thereby motivating them to reduce discrepancies between their behavior and these standards. Strategies grounded in this theory enhance honesty by using mirrors, cameras, or other self-focus cues to heighten children's self-awareness and make discrepancies between their behavior and moral standards more salient.

Moral-emotion inductions represent another pathway within this category, because selfconscious emotions such as guilt, shame, and pride arise when individuals appraise their behavior in relation to internalized moral standards (Tangney et al., 2007). These emotions translate self-evaluation into internal costs and rewards: anticipated guilt or shame can make dishonesty feel internally costly, whereas anticipated pride can make honesty internally rewarding. Accordingly, experimental studies have sought to promote children's honesty by eliciting moral emotions before children had an opportunity to lie (Ma et al., 2022 (Ma et al., , 2026)).

Although these standards and emotions may themselves be shaped by socialization, the interventions in this category are distinguished by their proximal focus on children's selfevaluation at the moment of choice.

Despite the diverse theoretical foundations of these strategies, empirical evidence regarding their effectiveness remains fragmented and inconsistent. Positive moral stories have increased preschoolers' truthful admissions in some studies, whereas other studies have found no reliable effects of storytelling on children's honest behavior (Aydin, 2024; Butean et al., 2021).

Evidence for promises-based strategies is similarly mixed, with some studies reporting reduced dishonesty or increased truthful reporting following a promises to act honestly (Heyman et al., 2015; Kanngiesser et al., 2021; Lyon & Dorado, 2008; McWilliams et al., 2021; Talwar et al., 2002), whereas others have observed null effects (Bender et al., 2018; Evans et al., 2018; Evans & Talwar, 2024; Talwar et al., 2004 Talwar et al., , 2018)). Approval-based strategies likewise show heterogeneous effects: adult approval has been shown to reduce lying among children aged 4-8 years (Talwar et al., 2015), but its effectiveness may vary with age (Sai et al., 2020) and with the relational context in which approval is anticipated (Zhao et al., 2025). Finally, even strategies with relatively broader empirical support, including injunctive-norm, self-awareness, and moralevaluation strategies, have not produced uniformly positive effects across studies (Alempaki et al., 2021; Beaman et al., 1979; Evans & Talwar, 2024; Fu et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2022 Liu et al., , 2026;; Sai et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings indicate that the evidence base is not only mixed within individual strategies but also fragmented across different types of honestypromotion strategies.

This fragmented evidence base raises three related questions: whether honesty-promotion strategies produce reliable effects overall, whether their effectiveness differs across strategies, and what factors account for variation in their effects. Conventional meta-analysis can address the first question by estimating the pooled effect of honesty-promotion strategies and, when sufficient data are available, investigate the third through moderator analyses or meta-regression (Borenstein, 2009). However, conventional meta-analysis cannot directly estimate the relative effectiveness of multiple strategies when head-to-head comparisons are sparse. This limitation is particularly relevant to the present literature, in which different honesty-promotion strategies have typically been evaluated against control conditions rather than directly against one another.

Network meta-analysis extends conventional meta-analysis by integrating direct and indirect evidence within a single network, thereby enabling estimation of the relative effectiveness of multiple strategies even when some strategies have not been directly compared (Harrer et al., 2021; Lumley, 2002). Thus, a network meta-analytic approach provides a framework for simultaneously evaluating the overall effectiveness of honesty-promotion strategies and their relative effectiveness across strategy types, while also allowing the available evidence to be examined for potential sources of heterogeneity.

Accordingly, we conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis of experimental studies testing strategies designed to promote honesty and reduce dishonest behavior among children, including both lying and cheating. We aimed to estimate the effects of individual honesty-promotion strategies, compare their relative effectiveness, and examine whether these effects varied by children's age, cultural context, and experimental paradigm. The three broad strategy categories provide a conceptual framework for organizing the heterogeneous intervention literature, while preserving distinctions among the specific strategies evaluated in the network meta-analysis. This synthesis allows us to identify which strategies are most effective, determine the conditions under which their effects vary, and evaluate what these patterns may reveal about the psychological processes that support honesty in childhood.

Method Search Strategy and Information Sources

The search strategy was developed by two authors in consultation with the research team.

We conducted a systematic literature search of the Web of Science Core Collection using the following search string: child AND (lying OR lie OR cheat OR honest* OR decept* OR dishonest*) AND (reduc* OR promot*). The search was restricted to peer-reviewed articles in psychology, education, social sciences, neuroscience, economics, and multidisciplinary sciences.

The search covered records published through May 31, 2025, yielding 1,082 records. We also screened the reference lists of relevant prior studies and reviews to identify additional eligible publications not captured by the database search. The review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021).

All retrieved records were independently screened by two authors. At the initial stage, titles and abstracts were screened to identify potentially relevant records and remove duplicate records. In the second stage, two authors independently examined the full texts of potentially eligible articles against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, adjudication by the third author.

Eligibility Criteria and Data Extraction

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1) experimentally evaluated a strategy designed to promote honesty or reduce dishonest behavior; (2) included child participants younger than 18 years old ; (3) employed a between-subject design; (4) assessed dishonest behavior, including lying or cheating, using an experimental paradigm; and (5) provided sufficient information to calculate an effect size.

Using a pre-specified coding form, two authors independently extracted study-level, participant-level, and experimental characteristics from all eligible studies, including first author, publication year, study location (country), cultural context, participants' mean age and age range, experimental paradigm, type of dishonesty (lying or cheating), strategy type, and outcome data required for effect-size calculation.

The two authors independently classified the specific strategies into three predefined higher-order categories-social-influence strategies, physical-context strategies, and internalstandard activation strategies-based on each strategy's primary proximal target. Individual strategies served as the intervention nodes in the network meta-analysis, whereas the three higher-order categories provided a framework for organizing and interpreting the intervention literature. Classification decisions were informed by the descriptions and theoretical rationales reported in the original studies. Detailed coding rules are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a senior author.

Effect Size Calculation

Most of the included studies analyzed dishonest behavior as a binary outcome using logistic regression, with children's dishonest versus honest behavior as the outcome.

Accordingly, the odds ratio (OR) was selected as the primary effect-size metric. When reported, we extracted the OR and its corresponding standard error from the original analyses. When these estimates were not reported but sufficient data were available, ORs were calculated from the corresponding 2 × 2 contingency tables. The effect size was expressed as the natural logarithm of the odds ratio (log OR), with its corresponding standard error (SE) calculated from the underlying 2 × 2 contingency table. Positive log OR values indicated greater odds of honest behavior in the intervention condition relative to the comparator, whereas negative values indicated lower odds of honest behavior. A small number of studies reported continuous measures of dishonest behavior, such as the degree of over-reporting in dice-game paradigm. For these studies, group sample sizes, means, and standard deviations were extracted for each condition and used to calculate Cohen's d as a standardized between-group effect size. To place these effects on the same metric as the binary outcomes, Cohen's d values were converted to odds ratios (ORs) using established conversion procedures. The direction of the resulting effect sizes was aligned with the binary outcome analyses. Detailed formulas and variable definitions are provided in the Supplemental Material.

When a study assessed dishonest behavior using multiple measures, we selected the measure most comparable to those used in other studies, so that each comparison contributed only one effect size to the analysis. We also prespecified procedures for handling multiple and shared study arms. When a study contained multiple independent comparisons involving the same intervention-control contrast, these comparisons were combined using inverse-variance weighting. When multiple intervention arms represented the same strategy node in the network, they were combined into a single intervention arm by aggregating the corresponding participant counts. Similarly, when a single strategy was compared with multiple independent control arms, the control arms were combined before calculating the effect estimate. When different strategy nodes shared a common control group, the corresponding contrasts were retained separately and their within-study correlation was accounted for in the network meta-analysis.

Additionally, when any cell of a 2 × 2 contingency table contained a zero, we applied the Haldane-Anscombe correction by adding 0.5 to each cell (Sweeting et al., 2004) before calculating the odds ratio and its standard error. Last but not least, when studies provided incomplete numerical data, we contacted the corresponding authors to request the missing data.

When no response was received, we estimated the required values based on the information available in the published articles, taking care to minimize potential estimation errors. All studies involving estimated data were clearly flagged in the data files, and the corresponding estimation procedures were documented to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Data Analysis

Data analysis proceeded in three stages: (1) estimating the effects of individual honestypromotion strategies on children's dishonest behavior, (2) ranking these strategies according to their relative effectiveness, and (3) examining potential sources of heterogeneity in these effects.

Cheating and lying were analyzed separately as distinct outcome domains. All analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.5.2) using the metafor (Version 4.8.0), netmeta (Version 3.2.0), and gemtc (Version 1.1.0) packages.

Separate frequentist network meta-analyses (NMAs) were conducted for cheating and lying outcomes to synthesize direct and indirect evidence across multiple specific honestypromotion strategies. The frequentist framework was chosen to estimate relative treatment effects and their uncertainty using confidence intervals, and to rank strategies using P-scores, without requiring the specification of prior distributions for model parameters. The validity of each NMA was evaluated with respect to two key assumptions: transitivity and consistency (Salanti, 2012). For each outcome-specific network, the plausibility of the transitivity assumption was assessed by examining the distribution of potential effect modifiers, including children's age, cultural context, and experimental paradigm, across strategy comparisons. The consistency assumption, which concerns the coherence between direct and indirect evidence, was assessed using both global and local approaches. Global inconsistency was initially assessed using a design-decomposition test. When this test indicated significant inconsistency, a random-effects design-by-treatment interaction model was used for further assessment. Local inconsistency was evaluated using node-splitting analyses, which compared direct and indirect evidence for individual treatment comparisons. For each outcome-specific network, the network structure was visualized using a network diagram, with node size proportional to the number of participants and edge thickness proportional to the number of studies contributing to each pairwise comparison. P-scores were calculated to rank the specific strategies according to their relative effectiveness, with higher P-scores indicating greater relative effectiveness (Rücker & Schwarzer, 2015). Comparative effects across strategies were summarized using league tables.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, several study-level covariates were systematically coded, including children's mean age, experimental paradigm (TRP, SKT, POT, OP), cultural background (Western vs. Eastern), and publication year. Detailed descriptions of the experimental paradigms are provided in the Supplemental Material. The distribution of potential effect modifiers across treatment comparisons was examined descriptively to assess the plausibility of the transitivity assumption, particularly whether these covariates were comparably distributed across different strategy comparisons (Wilkie et al., 2026). For covariates deemed sufficiently balanced, multilevel random-effects meta-regression models were fitted with strategy type and each covariate entered as fixed effects to examine strategy effects while adjusting for the covariate. To investigate strategy-specific moderation, interaction terms between strategy type and each covariate were added to the corresponding meta-regression models. Joint Wald tests were used to assess whether the interaction terms were jointly equal to zero, testing whether the association between each covariate and effect size differed across strategies. Where appropriate, individual interaction coefficients were also tested using Wald tests. These analyses were considered exploratory because data were limited for some strategy-covariate combinations.

To assess the robustness of above findings, three complementary analyses were performed. First, a multilevel random-effects models was fitted to account for within-study dependencies, with specific strategy entered as a fixed effect and study included as a random effect. Second, Bayesian network meta-analyses were conducted, and the resulting relative effects and strategy rankings were compared with those from the frequentist NMAs to assess the robustness of the conclusions across statistical frameworks. Third, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially removing each study from the network to examine its influence on the estimated effects and the stability of the overall findings.

Potential publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots, where asymmetry may indicate small-study effects. Funnel plot asymmetry was formally tested using Egger's regression method (Egger et al., 1997). If evidence of asymmetry was detected, trim-and-fill analyses were performed to estimate and impute potentially missing studies, thereby adjusting for publication bias (Duval & Tweedie, 2000).

Finally, we conducted a separate multilevel random-effects meta-regression to test whether the effects of strategies included in both the cheating and lying analyses differed between the two types of dishonest behavior. The model included dishonesty type (cheating vs. lying), strategy type, and their interaction. Wald tests were used to assess the interaction between strategy type and dishonesty type, thereby testing whether the effects of the same strategy differed between cheating and lying. Although the analytical approach was similar to that used in the preceding strategy-specific moderation analyses, the research question differed.

Transparency and Openness

This review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidelines (Page et al., 2021). Neither the review protocol nor the analysis plan was preregistered. The extracted data underlying the meta-analyses, the coding manual, and the R scripts used for data analysis are provided in the Supplemental Materials to facilitate transparency and reproducibility.

Results

Study Characteristics

Figure 1 illustrates the literature search and study selection procedures. In total, 49 articles comprising 82 studies and 202 contrast-level effect sizes met the inclusion criteria. One study contributed data to both the cheating and lying analyses and was therefore counted in both categories. Of the included studies, 36 studies (66 effect sizes) examined cheating and 50 studies (136 effect sizes) examined lying. Among the lying studies, three examined prosocial rather than antisocial lying. These studies were excluded from the primary analyses to reduce conceptual heterogeneity and facilitate the interpretation of strategy effects. Consequently, the primary analyses comprised 36 studies (66 effect sizes) for cheating and 47 studies (130 effect sizes) for lying, corresponding to 82 unique studies and 196 contrast-level effect sizes overall. Analyses including the prosocial-lying studies are reported in the Supplemental Material.

The cheating analyses included 4,434 participants (mean age 5.52 years, range 1-13) whereas the lying analyses included 6,849 participants (mean age 6.45 years, range 3-16). The geographical distribution of studies differed between the two outcome domains. Cheating studies were conducted mainly in Eastern cultures-especially China (k = 31). Only a small number came from Western contexts, including the United States (k = 2), Canada (k = 1), Northern Ireland (k = 1), and Turkey (k = 1). Lying studies were geographically more diverse, with studies conducted in China (k = 22), Canada (k = 12), the United States (k = 5), India (k = 3), Singapore (k = 2), Turkey (k = 1), Italy (k = 1), as well as one cross-cultural study. The included articles were published between 1998 and 2025. Further details on the strategies, experimental paradigms, and characteristics of all study arms are summarized in the Supplemental Material.

Effectiveness of Strategies Targeting Children's Cheating Behavior

Network Structure and Assessment of Network Assumptions

The cheating studies network comprised 10 strategies, forming 11 pairs of direct comparisons and 2 closed loops (Figure 2A). Most evidence came from comparisons between individual strategies and the control condition. Environmental nudges were evaluated in 11 studies, moral reputation in 10, promises-based and approval-based strategies in 6 each, competence evaluation in 5, and self-awareness, positive moral stories, and negative moral stories in 3 each; peer comparison was examined in 2 studies. Two additional direct comparisons between active strategies were available: positive versus negative moral stories and moral reputation versus approval-based strategies. The design-decomposition test showed no significant evidence of global inconsistency, Q(2) = 2.35, p = .309. No statistically significant local inconsistency was detected (see Supplemental Material), supporting the assumption of consistency.

Overall Effects of Each Cheating-Reduction Strategy

Table 1 presents the network meta-analysis estimates for each strategy relative to the control condition. Statistical heterogeneity was low (I² = 12.8%, 95% confidence interval (CI) [0.0%, 40.9%], τ² = 0.031), and Cochran's Q test was not statistically significant, Q(38) = 43.55, p = .247. Random-effects models were nevertheless retained for subsequent analyses to accommodate potential variability across strategies, experimental paradigms, and study characteristics. Among social-influence strategies, promises showed a medium effect in reducing children's cheating behavior (OR = 3.95, 95% CI [2.31, 6.75], p < .001), whereas negative moral stories (OR = 2.94, 95% CI [1.64, 5.26], p < .001), moral reputation (OR = 2.84, 95% CI [2.00, 4.03], p < .001) and approval-based (OR = 1.97, 95% CI [1.35, 2.88], p < .001) strategies produced small but significant effects. But positive moral stories (OR = 1.24, 95% CI [0.65, 2.37], p = .509), competence evaluation (OR = 0.69, 95% CI [0.47, 1.00], p = .051) and peer comparison (OR = 0.64, 95% CI [0.35, 1.20], p = .164) showed no effective impact. Among physical-context strategies, environmental nudges produced a small but significant effect (OR = 3.02, 95% CI [2.33, 3.91], p < .001). Among internal-standard activation strategies, selfawareness (OR = 2.83, 95% CI [1.74, 4.59], p < .001) also produced small effects.

To examine the robustness of these findings to within-study dependence, we conducted an additional multilevel random-effects meta-analysis. The strategies that showed statistically significant effects in the primary network meta-analysis remained statistically significant in this analysis (see Supplemental Material).

Relative Ranking and Between-Strategy Comparisons

To examine the relative performance of all cheating-reduction strategies, we calculated Pscores to rank the strategies and conducted network-based pairwise comparisons between strategies. The P-scores for all strategies are presented in Figure 2B. Among the cheatingreduction strategies, promises achieved the highest P-score (0.913), followed by environmental nudge (0.764), negative moral stories (0.738), moral reputation (0.714) and self-awareness (0.711). Approval-based strategies (0.475) and positive moral stories (0.308) occupied intermediate positions, whereas competence evaluation (0.073) and peer comparison (0.065) ranked lowest. A Bayesian network meta-analysis yielded a broadly similar ranking pattern, with only a minor reversal in the relative ranking of environmental nudges and negative moral stories.

Detailed Bayesian results are provided in the Supplemental Material.

To complement the ranking analysis, network-estimated pairwise contrasts were examined to assess differences in effectiveness between individual strategies. The league table (Figure 2C) showed that all strategies with significant effects were more effective than competence evaluation and peer comparison. Promise was more effective than approval-based strategies and positive moral stories, while environmental nudge, negative moral stories, moral reputation and self-awareness were also more effective than positive moral stories.

Moderators of Strategy Effectiveness

To examine potential sources of heterogeneity, we fitted a series of multilevel metaregression models in which each moderator (mean age, cultural background and publication year) was entered separately as a main effect alongside strategy type. Experimental paradigm was not analyzed as a moderator because most cheating studies used the temptation-resistance paradigm, resulting in insufficient variation across paradigm categories. Across these models, none of the moderators showed a statistically significant main effect on effect sizes after adjusting for strategy type (Bs ranged from -0.61 to 0.00; see Supplemental Material). We only fitted an interaction model including mean age and strategy type to examine potential strategyspecific moderation effects. Results showed that the strategy-by-age interaction was not significant, F(8, 18) = 1.40, p = .262, suggesting no clear evidence that the effects of different strategies varied with children's age. These non-significant findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the available evidence may be insufficient to adequately assess moderation effects, highlighting the need for further studies with greater representation across moderator levels.

Publication Bias and Sensitivity Analysis

The examination of the contour-enhanced funnel plot for symmetry and the distribution of significance regions suggested a potential rightward positive publication bias (Figure 3A). This was further supported by a significant Egger's test, t(60) = 3.30, p = .002. But after applying the trim-and-fill method to adjust for potential missing studies, cheating-reduction strategies remained significantly more effective (OR = 1.65, 95% CI [1.33, 2.04], p < .001), confirming their overall effectiveness in reducing children's cheating.

To assess robustness, we conducted a leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis.

Excluding each study resulted in only minor fluctuations in heterogeneity (τ² ranging from 0.00 to 0.04), suggesting a generally stable heterogeneity structure. The LOO analyses showed that the direction and statistical significance of the pooled effects remained unchanged when individual studies were sequentially excluded, indicating that the findings were robust to the influence of individual studies. Detailed results from sensitivity analyses were reported in the Supplemental Material.

Effectiveness of Strategies Targeting Children's Lying Behavior

Network Structure and Assessment of Network Assumptions

The lying studies formed a network comprising 11 strategies, with 21 pairs of direct comparisons and 13 closed triangular loops (Figure 4A). most evidence came from comparisons between individual strategy and the control group: promises-based strategies were examined in 18 studies; positive and negative moral stories in 9 studies each; positive descriptive norms and injunctive norms in 8 studies each; self-awareness in 7 studies; negative descriptive norms in 6 studies; approval-based strategies in 4 studies; and direct observational learning, combined strategies, and moral-emotion induction in 3 studies each. In addition to these comparisons with the control group, the network included numerous direct comparisons between active strategies, providing opportunities to evaluate their relative effectiveness.

We next assessed the consistency assumption, which concerns the agreement between direct and indirect evidence. The initial design-decomposition test indicated significant global inconsistency, Q(13) = 30.33, p = .004. However, the corresponding test under the design-bytreatment interaction random-effects model was not statistically significant, Q(13) = 18.66, p = .134. Thus, evidence of global inconsistency varied across model specifications. Local inconsistency analyses further identified significant discrepancies between direct and indirect evidence for four comparisons: approval-based versus combined strategies (p = .002), approvalbased versus the control group (p = .008), combined strategies versus the control group (p = .033), and self-awareness versus the control group (p = .003). Detailed results of the nodesplitting analyses are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Overall Effects of Each Lying-Reduction Strategy

Table 2 presents the pooled effects for each strategy compared with the control group.

Assessment of between-study heterogeneity revealed substantial variability across comparisons, Q(60) = 105.76, p < .001. Accordingly, random-effects models were used for the subsequent analyses.

Among social-influence strategies, direct observational learning (OR = 6.99, 95% CI 15, 2.70], p = .009) yielded significant but small effects. No significant effects were observed for negative moral stories (OR = 1.31, 95% CI [0.89, 1.91], p = .167) or negative descriptive norm (OR = 1.25, 95% CI [0.77, 2.02], p = .367). Among the internal-standard activation category, moral emotions (OR = 5.17, 95% CI [2.62, 10.20], p < .001) showed a medium effect while self-awareness (OR = 2.33, 95% CI [1.48, 3.67], p < .001) showed small effect. No physical-context strategies were included in the lying studies. Besides, combined strategies (OR = 3.43, 95% CI [1.89, 6.22], p < .001), which simultaneously implemented two strategies, also showed significant effects in promoting honesty. To test the robustness of the findings to within-study dependency, we conducted a multilevel random-effects meta-regression.

The statistical significance of the effects remained unchanged, with all previously significant and non-significant effects retaining their respective significance status (see Supplemental Material for detailed results).

Relative Ranking and Between-Strategy Comparisons

P-scores were used to rank lying-reduction strategies and assess their relative performance (Figure 4B). Among the lying-reduction strategies, direct observational learning showed the highest P-score (0.948), followed by moral emotions (0.897). Combined strategies (0.753), approval-based strategies (0.697) and positive moral stories (0.676) exhibited comparable rankings. Several strategies, including self-awareness (0.526), injunctive norm (0.462), promises (0.386) and positive descriptive norm (0.334) fell in the middle range.

Negative moral stories (0.160) and negative descriptive norm (0.136) were ranked lowest. To assess the robustness of the relative ranking of strategies, a Bayesian network meta-analysis was further conducted. Notably, six articles were excluded from the Bayesian analysis because they reported only contrast-based outcomes (Kanngiesser et al., 2021 (Kanngiesser et al., , 2024;; Lyon et al., 2008; Sai et al., 2024; Talwar et al., 2018; Talwar et al., 2016), whereas the model required arm-level data.

Results showed that the rankings were identical across the two models. Thus, the Bayesian analysis generally supported the frequentist ranking results. Detailed Bayesian network metaanalysis results were provided in the Supplemental Material.

To compare strategies, we examined network-estimated pairwise contrasts. The league table (Figure 4C) further showed direct and indirect comparisons results among strategies. It indicated that direct observational learning and moral emotions were both significantly more effective than injunctive norm, promises, and positive descriptive norm, while direct observational learning also outperformed self-awareness. In addition, direct observational learning, moral emotions, combined strategies, approval-based strategies, and positive moral stories were all significantly more effective than negative moral stories and negative descriptive norm.

Moderators of Strategy Effectiveness

Following the same approach as in the cheating analysis, we first examined potential sources of heterogeneity by fitting separate multilevel meta-regression models for each moderator (mean age, cultural background, experimental paradigm, and publication year).

Results showed that cultural background showed a significant association with effect sizes, with weaker lying-reduction effects observed in Western compared with Eastern contexts (B = -0.33, 95% CI [-0.65, -0.01], p = .044). We then selected moderators (cultural background and mean age) with adequate representation across strategies and fitted interaction models with strategy type to examine strategy-specific moderation effects. Neither cultural background nor mean age significantly moderated the effects of strategy type, as indicated by the nonsignificant tests for cultural background, F(7, 28) = 1.22, p = .322, and mean age, F(10, 25) = 1.41, p = .232.

Detailed results of moderator analysis are provided in Supplementary Material.

Publication Bias and Sensitivity Analysis

To assess potential publication bias, the contour-enhanced funnel plot appeared broadly symmetrical, although the distribution of significance regions suggested potential bias (Figure 3B). This was further supported by a significant Egger's test, t(96) = 2.99, p = .004. However, after adjustment for potentially missing studies using the trim-and-fill method, lying-reduction strategies remained significantly more effective than the control condition (OR = 1.78, 95% CI [1.49, 2.13], p < .001), suggesting that the overall effect was robust to potential publication bias.

Leave-one-out analysis was used to assess robustness and sensitivity. Results showed only minor fluctuations in heterogeneity (τ² ranging from 0.15 to 0.19), suggesting a generally stable heterogeneity structure. The statistical significance of none of the intervention effects changed when individual studies were excluded, indicating that the estimated effects were robust to the influence of individual studies. Detailed results for the LOO analysis are reported in the Supplemental Material.

Differences in Strategy Effects Between Cheating and Lying

Among the strategies examined across the two meta-analyses, only five had been investigated in both cheating and lying studies: positive moral stories, negative moral stories, approval-based strategies, self-awareness, and promises. The number of studies and the effect estimates obtained from the respective network meta-analyses for these shared strategies are presented in Table 3.

To examine whether the effects of these strategies differed between cheating and lying, we conducted a multilevel random-effects meta-regression, using lying as the reference category because it was represented by a larger number of studies. The model showed no statistically significant overall difference between the two types of dishonest behavior in the effects of honesty-promoting strategies (B = 0.03, 95% CI [-0.33, 0.39], p = .859). However, the joint test of the strategy-by-behavior-type interaction terms was significant, F(4, 37) = 4.24, p = .006, indicating that the differences in strategy effects between cheating and lying varied across strategies. Follow-up tests of strategy-specific contrasts showed that positive moral stories had a significantly weaker effect on cheating than on lying, t(76) = -2.23, p = .029. The difference for promises was marginally significant, t(37) = 1.95, p = .059, whereas no statistically significant differences were detected for the other shared strategies. Detailed model results are provided in Supplementary Material.

Discussion

The present network meta-analyses provide a comparative synthesis of experimental approaches designed to promote honesty in children. Drawing on 82 studies with more than 11,000 participants in the primary analyses, we found that many strategies reduced cheating or lying relative to control conditions, indicating that children's honesty is responsive to targeted psychological approaches. Effects were generally small to moderate, although their magnitude varied across strategies and the ranking patterns differed between the cheating and lying networks. Effective approaches were represented across the three broad categories considered here: social influence, environmental structuring and internal-standard activation. Together, these findings suggest that children's honesty can be promoted through multiple approaches, while highlighting the importance of distinguishing between strategies aimed at reducing cheating and those aimed at reducing lying.

Among social-influence strategies, effectiveness differed markedly between cheating and lying. Promise and approval-based strategies showed significant effects in both networks, suggesting that explicit commitment and anticipated social evaluation may provide relatively general routes to promoting honesty (Evans & Talwar, 2024; Hertwig & Mazar, 2022). Other strategies were more outcome-specific. In the cheating network, negative moral stories, moral reputation reduced dishonesty, whereas positive moral stories, peer comparison, and competence evaluation did not. This pattern suggests that cheating during task performance may be especially responsive to cues that make negative moral consequences, or reputational concerns before or during action. In the lying network, direct observational learning showed the strongest effect, and positive moral stories, injunctive norms and positive descriptive norms also reduced lying. This pattern is consistent with social learning accounts and with evidence that positive honesty-focused stories can promote truth-telling more effectively than stories emphasizing the negative consequences of dishonesty (Bandura, 1991; Ding et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2014). By contrast, negative moral stories and negative descriptive norms did not significantly reduce lying, indicating that messages centered on dishonest behavior or its negative prevalence may be less effective for promoting confession. Together, the effectiveness of social-influence strategies may depend not only on whether social information is provided, but also on the type of social cue and its fit with the behavioral demands of cheating versus lying.

For physical-context approach, environmental nudges produced significant reductions in children's cheating in the available studies. Grounded in nudge theory, these strategies modify the immediate choice architecture by making dishonesty more effortful or less accessible, rather than by directly changing children's moral motivation (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). This mechanism may be particularly effective in childhood, when still-developing behavioral control makes children more sensitive to immediate opportunities and constraints (Steinbeis, 2018a (Steinbeis, , 2018b)). It may also explain why environmental nudges were effective in cheating contexts: cheating often occurs spontaneously during task execution, allowing changes in the immediate environment to target the moment at which dishonest behavior occurs. However, nearly all available evidence on environmental nudges comes from the same research group (e.g., Zhao et al., 2020 Zhao et al., , 2021 Zhao et al., , 2022)), limiting confidence in their generalizability. Existing studies have also focused almost exclusively on cheating, leaving unclear whether similar nudges can reduce lying at a later reporting stage (Skowronek, 2025). Future research should independently replicate these effects across laboratories and cultures and test whether environmental nudges extend from rule-based cheating during task execution to truth-telling during later reporting.

Internal-standard activation strategies showed broadly consistent evidence of effectiveness. Self-awareness reduced both cheating and lying, suggesting that self-focused cues promote honesty by making internal moral standards salient at the point of decision, consistent with objective self-awareness accounts (Duval & Wicklund, 1972; Diener & Wallbom, 1976).

Moral emotions showed one of the strongest effects in the lying network, indicating that guilt, shame or pride may support truth-telling by increasing the internal cost of dishonesty or the selfevaluative value of honesty (Tangney et al., 2007; Malti & Krettenauer, 2013). Because moralemotion strategies were represented only in the lying network, whether similar effects extend to cheating remains unclear. Taken together, internal moral standards can provide a common route to honesty across forms of dishonesty, but further research is needed to determine whether other internal-standard strategies, particularly moral-emotion approaches, can similarly promote honesty across different forms of dishonest behavior.

Ranking Patterns and Cross-strategy Differences

The ranking results provide useful but mainly descriptive evidence about the relative performance of honesty-promotion strategies. Because P-scores summarize the probability that one strategy outperforms others but do not establish statistically reliable between-strategy differences on their own, they should be interpreted alongside pairwise contrasts (Rücker & Schwarzer, 2015). For cheating, promises, environmental nudge, negative moral stories, moral reputation, and self-awareness ranked highest, suggesting that commitment, opportunity structure, reputational concern, negative moral consequences, and self-monitoring are promising targets for reducing cheating (Evans & Lee, 2010; Fu et al., 2016; Heyman et al., 2015; Piazza et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2020 Zhao et al., , 2021 Zhao et al., , 2022 Zhao et al., , 2023)). Pairwise comparisons further indicated that promises was more effective than approval-based strategies. This difference may partly reflect the distinct social cues conveyed by these strategies. Promises potentially make honesty a selfrelevant social obligation rather than simply a behavior that others approve of (Kanngiesser et al., 2017). By contrast, approval-based strategies primarily communicate the prospect of social approval or disapproval. Besides, positive moral stories were less effective than the five highestranked strategies. This may be because positive outcomes conveyed through hearing stories are less salient when children face an immediate opportunity to gain a reward through cheating.

Given the limited evidence base, further research is needed to clarify when and how positive moral stories can promote honesty. Finally, competence evaluation and peer comparison were less effective than strategies with significant effects. Although evidence for both was limited, praising children or their peers for being smart may inadvertently encourage dishonesty by making competence evaluation a salient goal.

For lying, direct observational learning and moral emotions ranked highest, followed by combined strategies, approval-based strategies, and positive moral stories. This pattern suggests that truth-telling may be especially promoted by concrete models of honesty, moral-emotional consequences, and positive social or moral exemplars, consistent with social learning and moralemotion accounts (Bandura, 1991; Tangney et al., 2007). Pairwise contrasts showed that direct observational learning and moral emotions outperformed injunctive norms, promises, and positive descriptive norms, and that direct observational learning also outperformed selfawareness. These contrasts suggest that concrete and emotionally salient approaches may provide more immediate guidance for honest behavior than strategies relying primarily on normative information, future commitments, or self-monitoring. In addition, direct observational learning, moral emotions, combined strategies, approval-based strategies, and positive moral stories were more effective than negative moral stories and negative descriptive norms. This reminds us that strategies emphasizing negative moral consequences or the prevalence of dishonesty showed comparatively limited effectiveness. Overall, the rankings identify promising strategies, but the pairwise evidence suggests that fine-grained claims about superiority among leading approaches should remain cautious.

Moderation Effects

Moderator analyses provided limited evidence that strategy effects varied systematically across study characteristics. For cheating, age group, cultural background, and publication year did not show significant main effects after adjusting for strategy type, and experimental paradigm could not be examined because most studies used the temptation-resistance paradigm.

The only strategy-specific effect was that promises was weaker among younger than older children, suggesting that explicit commitment may depend on children's developing understanding of promises, social obligations, and self-consistency. This finding was exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously.

For lying, cultural background was the only significant moderator, with weaker effects in Western than Eastern samples. This pattern may reflect the closer alignment between many honesty-promotion strategies and interdependent cultural contexts, where children's moral behavior may be especially tied to social expectations, relational obligations, and concern for others' evaluations (Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Zhao & Kushnir, 2019). Neither the cheating nor the lying analyses identified significant strategy-specific moderation effects. However, this does not necessarily indicate that the moderators had similar effects across strategies, as the findings may partly reflect the inadequate distribution of moderator variables across studies.

In addition, we conducted another multilevel meta-regression to examine whether the effects of strategies differed between cheating and lying. Approval-based strategies, self-awareness, and promises showed significant effects in both types of dishonest behavior.

Although negative moral stories showed a significant effect only in cheating studies, their effects did not differ significantly between cheating and lying. In contrast, positive moral stories showed different patterns of statistical significance across the two types of dishonest behavior, and the direct comparison indicated a significant difference in their effects. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of some strategies may depend on the type of dishonest behavior being targeted, although such differences were not evident across all shared strategies. However, the evidence base for the shared strategies was larger for lying than for cheating, which may limit the robustness of the cross-behavior comparisons and warrants caution in interpreting these findings.

Robustness of The Evidence

Robustness analyses suggested that the main conclusions were generally stable, although some caution is warranted. For both cheating and lying, funnel-plot inspection and significant Egger's tests indicated potential publication bias or small-study effects. However, trim-and-fill analyses showed that the overall effects remained significant after adjustment for potentially missing studies, suggesting that the general effectiveness of honesty-promotion strategies was not fully explained by publication bias. Leave-one-out analyses further indicated that excluding individual studies produced only minor fluctuations in heterogeneity and did not change the direction or statistical significance of the pooled effects. Thus, the overall evidence appears reasonably robust, although conclusions about specific strategies-especially those supported by fewer studies or sparse direct comparisons-should still be interpreted cautiously. We did not conduct a RoB 2.0-based sensitivity analysis (Sterne et al., 2019) because some RoB 2.0 domains are not readily applicable to behavioral experiments on children's dishonesty, and excluding studies with any risk-of-bias concerns could substantially reduce the available evidence and alter the network structure.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although our analytic approach integrated direct and indirect evidence, several caveats remain. The network structure contained relatively few closed loops, constraining the precision of indirect estimates, especially for cheating. Some local inconsistencies between direct and indirect evidence were also detected, warranting caution in interpreting comparisons involving these parts of the network. Future work should expand multi-arm designs directly comparing active strategies. Additionally, moderator variables were unevenly distributed across strategiescertain age groups or cultural contexts were represented in only a subset of strategy typeslimiting the generalizability of specific moderation findings. This uneven evidence base also underscores the need to move beyond isolated strategies and examine how complementary approaches may work together. In the lying literature, the small number of studies evaluating combined strategies showed promising effects, suggesting that addressing dishonesty across multiple dimensions simultaneously may be a fruitful direction for future research. Finally, variation in task paradigms and operational definitions of dishonesty may have contributed to residual heterogeneity despite standardized effect-size conversion. Greater harmonization of paradigms and measurement practices would enhance comparability across studies.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings show that psychological strategies can reduce children's dishonesty, but their effects are modest and heterogeneous. Effective approaches were observed across social-influence, internal-standard activation, and environmental-contextual strategies, suggesting that honesty can be promoted through multiple pathways. Some strategies generalized across cheating and lying, whereas others were outcome-specific, highlighting the need to match interventions to the form and timing of dishonesty.
