Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Empirical language research with deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) groups often includes proficiency, age of acquisition, or language background measures but excludes people's socioemotional context. Measuring socioemotional context depends on characterizing the socioemotional experiences associated with DHH language development and use. We used the biopsychosocial model of arousal regulation as a theoretical lens to explore 11 qualitative interviews with DHH participants. Using reflexive thematic analysis, we developed three characteristic themes: 1) Deafness is not a monolith, 2) Name it to tame it -Language Anxiety, and 3) Positive roots for resilient shoots. These themes show that DHH language experiences are heterogeneous, that negative experiences can represent language anxiety, and that positive experiences modulate arousal regulation. Our study highlights new socioemotional considerations for language processing research like how emotions influence language use, how motivations support language skills, and how communication strategies impact social interactions within DHH communities.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1. Background

When we explore how language is associated with emotions, these social and emotional factors can inform language use and behavior, motivation to learn languages, and the ways people use languages to connect with others. For example, positive language experiences, like those stemming from social support, encourage positive behaviors such as seeking out low-stakes opportunities to improve skills or building resilience by developing cognitive reframing strategies (Atasheneh & Izadi, 2012; Jackson, 2011; Radford et al., 2026; Toyama & Yamazaki, 2021). Conversely, negative language experiences are related to avoidant behaviors like taking fewer language classes and reducing language practice. Increased negative emotion associated with language is consistently correlated with decreased language performance and weaker skill (Argaman & Abu-Rabia, 2002; Chen & Chang, 2017; Horwitz, 2001; MacIntyre, 2017; Pizzie et al., 2026; Pizzie & Kraemer, 2019; Teimouri et al., 2019; Zhang, 2019).

Regardless of valence, emotional language experiences are particularly relevant because they might inform patterns of behavior and performance, such as who seeks out language-learning opportunities and who develops proficiency.

In hearing populations, negative language experiences have a well-established body of literature, including measuring long-term outcomes (Horwitz, 2001; for a review see Kim et al., 2025; Liu & Dong, 2022). However, negative emotional experiences connected to language have not been sufficiently explored in deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) groups. While some DHH people experience language deprivation (Hall et al., 2023; Kushalnagar et al., 2017 Kushalnagar et al., , 2020) ) and many face societal pressures to become proficient in ASL and reading and writing English (Mayer, 2012; Traxler, 2000), which could encourage negative associations, only a few, small studies examine emotion and language in DHH groups (Aldalur et al., 2021; Booth-Butterfield et al., 1991; Lee et al., 2020; Pizzie et al., 2026; for a discussion see Kim et al., 2025). For DHH people, understanding how negative experiences with language are related to their language background and development could elucidate ways to provide more supportive language environments during development.

Negative language experiences can be conceptualized as language anxiety, a negative emotional response to expressing or understanding language (for a review see Kim et al., 2025). Many language anxiety studies in hearing groups include established measures of emotional associations with language, such as reading anxiety, "foreign" or world language anxiety, second language anxiety, and writing anxiety (Horwitz, 2001; Ramirez et al., 2019). However, there is a persistent lack of similar, accessible measures and research studies to understand associations between language and emotion for DHH groups (Kim et al., 2025). Some DHH people might be at greater risk for developing language anxiety due to increased risks of language delay, deprivation, and adverse childhood communication experiences (Hall et al., 2025; Kushalnagar et al., 2017 Kushalnagar et al., , 2020)). Approximately 70-95% of DHH children are born to at least one hearing parent, and a high prevalence of those children do not receive early access to American Sign Language (ASL), a visually accessible, natural language (Humphries et al., 2012; Mitchell & Karchmer, 2004; Office of Research Support and International Affairs, 2014). Past work with DHH samples has shown that negative language experiences are associated with poorer long-term language outcomes (Aldalur & Pick, 2022; Hall et al., 2023; Kushalnagar, Paludneviciene, et al., 2020). Given prior research, this study aims to characterize DHH language experiences as arousal-regulation processes (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996; Bruce et al., 2017; Hall et al., 2025; Kushalnagar, Paludneviciene, et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2020). This paper suggests new avenues in language research by exploring DHH language experiences as arousal regulation processes, characterizing these processes through the lens of affective science, and by bringing a better understanding to how social, physiological, and cognitive components of emotions inform the relationship between language and emotions. Additionally, understanding how challenges during DHH language development may be linked with increased risk for language anxiety and decreased language proficiency will broaden language anxiety research beyond the spoken modality. By developing themes from qualitative interviews, this work demonstrates that language anxiety is a genuine, relatively common experience in DHH people and has many parallels to language anxiety in hearing people. We also explore positive language experiences and highlight those that typically coincide with protective factors, such as cognitive reframing or social support, which have been studied as language anxiety interventions in hearing groups (Atasheneh & Izadi, 2012; Kim et al., 2025; Szyszka, 2017). By exploring and characterizing the relationship between various emotional experiences and language, these findings clarify the importance of considering socioemotional factors in language development in DHH communities.

1.2. Theoretical framing

Evaluating language experiences from an affective science perspective clarifies patterns of motivation and behavior and tells us about who chooses to learn language and who can develop proficiency. The biopsychosocial model of arousal regulation is a useful theoretical model because it accounts for valence, situation-specific factors, and individual characteristics like attitude (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996). Language experiences like language anxiety are goal-relevant situations with the aim of interpersonal communication that vary by age of language acquisition, skill, exposure, and motivation (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996; Oxford & Shearin, 1994). They require integrating cognitive, physiological, and socioemotional aspects, often simultaneously.

Managing this integration is known as arousal regulation, where internal or external arousal is related to autonomic nervous system activation from a stimulus that drives attitude (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996).

In the biopsychosocial model of arousal regulation, experiences are evaluated iteratively, integrating biological, psychological, and social factors (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996). The main process for novel, goal relevant experiences first involves a situation-arousal response, in which the experience is modulated by cognitive appraisal --a subjective interpretation of the potential challenges in the situation and one's own resources to address them. The result of this initial appraisal is often strongly influenced by inter-and intra-personal factors such as self-esteem and the presence of evaluative versus supportive onlookers. It is further influenced by biological factors such as physiology and genetics. This results in an initial physiological arousal, for example, a quickened heart rate. A variety of cognitive and biological factors influence how much attention is given to these physiological responses (i.e. if someone is predisposed to anxiety, they are likely to pay more attention to their faster heart rate). The combination of those factors leads to the affective or emotional response component. This can involve behavioral and cognitive strategies to regulate the experience, such as deep breathing, defensive behaviors, or cognitive reframing (Gross, 2015). In familiar situations, a secondary process can occur, in which the situation-arousal component is quickly affiliated with a previously learned emotional response.

As an example of the main process, a hypothetical DHH person writes back and forth with others and initially feels nervous and tense -a situation-arousal response.

Then they begin to worry about how they form or construct sentences and feel their heart begin to race -a physiological response. They then respond to the writing experience with anxiety -an emotional response. Over repeated occurrences, they learn to pay more attention to their negative response and associate it with language use. This conditioned association is also known as the secondary process, where similar experiences quickly elicit the emotional response. By exploring the connection between biopsychosocial model of arousal regulation and the emotional content of interviews, we can better understand socioemotional language processing.

1.3. Research questions and assumptions

This research assumed deaf epistemologies (Young & Temple, 2014) with a social constructivist approach, where knowledge of deaf culture and ASL/English language skills were built from personal, individual and systemic experiences. This work also assumed a relativist ontology, where language was central to creating realities that were subjective and shaped by biopsychosocial constructs. These approaches assumed reflexive, co-constructed meaning between researchers and participants. The research questions (RQs) used to explore language experiences in this study are as follows: RQ1) How do DHH people conceptualize their language experiences? RQ2) What negative language experiences and responses do DHH people share? RQ3) What positive language experiences and responses do DHH people share?

We composed structured interview questions that matched our broader research questions. To explore these RQs, we conducted thematic analysis, which calls for a reflexive and iterative process of theme development from qualitative interview data (Braun & Clarke, 2021a). Thematic analysis is not one approach to data analysis, but instead a family of approaches. To capture the diversity present in the DHH communities, we used a team to do the coding and reflexive analysis. We hoped that by having multiple perspectives, we would be able to more fully capture the breadth of experience that we expected to be represented.

2. Method

We conducted qualitative interviews to elicit data about participants' language experiences and analyzed the data using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021b). We reflexively developed a simple codebook where codes, often only one word, functioned as a simple label for video narrative content. This allowed us to incorporate multiple perspectives by DHH coders and keep the data as true to original form instead of translating content into written English. The coded video narratives were then used to organically develop themes. Data collection was completed when the research team agreed upon achieving data saturation, namely including a range of participant perspective and backgrounds to ensure information redundancy (Braun & Clarke, 2019).

2.1. Participants

>Participants

2.1.1. Criteria

The participants for this study were 11 DHH adults, ages 18-60 years old with self-reported basic proficiency in both ASL and English. Participants were recruited from the local community and university, by advertisements with emails, flyers, the University's internal social networking platform, and word of mouth.

2.1.2. Informed consent process

To ensure eligibility, participants completed an online screening form in Qualtrics (Qualtrics, 2025). Participants were encouraged to communicate their language preferences and offered accommodations such as interpreters, English captioning, or the option to work with a variety of researchers (e.g., native deaf, late-deafened, hearing CODA, etc.), and written/signed communication modalities for consent and study materials. Reading material in this study was written at or below a 5 th -grade reading level to ensure language access (Hartman et al., 2019). Participants completed informed consent and a video release, and researchers were available throughout to answer questions. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Gallaudet University IRB. Participants were compensated for their time in cash or gift cards.

2.1.3. Demographics

Participants ranged from age 19 to 57 (median = 22 years old), were mostly female (54.5%; N = 9), mostly non-Hispanic or Latino/a/x (81.8% not; N = 9), had a hearing status of deaf (N = 7) or hard-of-hearing (N = 4), mostly used hearing aids (54.6%; N = 9), and had a high level of education (100% completed at least 1 year of undergraduate education; N = 9). We interviewed people with varying ages of ASL acquisition, ranging from infancy to young adulthood, to capture diverse language experiences. The modal age of ASL age of acquisition was young childhood (age 4 to 10; NA = 2).

2.2. Procedures

>Procedures

2.2.1. Qualitative interview question development

Question development for the qualitative interviews was an iterative process involving our whole research team and several DHH language consultants. Questions were developed in both English and ASL. Signing in ASL exists on a spectrum of styles due to English influence (Neidle & Nash, 2015). Questions were developed in two different styles: one in a "more ASL" style, and another in "contact" style. Interviewers were encouraged to start with contact style, which is a more "middle ground," and to adjust as needed based on the signing style of the participants. We ultimately settled on twenty questions after several rounds of discussion and editing from our whole team and feedback from language consultants. Some example questions include the following: "Growing up, what languages did you use at home?" and "Think about growing up with friends or peers. What negative emotions (ex. Lonely, awkward, uncertain) did you experience when communicating with them?" See Supplementary Interview questions 1-20 for the full list.

2.2.2. Qualitative interview data collection

Interviews were semi-structured and all participants were asked the same core set of interview questions with additional flexibility to follow-up or encourage interviewees to provide more context. Participants were given a list of questions ahead of time, and interviewers largely followed the question list. Our questions were designed to capture participants' language experiences, contexts, and the emotions associated with them. To this end, the questions were designed to be open-ended, allowing participants to provide as much relevant information as possible. Interviewers were also encouraged to ask follow-up questions to draw out participants when they gave short answers.

Interviews included approximately 20 questions conducted over roughly an hour, and participants responded with a mix of personal and broader, global experiences that described what DHH people generally experience. The length of interviews ranged from 30 to 90 minutes. After completing the interviews, interviewees were sent a 5-minute survey of demographic information. Two interviewees did not respond to the survey and did not provide demographic information.

2.2.3. ASL data analysis considerations

Because ASL does not have a written orthography, we are limited in our ability to create an ASL transcript. Instead of translating to an English transcript, which would lose some of the original data meaning, we used ELAN (ELAN, 2025), a software tool developed by the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics, that allows time-linked annotations to be added to the source ASL recording. Although Braun & Clarke (2021b)'s reflexive thematic analysis guidelines include a transcription step followed by a coding step, we chose to maintain the linguistic integrity of ASL and used a team of coders who were all fluent signers with diverse DHH perspectives to reflect the diversity of DHH communities. In doing so, our codebook consisted of simple, often one-word labels for context and emotion, such as "School" and "Home," or "Uncomfortable" and "Frustrated." Our codebook essentially replaced the transcription step and allowed for researchers to easily reference a code label and know the general content of a narrative. Analysis by way of data familiarization, theme development, and refinement, required the lead author to regularly refer to video narratives, reflexively and organically develop patterns where specific contexts and emotions were associated with each other, and finalize developed themes. Participant ASL quotes were kept in ASL video format until the final drafting of this paper, where the lead author interpreted selected quotes into written English. The coauthor team, made up of bilingual hearing and DHH researchers, verified that the final written quotes were faithful to the original ASL recordings.

2.2.4. Developing a codebook and ELAN template

Our codebook was developed in an iterative team process after conducting pilot interviews, which used the same interview questions, protocol, and deaf interviewers as used in the actual study. These pilot interviews took place ahead of data collection and helped our researchers practice data familiarization, as the team reflexively coded the pilot interviews with phrases. The codebook, created after discussions of these phraselength codes, contained two classes of often one-word codes: one for emotional content (e.g. loneliness) and one for non-emotional content (e.g. technology). Each class of code was given its own tier in the ELAN template and each researcher was given a designated tier of each class. In this sense, the final codebook applied to interview data functioned as a key for video narrative markers ahead of reflexive thematic analysis, rather than a more typical codebook function for template analysis. Interviews were coded by individual DHH researchers, then peer-checked with round-table discussions.

This process ensured some consistency in how video content was labeled, e.g. researchers would agree that a narrative about language deprivation with parents would receive a "Home" label, and scaffolded the coding learning process for newer researchers. Annotations were finalized by the lead author, who then attributed codes to either ASL or English after the team decided to explore code frequencies across each language. The lead author also developed themes from the emergent codes (see Supplementary Figures 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8for code frequency visualizations). This allowed her to provide a check on the variation that naturally occurs when using multiple researchers for the coding process. Theme development by the lead author was conducted by grouping annotations from DHH researchers into five interview question clusters: Early home and social experiences, early school experiences, current ASL experiences, current English experiences, and overall emotion and identity tied to language.

Reflexive thematic analysis sought both explicit and implicit meanings and was informed by a deductive orientation (Braun & Clarke, 2021b). The lead researcher then developed three main themes from these five clusters. Data triangulation, crystallization, and trustworthiness were considered achieved after coder peer-checks arrived at the same codebook label application, multiple participant perspectives across varied age cohorts (18-60 years old) converged on these five clusters, and developed themes were validated by deaf coauthors' lived experiences and found dependable by way of our iterative analysis.

3. Results

>Results >Theme 1: Deafness is not a monolith

3.1.1. Experiences tied to school

Across all interviews, participants reflected on their language experiences in the school environment and the language of education, which shaped expectations for accessibility and academic rigor. For many DHH people, school settings can be the first environments that provide exposure to a fully accessible, visual, signed language (Henner et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2025). This was reflected in how participants consistently described their language experiences, first by the type of school program (e.g., deaf residential or hearing school), followed by the language or code used (e.g., ASL, cued speech), and language access. Though there was a consistent pattern, this sample of 11 participants reported attending 27 different types of K-12 school programs. Notably, this diversity in programs was independent of common school transitions between elementary, middle, and high school. For some participants (i.e., Participants 50, 139, and 390), changes in schooling resulted from language access needs. These participants switched from hearing programs to mainstream signing programs or deaf schools to access ASL and for cultural immersion. Others (i.e., Participants 140 and 355) switched from signing to mainstream programs to pursue greater academic rigor, though they lamented a tradeoff between language access and community for educational opportunities. Even after all 11 participants chose to attend an ASL-signing college or university, there was high variability in when and where certain languages were used in educational contexts. Some participants felt free to choose between ASL and written or spoken English, while others felt a lack of language choice. The variability in educational contexts, language experiences, and emotional associations is not well-described by a singular, monolithic experience of deafness. Instead, the varied educational choices, accessibility, and language and intellectual needs within DHH communities reflect a more complex reality.

3.1.2. Experiences tied to home, social, and cultural contexts

Participants also used home, social, and cultural frames of reference to organize their thoughts and interpretations of language experiences and developed varying positive or negative behaviors, even if they had similar high arousal levels reflecting stress in similar contexts. Participants 32 and 221 used spoken or written English at home with their families and ASL in social, deaf-culture-friendly contexts. They reported high levels of arousal across different language contexts, but differed in their long-term interpretations, reflecting differences such as self-perceived skill and language-learning attitude. Both participants grew up with some access to sound, attended oral mainstream programs, and learned ASL as young adults. Participant 32 felt stressed by language inaccessibility at home and afraid when signing with peers perceived to have stronger signing skills. This participant learned to worry about being judged by others because of their self-perceived weaker signing skill and socialized more with people who also learned ASL later in life. They shared feeling as though they needed to know all the ASL language rules before engaging in more challenging language contexts. In contrast, Participant 221 similarly felt stressed by home language inaccessibility but was unafraid to sign with peers who were stronger signers. Participant 221 learned to accept the risk of making mistakes and socialized with signers of varying skill levels. This willingness to try different language experiences might have supported their language development and involvement in DHH communities. Participants 32 and 221's experiences cannot be characterized by a singular "deaf" experience, even though they experienced similar contexts.

Language experiences were also influenced by multicultural perspectives that supported or isolated participants. For example, Participants 267, 140, 50, and 281 were all first-or second-generation immigrants and grew up in non-signing and non-English-speaking home environments. For these participants, ASL and English were often a second/written language, learned and practiced in formal education. In these instances, language acquisition was more complex, and opportunities to gain proficiency were relative to specific people and settings, such as Spanish at home with family, English or ASL at school with peers, and English or ASL in social settings with friends, which were welcoming or isolating depending on the context. Some cultural backgrounds also held different beliefs about deafness and disability, influencing family awareness of accommodations and increasing the risk of loneliness and language deprivation (e.g., Participants 50, 140). Accordingly, given our sample's variety of multicultural perspectives and their impact on behavior, deafness is better understood as a multidimensional, intersectional experience.

3.1.3. High variability in early school communication method used, perceived language choice, and associated preference

Early education communication experiences reported by our participants were incredibly diverse in language and level of empowerment, often so varied that many of these needs could have been contradictory from one participant to the next; for example, one participant felt confident signing and preferred ASL, while another felt discouraged using ASL and wanted to avoid using it. Our sample reported using ASL, written English, spoken English, Signed Exact English, Pidgin Signed English, Simultaneous Communication (SimCom; ASL and spoken English simultaneously), and Cued Speech. Participants also reported wide variation in communication choices and preferences; some had no autonomy, while others were empowered to choose a specific school and language of education. As a group, participants reported variable access to signed language and mixed emotions relevant to signing over time.

Participants 390, 355, 139, and 50, whose caregivers supported child-led choice in early education program enrollment based on the child's personal language preference, most benefited from learning through ASL, yet all learned to sign at different ages and their feelings of arousal and appraisal changed over time toward ASL. Participants 390, 139, and 50 switched from a non-signing, mainstream school where most classmates were hearing to a signing deaf school, which initially led to increased arousal responses such as discomfort and tension when making mistakes. Later, they learned to connect positive language experiences with signing school settings and continued to choose schools that supported their ASL development. Individual access to the early school communication method, language exposure, and sense of choice often changed throughout their K-12 educational career, supporting the idea that there is no one-sizefits-all deaf experience.

Participants reported a variety of responses and emotions related to access and communication modality choices. For example, Participant 139 shared, while furrowing their brows, "if I have an option, I feel fine. If I can choose -I want to speak, I want to sign -I feel fine, unfurrows brow, that feels comfortable. When someone tries to force me [to use a language], in a situation where I don't feel comfortable, shoulders tense and rise up, and I feel like I have to codeswitch, then I feel more anxiety, more flustered, more likely to make mistakes" (Supplementary Video 1). Their visible brow furrowing and tension represent implicit negative arousal responses to the situation. Participant 139 articulated their positive responses with language flexibility and their negative experiences with language stringency. Together these clarify the influence of specific contexts and subjectivity on language choice and accessibility experiences, demonstrating that deafness is made up of heterogeneous realities.

Theme 1 Summary

The primary features in our data characterizing language and communication experiences are the variability and diversity of languages, accessibility, and socioemotional context. We observed that DHH participants described their diverse language experiences by specific physical and sociocultural contexts, separating home, school, and interpersonal interaction. By analyzing arousal regulation responses, we showed that variance is the common thread across participants' language experiences and the emotions regarding them.

3.2. Theme 2: Name it to tame it -Language Anxiety

Language anxiety is a situation-specific, negative emotional response associated with using, understanding, and expressing language, that is often driven by avoidance (Horwitz et al., 1986; Horwitz, 2001; Kim et al., 2025; MacIntyre & Gardner, 1991).

Language anxiety occurs over time: repeated negative emotional experiences with language skills can create negative associations, leading to broader patterns of avoidance and worry. Our interview questions specifically asked about negative language experiences, seeking to understand how these emotions occurred and how they could be characterized. Participants both described and displayed negative emotions, revealing main and secondary associations of arousal with language experiences. These emotions of anxiety and stress suggest that naming these experiences collectively as "language anxiety" could inform how socioemotional factors contribute to language processing and could foster empowerment through selfawareness. This can then offer an area for situation-arousal regulation and an opportunity to develop a sense of control amid uncontrollable factors (Gross, 2015; Torre & Lieberman, 2018), like recognizing the effects of anxiety, rather than just labeling oneself "bad at language." Following the idea that names have power, or that emotion labeling is beneficial to regulating them (Lieberman et al., 2007; Lieberman, 2007), naming negative emotional responses affiliated with language can be the first step to taming them and finding regulation strategies.

3.2.1. Negative emotions associated with language

Negative emotional responses like loneliness or helplessness, common in our sample, were tied to language experiences and key to understanding experiences of language anxiety. For example, Participant 281 shared, "I felt awkward in elementary [school] for a lot of reasons. Two main [reasons]: language was hard for me. Then because, raises brows, I was taken out of class for speech therapy, gestures "what?" with palm up." "I think that isolated me a little bit" (Supplementary Video 2). The challenges of spoken language inaccessibility and disrupted in-class time felt greater than Participant 281's personal capabilities could overcome, leading to negative appraisal, awkwardness, isolation, and ostracism. This participant expressed increased arousal during the interview by tensed shoulders when gesturing and descriptions of feeling awkward and alone. Implicitly, persistent awkwardness and a belief that language was hard for them were characteristic of their language anxiety. Recognition of language anxiety as an obstacle to language learning may have been empowering for this participant and helped them navigate language processing challenges.

Negative responses to language experiences are often related to broader identity-related appraisals, linking decreased signing skills or lack of access to signed language to a weakened deaf identity. Understanding how language anxiety may intersect with language, identity, and sense of belonging could be valuable for regulating negative affect and building a stronger sense of self. Participant 390, for example, sometimes felt, "not deaf enough," sharing, "looking off to side, my sign[ing] is, furrows brow strongly, wrong, or outdated. Furrows brow, that lowers my selfconfidence, I feel, gestures helplessly, that not good feeling to have" (Supplementary Video 3). Framing negative feelings toward signing and deaf identity through a language anxiety lens could address language skills and socioemotional outcomes by emotion regulation strategies. In later life, Participant 390 shared that they developed cognitive reappraisal skills through friends and support networks, learning to reframe negative language experiences by not taking them personally and by magnifying other positive experiences. Over time, they grew more confident in their deaf identity, became more comfortable in DHH communities, and gained more opportunities to improve their signing skills. Support for the benefits of labeling such experiences, language anxiety, come from existing findings in hearing groups, where emotion labeling reduced physiological arousal relevant to presentation anxiety (Niles et al., 2015). Personal benefits from adopting arousal regulation strategies can be greater when the broader source of negative emotions is named, such as language anxiety.

3.2.2. Specific to a language

Negative language experiences shared by deaf participants were often considered distinct between ASL or English and thought of as independent constructs.

As described by Participant 390, "many deaf people struggle with English, writing English, furrows brows, struggle to write or understand English. [They] so often, widens eyes, misunderstand or are, furrows brows, unclear, which can cause anxiety. And also, some people in the deaf community don't feel confident about their ASL level. Or they feel, sticks out tongue for emphasis, incompetent at ASL and feel anxious that people are secretly bored or judging their signing" (Supplementary Video 4). Others, like Participant 351, also described how their peers distinguished negative emotional responses between ASL and English through struggling to write or understand signing. These perspectives clarify that DHH participants can feel anxious about either ASL or English, and for those who feel anxiety toward both languages, that anxiety is specific to language use.

3.2.3. Increased language anxiety and decreased language proficiency

Participants' description of their anxiety demonstrated that the extent of arousal regulation, or the ability to moderate their negative emotion or arousal, depended on the language skills and self-perceived capacity to demonstrate these skills, like producing or understanding signs, reading, or writing. For example, Participant 32 described their ASL anxiety as, "anxiety about, pauses, looks away, being able to, tenses shoulders, properly express myself in that language, understand that language" (Supplementary Video 5). This participant drew on their experience as a late signer and conveyed their negative appraisal of struggling to produce signs. The pauses and tension when thinking about using ASL due to decreased sign understanding and production illustrate the association between anxiety and both perceived and measured proficiency, where weaker skills correlate with decreased proficiency and vice versa. Similarly, regarding negative responses toward English, Participant 217 and others mentioned struggling to communicate through writing due to fear of making grammatical or spelling mistakes, which kept them from opportunities to practice writing skills. Recognizing language anxiety when generating language and creating strategies to ameliorate it can relieve the anxiety and lead to improved proficiency.

3.2.4. Learned over time

Participants learned over repeated negative language experiences, without specifically naming the emotion, to increase attention and develop a strong negative interpretation with similar occurrences, thus perpetuating a sense of helplessness and negativity with language. Participant 267 described discomfort and hesitance toward asking others to slow down from past negative conversation experiences, implying an emotional quagmire that they felt stuck in. Participant 50 described a similar, repeated association; "when you don't understand what a person is saying, [if it's] very important, it increases anxiety because you miss important information. If you ask again, widens eyes, being in a group is hard -they all stare at you, narrows eyes, holds both hands with palms up" (Supplementary Video 6). Beyond the negative appraisal of missing information was increased negative arousal toward asking people to repeat themselves and anticipating repeated failure to understand or frustration on the part of the other person. The strength of this repeated negative experience was exemplified by the participant's emotional reaction: "if you ask again," was paired with a rapid physiological, emotional, and cognitive response. Behaviors like nodding as if a person understands when they do not can lead to similar negative associations and a heightened, automatic aversion to the situation (Sobue, 2025; Terui, 2012). Negative, habituated occurrences of communication breakdowns, negativity, and frustration in DHH groups are likely associated with increased language anxiety and might relate to avoidance as found in hearing groups (Argaman & Abu-Rabia, 2002; Horwitz, 2001; MacIntyre, 2017).

Long-term effects of negative emotional responses include quick elicitation of negative emotional responses that can be difficult, but not impossible, to unlearn. Some participants like Participant 221 have begun to develop cognitive strategies to overcome deep-rooted the negative emotion(s) linked to learning language. They described difficulty with disconnecting from conditioned fear and trepidation, nervously laughing to diffuse tension when reflecting on past language learning experiences. They also cognitively challenged their fear and recognized a past personal success in learning a new language. Even if a language experience elicits helplessness or anxiety, emotion naming can counter such emotions and encourage new language processing pathways.

Theme 2 Summary DHH participants described many negative language experiences. These experiences were rooted in negative emotions specific to a language, skill, and skill level, were related to decreased language skills, and were conditioned over time through repeated situations and negative outcomes, creating fear or anxiety responses that were easily triggered by attempts to use language skills or language-related situations. Being able to label these experiences, "language anxiety," can help DHH people and researchers have language to understand interacting internal and external factors, such as DHH identity, language proficiency, and available accommodations, and develop mitigation strategies.

3.3. Theme 3: Positive roots for resilient shoots

Past research has shown that some protective factors such as emotion regulation strategies reduce language anxiety, free up working memory resources, and improve language task performance in hearing samples (Kim et al., 2025). Although much of the focus has been on negative relationships with language, participants also shared many positively-valenced experiences with language. Positive emotions were often characterized by a sense of satisfaction and an improved feeling of resilience, which protected against adverse communication experiences. DHH people have significant diversity in their language experiences (Aldalur et al., 2021; Beal-Alvarez, 2014; Hall et al., 2025), yet our sample repeatedly demonstrated that positive responses and resilience modulated individual arousal regulation.

3.3.1. Sense of satisfaction

Developing a sense of satisfaction prompted participants to seek opportunities to use language. This was often rooted in having support from others and cultural or linguistic pride. For example, Participant 50, a hard-of-hearing signer who learned ASL in late elementary school, shared how a fellow hard-of-hearing peer left an indelible mark on their ASL acquisition. "They're a good person, smiles, and a mentor to me. I picked up ASL fast. No, [they didn't speak with me], they tried to convince me to use more ASL and sign. Yes, smiles and laughs, [sometimes we spoke when] needed, which was important" (Supplementary Video 7). Having a peer who could accommodate language needs in the early stages of developing ASL skills encouraged Participant 50's positive appraisal. Their peer's presence and gentle expectation to sign also led to a situational-arousal response of hope and comfort, as Participant 50 observed the rapid pace of their own language improvement after early-life language deprivation. Similarly, Participant 132 felt supported among those who could match or accommodate their signing level and foster a comfortable, non-judgmental language environment. By experiencing positive ASL experiences with peers, both participants developed contentment and personal abilities to seek out and navigate other ASL experiences. As a result, they could find and focus on positivity, groups where they felt that they belonged, and support networks to connect with in challenging experiences.

Participant 267 and Participant 32 also found positivity and satisfaction in cultural and linguistic pride. Regarding ASL, Participant 267 said, "I feel, beaming smile, big, confident signs, very positive, because ASL is a language itself, gestures "duh!" with both palms up…I feel positively about it [ASL]. I, expression relaxes, smiles contentedly, like teaching people ASL, signs bigger, nods head, a lot" (Supplementary Video 8). In contrast to Participant 50 and 132, they exhibited high arousal through gestures and larger signing, yet still positively appraised their language use. Their situation-arousal response is mixed in arousal levels and actions, reflecting both top-down and bottom-up cognitive processes when explaining and defending ASL as a natural language. Over time, they developed a strong, positive association with ASL and a desire to teach signs to others, which furthered their sense of satisfaction within DHH communities.

Participant 32 similarly found contentment within English communities, like book lovers or writers, which fostered positive language experiences through familiarity. They shared that they, "feel positive, straightens posture, when I'm, starts laughing and smiling, writing essays for school. English grammar, pauses, lightly laughs, can be so fun. I feel like, pauses, takes on an expression of conviction, I feel like, widens eyes for emphasis, I know 100% what I'm doing, like I have control. Like, the grammar rules are already set up that I grew up learning" (Supplementary Video 9). Here, they exhibited high arousal and excitement toward writing and positively appraised writing situations.

Their happy body language represented an attentive situation-arousal response and their emphasis on language knowledge implied that familiarity was a key aspect of their positive experiences. With repeated positive outcomes that encouraged greater skill and positivity, Participant 32 learned to leverage English-language experience, actively seek out writing opportunities, and develop resilience through satisfaction.

3.3.2. Protective factors that encourage resilience

Participant reports illustrated that positivity was often tied to greater resilience.

Resilience came from protective factors that countered negative language experiences: an ability to take personal interest, social support, a low-stakes opportunity, and cognitive reappraisal, an emotion regulation strategy where one rethinks an interpretation. For example, regarding writing about topics of personal interest, Participant 140 said, "I hate writing, I, rolls eyes, signs slower and more exaggeratedly, hate writing, vomit, actually loathe it. But for research I'm doing now, pauses, signs enthusiastically, I like it, signs smaller, how? I don't know why but I like it. Writing is fun, enjoyable, I'm making thoughtful ideas, processing information. Other times when I read, takes on excited, "aha!" expression, I think, oh I see! How do I express this idea?

Through that I think the more I use English, the more enjoyable it becomes, and becomes more positive" (Supplementary Video 10). For writing, this participant had a generally negative appraisal but now admits to a high arousal, positive appraisal toward writing when it is about a personally interesting topic. Their initial negative situationarousal response decreased, and they responded positively, reframing the situational context and emotional appraisal. Instead of immediately viewing writing as something to loathe, they saw it as a skill for communicating about a passion project and, when about an interesting topic, a way to change an outlook.

Another way that participants developed resilience involved reframing, often through social support and low-risk opportunities. Participant 267 shared about learning ASL, "most people are supportive, smiles. Sometimes I will feel like, gestures, "wait!" oh I'm so sorry! That's not the right sign! And people will respond, gestures, "don't worry about it!" It's fine, it's fine! And give me positivity" (Supplementary Video 11). Here, the initially negative appraisal and high arousal level after making a mistake shifted to a more forgiving emotion in response to others' support. When making mistakes, as people often do when learning a new language, this person received frequent, casual, supportive responses from others that encouraged resilience in making mistakes and continuing to sign.

Additionally, regardless of external responses, participants could still reframe their experiences to find positivity and resilience from internal resources. Participant 281 shared, "when I'm signing publicly, like over video call, sometimes I feel a little bit awkward, but I don't care, begins to sign assuredly, what other people think, starts laughing and smiles, when they stare. I don't feel the same necessarily about when I'm, pauses to consider wording, adapting to the communication [sign] preferences of another person" (Supplementary Video 12). An initial, slightly negative appraisal of stares from strangers while speaking and signing in public became more neutral by shifting attention away from emotional discomfort and toward accessible language. Over time, they positively reinforced their use of ASL despite initial associations with discomfort. Other DHH participants also used cognitive reappraisal, a strategy to change how to think about a situation, to promote personal resilience; Participant 32 learned to view variability in language skills as learning opportunities, and Participant 217 gained experience solving communication challenges. These strategies caused short-term and long-term positive language experiences, protecting against negative experiences.

Theme 3 Summary Encouraging language experiences among DHH participants were based on a fulfilled sense of satisfaction and protective factors stemming from positivity. As a result, many sought additional language opportunities to develop their language skills and resilience. Strategies to improve language skills and experiences parallel those observed in hearing groups which suggest areas for future research.

4. Discussion

This study characterized how DHH people think about their language experiences with respect to valence and found parallels to Horwitz (2001)'s past work on spoken language attitudes and Ramirez et al. (2019) and Kim et al. (2000)'s studies on resilience-encouraging behaviors. Regarding RQ1 (how do DHH people conceptualize their language experiences?), we developed Theme 1: Deafness is not a monolith. This idea dovetailed with past findings where DHH experiences with sign language ranged from self-confidence-building to dismaying, and everything in-between (Aldalur et al., 2021; Radford et al., 2026). If we are to think about comparable past research done in hearing populations, a group of writers might have had similar situational appraisals toward negative writing experiences, and diverse positive or negative reactionary behaviors (Cheng, 2002; Horwitz, 2001; MacIntyre, 2017).

Similarly, DHH and hearing speakers had more positive language experiences when multiculturalism and multilingualism were considered (Dewaele et al., 2008) and fewer when language access was deprioritized, risking harms to cognitive and socioemotional development (Aldalur et al., 2021; Emmorey, 2021; Hall, 2017; Hall et al., 2025; MacSweeney, Capek, et al., 2008; MacSweeney, Waters, et al., 2008; Twomey et al., 2020). Our work contributed to this literature by examining language experiences as arousal-regulation processes, considering the role of emotion in language processing.

With the biopsychosocial model (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996), we framed negative language experiences as language anxiety and suggested that language anxiety is genuinely experienced by DHH participants. This answered RQ2 (what negative language experiences and responses do DHH people share?) through Theme 2: Name it to tame it -Language Anxiety. For example, several participants' negative emotional responses to particular language contexts, like struggling to write in school or lipread a parent at home, align with past studies, which found such negative experiences to be prevalent in DHH communities (Hall et al., 2025; Kushalnagar et al., 2017; Kushalnagar, Paludneviciene, et al., 2020; Kushalnagar, Ryan, et al., 2020).

Recognizing language anxiety as a construct experienced by DHH samples can yield insights into how emotion influences language processing beyond the spoken modality.

Our participants also distinguished between negative language experiences in ASL and English, which aligns with past language anxiety research that focused on hearing bilinguals (MacIntyre & Gardner, 1991; Teimouri et al., 2019). Additionally, 7 of our 11 participants (including 2 participants who did not complete demographics but shared information during interviews) had ASL ages of acquisition after age 5 and discussed increased negative emotion with respect to lower language skill. This suggested that language deprivation and adverse childhood communication experiences heighten the risk for language anxiety. Although language deprivation is rare in hearing samples, it is common in DHH samples (Hall, 2017; Humphries et al., 2012; Lane, 1989). For participants who experienced language deprivation, chronic challenges to language learning were observed through conditioned arousal responses. This is consistent with longitudinal language anxiety work which found that behaviors in such emotional contexts are learned over time (Horwitz, 2001; Kim et al., 2025; Liu & Dong, 2022; Piechurska-Kuciel, 2011). Further work should explore whether experiences of language anxiety in DHH samples fully parallel those of hearing samples; unique aspects like higher prevalence of language deprivation and adverse childhood communication experiences warrant more research into long-term impacts on proficiency and well-being.

Individual differences like the age of accessible language exposure, level of language anxiety, and self-perceived proficiency will also be key to investigating factors that contribute to positive language experiences and resilience. In response to RQ3 (what positive language experiences and responses do DHH people share?), we developed Theme 3: Positive roots for resilient shoots. Participant experiences like satisfaction and positive social interactions encouraged resilience and reflected factors found to be effective against language anxiety in hearing groups (Kim et al., 2025; Toyama & Yamazaki, 2021). For example, DHH participants benefited from supportive learning environments, cognitive reappraisal techniques, and low-stakes opportunities to practice skills, which matches benefits studied in hearing groups (Atasheneh & Izadi, 2012; Kim et al., 2025). Further research can explore whether DHH groups benefit from other factors found to mitigate negative self-evaluation and foster positive language learning behaviors in hearing groups. Other factors like DHH identity and community involvement are unique to DHH communities (Jackson, 2011; Radford et al., 2026) and would be integral to investigating emotion and language processing with DHH groups.

4.1. Limitations

Our work characterized several patterns in DHH language experiences but was not without limitations. First, all 11 participants used and preferred ASL, choosing to conduct interviews in ASL with DHH interviewers; our sample did not include individuals who never learned to sign or who prefer communicating in spoken English. This could have narrowed the scope of our interpretation of the results, such as by not including the experiences of a non-signing person assimilating into hearing groups. Recruiting from a bilingual, ASL/English-signing community within a bilingual university also meant that our sample was biased toward DHH signers who were comfortable sharing their language experiences and might not be fully representative of the broader DHH experience. We tried to account for that with respect to our results by providing opportunities for interviewers with varying language backgrounds and recruiting participants with a variety of ASL age of acquisition. Regarding a separate limitation, interviews were conducted in different spaces to accommodate participant comfort and preference; data collection and video recordings were conducted from different angles and backgrounds, posing some challenges in describing facial expressions and physiological arousal.

Additionally, theme development was conducted by a hearing, native signer with auditory processing challenges. Though the lead researcher has lived experience in, a strong connection to, and empathy for DHH communities, as well as bilingual ASL/English fluency, she does not have the same lived experience as a DHH person, which may have introduced "hearing privilege" to the theme development (Bauman, 2004; Eckert & Rowley, 2013; Henner & Robinson, 2023; Humphries, 1977; Robinson & Henner, 2017). To mitigate bias and influence, many steps in our analyses prioritized the input and analytical skills of DHH researchers and empowered all to influence our results and conclusions equitably. For example, time-stamped video annotations were completed and reviewed by DHH researchers. The research team and coauthors also include DHH researchers who were empowered to provide feedback on and revise developed themes.

4.2. Conclusions

By using the biopsychosocial model of arousal regulation to analyze DHH language experiences, we developed themes that address how participants think about and create emotional associations with ASL and English (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996). This research suggested novel directions and applications, including understanding how language anxiety influences DHH language acquisition and proficiency. By collaborating with DHH communities and DHH researchers, strategies can be made to address negative emotional experiences associated with language and remediate their effects.

Examining DHH language experiences as arousal-regulation processes provided strong evidence that emotional responses, both positive and negative, play an important role in physiological, cognitive, and behavioral language-related outcomes. Our results demonstrated that many individuals who described language deprivation and adverse childhood communication experiences also reported negative emotionality tied to language, often persisting into adulthood. Further research will need to explore the relation between language anxiety, language deprivation, and adverse childhood communication experiences (Kushalnagar et al., 2017; Kushalnagar, Paludneviciene, et al., 2020; Kushalnagar, Ryan, et al., 2020), and whether these factors predispose or heighten the risk for language anxiety.

Interventions to improve language development should draw on positive language experiences described by DHH people.
