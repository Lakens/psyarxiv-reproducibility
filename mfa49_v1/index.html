What we found

We found 27 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Purpose: The present study has two aims: to identify literacy profiles based on text reading efficiency, spelling, and reading comprehension in adolescents and young adults with and without dyslexia, and to determine whether cognitive and linguistic predictors differ across profiles. Method: A total of 199 native French speakers aged 15-30 years (104 with dyslexia, 95 typical readers) completed standardized literacy, cognitive, and linguistic tests. We used kmeans clustering and multiple regression analyses. Results: Four profiles emerged: proficient and impaired (i.e., high vs. low scores in all domains), resilient (i.e., low text reading efficiency and spelling but preserved comprehension), and poor comprehender (i.e., the reverse pattern). Phonological awareness predicted text reading efficiency and spelling in most profiles, whereas vocabulary predicted reading comprehension only in resilient and proficient profiles. Conclusions: These findings suggest that literacy profiles extend beyond diagnostic categories and rely on distinct mechanisms, which calls for individualized assessments and intervention.

Educational relevance and implications statement

Our findings show that the diagnostic label of dyslexia does not capture the diversity of literacy skills in adolescents and young adults: some students with dyslexia read slowly but understand texts well, whereas some typical readers read fluently but understand texts poorly. Thus, we suggest that educators assess reading speed and accuracy, spelling, and reading comprehension separately, instead of relying on a single diagnostic category. Such profilebased assessments might help practitioners to choose the support that is adapted to each student. For example, students who read slowly but understand texts well might benefit from accommodations (e.g., text-to-speech software or extra time) and from vocabulary support, while students with poor comprehension might benefit from oral language and comprehension strategy instruction. Taken together, this approach would allow practitioners to build on each student's cognitive strengths, which might support their academic success.

Heterogeneity in literacy profiles among adolescents and young adults: a multidimensional person-centered perspective to dyslexia 1. Introduction

Developmental dyslexia (hereafter, dyslexia) is a persistent learning disorder characterized by inaccurate or slow and effortful word reading and by poor spelling (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). However, despite numerous attempts to define it, considerable controversy remains regarding its conceptualization and identification (see Elliott & Grigorenko, 2024, for a comprehensive review). A recent Delphi study by Carroll et al. (2025) defined dyslexia as "a set of processing difficulties that affect the acquisition of reading and spelling," noting that "some or all aspects of literacy attainment are weak relative to age, instruction, and other attainments," with reading comprehension recognized as a potential secondary deficit. One major source of complexity lies in the traditional reliance on fixed statistical cut-offs to establish a diagnosis. This approach is problematic because foundational reading skills (e.g., text reading efficiency) are continuously distributed, and struggling readers occupy the lower tail of this distribution (Shaywitz et al., 1992). Moreover, little is currently known about how dyslexia manifests across distinct literacy domains within the same individual. Thus, classical categorical thresholds are increasingly challenged by multi-domain, person-centered frameworks applied to transdiagnostic samples (Astle et al., 2022; Catts & Petscher, 2022). Studies grounded in this perspective (e.g., Bathelt et al., 2018; Siugzdaite et al., 2020; Vaidya et al., 2020) account for individual-level heterogeneity by identifying specific skill profiles, which allows them to move beyond the univariate thresholds implied by standard nosological definitions. In this framework, dyslexia is no longer treated as a discrete entity bounded by arbitrary cut-offs, but as a multidomain construct characterized by intraindividual profiles of strengths and weaknesses, as suggested by the Research Domain Criteria framework (RDoC; Casey et al., 2014; Cuthbert & Insel, 2013).

1.1. Literacy profiles characterized in the literature

Previous studies have described recurrent profiles in key literacy domains (i.e., text reading efficiency, spelling, and reading comprehension), which has paved the way for a description of reading ability as a spectrum, beyond a simple 'typical' versus 'dyslexic' categorization. However, most of these studies examined each literacy domain separately, and only a few studies have modeled how these skills combine within unified profiles. By unified profiles, we refer to profiles derived from the joint analysis of several literacy skills measured in the same individuals: each profile describes a specific configuration of relative strengths and weaknesses across domains (e.g., weak text reading efficiency with preserved reading comprehension), and each individual is assigned to the profile that best matches their own configuration of scores (e.g., through cluster or latent profile analysis). Investigating such profiles, which capture intra-individual variation across literacy domains, could yield a more comprehensive understanding of individual developmental pathways (e.g., Astle et al., 2022; Catts & Petscher, 2022). Indeed, considering text reading efficiency, spelling, and reading comprehension together provides a richer picture of literacy than focusing solely on text reading efficiency or word decoding. Moreover, unified profiles that identify relative strengths and weaknesses might help to uncover the specific mechanisms that give rise to them and to guide targeted interventions on these crucial literacy skills (van Viersen et al., 2017).

To our knowledge, only the Finnish longitudinal study by Torppa et al. (2007) has computed unified profiles based on several reading skills. Conducted during the first two years of reading acquisition in a transparent orthography, this study identified five distinct profiles among beginning readers: a "good reader" group (i.e., high proficiency in both word recognition and comprehension), an "average reader" group, a "poor reader" group, a "poor decoder" group (i.e., weak word recognition with average comprehension), and a "poor comprehender" group (i.e., adequate word recognition with poor comprehension). These findings show that both dissociated and non-dissociated profiles of word recognition and comprehension can be observed early in reading development. In adolescence and adulthood, non-dissociated profiles can also be expected. For example, a poor reader profile might gather individuals with clinically meaningful weaknesses in all reading skills; such a profile would mainly include individuals with dyslexia, alongside some typically developing readers (de Bree et al., 2022; Snowling, 2001). In contrast, a good reader (or proficient) profile would include individuals with strengths in all domains, as well as individuals with a history of dyslexia who achieve typical performance by late adolescence or adulthood (Cavalli, Chanoine, et al., 2024; Lefly & Pennington, 1991). Within this proficient profile, particular attention should be paid to compensated readers, that is, individuals with a history of dyslexia who attain typical reading performance despite persisting cognitive or neural weaknesses.

However, the study of Torppa et al. (2007) and the broader literature also show that some individuals present a dissociated profile. Dissociated profiles have mainly been described with respect to reading comprehension: some individuals achieve strong reading comprehension despite weak text reading efficiency. These readers are termed "resilient in reading comprehension" (hereafter, resilient RC) and have been documented in a handful of studies (Binder et al., 2012; Jackson & Doellinger, 2002; Lefèvre et al., 2025; Welcome et al., 2009 Welcome et al., , 2010)). Resilience in RC thus refers to a profile in which individuals achieve typical or strong reading comprehension despite impaired text reading efficiency or decoding. Note that compensation and resilience are distinct notions: compensation involves broad improvements in literacy, whereas resilience specifically refers to strong reading comprehension outcomes even when low-level processes remain weak (Chyl et al., 2021; Snowling, 2013). This distinction is important because reading comprehension is not a core deficit in dyslexia, but is secondarily affected by decoding or reading inefficiencies.

Finally, dissociated profiles involving spelling introduce an additional dimension of heterogeneity. Spelling difficulties are among the most persistent impairments in dyslexia, reflecting enduring difficulties with phonology-to-orthography mappings and orthographic memory (Faísca et al., 2023; Reis et al., 2020), and they sometimes diverge from reading performance. Indeed, although reading and spelling are closely linked (Treiman et al., 2025), cross-sectional and longitudinal studies have documented dissociations in both directions: some individuals show good spelling despite poor reading, whereas others show fluent reading alongside persistent spelling deficits (Fayol et al., 2009). These subgroups have been observed in various writing systems, but their stability seems to vary. For instance, Finnish studies found stable profiles of impairment restricted to reading or to spelling throughout elementary school (Torppa et al., 2017). In contrast, studies of German-speaking samples suggest that spelling-only difficulties are less stable and might resolve over time (Moll et al., 2020). More broadly, cross-language comparisons indicate that the persistence of these dissociations is influenced by orthographic transparency (Kang et al., 2016; van Viersen et al., 2015; Werth, 2018). Taken together, these findings suggest that spelling constitutes a separable dimension of literacy impairment, which calls for spelling-focused diagnostic tools and targeted interventions alongside traditional reading instruction (Kang et al., 2016).

However, characterizing literacy profiles in terms of strengths and weaknesses is only a first step: identifying the cognitive and linguistic mechanisms that underlie these profiles is equally important.

1.2. Mechanisms underlying heterogeneous literacy profiles

According to the multiple deficit model (McGrath et al., 2020; Pennington, 2006), dyslexia arises probabilistically from multiple interacting risk factors, which results in a wide spectrum of severity levels. These risk factors specifically affect promotive factors, that is, the cognitive and linguistic abilities that all readers typically use in reading development (Slomowitz et al., 2021). Phonological processing is a central promotive factor of reading acquisition, and it is at risk in dyslexia (Ehri, 2014). Indeed, individuals with dyslexia frequently show weaknesses in phonological awareness, phonological short-term memory, phonological decoding, and rapid automatized naming (RAN), and these difficulties persist well into adulthood in some of them (Hoeft et al., 2007; Melby-Lervåg et al., 2012; Stanovich & Siegel, 1994; Swanson & Hsieh, 2009; Wagner & Torgesen, 1987). Like word reading, spelling depends heavily on phonological abilities, in particular on the capacity to segment spoken words into sounds and to map them onto graphemes (Ardanouy et al., 2024; Ehri, 2014). This explains why reading and spelling proficiency are so tightly connected: accurate phonological representations are necessary both to map spoken forms onto written symbols and to consolidate orthographic representations (Olofsson et al., 2015; Snowling et al., 2003).

However, some readers with dyslexia perform better than predicted by their weak literacy precursors; these readers are termed compensated or resilient RC readers. Within this framework, compensation and resilience in RC are thought to arise from protective factors, that is, specialized mechanisms engaged specifically by individuals who experience difficulties (Slomowitz et al., 2021). Protective factors have a gap-closing function: they operate through resilience or compensation mechanisms and improve the observable literacy performance of individuals with dyslexia. With respect to the compensated profile, the interactivecompensatory model of reading (Stanovich, 1980) posits that impairments in one reading subsystem (e.g., phonological decoding) can be offset by an increased reliance on alternative knowledge sources. This view is in line with the framework proposed by Livingston and Happé (2017), who define compensation as 'the processes contributing to improved behavioural presentation of a neurodevelopmental disorder, despite persisting core deficit(s) at cognitive and/or neurobiological levels' (p. 731). At the mechanistic level, this form of adaptation is thought to involve alternative pathways that bypass deficient neurocognitive networks (Hoeft et al., 2011). In dyslexia, strong oral language skills (e.g., vocabulary, morphological awareness, grammar, and verbal working memory) act as protective buffers that support academic achievement (Cavalli et al., 2017; Law et al., 2015; van Viersen et al., 2019). Thus, individual reading profiles seem to reflect the dynamic interaction between phonological deficits and the available compensatory resources (Bishop & Snowling, 2004; Haft et al., 2016).

Moreover, neuroimaging studies suggest that compensation extends beyond behavioral strategies and involves neural reorganization and the recruitment of regions outside the canonical reading network, such as domain-general working memory and semantic systems (Cavalli, Chanoine, et al., 2024; Hoeft et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2021). These adaptations might include a functional shift toward semantic-based word processing and an atypical hemispheric organization (Gang & Siegel, 2002; Law et al., 2015), which suggests that compensation is a robust neurocognitive strategy based on neural reuse.

With respect to resilience in RC, the Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer, 1986; Hoover & Tunmer, 2018) posits that reading comprehension is the product of word decoding and listening comprehension, which explains why decoding deficits often cascade into comprehension difficulties (Hoover, 2024; Snowling et al., 2020). The Active View of Reading proposed by Duke and Cartwright (2021) extends this framework to a broader array of language structures and to crucial "bridging processes" (e.g., morphological awareness and vocabulary). This extended model might account for resilient RC readers, that is, individuals with dyslexia who achieve typical reading comprehension despite persistent word-level impairments. Indeed, longitudinal and cross-sectional studies suggest that these readers bypass decoding bottlenecks by relying on strong semantic abilities, morpho-syntactic processing, and top-down inferencing (Binder et al., 2012; De Rom & Van Reybroeck, 2024; Farris et al., 2021; Jackson, 2005; Law et al., 2015; Lefèvre et al., 2025; Welcome et al., 2011).

In adults, resilience seems to be sustained by context-sensitive strategies and by a strong vocabulary, which contribute to comprehension independently of fluency levels (Chyl et al., 2021; de Bree et al., 2022; Werth, 2018). Taken together, the dynamic interaction between promotive factors (at risk or not) and protective mechanisms might account for the diverse literacy profiles described above.

1.3. The present study

As stated above, the definition and identification of dyslexia raise two main issues: the diagnosis relies on fixed univariate cut-offs applied to skills that are continuously distributed, and dyslexia manifests differently across literacy domains from one individual to another. To address these conceptual and diagnostic challenges, the present study adopted a transdiagnostic perspective to examine the heterogeneity of written language profiles in adolescents and young adults with and without dyslexia. In line with integrative theoretical frameworks of written language (e.g., Kim, 2020) and with the RDoC framework (Casey et al., 2014; Cuthbert & Insel, 2013), we evaluated text reading efficiency, reading comprehension, and spelling simultaneously, instead of relying on single-domain, threshold-based definitions of dyslexia. In addition, we examined how these literacy profiles relate to core cognitive and linguistic abilities (i.e., phonological awareness, listening comprehension, vocabulary, and non-verbal reasoning).

The present study had two aims. The first aim was to identify distinct profiles of written language skills in this population. We expected to find a proficient profile characterized by strong literacy skills overall, comprising mainly typical readers but also compensated individuals with a history of dyslexia who have achieved age-appropriate performance. In contrast, we expected an impaired profile consisting mainly of individuals with a diagnosis of dyslexia who continue to show persistent literacy weaknesses in several domains into adulthood (Cavalli et al., 2018). Finally, we hypothesized that a dissociated profile would emerge, marked by resilience in reading comprehension, in which individuals show relatively preserved reading comprehension despite marked deficits in text reading efficiency. The second aim was to determine whether the contribution of cognitive and linguistic skills differs across the identified profiles, in order to shed light on the mechanisms that give rise to specific literacy patterns. More precisely, we hypothesized that phonological awareness would remain a foundational promotive factor of decoding (here, text reading efficiency) and spelling in all profiles. In contrast, we expected higher-order language skills (e.g., vocabulary and listening comprehension) to play a stronger role in sustaining reading comprehension when text reading efficiency is impaired.

By integrating spelling into a multidimensional framework and by examining both literacy performance and its cognitive underpinnings in adolescents and young adults (aged 15 to 30 years), that is, in an older sample than the beginning readers studied by Torppa et al. (2007), the present study extends the current literature in two ways. First, at the methodological level, it shifts the focus away from arbitrary univariate cut-offs toward a person-centered approach, in line with the RDoC framework, which makes it possible to capture intra-individual variation.

Second, at the conceptual level, the focus on adolescents and young adults allows us to examine how compensatory pathways (e.g., oral language strengths that buffer lower-level decoding deficits) operate once the reading system has matured. Thus, determining how core cognitive abilities support distinct profiles should provide a more fine-grained account of the mechanisms underlying literacy attainment in adolescence and early adulthood.

>Method

2.1. Participants

A total of 199 participants (59 males, 140 females) took part in the study: 82 high school students and 117 university students. They were aged between 15 and 30 years (M = 18 years 7 months, SD = 2 years 7 months) and were recruited through their high school or university. All participants were native French speakers with no reported history of neurological or psychological disorders, and all had normal or corrected-to-normal hearing and vision. All participants gave their consent by signing a consent form and confirmed their agreement orally. For minors, parents were informed and gave their consent for their child's participation. About half of the participants had a diagnosis of dyslexia (n = 104; hereafter, DYS) and the other half were typical readers (n = 95; hereafter, TR). To be included in the DYS group, participants had to report a diagnosis established by a speech therapist or another health professional. As shown in Table 1, the DYS group presented clear evidence supporting the validity of their diagnosis. First, they showed significantly lower text reading efficiency on the Alouette test (Lefavrais, 1965), a test that is currently used to screen for dyslexia in children (Bertrand et al., 2010) and adults (Cavalli et al., 2018). Second, their scores on the Adult Reading History Questionnaire (ARHQ; Lefly & Pennington, 2000) were significantly higher on average than those of TR, and the ARHQ confirmed 84% of the diagnoses (cutoff score = 0.47; Cavalli, Brèthes, et al., 2024; see also Supplemental analysis S3 for additional information on the ARHQ). For the purpose of the experiment, we only used the first 337 words (i.e., 17 sentences).

2.2.6. Spelling

In this computerized timed task, participants heard 80 words selected from the Lexique database (New et al., 2001) and were instructed to write each of them down accurately on a sheet of paper. They had 3 s to write each word before hearing the next one, and they were instructed to move on to the next word if they were unable to transcribe the current one.

Moreover, participants were asked not to revise the spelling of previously written words. Half of the words were consistent and the other half inconsistent in terms of spelling. The words also varied in written frequency (M = 47; SD = 100) and in length (from 3 to 8 letters; M = 6; SD = 1.35), and comprised one or two syllables. They were recorded by a native French speaker in a soundproof room. The final score was the number of correctly spelled words (maximum score = 80).

2.2.7. Non-verbal reasoning

Non-verbal reasoning was assessed with different measures in university and high school students. University students completed the Standard Progressive Matrices (Raven et al., 1995); the final score was the number of correctly completed items (maximum score = 60).

High school students completed the coloured matrix task from the WISC-V battery (Wechsler, 2016), administered in accordance with the WISC-V guidelines (i.e., the task was discontinued after three consecutive errors).

2.3. Data analysis plan

Because two different tasks adapted to the age of each sample were used to assess nonverbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning scores were converted into z-scores within each sample. All other scores were standardized across the merged samples. The effect of age was statistically controlled when it was significant, which was the case for text reading efficiency, vocabulary, reading comprehension, spelling, and phonological awareness. To identify behavioural profiles, we used an unsupervised (i.e., data-driven) machine learning algorithm, namely, k-means clustering, which partitions the data into distinct clusters.

We determined the optimal number of clusters with the R package NbClust (Charrad et al., 2014). Moreover, to overcome the sensitivity of k-means to the initial choice of centroids (Fränti & Sieranoja, 2019), we ran the algorithm 1,000 times with the kmeans.run function of the R package bios2mds (Pelé et al., 2020). Each participant was then assigned to the cluster to which they were most frequently allocated across runs.

3.1. Identification of literacy clusters

The k-means clustering analysis revealed four distinct profiles that differed in text reading efficiency, reading comprehension, and spelling (see Figure 1A for a visualization of the clustering results and Table 3 for descriptive statistics). As can be seen in Figures 1B and 1C, two profiles were homogeneous across all written abilities. Cluster 1, labelled impaired (nDYS = 39; nTR = 3), showed low scores in text reading efficiency, reading comprehension, and spelling. Cluster 2, labelled proficient (nDYS = 5; nTR = 34), showed high scores in text reading efficiency, reading comprehension, and spelling. In contrast, two profiles were heterogeneous. Cluster 3, labelled resilient RC (nDYS = 38; nTR = 10), showed low scores in text reading efficiency and spelling but high scores in reading comprehension. Cluster 4, labelled poor comprehender (nDYS = 22; nTR = 48), showed high scores in text reading efficiency and spelling but low scores in reading comprehension. The detailed distribution of the participants across samples and the comparisons on age are provided in Supplemental analyses S1. The analysis of variance (ANOVA) conducted on text reading efficiency yielded a significant effect of group (F(3, 195) = 116.90, p < .001, η² = 0.64). All pairwise comparisons were significant (Bonferroni-corrected; all ps < .001). The impaired cluster showed the lowest text reading efficiency, followed by the resilient RC cluster and the poor comprehender cluster, and the proficient cluster showed the highest text reading efficiency. Thus, text reading efficiency clearly distinguished the four profiles.

Similarly, the ANOVA conducted on spelling yielded a significant effect of group (F(3, 195) = 81.63, p < .001, η² = 0.56). All pairwise comparisons were significant (Bonferronicorrected; all ps < .01). The impaired cluster showed the lowest spelling scores, followed by the resilient RC cluster and the poor comprehender cluster, and the proficient cluster showed the highest spelling scores. Thus, spelling followed the same ordering as text reading efficiency.

The ANOVA conducted on reading comprehension yielded a significant effect of group (F(3, 195) = 131.00, p < .001, η² = 0.67). Pairwise comparisons revealed that the impaired cluster had significantly lower scores than the resilient RC cluster (t(88) = -12.28, p < .001) and the proficient cluster (t(79) = -13.85, p < .001), but did not differ from the poor comprehender cluster (t(110) = 0.02, p ≈ 1). The resilient RC cluster had higher reading comprehension scores than the poor comprehender cluster (t(116) = 13.87, p < .001), but did not differ from the proficient cluster (t(85) = -1.43, p = .92). Finally, the poor comprehender cluster had lower reading comprehension scores than the proficient cluster (t(107) = 15.44, p < .001). Thus, reading comprehension distinguished two levels of performance: the resilient RC and proficient clusters on the one hand, and the impaired and poor comprehender clusters on the other hand. The comparisons of the clusters on the cognitive and linguistic variables (i.e., listening comprehension, non-verbal reasoning, phonological awareness, and vocabulary) are provided in Supplemental analyses S2. We then examined the cognitive and linguistic factors underlying literacy performance in the four profiles. For this purpose, we conducted a series of multiple linear regression analyses to determine which cognitive and linguistic skills significantly predicted each written language outcome within each cluster.

By using a transdiagnostic sample, we sought to go beyond rigid diagnostic categories and to examine the characteristics that are shared or that differ across profiles, in line with the RDoC framework (Casey et al., 2014). We hypothesized that a proficient profile, an impaired profile, and a dissociated profile marked by resilience in reading comprehension would emerge, and that the contribution of cognitive and linguistic skills would differ across these profiles. The results can be summarized as follows. First, the cluster analysis revealed four distinct profiles based on three literacy skills (i.e., text reading efficiency, spelling, and reading comprehension): impaired readers (i.e., weaknesses in all literacy skills), resilient RC readers (i.e., weaknesses in text reading efficiency and spelling alongside strong comprehension), poor comprehenders (i.e., strengths in text reading efficiency and spelling alongside weak comprehension), and proficient readers (i.e., strengths in all skills). Thus, the three expected profiles emerged, together with a poor comprehender profile that we had not explicitly predicted. Each profile showed a specific pattern of literacy performance along two main dimensions, namely, text reading efficiency and reading comprehension. Second, the multiple regression analyses showed that the predictors of literacy outcomes varied across profiles, which suggests a differential reliance on key cognitive and linguistic factors.

4.1. Descriptive heterogeneity of readers

Two profiles identified by the cluster analysis were non-dissociated, namely, the impaired and the proficient clusters. The impaired cluster mainly consisted of individuals with dyslexia, which confirms that a substantial subset of individuals with dyslexia (36%) shows weaknesses in all written skills. Interestingly, three typical readers were also found in this cluster, which is in line with the observation that not all individuals who meet the criteria for a diagnosis are identified (see Barbiero et al., 2012). In contrast, the proficient cluster mainly comprised typical readers, with a small proportion of individuals with dyslexia (3%). The presence of individuals with dyslexia in the proficient cluster suggests that compensation has occurred, as their performance in both reading and spelling closely matched that of typical readers. Note that the proportion of compensated individuals in our study is much lower than the 20% to 25% estimated by Lefly and Pennington (1991). However, the exact prevalence of compensated individuals remains uncertain because the cognitive mechanisms underlying compensation are still poorly understood. In particular, it is still unclear whether these cognitive processes directly enhance reading proficiency in compensated individuals or are merely incidental, and whether variations in their use contribute to individual differences in compensatory abilities (see Livingston & Happé, 2017).

The cluster analysis also revealed two dissociated profiles, namely, the poor comprehender and the resilient RC clusters. The poor comprehender cluster (i.e., weak reading comprehension despite average text reading efficiency and spelling) was mainly composed of typical readers (68%), with a minority of individuals with dyslexia (32%). These findings corroborate those of Torppa et al. (2007), who observed similar dissociated profiles in elementary school children. Although these profiles might emerge early, longitudinal studies are needed to establish their stability over time. The resilient RC cluster (i.e., good reading comprehension but weak text reading efficiency) mainly included individuals with dyslexia (78%), alongside a minority of typical readers (22%); resilient readers with dyslexia represented 41% of the dyslexic sample. In line with previous studies (Binder et al., 2007; Jackson, 2005; Jackson & Doellinger, 2002), our findings suggest that resilience in reading comprehension is more frequent than compensation for reading and spelling difficulties.

Crucially, the emergence of a resilient RC profile is not predicted by the Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer, 1986; Hoover, 2024). This model conceptualizes reading comprehension as the product of decoding and oral comprehension, which implies that individuals with impaired decoding should always show lower text comprehension than individuals with typical decoding skills, regardless of their oral language strengths. However, our findings challenge this assumption: the resilient RC and proficient clusters did not differ significantly in reading comprehension, although the resilient RC cluster had significantly lower text reading efficiency. Thus, models of reading comprehension should account for the heterogeneity of reading comprehension profiles in order to inform interventions adapted to each reader.

4.2. Cognitive and linguistic mechanisms underlying the profiles

The multiple linear regression analyses showed that phonological awareness, vocabulary, and non-verbal reasoning are robust predictors of literacy outcomes, but that their influence is strongly moderated by the profiles. Phonological awareness was a nearuniversal predictor of text reading efficiency, in line with previous studies (Melby-Lervåg et al., 2012). However, its effect was reduced to a mere trend in resilient RC readers. Because the text reading efficiency scores of the resilient RC cluster were low, although significantly higher than those of the impaired cluster, we can hypothesize that the cognitive mechanism by which phonological awareness supports reading efficiency might not be active in resilient readers, whereas it seems to be active but impaired in impaired readers. This hypothesis is in line with the multiple deficit model (McGrath et al., 2020), according to which different mechanisms can cause the same reading impairment. This finding also diverges from the framework of Slomowitz et al. (2021), which assumes that promotive factors function uniformly across reader profiles.

A similar moderation by profile was observed for the relationship between phonological awareness and spelling: phonological awareness significantly predicted spelling in all profiles except the proficient one. This result suggests that spelling in proficient readers is no longer driven by phonological awareness, whereas readers in the other profiles might continue to rely heavily on strategies that are typical of early spelling acquisition. Indeed, Ardanouy et al. (2024) showed that the contribution of phonological skills to spelling is predominant at the beginning of elementary school and then tends to decrease. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify how different underlying weaknesses affect long-term spelling development.

Moreover, the expected link between vocabulary and text reading efficiency (Rose & Rouhani, 2012; Torppa et al., 2010; van Viersen et al., 2017) was only present in the nondissociated profiles (i.e., proficient and impaired). In contrast, vocabulary seems to be dissociated from text reading efficiency in poor comprehenders and resilient RC readers.

Given that children with dyslexia often show early vocabulary deficits (Torppa et al., 2010; van Viersen et al., 2017), these results call for longitudinal studies. In particular, it remains to be determined whether poor comprehenders and resilient RC readers experience an early stagnation of vocabulary that causes them to miss the critical developmental window during which vocabulary is used to build text reading efficiency. Spelling showed a similar pattern with respect to vocabulary, which might be explained by the same factors.

Finally, an unexpected negative association between spelling and non-verbal reasoning (i.e., progressive matrices) emerged only in the resilient RC profile. Although literacy outcomes are generally not related to measures of intelligence (Toffalini et al., 2017), some studies suggest that spelling difficulties might manifest differently in children with higher non-verbal abilities under specific conditions (Silverman, 1989). Nevertheless, the pairwise comparisons reported in Supplemental analyses S2 showed no difference in non-verbal reasoning between the resilient RC profile and the other profiles, which does not support the hypothesis that this group has superior non-verbal reasoning. Further studies are needed to uncover the cognitive mechanisms underlying this interaction.

The predictors of reading comprehension showed the greatest divergence across profiles. Text reading efficiency and vocabulary emerged as significant predictors, but their influence was not uniform. Interestingly, text reading efficiency positively predicted comprehension in the impaired and proficient profiles, whereas the relationship was negative in the resilient RC and poor comprehender profiles. This suggests that, in these two profiles, higher text reading efficiency does not necessarily translate into better comprehension, which might point to a competitive relationship between the two skills. These findings extend those of Brèthes et al. (2022), who showed that text reading efficiency predicts reading comprehension in typical readers but not in individuals with dyslexia. Moreover, the personcentered approach used in the present study allowed us to identify more fine-grained profiles and to isolate specific mechanisms that might otherwise remain hidden. Vocabulary also played a selective role, as it significantly predicted comprehension only in the proficient and resilient RC profiles. For the resilient RC profile, these two findings are consistent with recent results by Lefèvre et al. (2025), who showed that the discrepancy between fluency and comprehension is partially mediated by vocabulary. In contrast, the absence of a vocabulary contribution in the impaired and poor comprehender clusters is in line with the findings of Oslund et al. (2018), who reported that the predictive power of vocabulary is significantly reduced in students with poor comprehension skills. Taken together, these results challenge the predictions of the Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer, 1986), according to which reading comprehension is governed by a multiplicative relationship between decoding and linguistic comprehension, both bounded between 0 and 1. In contrast, our findings show that competitive or inverse relationships (i.e., negative coefficients) can emerge within specific literacy profiles, such as resilient RC readers and poor comprehenders. The Active View of Reading (Duke & Cartwright, 2021) offers a broader framework to account for reading comprehension achievement. In line with this model, our results suggest that vocabulary, as a key bridging process, plays an important role in supporting resilience in reading comprehension (Binder et al., 2012; De Rom & Van Reybroeck, 2024; Farris et al., 2021; Jackson, 2005; Law et al., 2015; Lefèvre et al., 2025; Welcome et al., 2011).

4.3. Limitations

One limitation of the present study is that clusters were labelled on the basis of a descriptive inspection of the profiles and of their relative performance. Indeed, scores could not be compared with a normative sample because the reading comprehension task is not psychometrically normed. However, standardized reading comprehension assessments for French-speaking adolescents and adults are scarce and are typically restricted to cloze tests or short passages, which makes them inadequate to evaluate literal and inferential processes concurrently. A second limitation concerns the predictors. Future studies could include additional predictors of spelling, such as graphotactic and morphological skills (, e.g., Ardanouy et al., 2024), as well as domain-general skills (e.g., executive functions or metacognition), which are likely to influence the acquisition of reading and spelling. A third limitation pertains to participant selection, which was based solely on the distinction between typical readers and individuals with dyslexia. However, reading and spelling impairments are also present in other conditions, such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or autism spectrum disorder. Including individuals with these diagnoses could broaden the range of observed profiles and provide a more comprehensive understanding of reading and spelling skills. Finally, the clustering analyses used in our study might not capture very rare or idiosyncratic profiles, and might thus overlook profiles that could offer valuable insights from a neuropsychological perspective.

4.4. Perspectives

The behavioural profiles identified in our study represent a first step toward characterizing the diversity of reading skills. These profiles make it possible to classify individuals on the basis of their own behavioural patterns, which provides more fine-grained information than a broad diagnosis of dyslexia. For example, individuals might show different levels of proficiency across skills, and these skills might interact differently depending on the individual's cognitive profile. Thus, our findings suggest that distinct literacy profiles might arise from specific cognitive mechanisms that unfold over development. An important question for future research is to determine when this differentiation occurs and what triggers it.

Behavioural profiles and their cognitive underpinnings should be further explored in order to design personalized educational and therapeutic interventions. Indeed, dyslexia manifests with considerable variability, which calls for a characterization of this heterogeneity at the behavioural level in order to accurately describe individual differences. Well-defined profiles should thus facilitate individualized educational adaptations. In addition, in line with the multiple deficit model (McGrath et al., 2020; Pennington, 2006), the characterization of dyslexia should include the etiological deficits that underlie the different behavioural profiles.

Finally, for individuals with reading comprehension difficulties (i.e., in the impaired and poor comprehender clusters), interventions should focus on semantic skills (Landi, 2010).

4.5. Conclusion

To conclude, the present study used a data-driven clustering approach that identified four distinct literacy profiles in adolescents and young adults. These profiles are consistent with the existing literature, but they are not confined to specific diagnoses. This porosity between diagnostic categories might stem from various factors, including resilience mechanisms that rely on the balance between individual cognitive and linguistic skills, as suggested by the multiple linear regression analyses. Further studies, in particular longitudinal ones, are needed to determine how these profiles emerge and to design interventions adapted to each of them.

To verify whether the clustering process resulted in groups with distinct age profiles, we conducted a one-way ANOVA on participant age across the four clusters. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of cluster on age in months (F(3, 195) = 4.38, p < .01). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted to determine specific differences between clusters.

As shown in the analysis, participants in the "resilient RC" (M = 231, SE = 4) and poor comprehender clusters (M = 231.0, SE = 3.59) were significantly older than those in impaired cluster (M = 210, SE = 5; t(195) = -3.27, p < .01 and t(195) = -3.69, p < .01, respectively).

Additionally, participants in poor comprehender cluster were significantly older than those in proficient cluster (M = 215, SE = 5; t(195) = -2.77, p < .05). No significant age differences were found between impairment and proficient clusters (t(195) = -0.75, p ≈ 1), proficient and resilient RC clusters (t(195) = -2.44, p = .09), or between resilient RC and poor comprehender clusters (t(195) = -0.15, p ≈ 1). The cluster comparison show that impaired and proficient clusters are in average not different from each other but in average younger than the resilient RC and poor comprehender clusters that are not significantly different from each other.

Together, the group comparisons and the distributional discrepancies suggest that the cluster analysis is not merely a reflection of developmental dynamics, but rather points to a population selection bias inherent to university enrollment.

First, the lack of significant age differences between the "impaired" and "proficient" clusters, despite an unbalanced distribution of participants from both samples (university and highschool) across these groups, suggests that age is not the primary factor determining membership in these specific clusters. A similar pattern is observed between the "resilient RC" and "poor-comprehender" clusters.

Rather than chronological age, the unbalanced distribution across these clusters likely stems from the fact that our university sample is less representative of the general population than our high school cohort. Our high school sample intentionally included vocational and technical students, demographics that are traditionally underrepresented in university settings, whereas the university sample consists primarily of students from general academic tracks. Consequently, the observed cluster patterns likely reflect the academic 'filtering' processes associated with higher education. This suggests that the clusters identify trait profiles that are differentially distributed across academic trajectories, rather than being an artifact of chronological age.

S3. Self-reported experience of reading level across the clusters as a proxy for dynamic of compensation and resilience

To study individual variations in perceived historical reading experiences and investigate subjective developmental trajectories, we analyzed the total score of the Adult Reading History Questionnaire (ARHQ). Because the ARHQ relies entirely on retrospective self-reports regarding reading acquisition, addressing recollected milestones, remembered delays in learning, perceived proficiency, and memories of difficulties in language-related classes, it serves as an index of an individual's self-reported history of reading impairment during the early stages of elementary school.

A one-way between-subjects ANOVA revealed that the mean ARHQ score differed significantly across the four data-driven clusters (F(3, 195) = 25.30, p < .001, η² = .28). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni corrections showed that the "impaired" cluster (Cluster 1) reported significantly higher ARHQ scores compared to both the "proficient" cluster (Cluster 2; t(195) = 7.70, p < .001) and the "poor comprehender" cluster (Cluster 4; t(195) = 5.12, p < .001). This confirms that individuals in the "impaired" cluster recalled experiencing substantially greater literacy difficulties during childhood than those in the "proficient" and "poor comprehender" groups. Interestingly, the difference in ARHQ scores between the "impaired" cluster and the "resilient" cluster (Cluster 3) was not statistically significant.

Conversely, the "proficient" cluster (Cluster 2) exhibited significantly lower ARHQ scores than all other groups, indicating the lowest level of self-reported historical impairment. Beyond the contrast with the "impaired" group, the "proficient" cluster scored significantly lower than the "resilient" cluster (t(195) = 6.76, p < .001) and the "poor Comprehender" cluster (t(195) = 3.57, p < .01). Finally, the "resilient" cluster scored significantly higher on the ARHQ than the "poor comprehender" cluster (t(195) = 3.98, p < .001), underscoring a heavier burden of selfreported historical reading difficulties in the "Resilient" group despite their current behavioral presentation.
