Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Full text

Abstract

Signaling mate desirability is central to a well-functioning online dating market. This metaanalysis examines how profile text signals the owner's desirability. Drawing on research on negativity and cue diagnosticity, warranting theory, and signaling theory, I test how the valence (positive vs. negative) and explicitness (explicit vs. implicit) of text-based cues shape viewers' reactions. I analyzed 13 experiments (53 effect sizes) drawn from a scoping review of online dating research. Text-based cues have a small, heterogeneous effect on evaluations, dating intentions, and behavior toward the profile owner (g = 0.25). Negative cues move responses (g = 0.38), whereas positive cues do not (g = -0.07). Explicit and implicit cues do not differ overall, but the gap between negative and positive cues is larger for explicit cues. The results show that strategic verbal content in online dating profiles works as a screening device, carrying meaningful information about undesirable traits in a partner.

[div-01]

Profile text offers a productive context in which to build this understanding. Before any interaction, users decide whether to pursue a potential partner largely on the basis of the profile (Finkel et al., 2012), and impressions formed from profiles shape how first dates unfold (Sharabi, 2021). Both photos and text signal who the owner is, and both can mislead. Their signals, however, work through different processes: responses to photos are dominated by physical attractiveness (Fiore et al., 2008), whereas text signals through what is stated and how it is written. Text therefore offers a cleaner context in which to manipulate a single cue and observe its effect, without the cue being confounded with the owner's appearance.

Research has examined in detail what daters say about themselves and how accurately they say it (Ellison et al., 2006; Toma et al., 2008). Less is known, in a cumulative way, about PROFILE TEXT AND DESIRABILITY 3 which features of profile text change how readers respond. Experiments that manipulate profile text have accumulated across communication, social psychology, criminology, and health research. They vary language errors (van der Zanden et al., 2020), originality (van der Zanden & Schouten, 2024), self-disclosed attributes such as smoking or a criminal record (Evans, 2019; Wang & Yang, 2024), and statements of belief (Green et al., 2026). Each examines a few specific cues, and they rarely cite one another. Taken together, however, they speak to a question that has not been answered directly: How does text in online dating profiles signal the desirability of the profile owner?

In this article, a profile cue is any observable piece of information in a profile that a reader can use to infer an attribute of the profile owner (Brunswik, 1956; Funder, 1995). Profile text is the medium; cues are the units of information that the text carries. A single selfdescription can contain several cues, such as a stated habit, a stated belief, and a pattern of spelling errors. Two features of text-based cues are central to how they signal desirability. The first is valence. A cue can convey an attribute that is desirable in a partner, such as originality, or one that is undesirable, such as a criminal record. Theories of impression formation predict that negative information carries more weight than positive information (Baumeister et al., 2001; Skowronski & Carlston, 1989). The second is explicitness. A cue can be stated by the owner ("I smoke") or given off by the way the profile is written (a profile full of spelling errors; Goffman, 1959; Gosling et al., 2002). Warranting theory predicts that information the owner cannot easily control is more credible and therefore more influential (Walther & Parks, 2002). The two features may also combine: signaling theory suggests that whether a cue is believed depends on what it costs the sender to produce it, and a favorable claim about oneself costs less than an unfavorable one (Donath, 2007).

This meta-analysis synthesizes these experiments. It estimates the overall effect of textbased cues on responses to the profile owner, tests competing predictions about valence and explicitness, and maps where the evidence is thin.

Theoretical Background

Why Profile Text Matters

Compared with a face-to-face encounter, a profile offers a limited and static set of cues: it is composed in advance, cannot respond to the reader, and carries few of the nonverbal signals people use to judge one another in person. Social information processing theory holds that when nonverbal cues are limited, people rely on the verbal and textual cues that remain (Walther, 1992), and the hyperpersonal model adds that senders can craft these cues selectively (Walther, 1996). Both imply that profile text should shape partner judgments.

In the limited space of an online dating profile, daters present themselves strategically and emphasize favorable attributes (Ellison et al., 2006), and small discrepancies between profiles and reality are common and, to a degree, expected (Ellison et al., 2012; Toma et al., 2008). Readers therefore face an interpretive problem: profile text is informative, but much of it is produced to impress.

Profile text carries cues in two ways. The first is content, meaning what the text says about the owner, including stated traits, habits, beliefs, and history. The second is form, meaning how the text is written, including spelling, grammar, originality, and the level of detail; such features vary with the writer and are used by readers, although not always accurately (Toma & Hancock, 2012; van der Zanden et al., 2020). Content is chosen deliberately, whereas form is partly deliberate and partly a by-product of writing. This distinction underlies the explicitimplicit contrast tested below.

The question of how text signals desirability can be divided into two parts. The first is whether a cue tells the reader something about the owner that bears on their value as a partner.

The second is whether the reader believes it. Research on negativity mainly addresses the first part, the informativeness of a cue. Warranting and signaling theories mainly address the second, its credibility. Whatever the answer to these two questions, readers rely on profile text when they judge a potential partner, so cues in profile text should affect how they respond to the owner.

H1: Text-based profile cues affect evaluations of, intentions toward, and behavior toward the profile owner in the direction implied by the cue's desirability.

Negativity and Diagnosticity

Research on impression formation consistently finds that negative information has more influence than comparable positive information (Baumeister et al., 2001; Rozin & Royzman, 2001). The diagnosticity account explains this asymmetry by the information value of a cue (Skowronski & Carlston, 1989). A cue is diagnostic when it helps a perceiver distinguish between kinds of people. Because most people behave positively most of the time, positive behavior is common and says little about any particular person, whereas negative behavior is rarer and more informative about character (Fiske, 1980; Reeder & Brewer, 1979).

The diagnosticity account also specifies where negativity effects are strongest: in judgments of morality and character, where a single negative act is taken as revealing, rather than in judgments of ability, where positive information can be more telling (Skowronski & Carlston, 1987). Judgments of a potential partner rest heavily on character, such as whether the person is trustworthy, kind, and reasonable. Partner choice also appears to begin with screening out people who lack necessary qualities or who show traits that rule them out (Jonason et al., 2015; Li et al., 2002). Both points suggest that negative cues should weigh heavily in partner judgments.

Online dating profiles add a further reason to expect negative cues to be especially diagnostic. Because profiles are expected to present the owner favorably (Ellison et al., 2006 (Ellison et al., , 2012)), positive content is the norm and carries little information, whereas a negative cue departs from what readers expect and stands out.

H2: Negative text-based cues have larger effects than positive text-based cues.

Warranting: Control Over Cue Production

Warranting theory addresses how people judge the credibility of online self-presentations (Walther & Parks, 2002). A warrant is the connection between an online self-presentation and the person behind it. Information that the self-presenter can easily manipulate has low warranting value; information that is harder to manipulate has higher warranting value and should influence impressions more (DeAndrea, 2014; Walther et al., 2009).

Profile text is entirely self-generated (vs. other-generated; Walther et al., 2009), but the cues it carries still vary in how much control the owner has over their production. Explicit cues are claims the owner makes about themselves, such as "I have a degree with honors," "I am a smoker," or "I believe the election was rigged." Implicit cues are not asserted; the reader infers them from how the profile is written, for example from spelling and grammar errors, original or inappropriate phrasing, or concrete verifiable details. This distinction parallels the difference between identity claims, which people make deliberately to convey how they wish to be seen, and behavioral residue, which they leave without intending to (Gosling et al., 2002). In Goffman's (1959) terms, implicit cues are given off rather than given. Because implicit cues are less likely to be noticed and controlled by the writer, warranting theory predicts that they should carry more weight.

H3: Implicit text-based cues have larger effects than explicit text-based cues.

Signaling: The Cost of a Cue

Signaling theory offers a slightly different account of credibility. A signal is credible when it is costly to produce or when faking it would carry a penalty (Spence, 1973; Zahavi, 1975). Donath (2007) applied this logic to online self-presentation: signals that are cheap to fake, such as claims about oneself, are discounted, whereas signals that are costly or tied to verifiable facts are trusted. Persuasion research makes a related point. Communicators are judged more credible when what they say departs from what their position or interest would lead the audience to expect, as when a speaker argues against their own interest (Eagly et al., 1978; Walster et al., 1966).

Applied to profile text, signaling theory does not treat all explicit cues alike. Explicit positive cues ("I am ambitious," "I graduated with honors") cost little to produce and serve the writer's interest, so readers may treat them as cheap talk. Explicit negative cues ("I am on parole," "I smoke") may work against the writer's interest. A person has little reason to invent them, so they are more likely to be believed.

From this perspective, explicitness and valence should interact. The negativity asymmetry should be largest for explicit cues, where positive claims are cheap and negative disclosures are costly, and smaller for implicit cues, which the owner does not deliberately produce. Because the theories make partly competing predictions and few studies cross both features, I treat this as a research question.

RQ: Does the difference between negative and positive cues depend on whether the cue is explicit or implicit?

Method

Source of Studies

Studies were drawn from a scoping review of quantitative online dating research (Authors, Blinded), conducted following PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021; see Figure A1 in Online Appendix A). The review searched Communication & Mass Media Complete, PsycInfo, Sociological Index, and Web of Science for English-language articles that combined online dating keywords (e.g., dating app*, online dating) with relationship keywords (e.g., relation*, intima*) in the title or abstract. Searches and reference checks yielded 1,413 unique records. Eligible articles were peer-reviewed, published between January 1, 1996, and April 1, 2026, reported original quantitative research, and examined the link between online dating strategies and relational outcomes. Relational outcomes were defined as cognition, emotion, and behavior indicative of the progression of a relationship initiated through online dating, including interpersonal ratings (e.g., attractiveness, trustworthiness, likability), intentions to pursue a relationship (e.g., desire to date or meet, sending messages), and behavioral outcomes such as matches.

Screening proceeded in two stages. At each stage, one author screened all records and an AI model (Claude) screened the same records independently as a check; disagreements were resolved by both human authors against the criteria. Agreement between the author and the AI was 96% at the title-and-abstract stage (Krippendorff's α = .84) and 91% at the full-text stage (α = .80). A second author independently screened a random 10% of records at each stage; agreement among the three coders was 95% (α = .85) and 88% (α = .82), respectively. The review included 112 articles reporting 134 studies.

This meta-analysis was conducted by a single researcher with AI assistance rather than by a team of human coders. The coding criteria were explicit and were refined until they could be applied without ambiguity, the AI model applied them consistently across studies, my independent coding of a random sample agreed fully with the model's, and I verified every inclusion and exclusion decision against the original articles.

From the scoping review's corpus, I identified all experiments that manipulated a feature of an online dating profile and measured a response to the profile owner (40 studies) by searching the review's coded records for experimental designs with profile manipulations. I developed the eligibility criteria and coding scheme (Online Appendix F) and refined them iteratively with an AI model (Claude Opus 5.5, Anthropic): the model applied the criteria to the candidate studies, I reviewed its decisions, and the criteria were clarified wherever a decision raised a question about how they should apply. The model then screened the 40 candidate studies for eligibility and coded the explicitness, valence, and certainty of each cue contrast. To assess reliability, I independently and blindly coded a random 10% sample: 4 of the 40 studies for eligibility and 3 of the 30 cue contrasts for explicitness, valence, and certainty. Agreement was complete (4/4 eligibility decisions, including the reason for exclusion; 9/9 cue-coding judgments; Krippendorff's α = 1.00). I then checked every excluded study to confirm that its exclusion was warranted and checked the coding of every included study against the original article; no errors were found. A coding workbook and procedure document record every inclusion, exclusion, and coding decision and its rationale.

Eligibility Criteria

Study Selection

Online Appendix A summarizes the selection. Of the 40 candidate studies, four were not profile-cue experiments (e.g., ratings of isolated screen names; a contrast effect on a different target), three manipulated messages rather than profiles, nine manipulated photos or other visual cues, two had no manipulated contrast (a measured predictor; a trial-level regression), and three manipulated a feature without a desirable level (e.g., the presence vs. absence of a selfdescription). This left 19 eligible studies. Six of these were excluded because the valence of the cue could not be determined with confidence (e.g., disclosing a gluten-free diet; high vs. low ambition), and three contrasts were removed from studies that were otherwise retained (see Coding). The final sample comprised 13 studies from nine articles, with 21 cue contrasts and 53 effect sizes.

Coding

Explicit Versus Implicit Cues

For each manipulation, I identified the attribute the cue conveyed to the reader and asked whether the profile text asserted it. A cue was coded as explicit when the text asserted the attribute ("I am / I have X"), as with a stated parole status, smoking status, autism diagnosis, endorsement or rejection of a conspiracy theory, or stated achievements. A cue was coded as implicit when the attribute was not asserted and had to be inferred from how the text was written or from incidental verifiable detail. Examples are spelling and grammar errors (suggesting carelessness or low intelligence), original or inappropriate phrasing (suggesting creativity or poor judgment), and a link to a professional web page (suggesting credibility).

Valence

Valence was coded as the desirability, for a romantic partner, of the attribute the cue conveys, read literally in the study's context and judged against the default profile (no mention, error-free, or conventional text). Desirability was judged by ordinary convention about partner qualities. The study context was used only to establish what the cue conveyed. For example, an autism diagnosis disclosed to explain atypical behaviors described in the profile was coded as a negative cue, although the original authors predicted that the label would improve evaluations.

A cue was coded as uncertain, and not analyzed, when its desirability for a partner was ambiguous or contested, or when the design had no neutral comparison, so that the contrast mixed a positive and a negative statement. Four kinds of cue were uncertain on the first ground: a gluten-free diet, which may signal a health-conscious or a high-maintenance partner; disclosure of past victimization, which is not a trait of the person and has ambiguous implications; informal "errors" such as emoticons and abbreviations, which may read as casual or careless; and vaccination status, which was politically contested during the study period and whose evaluation, according to the primary studies, depended on raters' ideology. Two contrasts were uncertain on the second ground, meaning that they had no neutral condition: high versus low ambition, where the low condition stated that education was unimportant, and positive versus negative wording of the same behaviors.

Outcomes

Eligible outcomes (criterion d) were evaluation (interpersonal ratings of the owner, such as attractiveness, likability, trustworthiness, and trait impressions), pursuit intention (romantic interest and desire to date, meet, or contact the owner), or behavior (matches in an audit study and swiping on the target in a mock dating app); outcome type defined eligibility and was not analyzed as a moderator. When a study reported a battery of five or more outcomes of the same type, the items were averaged into one composite per condition, assuming a correlation of .50 among items. This applied to a trait-impression battery in one article and a six-item datingintention battery in another. Traits without a clear desirable direction (e.g., "unique," "intuitive") and non-romantic intentions (befriending) were excluded.

Effect Sizes

Studies included in the meta-analysis are marked with an asterisk in the reference list.

Effects were computed as Hedges' g and coded in the desirability direction, so that a positive g means the cue moved the outcome as its valence implies: a desirable cue raised, or an undesirable cue lowered, evaluation, intention, or behavior. Means and standard deviations were used where available. F tests with one degree of freedom were converted with d = 2√(F/N). For PROFILE TEXT AND DESIRABILITY 13 within-subject designs, d was computed from condition means and the average standard deviation, with sampling variance based on an assumed correlation of .50 between repeated measures (Morris & DeShon, 2002). For the audit study, the outcome was binary (match or no match), so the log odds ratio was converted to d by multiplying it by √3/π; this conversion rests on the logistic distribution, whose standard deviation is π/√3 (Chinn, 2000). When a factor of interest was crossed with other manipulated factors, cell means were pooled across those factors.

All effects were re-extracted from the original articles (hosting studies extracted from the scoping review; Authors, Blinded). Re-checking corrected several values and added outcomes that had been missed, including outcomes that were reported in tables but not discussed in the text. No imputed effects remain. Every effect was verified against the original article, and each effect in the coding workbook carries its valence rationale, the statistics used, and its source.

Risk of bias was appraised with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool criteria for randomized controlled studies (Hong et al., 2018), and design features (sample, pretesting of stimuli, manipulation and attention checks, preregistration, and setting) were recorded; both are reported in Online Appendix C.

Analytic Strategy

Analysis was run on RStudio 26.08.1, using packages named "metafor" (4.4-0), "openxlsx" (4.2.5.2), "ggplot2" (3.4.4). The main model was a three-level random-effects model with effects nested in studies (metafor; Viechtbauer, 2010), which allows variance between effects within studies as well as between studies (Assink & Wibbelink, 2016; Cheung, 2014).

Inference used cluster-robust standard errors clustered by study (Hedges et al., 2010) with t tests.

Because robust tests can be imprecise with few clusters (Tipton, 2015), model-based p values are reported alongside where the two diverge. As a complement, effects were averaged within study (r = .50) and pooled with REML and the Knapp-Hartung adjustment (Knapp & Hartung, 2003), from which I report τ², I², and the 95% prediction interval (IntHout et al., 2016).

Subgroup estimates come from separate three-level models fitted within each subgroup, and differences between subgroups were tested by meta-regression. H2, H3, and the RQ were tested by meta-regression on valence, explicitness, and their interaction. A moderator contrast in a three-level model combines comparisons made within studies and between studies. To show where each contrast came from, the valence and explicitness contrasts were also split into a within-study component (the deviation from the study mean) and a between-study component (the study mean).

Publication bias was assessed at the study level with a contour-enhanced funnel plot (Peters et al., 2008), Egger's regression test (Egger et al., 1997), trim-and-fill (Duval & Tweedie, 2000), PET-PEESE (Stanley & Doucouliagos, 2014), and a three-parameter selection model (Vevea & Hedges, 1995). Robustness was examined by excluding the audit study, excluding within-subject designs, and leaving out one study at a time.

Results

Description of the Sample

Online Appendix B lists the included studies, and Online Appendix C summarizes their design features. The 13 studies were published between 2016 and 2026. Four were conducted in the United States, five in the United Kingdom, and four in the Netherlands. Twelve experiments included 3,699 participants, and one audit study analyzed 36,000 match attempts on dating apps.

Eleven studies used between-subjects designs and two used within-subjects designs. All studies met the appraisal criteria for randomized studies (Online Appendix C).

Cues were unevenly distributed across the four combinations of explicitness and valence.

Explicit negative cues were the most common (8 studies, 31 effects), including parole status, smoking, conspiracy endorsement, femmephobic statements, and an autism diagnosis. Implicit negative cues, all concerning language errors or inappropriate text, appeared in four studies (13 effects). Explicit positive cues appeared in three studies (6 effects): rejection of conspiracy theories in two studies from one article and self-promoting statements of achievement in one.

Implicit positive cues appeared in two studies (3 effects): original text and verifiable detail. By outcome type, there were 31 evaluation effects (12 studies), 18 pursuit-intention effects ( 9studies), and 4 behavioral effects (2 studies).

H1: Overall Effect

Text-based profile cues had a small effect in the expected direction, g = 0.25, 95% CI [0.08, 0.41], p = .007. The study-level model gave a similar estimate, g = 0.24, 95% CI [0.09, 0.38]. Heterogeneity was large, Q(52) = 691.9, p < .001. In the three-level model, 60% of the total variance was attributable to differences between effects within studies, 35% to differences between studies, and 5% to sampling error. At the study level, I² = 93.2%, and the 95% prediction interval ranged from -0.28 to 0.75, meaning that the effect of a new text cue in a new study could plausibly be zero or reversed. H1 was supported, with the caveat that the average conceals wide variation.

H2: Negative Versus Positive Cues

Figure 1 shows the effects of negative and positive cues. Negative cues had a reliable effect, g = 0.38, 95% CI [0.14, 0.63] (12 studies), whereas positive cues had no detectable effect, g = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.22, 0.09] (4 studies). The contrast was significant, b = -0.82, 95% CI [-1.15, -0.49], p < .001.

The source of this difference matters. Three studies tested both a positive and a negative cue on the same outcomes. Within these studies, negative cues had much larger effects than positive cues, b = -0.97, 95% CI [-1.32, -0.62], p < .001. Between studies, however, studies of negative cues did not show larger effects than studies of positive cues, b = -0.09, 95% CI [-0.75, 0.56], p = .76. H2 was supported, but the support comes from within-study comparisons. These comparisons hold samples, profiles, and measures constant, but they involve only three studies, two of which come from one article.

H3: Explicit Versus Implicit Cues

Explicit cues (g = 0.24, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.51]; 9 studies) and implicit cues (g = 0.22, 95% CI [0.03, 0.40]; 5 studies) did not differ, b = 0.03, 95% CI [-0.27, 0.33], p = .81. The betweenstudy component was also near zero (b = -0.10, p = .48), and because only one study manipulated both an explicit and an implicit cue, a within-study comparison could not be estimated. H3 was not supported.

RQ: Explicitness by Valence

Online Appendix D shows the effects by explicitness and valence. Explicit negative cues had an effect of g = 0.44, 95% CI [0.05, 0.83] (8 studies), and explicit positive cues an effect of g = -0.11, 95% CI [-0.68, 0.45] (3 studies). Publication Bias and Robustness

The contour-enhanced funnel plot (Online Appendix E) showed no clear asymmetry.

Egger's test was not significant (z = -0.18, p = .86), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies on the side indicating suppression. The PET intercept was 0.27, 95% CI [-0.11, 0.64], and the PEESE intercept 0.22, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.48]. A three-parameter selection model gave an adjusted estimate of g = 0.22, 95% CI [0.02, 0.43], and the likelihood-ratio test did not favor selection (p = .90). With 13 studies these tests have low power, so the absence of evidence for bias is not strong evidence of its absence.

The overall effect was robust. It was similar when the audit study was excluded (g = 0.24, 95% CI [0.07, 0.41]) and when within-subject designs were excluded (g = 0.26, 95% CI [0.07, 0.46]), and leave-one-study-out estimates ranged from 0.21 to 0.28. In exploratory analyses, effects were smaller in samples with a higher proportion of women (b = -0.07 per 10 percentage points, p = .031, 12 studies). This association depended on two single-sex samples and is not interpreted. Mean sample age was unrelated to effect size (p = .98).

Two cautions apply. The asymmetry is driven by comparisons within three studies, two of them from one article; between studies, those testing negative cues did not show larger effects.

In addition, valence is confounded with content. The negative cues studied are often morally loaded, whereas the positive cues are comparatively mild, and excluding cues of contested desirability, such as vaccination status, narrowed the set of positive cues further. Part of the asymmetry may therefore reflect the cues researchers chose to study rather than valence as such.

Explicit Versus Implicit Cues

Warranting theory predicts that cues the owner cannot easily control should carry more weight. The data do not support this prediction in its simple form: implicit cues were not more influential than explicit cues, and among negative cues, explicit cues had a descriptively larger effect (0.44 vs. 0.26), although this difference was not significant. Three explanations may account for this pattern.

First, a warrant may depend on motive as well as control. Warranting theory locates the warrant in the reader's judgment of whether the self-presenter could have manipulated the information (DeAndrea, 2014; Walther & Parks, 2002), and readers are also likely to consider whether the self-presenter had reason to. A statement such as "I am on parole" is fully under the owner's control, yet the owner has no motive to fabricate it. Read this way, the explicit negative result is consistent with warranting theory once the self-presenter's interest is taken into account, and with the signaling and persuasion findings that self-damaging messages are believed (Donath, 2007; Walster et al., 1966). Second, implicit negative cues are ambiguous. A language error can reflect carelessness, haste, or writing in a second language, and readers may not notice it at all; in one included study, only about a third of participants reported noticing the errors (van der Zanden et al., 2020). Third, the two kinds of negative cues differ in domain. The implicit negative cues here concern competence, whereas most explicit negative cues concern morality, and negativity effects are stronger for morality than for ability (Skowronski & Carlston, 1987).

For positive cues, the pattern ran the other way: explicit positive cues had no effect, and implicit positive cues had a small and very uncertain effect. This fits the expectation that selfpromoting claims are cheap talk, whereas desirable qualities that are shown rather than stated may be more convincing (Donath, 2007). How Profile Text Signals Desirability

The results suggest a tentative account. Profile text works mainly as a filter: its most reliable effect is to give readers reasons to rule someone out. The credibility of a cue appears to depend on cost and interest, so that cues that cost the writer something, such as admissions of undesirable traits, are taken at face value, while cues that serve the writer's interest are discounted. Readers also judge profiles against a positive default, so departures from the expected favorable presentation carry the information. In this account, the theories address different parts of one process: negativity and diagnosticity explain why a cue is informative, and warranting and signaling explain why it is believed. Explicit negative disclosures satisfy both conditions, which may be why they had the largest effects. This account has implications for online dating as a media-market (Hu & Markowitz, 2026). Market design treats credible signaling as a condition for a matching market to function without congestion (Roth, 2015). Profile text appears to support this function asymmetrically. It PROFILE TEXT AND DESIRABILITY 21 conveys credible reasons not to pursue someone, which helps daters screen options, but conveys little credible information about desirable qualities, which limits its usefulness for finding good matches among many acceptable-looking profiles. In market terms, profile text is better at clearing poor matches than at directing attention toward good ones. Because daters accordingly present uniformly favorable profiles (Ellison et al., 2006), readers may search mainly for signs of trouble.

These findings contribute to online dating research in three ways. They bring a scattered experimental literature into one framework defined by valence and explicitness. They favor a negativity and signaling account of profile text over a simple warranting account and suggest how warranting theory might be refined for self-generated content. And they show which kinds of profile cues carry credible signals in a matching market, while revealing that the cues most needed to direct attention toward good matches, desirable qualities, are the least studied. For profile writers, the findings suggest that avoiding undesirable signals may matter more than adding desirable claims; for platforms, making verifiable information visible might help desirable qualities register. Neither suggestion has been tested directly.

Limitations

The sample is small: thirteen studies, with two to eight per cell, limit power and precision, and cluster-robust tests with few clusters can be imprecise. Four of the 13 studies come from one article on conspiracy beliefs, and the valence contrast and the interaction rely largely on three studies that tested both valences. Screening and coding were conducted by a single researcher with AI assistance, and the reliability sample was small; the valence rule also excluded several cues whose desirability was ambiguous or contested; a different rule could change the set of positive cues analyzed. Valence and explicitness are confounded with cue content and domain, and no study crossed the two for the same attribute. Within-subject variances and composite outcomes assumed correlations of .50, and the audit study treated each match attempt as independent, which understates its sampling variance, although excluding it did not change the overall effect. Several studies did not report manipulation or attention checks, and five of the 13 were not preregistered (Online Appendix C). Most outcomes were ratings of hypothetical profiles, behavioral outcomes came from only two studies, samples came from the United States, the United Kingdom, and the Netherlands, and tests for publication bias have low power with 13 studies.

Future Directions

Experiments could cross explicitness and valence within a single attribute, for example by comparing a stated claim of conscientiousness with an error-free profile. Manipulating whether a cue is verifiable or costly to produce would test whether credibility rather than valence explains the asymmetry. The largest gap concerns positive cues: studies should compare a desirable cue with a profile that omits it, and include positive cues whose desirability is widely shared. Behavioral outcomes on real or realistic platforms would show whether these patterns hold in actual decisions and how they shape congestion and matching at the market level.

Conclusion

Profile text conveys information about a potential partner's desirability, but mainly by signaling reasons for caution. For online dating as a media-market, this means that profile text helps daters screen out poor matches but does little to signal good ones. These conclusions rest on a small and uneven body of experiments, and the communication of desirable qualities through profile text remains largely untested.

Discussion

Profile text has a small and variable effect on how readers respond to the profile owner.

Negative cues have effects, whereas positive cues, on average, do not, and this asymmetry is concentrated in explicit cues; both findings rest mainly on comparisons within a few studies.

Negative Versus Positive Cues

Undesirable information in a profile, such as a criminal record, smoking, conspiracy beliefs, prejudiced language, or language errors, lowered evaluations and interest. Desirable information, such as rejecting conspiracy theories, stated achievements, original writing, or verifiable detail, did not raise them. This asymmetry fits the diagnosticity account of negativity (Skowronski & Carlston, 1989), and three features of online dating may strengthen it. First, profiles are expected to be favorable (Ellison et al., 2006 (Ellison et al., , 2012)), so positive information confirms what readers already assume, whereas negative information is unexpected and informative. Second, daters face many options and need to narrow them quickly (Finkel et al., 2012); information that rules a person out serves this task better than information that adds a small advantage, consistent with the weight of dealbreakers in partner choice (Jonason et al., 2015). Third, many negative cues in this sample were self-disclosures that may work against the writer's interest, and such disclosures are more likely to be believed (Eagly et al., 1978).

Positive cues showed no effect at all rather than a smaller one. Two features of the primary studies may explain this. Control profiles were already pleasant and contained no negative information, leaving little room for one more favorable detail to raise evaluations. Selfpromoting claims may also be read as ordinary impression management: in Wotipka and High (2016), profiles with more selective self-presentation were rated as less socially attractive and less trustworthy.
