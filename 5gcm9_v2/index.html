Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

This paper develops an empirically informed urban aesthetics by introducing interestingness as a complementary category to beauty. While beauty has long defined architectural value through harmony and fittingness, urban life also requires experiences of dissonance, openness, and surprise. I develop this distinction by contrasting Scruton's sacred ideal of architectural beauty with Mumford's conception of the city as secular and dramatic. Rather than centering urban aesthetics on beauty or simply endorsing the pluralism common in contemporary philosophy of the city, I explore beauty and interestingness as two indispensable dimensions of the psychology of urban public life.

Methodologically, I advocate a middle way that combines philosophical reflection with empirical research in psychology and neuroscience. This interdisciplinary perspective motivates an account of aesthetic emotions linking experiences of beauty and interestingness also to urban wellbeing. Taken together, these considerations allow me to outline a theory of the built environmentespecially urban public space-as an aesthetic interface, drawing on concepts of perception, affect, cognition, affordances, and civic participation. On this account, beauty provides coherence, while interestingness sustains vitality.

1. Introduction 1

Beauty has long been the dominant aesthetic category in the philosophy of architecture. Roger Scruton has argued that beauty is not ornamental but integral to the rightness of building-part of practical reason itself (Scruton 1979). Beauty expresses how things ought to fit togetherstructurally, socially, and perceptually-within a shared lifeworld. For Scruton, beauty, understood as harmony and coherence, has a moral and almost liturgical dimension. It manifests the fitting order through which human beings inhabit and sustain a meaningful world. He also sees this ideal of beauty under threat in the contemporary city, "in which sheets of glass mirror each other's emptiness across streets that die in their shadow. The facelessness of such a city is also a kind of godlessness" (Scruton [1979 (Scruton [ ] 2013, xii), xii).

Such passages reflect what the urbanist Lewis Mumford (1961, 573) observed about the historical function of cities: they have evolved from temple-centered settlements of antiquity into secular metropolises, replacing the gods and the sacred, establishing their own rules of order and meaning. "When cities were first founded […] the mission of the founder was to put the gods in their shrines. The task of the coming city […] is to put the highest concerns of man at the center of all his activities." Despite the overall acknowledgement of the city's evolution from the sacred to the secular, Scruton and Mumford significantly differ in their conception of the ideal city. Scruton's aesthetics of architecture appeals to a sacred order that reveals how things ought to be-a normative vision grounded in a traditional notion of moral rightness. Mumford's vision of the city, by contrast, expresses a secular order that shows how things can be. His is an aesthetics of the possible rather than the prescriptive. Scruton and Mumford also differ in their assessment of the city dweller, with Mumford giving them a more distinct voice:

And if there is a deep reluctance on the part of the true city dweller to leave his cramped quarters for the physically more benign environment of a suburb-even a model garden suburb!-his instincts are usually justified: in its various and manysided life, in its very opportunities for social disharmony and conflict, the city creates drama; the suburb lacks it ([1937] 2011, 29).

Mumford's vision of the city provides what Scruton's architectural theory lacks: an affirmative aesthetics of complex urban life. 2Contemporary philosophy of the city likewise acknowledges aesthetics as a constitutive rather than supplementary dimension of urbanity (Lehtinen 2020a; Blakey and Barron 2025). It highlights the plurality of sensory, affective, and processual dimensions that shape urban experience. This includes the aesthetics of urban smell, touch, and hearing (Diaconu et al. 2011; Low and Kalekin-Fishman 2018); multispecies urban planning and aesthetics (Wilson 2025; Raymond et al. 2025); and the aesthetics of digital and media urban environments (Lehtinen 2020b; Degen and Rose 2024). As such, much of urban theory does not prioritize central aesthetic dimensions but rather foregrounds the plurality of appearances and expressions in the city. I propose a middle way. Conceptually, rather than accepting either the singularity of beauty or the sheer plurality of urban aesthetics, I aim to identify core dimensions that contribute centrally to urban life. While beauty is one such dimension, I will discuss interestingness as an equally central aesthetic quality of urbanity-sustaining curiosity and engagement. Methodologically, rather than relying solely on philosophical claims or single case studies from urban scholarship, I introduce a philosophically guided, empirically informed analysis of the psychology of urban experiences, drawing on empirical aesthetics and the emerging fields of neuroarchitecture (Coburn et al. 2017) and neurourbanism (Adli et al. 2017).

The claims advanced in this essay necessarily have normative implications and bear on pressing social issues, such as the erosion of civic engagement and dwindling well-being among city dwellers. Where Scruton sees "godlessness," others have diagnosed a burgeoning "fear of exposure"-a tendency to withdraw from public space rather than engage with it (Sennett 1990, xii). As Sennett warns, "the everyday negotiation of differences has been largely aborted" (2020, 57), despite the fact that such negotiation should be a central part of our democratic and sentimental education. His proposed remedy differs from Scruton's ideal of harmony. Sennett instead advocates an urbanism of openness: cities should provide not only inviting public spaces but also transitional spaces, porous boundaries and shared border zones between neighbourhoods and communities, where encounters with unfamiliar people and practices become part of everyday life. Such places cultivate the capacity to tolerate ambiguity, negotiate differences, and remain open to the unexpected rather than retreat into homogeneous enclaves. The city can-and in fact should-comprise elements of harmony alongside disorder, openness, and challenge. 3 Whereas beauty reconciles differences, interestingness sustains them and asks of the perceiver an openness to contrast, incompleteness, and the unexpected. To design and understand cities that remain socially and psychologically alive, we must combine an aesthetic of coherence with one of curiosity.

This essay proceeds in four parts. First, I identify the central limitations of Scruton's account of the beauty of architecture. Second, I introduce a framework of aesthetic emotions and discuss to what extent insights from the (neuro-)psychology of architecture could help us overcome the limitations identified in the first part. Third, I relate the debate on aesthetics to the concept of affordances (opportunities to act) in the city. In the conclusion, I outline an integrative perspective for studying the urban realm as an aesthetic interface that supports well-being and civic life-a perspective that necessarily includes beauty as well as interestingness as core dimensions.

2. Philosophical Accounts of Architectural Beauty

Scruton's account of architecture remains one of the most articulate attempts to link aesthetics with the experience of inhabiting the built environment. His defense of the vernacular-buildings that grow out of local traditions and express a sense of belonging-offers a rigorous philosophical account of how architectural beauty is felt. While Scruton's traditionalism makes him an easy target for criticism and even ridicule (Fitzgerald 2023), his reflections on the moral and perceptual dimensions of architectural experience deserve to be taken seriously.

2.1 Beauty as Fittingness

Scruton claims that beauty arises from the fitting relation between a structure, its setting, and human life. Beauty, in his view, is not reducible to subjective preference; it is a mode of apprehending how things ought to be ordered in relation to human life. Architectural beauty, accordingly, is realized in the rightness of fitting between a building's form, purpose, and context, both spatially (i.e., its surroundings) and temporally (i.e., its historical setting). What makes Scruton's position inherently useful for a theory of urban experience is that he does not focus on the "[r]avishing beauties" of grandiose architecture, which he considers "less important in the aesthetics of architecture than things that fit appropriately together, creating a soothing and harmonious context, a continuous narrative as in a street or a square, where nothing stands out in particular, and good manners prevail" (Scruton 2009, 19).

Architecture, in Scruton's terms, is a moral art. It unites practical reason (what serves human purposes) with aesthetic experience (what appears fitting and even intelligible). Despite its relation to practical reason, Scruton specifies the aesthetic experience of architecture as a form of disinterested contemplation: a stance in which we attend to the world for its own sake rather than seeing architecture as a means for satisfaction or gain. "Aesthetic understanding, in the sense of the imaginative contemplation of an object for its own sake, is an important part of everyday life" (Scruton [1979 (Scruton [ ] 2013, 220), 220). In such experiences of beauty, the world presents itself as meaningful, inhabitable, and rightly ordered. When Scruton speaks of fittingness, he describes a structure of experience in which perceptual engagement and practical expectations coincide. Beauty is a form of harmonious perceptual attunement.

Compare this to Paul Guyer's less moralized, more cognitivist account of beauty, drawn from Kant. While Scruton's analysis of beauty also draws on Kant, Guyer's reading treats architectural beauty not primarily as a moral property but as a type of adherent beauty-a reflective harmony between imagination and understanding that remains conditioned by the building's purpose, so that form and function reinforce rather than merely coexist with one another Beauty in architecture arises from the pleasurable interplay of form and function, reflecting a sense of intelligibility and purposiveness rather than moral rectitude (Guyer 2011). Beauty then constitutes a moment in which perceptual and cognitive capacities align.

Both Scruton and Guyer describe the role of harmony in the experience of architectural or urban beauty. On their reading, beauty is not a property of objects but a relation of citizens and architectural form: it is the way the world presents itself as meaningful, inhabitable, and rightly ordered to us. When Scruton evokes fittingness and Guyer reflective harmony, they each also describe a structure of experience in which perceptual and practical or cognitive expectations momentarily coincide. Beauty, in this sense, is a form of perceptual attunement to order. The value we track in beauty is not just hedonic or already moral but more broadly experiential: it consists in the felt sense that one's surroundings make sense-that form and purpose, or form and cognition, align in perception.foot_3

2.2 Three Fallacies

Despite their differences, both Scruton's moral realism and Guyer's reflective cognitivism provide theories that explain architectural beauty through the experience of perceiving and inhabiting the built environment. Yet, this reconstruction also exposes some limitations of their accounts, which I illustrate mainly with respect to Scruton.

(1) First, despite his emphasis on vernacular experience and on how aesthetics shapes everyday life, Scruton's aesthetic theory relies on a very specific kind of judgment. For him, to experience beauty is to adopt a contemplative stance in which one judges the object "for its own sake" (Scruton [1979 (Scruton [ ] 2013, 220), 220). Aesthetic appreciation of architecture as an evaluative actmarked by a disinterested judgment-is separated from embodied engagement with it. This move reduces architectural experience to pure reflection, detached from engagement and participation. Scruton's framework thus continues to privilege aesthetic judgment over lived experience, thereby undermining the everyday and participatory dimensions he claims to defend. I call this the disembodiment fallacy. Such a fallacy rests on the mistaken assumption that, to arrive at aesthetic judgments and insights into the fittingness of architecture, we must adopt a disembodied or disengaged stance (Fingerhut 2026a). 5(2) Second, Scruton's conception of beauty remains tightly bound to his moral realism. Beauty expresses 'how things ought to be ordered' and thereby presupposes a substantive view of value that derives from his own evaluative commitments in the moral domain, which he then projects onto what he takes to be aesthetic values for urban dwellers. This tendency to reduce the aesthetic to moral order can be called an axiological fallacy. More specifically, this is a direct value transfer fallacy: it consists of translating a moral value into an aesthetic one.

(3) Third, Scruton also reduces aesthetic value to one dimension: beauty. He thereby neglects aesthetic values related to complexity, disturbance, or variety that might also be central to urban vitality. This is another axiological fallacy, more specifically a value-reduction fallacy-one that assumes, without sufficient reason, the primacy of one aesthetic value over others. Here, "without sufficient reason" means without engaging broader theories of the aesthetic or empirical work investigating how urban dwellers in fact appreciate the built environment aesthetically. Scruton might argue that there is sufficient reason-namely, moral reason-to focus on beauty. Rather than engaging directly with this argument, I propose an alternative. Against aesthetic value reductionism, I will demonstrate that urban dwellers de facto track a plurality of values in urban environments. Fittingness captures only one essential dimension-best understood as the experience of coherence. Yet, cities not only fit; they also provoke, invite, and surprise. The aesthetic life of the city depends as much on curiosity as on experienced harmony.

As an alternative to direct value transfer, I will discuss how potential social and moral values are more indirectly tracked via aesthetic categories. Beauty and interestingness relate more directly to cognitive engagements, such as exploration, discovery, and learning. They do not prescribe a determinate social action. Yet a good urban life is measured by both practical and aesthetic values, and the role of the latter should be philosophically explored in its own right, without being tied in advance to moral claims. Overall, such a philosophy-of-mind perspective carries fewer theoretical commitments. It aims to establish initial correlations between aesthetics and patterns of behavior or dimensions of well-being, leaving their moral status to be addressed in subsequent steps.

3 Toward an Embodied Aesthetics of the City

3.1 Aesthetic Emotions

The relationship between embodiment and aesthetic value judgements can be addressed via theories of aesthetic emotions. Aesthetic emotions have their grounding in philosophical sentimentalism. Sentimentalism claims that emotions not only track values but constitute them, in the sense that only the emotions can explain their motivational force (i.e., an emotion not only tells us that something matters but also moves us to respond to it). This has been established mainly for moral values and in the social domain-think of shame or guilt, which track transgressions against moral values. This sentimentalist tradition, beginning with Hume (1739), has been taken up in contemporary psychological and philosophical accounts (Nichols 2004; Prinz 2016).

Aesthetic emotions then can be understood as tracking values in cultural domains, under which the built environment falls. Whereas moral judgments often involve negative judgments (blame, anger, shame) aesthetic emotions typically function as judgments of praise. The crucial point for the argument of this essay is that aesthetic judgments need not be separated from embodied responses. On the contrary: We would not arrive at a judgment of beauty regarding our built environment unless we were able to tap into our embodied emotions. Even what might be considered the reflective or contemplative component of aesthetic appraisal is itself part of this embodiment-a sensorimotor engagement that probes to what extent objects meet our perceptual habits as well as feedback from our own bodily states.

3.1.1 Beauty Revisited

Emotions engage us meaningfully with the world and structure our behavior. In the case of feelings of beauty-which thus far have been central in an aesthetics of architecture and the city-the motivation may be to savor, linger, or move toward what elicits the emotion. Another way to frame this is that, once we encounter a well-ordered environment, we are pleasurably reassured of the proper functioning of our sensory and cognitive capacities. This interpretation is also compatible with Guyer's Kantian account of architecture (2011). He situates architectural beauty as related to a free and harmonious interplay between our cognitive faculties. Aesthetic satisfaction arises from "the harmony between imagination and understanding," and the resulting pleasure marks a moment in which our cognitive faculties function fluently.

Yet-or so Guyer argues-this is not enough. In the case of architectural beauty, this also involves the relation between form and function. For objects with a use, the harmonious play of imagination and understanding is "constrained by our requirement that the form first be suitable to the use of the object" (Guyer 2002, 450); in another formulation, form and function must appear "inexplicably but perfectly suited to each other" (Guyer 2014, 439). For me, this Kantian demand-that a building's form must appear suited to its purpose-is too strong for most everyday experiences of architecture.

Feelings of beauty, in my account (Fingerhut and Prinz 2020), are expressions of an emotion of pleasure with respect to form and are indeed experienced when things seem to fall into order. They comprise multiple distinct feelings that are often lumped under the label 'beauty.' They include formal beauty (e.g., elegance, harmony, balance), pleasures of fluency (Winkielman et al. 2003), and potentially even feelings of attraction (e.g., in cases of physical beauty). Central to an account of aesthetic emotions is the idea that emotions have specific somatic profiles or embodiments. These profiles comprise bodily feedback as well as the bodily changes that structure how we cognitively engage with the environment. Bodily changes related to harmony and balance might include preparations for approach or even more complex cognitive processing. As I will discuss below, this could be called the 'cognitive style' of an emotion. Beauty experiences comprise heightened autonomic arousal as well as experiences of pleasure related to dopaminergic reward circuits (ventral striatum, OFC), which are widely understood to support valuation and action decisionmaking (Chuan-Peng et al. 2020; Kawabata and Zeki 2004). While not all beauty responses involve strong physiological changes, beauty experiences are consistently linked to positive affect, reward processing, and in some cases sympathetic activation. The cognitive style associated with feelings of beauty may include approach, and perceptual taking-in. Yet beauty also seems to require a cognitive element that separates beauty judgment from simple pleasures, which has been shown in experimental paradigms (Brielmann and Pelli 2017) and in analyses of our everyday use of the concept of beauty (Brielmann et al. 2021).

3.1.2 Interest

While some cognitivist readings of beauty are noteworthy (such as the 'stopping for knowledge'hypothesis with respect to beauty, see: Sarasso et al. 2020), the feeling of interest seems to be more directly related to epistemic gain and less to experienced pleasure. When we experience interest, we engage with stimuli that are new and complex enough to engage us and structured enough to make sense. It has been claimed that interest comprises an orienting response with a focused receptive attention (Silvia 2006) . Interestingly, experiences of interest often involve heart-rate deceleration reflecting such attentive engagement rather than high arousal (Petrie Thomas et al. 2011). Contemporary neuroscientific work similarly indicates that curiosity and interest rely less on reward circuits than aesthetic pleasure or beauty experience do, and more on exploratory, informationseeking networks supporting uncertainty reduction (Kang et al. 2009; Gruber et al. 2014; Gottlieb et al. 2013; Kidd and Hayden 2015). In short: where beauty engages valuation and affective reward, interest reflects curiosity-driven attention, making the two emotions distinct both in subjective feel and physiological signature.

Generally, emotions can be characterized along three central dimensions: affect, embodiment, and semantics. It will prove helpful for an understanding of my proposal to briefly address these dimensions. Affect comprises arousal and valence. Embodiment refers to bodily feedback in the sense of a somatic profile of an emotion that is also related to "action tendencies" (Frijda 1987 (Frijda , 1986) and the ensuing cognitive style of an emotion (Fingerhut and Kühnapfel 2026, in press). This explains how emotions set us up for specific sets of actions. A central example of such action preparedness is fight-or-flight preparation in the case of fear. In the case of aesthetic feelings (such as feelings of beauty) and other aesthetic emotions (such as interest and wonder), those instances of action preparedness are more directly related to assessments of how well the environment fits one's expectations or affords further exploratory engagement. In the case of beauty, the environment is experienced as more ordered than expected. Within the emotion of interest, it is experienced as warranting further exploration and suggests potential epistemic gain. These two aesthetic emotions, beauty and interest, can therefore also provide a productive lens for understanding the experiential and evaluative significance of urban environments, revealing how particular built settings become meaningful and appreciated.

A further way to characterize an emotion is by focusing on what the emotion is about, or what it tracks in the environment. Typically, one describes in this semantic dimension the formal object of an emotion or its "core relational theme" (Lazarus 1991), and the particular object, i.e., objects and situations that typically elicit the emotion. In the case of aesthetic emotions, among those particular objects crucially fall cultural artifacts (art, images, films, design, built environments, etc.). Aesthetic emotions therefore track the value of such artifacts for us, also making them a central component of an understanding of how we perceive and engage with architectural settings and urban environments. Yet within this dimension we can also identify another central aspect: namely, that beauty and interest capture core relational themes that are not directly related to survival (as in the core relational theme of 'danger to oneself' in the case of fear) or social situations (as in the core relational theme of 'transgression against social norms' in the case of anger). Beauty and interest involve more reflective assessments of situations or objects. This kind of reflective assessment of the epistemic potential of a situation is typical for aesthetic emotions (Fingerhut 2026b). In beauty, the theme might be 'things that fall into place' capturing fittingness and harmony in Scruton and Guyer, and in interest it might be 'new but comprehensible,' related to or promising learning and epistemic gain.

This is not the place to go into the details of such an account of aesthetic emotions. What I want to show, rather, is a way to conceive of aesthetic judgments as embodied (that therefore avoids the disembodiment fallacy from above) and that is related to a plurality of aesthetic values in the built environment (that therefore avoids the aesthetic value reduction fallacy). In recent years empirical aesthetics has explored the potential plurality of emotions related to art (Stamkou et al. 2024). In a seminal paper, Menninghaus and colleagues (2019) proposed a large list of candidates, across a wide range of domains from nature to music, using as a central criterion for aesthetic emotions their contribution to aesthetic praise. In their empirical work, they distinguish prototypical aesthetic emotions ('feeling of beauty' is listed among these) from epistemic emotions (such as 'sparked my interest' or 'made me curious') as well as from pleasing and negative emotions (Schindler et al. 2017). In our theoretical classification of aesthetic emotions (Fingerhut & Prinz 2020) we identify four categories of emotions that we all consider aesthetic. Feelings of beauty can be characterized as emotions of pleasure. Interest is an emotion of contemplation (as is curiosity, feeling absorbed, etc.). More intense forms could be characterized as emotions of amazement (such as the sublime, wonder, and awe), while more person-directed emotions are subsumed under emotions of respect (such as admiration) that arise when appreciation extends to agency, mastery, or authority behind artworks or cultural artifacts.

One central difference for the present paper from Menninghaus's account is that they see interest as a preparatory or orienting response that may lead to aesthetic emotions proper, while I treat interest as an aesthetic emotion in its own right and, correspondingly, acknowledge interestingness as an aesthetic value. As such, its functional description also goes beyond re-orienting, as interest motivates cognitive engagement and sustained attention. Which theory one follows is not central at this point; what is relevant is that both accounts provide an initial plurality of aesthetic emotions, which could prove helpful when applied to urban environments.

3.2 Three Aesthetic Dimensions of Architecture

Only very few studies on the urban environment assess interest as an emotion. The few that do suggest that interest is "sparked by unique, innovative, or culturally significant spaces," whereas "spaces promoting leisure, beauty, and social interaction" are more apt to elicit joy (Albeyboni and Raswol 2025). While interest as an emotion has therefore received little attention, the related property of 'interestingness' has engendered some relevant research. l will trace some of this evidence to suggest an empirically grounded way to counter the aesthetic value reduction fallacy regarding the aesthetics of the built environment.

Researchers on the neuropsychology of architecture (Coburn et al. 2019; 2020) have shown that aesthetic responses to architectural spaces reflect three latent psychological dimensions. These dimensions explain much of the variance in human ratings of interior, private spaces and emerged consistently across two experiments (Coburn et al. 2020). The first dimension the authors identifybased on ratings of private interiors-is hominess. Items that loaded highest on this factor include: 'hominess,' personalness,' and 'naturalness' (in this order). The authors relate this dimension explicitly to the Danish concept of hygge, which translates to "a feeling of coziness, warmth, and togetherness." It also aligns with Alexander's (1979) concept of wholeness and rootedness in space. As such, it already seems to track what Scruton subsumes under his beauty concept-namely, the wholeness of an aesthetic and moral unity, as well as a sense of belonging tied to continuity with tradition and environment. In Scruton's concept, architecture's "most important and lasting effects arise from the desire of ordinary people to 'get things right,' by producing arrangements and forms with which they can feel at home" (Scruton [1979] 2013, ix, my emphasis).

Yet beauty requires more than feeling at home. It requires coherent form. Coherent form has long been a mainstay in architectural theory. Scruton, for example, quotes Alberti's "architectural beauty (concinnitas) as the appropriate fitting of part to part" as central reference (Scruton 2009, 125). This is in fact the second dimension identified by Coburn and colleagues: coherence. Coherence is a dimension that reflects ratings of 'organization, beauty, valence,' and 'approachability' (in this order). While coherence is a label given by the researcher and not a single item participants were asked to rate, it captures what might be common to the subsumed ratings of organization and beauty-namely coherent form. Interestingly, the next items of this dimension-'valence' and 'approachability'-relate to central emotional components that I already associated with beauty above. 6 As should be clear by now, their study combined aesthetic ratings with ratings of basic affect (valence) or even action affordances (approachability). This relates to the question of whether 'coherent form' could be understood as a purely formal characteristic of the environment. Recall how both Scruton and Guyer, despite an interest in coherent form, are not formalists in a pure sense. They would, for example, claim that the appreciation of the beauty of architecture also requires an appreciation of form as a sort of achievement. A building, a square, or a street is for example appreciated as a harmonious resolution of competing demands, or as a successful expression of a function in a specific form. That coherent organization is a crucial part of architectural beauty in this sense and is reflected in the ratings of 'organization' that belong to the dimension of coherence.

Crucially, Coburn and colleagues also identify a final and third dimension: fascination. Fascination is again a label applied by the researchers and not something participants had to rate. It is supposed to capture the dimension that might underlie items such as 'complexity' and 'interest.' These items-alongside 'stimulation' and 'explorability'-load high on this dimension. This reflects Berlyne's classic work on interest and complexity (1978). Although the fascination dimension accounted for a smaller proportion of the variance in their data than coherence or hominess, it consistently emerged across different studies and was the only dimension that showed a stable, task-invariant neural signature in bilateral lingual gyrus, underscoring its robustness.

So, while Scruton's concept and ideal of beauty seem to capture both hominess (given his focus on the vernacular) and coherence (given his focus on fittingness), the dimension of fascination is underrepresented. Guyer, in his survey of eighteenth-century theories of taste, is a bit more open in this respect and references philosophers (Addison, Gerard) that discuss the taste for novelty as a distinct source of aesthetic pleasure alongside beauty and the great (2013). Guyer focuses on the architectural design of individual buildings rather than on urban living, and, in a similar vein, most studies in empirical aesthetics focus either on facades of single buildings or on interior spaces (e.g., the living room) and not the urban public. In the next sections, I will therefore explore how we can move from an aesthetics of architecture to an urban aesthetics. It is nonetheless notable that, already in architecture studies, interestingness together with fascination emerged as an independent dimension used by participants to rate their built environment.

3.3 Studies on Interestingness in Urban Space

The aim of this essay is to amend existing philosophical accounts of architectural aesthetics. I showed that aesthetic judgements need not be conceived as disinterested or disembodied. I then demonstrated that we rate our built environment according to dimensions that transcend beauty and encompass the dimension of interestingness. The evidence I have presented thus far focused on private and semi-private spaces, yet recently, public architecture has become a central focus as well. 7 This includes studies that aim to explore whether the aesthetic appearance of buildings in the urban environment also shapes people's emotional well-being and sense of agency and belonging. Dalton and colleagues (2025) report that almost 71% out of a UK sample (>2000) think we should invest more in buildings that positively affect how we feel and almost 67% agree that it is important that new buildings look interesting on the outside. Participants also rated both generic everyday urban architecture as well as visually rich, award-winning examples. While the frequency of beauty ratings was similar for both, the ratings of interestingness were significantly higher for the architecturally distinct buildings (Dalton et al. 2025).

In some of our own studies, we have explored how beauty and interestingness are perceived in private as well as in public spaces. The overall question was whether beauty and interestingness would relate to different motivations to act or to experiences of well-being. As a secondary question we wondered whether those relations would differ in private and public spaces. Such different contexts should play a decisive role for a comprehensive theory and science of the city. 8Let's start with public spaces. In one unpublished study (but see : Fingerhut et al. 2019), we examined how aesthetic impressions relate to behavioral tendencies in public interior spaces-a central topic in theories of interior urbanism (Marinic 2024). Generating visual models of different public interior spaces (resembling, for example, a railway station, a library, or a shopping mall) allowed us to manipulate the complexity of these spaces while retaining their overall type. We visually generated a basic version and a more open, multilayered version of the same space. 9 We found that beauty ratings were not significantly moved by this manipulation (although there was a tendency to rate our more complex spaces also as more beautiful). Yet the open, multilayered spaces were rated significantly more interesting.

Besides ratings of 'beauty' and 'interestingness,' we also asked our participants to what extent they felt the 'desire to meet other people' in those places or whether they would 'want to explore those spaces further' (Fingerhut, Ballestrem, et al. unpublished manuscript). Those ratings of social and exploratory affordances were significantly higher for the open, multilayered spaces. We also analyzed how the subjective aesthetic ratings of our spaces correlated to the ratings of affordances to meet and explore those spaces. Here, beauty ratings showed the strongest positive correlation with the desire to meet others in those spaces (r = .57, p < .01), indicating that aesthetically pleasing spaces tend to elicit a greater motivation to engage socially. Ratings of interestingness, by contrast, were most strongly associated with the desire to explore (r = .60, p < .01). These associations were confirmed by linear mixed-effects models that accounted for participant and trial variability: beauty significantly predicted meeting, and interestingness significantly predicted exploration, even when controlling for complexity ratings (all p < .001). Together, these findings suggest that beauty and interest might track different values in public interiors-that values captured by beauty ratings align more directly with social engagement, while interestingness motivates exploratory behavior.

In a much larger, ongoing citizen science experiment in Berlin, Germany, we complemented this controlled, image-based study with an exploratory study of real-world experiences. More than 2500 participants used an app (Your Emotional City) as part of a citizen science experiment, in which we asked them to rate their current environment for 'beauty,' 'interestingness,' and 'naturalness.' 10We triggered this assessment of the environment three times a day (for up to one week) and therefore received ratings for different urban settings, spanning private and public spaces. More than 30.000 reported moments allow us to examine how ratings of 'beauty,' 'interestingness,' and 'greenness/naturalness' relate to momentary emotional well-being. 11 Multilevel regression analyses revealed that both beauty (β = 0.34, p < .001) and interestingness (β = 0.29, p < .001) were significant positive predictors of emotional well-being (including happiness and felt energy), even when controlling for naturalness, perceived crowdedness of the spaces, and social context. 12What is particularly striking is that beauty and interestingness diverge in their associations with emotional well-being across spatial contexts. In private (home) settings, perceived beauty was a strong predictor of momentary well-being (β = 0.47, p < .001), whereas interestingness showed a smaller but still significant association (β = 0.14, p = .038). In public settings, this pattern was reversed: environmental interestingness was more strongly associated with well-being (β = 0.37, p < .001), while beauty showed a comparatively weaker effect (β = 0.15, p = .037). This suggests that familiar, private spaces may primarily support well-being through aesthetic harmony and comfort, while public environments contribute to well-being through stimulation and perceptual engagement. This finding is highly surprising. When we designed the study, we hypothesized that beauty and especially perceived greenness and naturalness would have the largest effects on well-being, independent of location. Yet we found no significant effect of rated naturalness. The pattern we found instead suggests that, in the public realm, aesthetic qualities related to open exploratory engagement become highly relevant and drive happiness and fill us with energy.

4. Non-Gibsonian Affordances: Atmosphere and Contemplation

With the empirical perspective on embodied aesthetic emotions (3.1) and the discussions of beauty, interestingness, and hominess in architectural (Section 3.2) and urban studies (Section 3.3), I have developed an alternative to the disembodied, beauty-centered view of the built environment defended by Scruton and others. In this final section, I revisit these findings through the concept of affordances, which has been argued to provide a bridge between aesthetic experience and possibilities for action in the built environment (Jelić et al. 2016).

The concept of affordance is potentially the most central term in embodied cognition accounts of architecture (Jelić et al. 2016; Gregorians and Spiers 2022) and thus far I have only addressed it in passing. Above, I spoke rather loosely of social affordances and affordances to explore. In ecological psychology, affordances are typically understood as more basic possibilities for bodily action-sitability, climbability, walk-throughability-available to be picked up in the environment (Gibson 1979). Most experimental research on affordances in architecture has focused on this level, for example in studies investigating whether participants perceive doors as passable or see stairs as walkable (Warren Jr. and Whang 1987; Djebbara et al. 2019). In my own work, I have targeted broader so-called place affordances, including perceivable opportunities to act in the built environment (Fingerhut et al. 2019; Hilton et al. 2025). When participants, for example, rate whether a space makes them want to explore, meet, or linger, they are responding to this richer layer of possibilities: not Gibsonian affordances in the strict sense, but higher-order, affectively and semantically enriched affordances that depend on cultural knowledge and social expectations.

In a similar vein, the neuroscientist Michael Arbib (2021) distinguishes what he calls 'affordances proper' related to wayfinding (i.e. basic, action-generating architectural affordances that facilitate movement, navigation, orientation) from 'non-Gibsonian affordances' in the architectural environment. The latter concept comprises prompts for emotional engagement-his key example here being atmospheres-or even aesthetic contemplation-his key examples being slowing, looking, reflecting. With aesthetic contemplation, he introduces a second class distinct from basic atmosphere or emotional engagement. While it is explicitly framed as containing reflection, aesthetic contemplation might still be based on emotions in his account. This becomes rather explicit in passages where Arbib echoes Frijda's account of refined emotions, which characterizes aesthetic emotions as "more felt than acted upon" (Frijda and Sundararajan 2007).

With this, Arbib sees aesthetic affordances to give rise to relatively disembodied, contemplative states, whereas atmospheric affordances remain more directly tied to bodily orientation and felt presence. This is where a problematic divide enters. The ensuing aesthetic emotions resemble the disembodied, contemplative emotional states that arise from cognitive evaluations of intrinsic qualities, rather than from sensorimotor or affective engagement. While Arbib then occupies a middle ground between Scruton's imaginative contemplation of an object "for its own sake" ([1979] 2013, 220) and action opportunities (after all, Arbib still considers them to be affordances), I would argue that they become too removed from what I would consider our everyday aesthetics of the urban environment and ignore the extent to which their cognitive style remains embodied. From my perspective, aesthetic emotions are embodied appraisals-shifts in posture, attention, readiness, and exploratory stance through which the built environment becomes meaningful. They also track different levels of properties, from the sensorimotor to the cognitive. They are not just the aftermath of embodied engagements; rather, they are a specific embodied articulation. Aesthetic emotions are not less embodied than our affective engagement with atmospheres; they are embodied ways of bringing forth further affordances to act and cognize.

Arbib's distinctions also narrow what are considered central examples in the aesthetic domain. Because aesthetic emotions are conceptualized as contemplative, appraisal-driven, and "refined," Arbib tacitly privileges beauty, coherence, and sacralized forms of architectural experience. The empirical work he highlights reinforces this bias-most notably studies associating sacred architecture with meditative neural states (Bermudez et al. 2017). Curiosity, interest, stimulation, and exploratory movement-the qualities our own studies show to be central in public urban settingsremain largely unaddressed. This results in an implicit value reduction (echoing the value reduction fallacy in Scruton) in which aesthetic significance becomes aligned mainly with contemplative beauty, sidelining the activating and exploratory dimensions.

While I agree that non-Gibsonian or non-classical affordances require additional explanatory work, aesthetic affordances cannot be simply reduced to the calm-contemplative features of beauty and harmony at the expense of interest in urban environments or even wonder in the face of architectural achievements. An integrative framework based in aesthetic emotions should make clear that aesthetic features of architectural spaces afford both contemplative as well as exploratory modes of engagement, and that both of those modes could be reflected in the aesthetic ratings assessed in empirical aesthetics. Beauty can motivate savoring and rest, while interestingness motivates investigation and epistemic openness. Both are embodied, both reshape fields of affordances, and both are essential for understanding how people inhabit the city.foot_12

5. Conclusion and Outlook

Based on some limitations I identified in prominent philosophical accounts of architecture and urbanity, I sketched an alternative theory that conceives of aesthetic engagements in the city as based on embodied aesthetic emotions that track both beauty and interestingness.

This paper began by revisiting Mumford's and Sennett's insistence that we value cities because they are challenging environments-they stimulate curiosity, sustain conflict and diversity, and invite exploration rather than withdrawal. Complementary to philosophical accounts of architectural beauty that privilege harmony, fittingness, and contemplative order, I argued that an adequate urban aesthetics should also reflect the exploratory and transformative character of cities and therefore incorporate at least one further aesthetic dimension: interestingness. Drawing on empirical aesthetics, neuroscience, and some of our own studies, I argued that interestingness is not a marginal or derivative property of the built environment but an independently operative aesthetic dimension, one that tracks informational richness, novelty, and the promise of rewarding engagement.

Across the wide range of research paradigms surveyed-from Scruton's traditionalist architectural theory to contemporary neuropsychology of architecture in affordance-based approaches-this exploratory dimension remains consistently underrecognized. Yet the empirical evidence I provided suggests that it plays a central role in how people inhabit and evaluate urban environments. Interestingness, more reliably than beauty, predicts exploratory tendencies in designed public interiors and momentary well-being in everyday urban encounters; it appears to be particularly salient in the public realm, where novelty and complexity can foster openness, curiosity, and social engagement.

Future theory and empirical research should further clarify how this dimension relates to broader constructs such as novelty-seeking, challenge, and environmental complexity, and should investigate how urban form can scaffold emotions-including those that motivate learning, engagement, and sustained interaction with the city and its inhabitants. My aim here, however, has been more modest: to show that interestingness should be further developed as a distinct aesthetic dimension in urban life. Acknowledging it alongside beauty allows for a richer, more empirically grounded account of urban aesthetics. If beauty provides coherence and intelligibility, interestingness supplies vitality and possibility. Cities require both. This perspective also addresses the philosophical fallacies identified earlier. By recognizing interestingness as a central dimension of urban aesthetic experience, my account avoids the valuereduction fallacy that privileges beauty at the expense of other aesthetic values. I addressed the related value-transfer fallacy by developing empirical approaches that relate aesthetic ratings to potentially beneficial actions and emotional well-being without collapsing aesthetic and moral values. Both domains may ultimately inform discussions about how cities should be planned and sustained, but they should remain conceptually distinct. By grounding aesthetic evaluations in embodied aesthetic emotions, I countered the disembodiment fallacy: aesthetic appraisals-even when containing reflective or contemplative elements-are realized through embodied engagements and remain related to broader fields of affordances in the environment. Understanding these embodied aesthetic appraisals, and how they modulate the field of invitations in urban settings, ought to be a central task for future research in embodied cognition and urban theory.
