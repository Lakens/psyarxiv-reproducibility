Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Talking in Timor-Leste: urbanization, peer input, and other influences on children's verbal environments

Introduction

The verbal environments of young children are key targets of research on language acquisition. Children's exposure to speech input as early as 7 months has been associated with individual differences in the pace of their subsequent language development (Newman et al., 2016; for review, see Coffey & Snedeker, 2025) and their language skills and academic performance years later in adolescence (Walker et al., 1994; Lee, 2011; Bornstein et al., 2014). These studies pose a number of theoretical questions that researchers still grapple with. How much speech do children need to hear to become fluent speakers of their language? What factors predict differences in speech input across households? And are some kinds of speech input more or less difficult to learn from than others?

While significant headway has been made for each of these questions, most research in the field is still limited to the study of children in North America and Europe, where 87% of studies on language acquisition originate (Kidd & Garcia, 2022). Similarly, a recent meta-analysis of 71 studies using speech input to predict children's language ability found 88% of studies were conducted in the U.S. or other English-speaking countries (Coffey & Snedeker, 2025). As a result, much of what we know about the relationship between children's exposure to speech and the pace of their language development is based on samples that represent only 15% of the world's population (Nielsen et al., 2017). In addition, much of the publicly available data on children's speech input in Africa, Asia, and Central/South America overrepresents higher income households in urban communities (Scaff et al., 2025), where the frequency of child-directed speech may be similar to that in North America (Cristia, 2023). For many of these regions, children residing in rural communities constitute the largest and fastest growing portion of the youth population (OECD, 2018). This is concerning for any theory of language acquisition, as it suggests we study learning in environments that are not representative of the average child's experiences globally.

Societies where children are frequently spoken to by adults are overrepresented

Cross-cultural studies that directly compare the amount of child-directed speech from adult caregivers find that children in middle-class U.S. or European homes hear anywhere from two to ten times as much speech per hour as children living in rural subsistence farming communities (Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Vogt et al., 2015; Katus et al., 2024). A systematic review of studies reporting on the frequency of infant-directed speech found that children growing up in urban North American communities are reported as hearing roughly four times as much speech as children growing up in farming or foraging communities in other regions, with no significant differences emerging between the latter two groups (Cristia, 2023). These widespread differences in speech may be related to urbanization. Cristia (2023) found that families from urban communities outside of North America produce comparable amounts of child-directed speech as those within North America. Within the U.S. and other English-speaking countries, socioeconomic status (particularly household education) is associated with the amount of speech directed to children (for reviews, see Piot et al., 2022 and Dailey & Bergelson, 2022). Thus, the relationship between child-directed speech and urbanization could be due to various downstream effects on the home environment, such as changes in caregiver behavior resulting from greater access to education (Rowe, 2008) and more household resources (Wolf & McCoy, 2018). This observation is important for home-based educational interventions, which are predicated on the idea that parents' knowledge of child development, and their capacity to support it, are key to the timely attainment of developmental milestones (Rowe, 2008).

Yet urbanization and household education are not consistently correlated with adult input outside of the U.S. A recent large-scale study of 1001 children growing up in 12 different countries, spanning both middle-class urban societies and subsistence-based rural societies, found no relationship between maternal education and adult or child speech (Bergelson et al., 2023). Other studies have found that caregivers in communities undergoing market integration, shifting from subsistence-based horticulture or agriculture to market-based economic activity, produce less child-directed speech in the home (Padilla-Iglesias et al., 2021; Cristia et al., 2023). Similarly, relocation from rural communities to urban and peri-urban communities has been associated with less adult verbal interaction (Fernald et al., 2012) and also slower child language development in at least one study (despite also finding a positive association with household education; Ma et al., 2024). For families living in rural areas outside of cities, education can create opportunities for parents to engage in the wage economy and pursue jobs that are often far from home, resulting in children being exposed to less speech from adults. Maternal and paternal education has also been found to differently impact the home language environment. A randomized field experiment in Ghana found that children of women who had been offered high school scholarships years prior engaged in more conversation with adults compared to a control group; in contrast, children of male beneficiaries heard less female speech than controls (Duflo et al., 2024). These findings suggest that the story is far less simple than has been assumed based on initial data from the U.S. and other highly urbanized countries.

Societies where children are frequently spoken to by other children are underrepresented

In smaller-scale societies with larger family sizes, peer cultures are an integral part of life for young children and alloparental care from other children is incredibly common (Zukow & Goldring, 2002; Lew-Levy & Amir, 2024). As a result, these children have been found to hear as much or more child-directed speech from other children as compared to adults, increasing with age (Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Cristia et al., 2019; Loukatou et al., 2022; Cristia et al., 2023). While observer bias may affect the size of population differences (e.g., Bunce et al., 2024; and discussion in Cristia, 2023), language socialization practices consistent with these differences have also been noted in relevant descriptive studies (e.g., Schieffelin & Ochs, 1986). Does speech from other children impact language development? The abundance of studies documenting whether and how children's language outcomes correlate with adult input (see e.g., Coffey & Snedeker, 2025) contrasts with a dearth of research linking it to speech from other children (siblings, cousins, friends, or classmates -peers, for short).

That said, sociolinguistic research shows that children eventually speak like their peers rather than their parents (Eckert, 1988; see Duncan & Paradis, 2020 for evidence from L2 learning in immigrant families), suggesting that input from peers is relevant to language learning. But there is substantial debate about how important peer input is prior to school entry. Some theories assume that speech from other children is less appropriate for learning (Nwokah, 1987; Tomasello & Mannle 1985; Mannle et al., 1992). Under these frameworks, we would predict that children exposed to more peer than adult speech should have protracted language development compared to children who are primarily spoken to by adults (Lieven, 1994). Some studies have found positive links between speech from peers or siblings and language development, specifically in contexts where children have frequent exposure to input from other children (e.g., in Vanuatu: Cristia et al., 2023; in U.S. preschool classrooms: Perry et al., 2018) and notably for L2 acquisition in bilingual homes (Bridges & Hoff, 2014; Duncan & Paradis, 2020). This is still compatible with claims that peer input is "lower quality": children may nonetheless learn something from others. There is a contrasting theoretical framework according to which peers are active contributors to children's language development (Hoff-Ginsberg, 1998; Oshima-Takane & Robbins, 2003).

A recent broad review takes a step further and argues that, in many societies, children build their own "peer cultures" (Lew-Levy & Amir, 2024), within which they create and transmit knowledge, including language. Based on the latter framework, we would predict correlations between adult input and children's language outcomes will be lower in communities where peers are children's primary source of language.

Very little research has addressed these predictions quantitatively in communities where peer speech is more common than adult speech. For example, Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow (2012) found children's receptive vocabulary was significantly associated with transcribed speech input from adults, but not with speech from other children in a Yucatec Mayan village. In contrast, Cristia et al. (2023) found that children's vocalization rates were significantly correlated with verbal input from other children in Vanuatu.

Understanding these relationships requires a consideration of complicated underlying causal chains. For instance, some children are exposed to more speech from other children because they are laterborn as opposed to firstborn (Oshima-Takane et al., 1996); a separate line of work suggests that the former exhibit slightly lower performance due to having more siblings, which has been associated with greater dilution of resources in the home (Blake, 1981; Downey, 2001). In addition, children with more siblings may hear less speech from adults overall (Laing & Bergelson, 2024).

Current Study

The current study responds to the pressing need for descriptive data on the early speech environments of children from societies that are underrepresented in the literature.

We present an analysis of child-centered long-form recordings gathered from a large-scale study of families in Timor-Leste, a country in Southeast Asia that achieved independence from foreign occupation in 2002. Most of the country's population (~70%) resides in rural areas and engages in subsistence agriculture; over 40% of the population lives under the international poverty line (CIA, 2024). Our study sample is drawn from different regions of the country, comprising communities both within and outside of the capital city Dili, located in the Dili Municipality (Figure 1). The country is highly multilingual: Tetun and Portuguese have official status, while English and Indonesian are considered working languages. In addition, there are 32 indigenous languages spoken throughout the country.

Timor-Leste has fertility rates at about 4 children per family, down from 7 in the years immediately following the war for independence (Government of Timor-Leste & UNICEF, 2020; Burke, 2022). Over 40% of the country's population is under the age of 15 (CIA, 2024). Household organization differs by village, with some villages having patrilocal arrangements (i.e., wives move to their husband's village of origin) and others having matrilocal arrangements (i.e., husbands move to their wife's village of origin). Child fostering is also very common, with children often being sent to live with relatives with varying levels of permanence (Spencer & Judge, 2021). Timorese children can begin preschool between the ages of 3 and 5, but as of 2019 only 24% were enrolled in a preschool program (Government of Timor-Leste & UNICEF, 2020). Preschool is more accessible in Dili than in other provinces, and community-based alternative preschools have also emerged in areas where there are no pre-existing ones (Dunne et al., 2025).

Data from national surveys suggest that less than half of preschool-aged children meet developmental norms in literacy (GDS, 2018), but very little research has examined individual differences in Timorese children's language development. A large sample of Timorese children aged 3 to 6 years were used to validate the East Asia-Pacific Early Child Development Scales (EAP-ECDS), which measures children's development across different domains, including early language and literacy skills (Rao et al., 2019). Timorese children from urban communities scored higher than children from rural communities overall, but no significant advantage emerged in any individual domain. No measures of speech exposure or the home environment were collected, and we could not locate any studies that examined children younger than 3 years old.

The present study seeks to contribute much-needed data on under-represented populations in language acquisition research, building on a collaboration between economists working in Timor-Leste and developmental scientists. This led to several strengths of the present work, including an examination of verbal input at a much larger scale than previously examined outside of the U.S. and Europe and a sampling of a wide variety of homes. It also posed unique challenges for tracking verbal interactions in the home. First, the scale of the study, which surveyed hundreds of participants across dozens of villages, meant that surveyors were limited in the time they could spend with individual families. Secondly, many different languages were spoken across these communities and multilingualism within the household was common. Most of the languages are also insufficiently documented by linguists to make transcription feasible at the scale of recruitment sought by the study. Finally, the surveyed communities reported high rates of alloparental care, such that children were watched by many different caregivers throughout the day. As a result, recording a child's interactions with a single caregiver was unlikely to be representative of their total speech input.

Fortunately, recent advances in automatic speech recognition have made it trivial to discreetly collect and analyze massive amounts of audio using wearable devices. Despite the difficulties of automatically parsing child-centered audio (Ryant et al., 2020; Perera et al., 2023), it is now possible to produce estimates of children's vocal production and their exposure to verbal input from others with a reasonable degree of accuracy from long-form (or daylong) recordings (LFRs). A representative snapshot of language exposure can be collected by leaving small recording devices in a shirt or vest worn by the target child over the course of a day. This audio is then submitted to a classification algorithm which attributes different segments of audio to different sources (e.g., speech vs. non-speech, male vs. female, etc.).

Estimates of vocal activity from LFRs have been shown to be highly correlated with transcribed measures of speech (Gilkerson et al., 2017; see Cristia et al., 2020 for review) as well as individual differences in children's language skills (Ma et al., 2024; see Wang et al., 2020 for meta-analysis). LFRs avert several problems associated with traditional methods for studying naturalistic speech. Long-form recording methods significantly reduce the amount of labor necessary to derive speech exposure estimates through transcription. As a result, they are easy to implement at a large-scale, permitting the collection of more and longer samples of naturalistic speech. The discreteness of wearable USB recorders can also improve the ecological validity of language samples, which may be affected by the presence of an observing researcher. LFRs have already been applied to data collection in remote, understudied populations (Casillas, et al., 2020 (Casillas, et al., , 2021;; Cristia et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024; Scaff et al., 2024) and the evaluation of early childhood education interventions (Weber et al., 2017; Dupas et al., 2023; Duflo et al., 2024).

In this paper, we make use of an open-source LFR software called Voice Type Classifier (VTC) (Lavechin et al., 2020) to provide a descriptive analysis of the early verbal environments of Timorese children. We frame our analysis in terms of the following research questions: How much verbal input are children exposed to from adults? And how much input comes from other children? Next, we will address two theoretical issues posed in our Introduction: Do contextual predictors such as education affect children's verbal environments? And how strongly correlated is children's verbal development to their exposure to input from adults vs. other children?

Methods

Participants

The full study sample (prior to exclusions) consisted of 281 families with young children who took part in a randomized control trial aiming to assess the effects of a light-touch intervention (see Supplementary Materials for details). The current study reports on the baseline recordings, which took place immediately following the first intervention action (in the treatment groups, either the mother or the father viewed a video). We excluded data from a child who was only 1 month old; all other children in the sample were at least 11 months of age. Maternal education was not available for one child.

Finally, only recordings with at least one full hour of audio were included, resulting in 6 exclusions. After these exclusions, we include data from 277 children (142 boys, 135 girls, as reported by parents). The children averaged 32.5 months of age (SD = 12.2; range: 11.5-57.9). Children had an average of 2 siblings (range: 0-7). Their average birth order was 2 (range 1-7). While we did not track children's motor development, 90% of children were able to have their weight measured while free standing and 95% were able to have their height measured while free standing. A summary of participants' characteristics can be found in Table 1 and Figure 2. Before collapsing across treatment and control conditions, we confirmed that there were no significant differences in any measure of vocal activity or child characteristics across conditions (Table 2).

Families were randomly sampled from 72 villages (suco), including 40 in the Dili Municipality and 32 across the municipalities of Bobonaro, Manatuto, and Viqueue (3 to 4 children per village). In broad terms, communities in and around Dili are more urban than the other municipalities, so we distinguish the two groups of communities as "urban"

versus "rural". All communities were reachable by car or motorbike. Most of the villages (in Dili or elsewhere) had a primary school, two-thirds had a health center, and about one-third had a market. Nearly all of the villages were involved in conflict during the war for independence. Households in the rural sample are mostly involved in farming and market activities.

To be eligible, a family had to include a couple (aged 20-45 years, married or in a long-term relationship) and at least one child between 1 and 4 years old. Household size averaged 5 people (range 2-11). Each parent was separately asked to report on which language they used most often at home. In total, 17 different languages were reported. The most commonly spoken language was Tetun Prasa, the official language of Timor Leste which serves as the mode of educational instruction and governmental affairs (124 mothers and 120 fathers). For 206 children, the mother and father spoke the same language; the remaining children (71) were reported as hearing at least two different languages at home. Regarding attained education, 22 mothers and 23 fathers reported no formal education, 95 mothers and 71 had some primary and/or secondary education, 120 mothers and 127 fathers finished high school, and 40 mothers and 56 fathers pursued continuing education.

Average education in urban communities (i.e., in the Dili municipality) was higher than in rural communities, with mothers reporting 11.6 and 9.1 years respectively, and fathers reporting 11.9 and 9.1 years.

Materials

Families were given USB recording devices placed in a t-shirt pocket designed specifically for the study. The USB devices have 8GB of storage and 14 hours of battery life, and they record with a sampling frequency of 15kHz and 4-bit depth (192kbps). To ensure recordings were not compromised by device malfunction, enumerators were asked to give families two USB recorders as a fail-safe. The t-shirt pockets were then sewn shut after the device was inserted, preventing the child from taking the device out.

Procedure

Five research teams, consisting of four local enumerators each, were deployed to the sucos to survey participants and collect audio recordings. Consent was obtained from both the enrolled caregiver and from the xefe suco or village leader. Following the baseline intervention survey, enumerators gave the USBs and t-shirts to consenting families, who were asked to let the devices record continuously until they ran out of batteries. USBs were distributed either first thing in the morning or no later than 3pm for households visited in the afternoon and collected either that evening or the following morning. As a result, most recordings began between 8am and 3pm. Prior to handing off the devices, surveyors recorded themselves saying the date and time, the name of the child, and the name of the suco. This was done to track when recording took place and to align recordings with participant survey data. Families were asked to avoid turning their mobile phones on speaker for the duration of the recording to prevent violations of privacy. USB recordings were downloaded to an external hard drive each night, and upon completion of the field work, uploaded to a central repository for further analysis.

Vocalization metrics

Audio recordings were submitted to Voice Type Classifier (VTC) to estimate the number of vocalizations from the target child and vocalizations close enough to be heard by the target child (Lavechin et al., 2020). VTC is an open-source speaker diarization software based on a neural network architecture that was trained on child-centered audio recordings from multiple languages across urban and rural settings. The software identifies instances of speech collected from child-worn recording devices and classifies these instances into one of four speaker categories: target child (i.e., the child wearing the recording device), female adult, male adult, or other child. For instances of overlapping speech, VTC will identify multiple active speaker categories. Using English-language child-centered speech data, Lavechin et al. (2020) found that VTC was more accurate than LENA, the most widely used speaker diarization software, in classifying all speaker categories. From the processed audio, we calculated vocalization counts per hour, as well as the total duration (in minutes) of vocalizations per hour, for each speaker category using the ChildProject package in Python (Gautheron et al., 2023).

A limitation of VTC is the inability to distinguish between vocalizations directed to the target child and vocalizations directed at others, a distinction that is relevant for children's learning (Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Shneidman et al., 2013; Weisleder & Fernald, 2013). To estimate child-directed speech, we distinguish vocalizations produced during conversational turns, defined as an audio sequence in which a target child vocalization occurred within a fixed interval of time before or after an utterance by another speaker. A systematic review of studies examining conversational turns between parents and their children found that the time between turns is typically much smaller than the 5-second interval used by systems like LENA (Nguyen et al., 2022). Other studies have thus recalculated conversational turns from shorter intervals (Scaff et al., 2024). We decided on a 1-second interval in our study. Although co-occurring vocalizations are not a perfect indication of conversation (Ferjan Ramírez et al., 2021), previous work with LENA has used this operationalizing of conversational turns to approximate child-directed speech (Romeo et al., 2018; Ellwood-Lowe et al., 2022). As an additional check, we compared the pitch of vocalizations within and outside of conversational turns across speaker types. With few exceptions, child-directed speech has been found to have a higher pitch than adult-directed speech pitch (Cox et al., 2023). Thus, we examined whether vocalization pitch was elevated when produced during a conversational turn. For each speaker type, pitch was measured by taking the median semitone of each vocalization and calculating the average across all vocalizations within versus outside of conversational turns. We found that pitch was higher within conversational turns for female adults (ß = 1.13, t = 34.59, p < .001) and male adults (ß = 0.58, t = 14.27, p < .001), as well as for other children (ß = 0.91, t = 27.91, p < .001).

The recording onset was determined by listening to the earliest audio file for the enumerator's speech. Because audio recording started and ended at different times for different participants, sometimes resulting in overnight recordings, we only consider audio data recorded between 7am and 9pm, a common span of time in which most families had some amount of audio and within which we believed families were the least likely to be sleeping (Figures S1-S3 in Supplementary Materials). Most children (231) had at least two viable recordings. In cases where children had multiple audio files collected on a single recorder (either due to intentional manipulation or hardware malfunction), these files were concatenated into a single recording. On average, we could include data from 8.2 hours (SD = 2.2; range: 2.4-12.4) recordings occurring between 7am and 9pm.

Validation

Because we use computational processes to derive our vocalizations metrics, it was important to know whether they accurately reflected children's verbal environments (i.e., validity) and whether they were self-consistent (i.e., reliability).

Validity. We employed a previously used validation procedure to compare VTC classification to human annotation of a subset of clips (Cristia et al., 2021; Scaff et al., 2024). Three research assistants were recruited from speech and language pathology programs to annotate sampled audio clips for speech from different speakers. Annotators were unfamiliar with Timorese culture and did not speak any of the languages spoken in the recordings. Two assistants were first trained on gold standard recordings that had previously been annotated by the lead author until their accuracy reached an acceptable threshold, measured by calculating their F-score (introduced in more detail below; Annotator1 = 89%, Annotator2 = 82%). The third assistant's accuracy was checked at the end of the annotation period with the other two annotators against a final gold-standard recording (Annotator1 = 65%, Annotator2 = 84%, Annotator3 = 71%). Together, they annotated 161 1-minute-long clips taken from 11 recordings. The number of clips annotated was limited by the time available to the research assistants; however, this number falls within the range of previous evaluations of the LENA long-form recording system (i.e., longer than 8 out of 30 studies reviewed in Cristia et al., 2020). Audio recordings were binned by target child age and a child's recordings were randomly selected from each bin to maximize the range of ages. The clips were sampled periodically from each hour of audio per child recorded, skipping the first 30 minutes of the recording to let families get used to the recording device and annotating 1 minute of audio for every hour thereafter (Scaff et al., 2024). By comparing human annotated recordings to VTC automatic outputs, we were able to calculate recall and precision scores for each speaker type using 100ms frames (Cristia et al., 2021), which are given in Figures 3 and 4. We took the harmonic mean of these measures to produce a single measure of accuracy called an F-score. We found F-scores of 55% for female adult, 43% for male adult, 36% for other child, and 60% for target child. These were somewhat lower than what had been found by Cristia et al. (2023) in Vanuatu (female adult = 60%, male adult = 56%, other child = 46%, target child = 73%) but comparable to averages found in previous validations of LFRs across different contexts (Cristia et al., 2021). That said, errors in frame-level accuracy do not represent our dependent measures for some of the analyses, namely vocalization counts. Following previous work in LENA, we calculated Pearson correlations between vocalization counts in human annotated clips and VTC-estimated counts (female adult r = 0.78, male adult r = 0.77, other child r = 0.75, target child r = 0.82).

Correlations were high for all speakers and comparable in size to those found for other corpora of long-form recordings (see Supplementary Materials in Bergelson et al., 2023).

Reliability.

To gauge the consistency of our measures, we calculated split-half correlations on each half of the recording. This allowed us to determine whether our estimates of verbal input were consistent throughout our recordings. To do this, we calculated vocalization measures for every hour of each recording and took the correlation between even and odd recording hours. We were unable to locate other studies using LFRs that employed such a split-half reliability evaluative procedure and as a result we did not have a comparative benchmark. However, we found high split-half reliability for all categories, suggesting high internal consistency across speaker categories (female adult r = 0.86, male adult r = 0.79, other child r = 0.91, target child r = 0.89).

Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (v 4.5.0). For each of our analyses, we constructed mixed effects Gaussian linear regression models using lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) The models took vocalizations per hour from each speaker type as the dependent variable. In addition to the predictor variables, each model controlled for the intervention trial arm. A separate analysis of the control group only is included in Supplementary Materials (Figures S4 and S5). Each model was also fitted with random intercepts by child (since almost all children contributed two recordings) and village (to control for regional effects). This gave the general model equation vocalizations ∼ predictors + condition + (1|child) + (1|village).

The first set of analyses examined differences in children's received input from various sources, using speaker identity as a predictor. To keep our analysis consistent with prior literature on verbal input, we report descriptive statistics in minutes per hour of vocalizations (e.g., Casillas et al., 2020; Cristia et al., 2023). To control for the varying length of recordings from different children, we report the means and standard deviations of vocalizations weighted by recording duration.

The second set of analyses examined child-and household-level factors that have previously been found to predict differences in children's language input. For this analysis, we use vocalizations counts per hour as our outcome variable, consistent with prior studies using LFRs (e.g., Gilkerson et al., 2017; Bergelson et al., 2023). We consider as predictors child age, child gender, caregiver's highest attained education, and number of siblings.

Because child birth order was almost perfectly correlated with sibship size (r = 0.96 we opted not to include it in our model. Likewise, because both parents resided at home in most cases (249), children's number of siblings was equivalent to household size. We fit separate models for each input type: female adult, male adult, and other children. We separately examine group-level associations with the urbanness of a community (i.e., whether families live in more urban communities in and around Dili or more rural areas in other municipalities instead).

The third set of analyses focused on predictors of key child vocalization rate.

Children's rate of vocalization in LFRs has been associated with their performance on formal measures of language ability (Gilkerson et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020) and has been used as a measure of vocal development in previous studies (e.g., Bergelson et al., 2023; but see Mitsven et al., 2022 for a critical comparison with other developmental measures). Here we use it as a proxy for individual differences in verbal development. We started by fitting a single model predicting child vocalization counts using the same childand household-level predictors as in the second analysis. We then fit three separate models, which included as additional predictors verbal input from each of the three input speaker types. To determine whether input improved the fit of our base model, we conducted likelihood ratio testing, comparing our original and new models, and reported corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) (Burnham & Anderson, 2004). Because vocalizations during conversational turns are calculated with reference to target child vocalizations, we do not use this measure as a predictor.

For each analysis, all continuous predictors were centered at zero and converted into z-scores. Thus, effect sizes are reported as standardized beta coefficients, which are interpretable in terms of standard deviation increases rather than exact values, allowing for comparability across variables with different scales. Because we cannot calculate a traditional R² for mixed effects models, we instead report Nakagawa's marginal R² as a measure of absolute goodness of fit for each model (Nakagawa et al., 2017). We use the R package performance to calculate this value (Lüdecke et al., 2021). In addition, we used the car package (Fox & Weisberg, 2019) to calculate variance inflation factors for each of our models to ensure our results were not affected by multicollinearity between predictors.

Results

How much verbal input are children exposed to from different speakers?

We found that children heard roughly 15.36 (SD = 6.11) minutes of vocalization per hour (Table 3). This represented an average of 6.29 min/hr of female adult, 2.09 min/hr of male adult, and 6.98 min/hr of other child vocalizations (Figure 5). We found more vocalization minutes per hour from other children than from male adults (ß = -1.30, t = -32.16, p < .001) or female adults (ß = -0.19, t = -4.60, p < .001), and more from female adults than male adults (ß = -1.11, t = -27.57, p < .001).

Children heard an estimated 6.17 minutes of vocalization per hour during conversational turns, or roughly 40% of total verbal input (Table 3). Considering vocalizations within conversational turns, other children vocalized most often as a whole and as a percentage of total verbal input (3.88 min/hr, 60%), followed by female adults (1.90 min/hr, 32%), then male adults (0.38 min/hr, 21%). These differences were statistically significant: other children vocalized more than male adults (ß = -1.50, t = -42.54, p < .001) and female adults (ß = -0.85, t = -24.05, p < .001), and female adults vocalized more than male adults (ß = -0.65, t = -18.49, p < .001).

What factors predict differences in children's verbal environments?

Our analysis revealed children's speech input is correlated with a number of different factors (Table 4). Older children were found to hear more input from other children than younger children (ß = 0.29, t = 5.18, p < .001) (Figure 6). In addition, children with more siblings were found to hear more input from other children than children with fewer siblings (ß = 0.22, t = 3.91, p < .001) (Figure 7). Neither age nor sibling number was significantly associated with differences in input from adults. Surprisingly, neither maternal nor paternal education was associated with vocalizations from any speaker type.

Because education attainment was higher among parents from urban (i.e. Dili) households than those in rural municipalities, we also examined differences in input between these groups by including a binary fixed effect (Dili versus not) and removing the random intercept for village. We found that children from Dili heard less verbal input from female adults (ß = -0.26, t = -2.30, p = 0.022) and male adults (ß = -0.35, t = -3.15, p = 0.002) (Figure 9). No differences in child age, number of siblings, or recording duration were found between these groups, nor were any differences in vocalizations from other children observed. In every model, no variance inflation factor value was greater than 1.60, well below the standard problematic range of 5 to 10 (Stine, 1995).

Does verbal input from other speakers predict children's vocalizations?

Target children produced 252 (SD=118) vocalizations per hour on average. Examining our base model in Table 5, we found that target child vocalizations were significantly and positively associated with child age (ß = 0.25, t = 4.46, p < .001) (Figure 6) and number of siblings (ß = 0.14, t = 2.44, p = 0.015) (Figure 7), suggesting that child volubility is associated with the presence of other children in the home and may grow with age. Child vocalizations were less frequent in households with higher maternal education (ß = -0.14, t = -1.99, p = 0.048) (Figure 8). Children from communities within the urban Dili municipality were also found to vocalize less often than children from rural municipalities (ß = -0.33, t = -2.90, p = 0.004) (Figure 9).

Fitting verbal inputs to this model, we found that vocalizations from every speaker type positively and significantly predicted child vocalizations and improved fit over the base model, as indicated by statistically significant likelihood ratio tests and reductions in AICc (Table 5). Verbal input from other children was most highly correlated with target child vocalizations (ß = 0.83, t = 30.35, p < .001), followed by female adult input (ß = 0.55, t = 17.26, p < .001) and male adult input (ß = 0.18, t = 4.72, p < .001) (Figure 10).

After adding other child vocalizations to the model, target child vocalizations were no longer significantly associated with child age, number of siblings, or maternal education, suggesting shared explanatory variance: child age and number of siblings were positively associated with other child vocalizations, and while maternal education was not significantly related to vocalizations in our models, it was negatively associated with number of siblings (r(275) = -0.23, p < 0.001). No variance inflation factor value exceeded 1.60.

Are associations between input and child vocalizations robust to speaker confusion?

We conducted an additional analysis to ensure that these correlations were significant after controlling for speaker misattribution by the algorithm. For instance, if VTC confused other children for the target child, then this could account for the large correlation between vocalizations by these speakers. We used an approach described in Gautheron et al. (preprint, 2025). We simulated 100,000 realistic datasets where the null hypothesis was true and thus any correlation between speakers would be spurious. These datasets were generated based on discrepancies in vocalization counts by speaker between the VTC and our human annotators in sampled clips and thus allowed us to incorporate the confusion error rates found in our dataset into determining the significance of our results. For target child and female vocalizations, where r(275) = 0.52 in our data, only 1.40% of datasets simulated under the null hypothesis of no association had correlations larger or equal in size to that, suggesting algorithmic confusion is unlikely to explain the level of association observed. The same cannot be said for correlations between target child and male adult vocalizations, where 12.50% of simulated datasets had correlations larger or equal to ours (r(275) = 0.22). Finally, for target child and other child vocalizations, only 1.10% of datasets produced larger or equally sized correlations (r(275) = 0.80). These results suggest that target child vocalizations were significantly associated with vocalizations from female adults and other children after controlling for algorithmic confusion, but that correlations with male adult vocalizations could plausibly be due to speaker misattribution.

Discussion

This paper provides a first look at the early verbal environments of young Timorese children. Our novel analysis relies entirely on a completely automated process run on 4200 hours of audio recordings collected from 277 children living in both urban and rural agricultural communities. The resulting increase in statistical power combined with the flexibility of our data collection method gives us a unique glimpse into the everyday lives of children in Timor-Leste.

First we measured how often children encounter verbal input from adults and other children. We found that children in Timor-Leste are exposed to about 15 minutes of vocalizations per hour. Most verbal input comes from other children (6.98 minutes; 45%), followed by female adults (6.29 minutes; 41%) and male adults (2.09 minutes; 14%). We can benchmark these results against those described in Cristia et al. (2023), which used VTC to compare speech input in two settings: rural, remote villages in Vanuatu, and urban English-speaking households in the U.S. (VanDam, 2018). We find that our Timorese children hear more verbal input than children in Vanuatu (11min/hr) and roughly as much verbal input as children in the U.S. (16.44 min/hr). However, the proportion of vocalizations from different speakers was more similar to Vanuatu, where children heard 42% from other children (compared to 27% in the U.S.) and only 12% from male adults (compared to 25% in the U.S.). Children in Timor-Leste heard more verbal input from all speaker types compared to children in Vanuatu (5.09 min/hr from female adults, 1.24 min/hr from male adults, and 4.89 min/hr from other children).

To approximate the proportion of child-directed verbal input, we also calculated vocalization minutes per hour during conversational turns, finding that 6.17 min/hr were produced during conversational turns. As with total vocalizations, most were produced by other children (3.88 min/hr, 60%), followed by female adults (1.90 min/hr, 32%), then male adults (0.38 min/hr, 21%). Our findings suggest that while total exposure to vocalizations from female adults and other children are similarly frequent, children engage in a majority of their conversations (over twice as many) with other children. These results align broadly with findings from studies of rural agricultural societies, where child-directed speech from adults is relatively infrequent and children spend much of their time under the supervision of other children (Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Cristia, et al., 2019; Cristia et al., 2023; Loukatou et al., 2022; Scaff et al., 2024).

Second, we examined associations between verbal input and characteristics of the home. We found that older children heard more speech from other children, consistent with previous studies of input in smaller-scale societies (e.g., Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Cristia et al., 2019; Cristia et al., 2023) where children are frequently reported as spending more time around their peers as they mature (see Lew-Levy & Amir, 2024). We also found an association between input from other children and the number of siblings in the home. Notably, the number of siblings was not related to adult input, contrary to a prior study finding less adult input in American homes with more siblings (Laing & Bergelson, 2024).

We did not find a significant association between verbal input and parental education. However, we did find that children with more educated mothers vocalized less.

We also found that children from urban communities in the Dili Municipality, who had higher household education, heard less adult verbal input and vocalized less than children living in rural communities elsewhere. Integrating our results with those summarized in the Introduction suggests that the relationship between household education and input may be more complicated than previously thought, particularly when considering societies where subsistence agriculture is common. In these societies, education can create opportunities for parents to engage in the wage economy and pursue jobs that are often far from home, resulting in children being exposed to less speech from adults (Ma et al., 2024). This was the causal interpretation put forward for the observation mentioned in the Introduction regarding the downstream effects of high school access on the later life outcomes of students from rural, low-income rural households in Ghana (Duflo et al., 2024). Using LENA long-form recording devices to track changes in the home, they found that children of the male scholarship recipients heard less speech from female adults than male controls. They attributed this to their finding that male scholarship recipients are less likely to live with their children, increasing the amount of daily labor single mothers engage in and reducing their opportunity to verbally engage with their children. Similarly, more educated parents in Timor-Leste might be more likely to engage in work outside of their villages, resulting in less time at home, particularly since alloparental care by children could ensure children's safety and well-being. Future research could consider collecting reports on who cares for the child throughout the recording day to directly test this predicted causal chain.

This would be relevant as we found some potential effects on children's vocalization rates:

Children with mothers who have graduated from high school vocalized less than children of mothers with less formal education, contrary to other studies using LFRs which find positive or null effects on child vocalizations (Gilkerson et al., 2017; Bergelson et al., 2023).

The observation that children from Dili vocalized less often than children in other regions is also consistent with this account.

Finally, we found that, after adjusting for speaker misclassification, verbal input from female adults and from other children were associated with target children's vocalizations, with input from other children having the largest effect size. This pattern of results resembles that documented in a smaller sample of children growing up in Vanuatu (Cristia et al., 2023), but differs starkly from some previous research documenting weaker (non-significant) relationships between children's outcome and peer input, as opposed to significant ones when predicting outcomes from adult input, in another small-scale community (Yucatec Mayan; Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012). The differences across studies are too numerous to shed light on potential causal chains that may explain divergences in results. Resolving this question quantitatively is important given contrasting theoretical frameworks, some positing that children provide lower quality input and others that argue peers support language development, as discussed in the Introduction. We are uncertain that a meta-analytic framework would be able to resolve these discrepancies, given the lack of research in this area. However, we believe that a collaborative effort similar to Bergelson et al. (2023), with greater representation of communities in which infants and young children spend substantial portions of their day with other children, may be able to make progress on these questions. Such a collaboration would allow quantitative tests of whether correlations between adult and child outcomes are truly weaker in communities where other children (as opposed to adults) provide larger amounts of input; and under what circumstances peer input may be a better predictor of outcomes than adult input.

Limitations

While our analysis has numerous advantages over traditional methods for researching children's verbal environments, it also possesses limitations. In particular, VTC is more accurate for female adult and target child vocalizations than for male adult and other child vocalizations (Lavechin et al., 2020). This reflects speaker type imbalances in training data, with male adult and other children's vocalizations being rarer than target child and female adult vocalizations. Thus, one remedy would be for researchers to record more audio in settings where conversations between children and their fathers or peers are prevalent, increasing the amount of training data for these speaker types. A newer version of VTC with higher classification accuracy for these speaker categories is currently being developed (Charlot et al., 2025). A reanalysis of our data using this version of VTC replicates most of the findings we described in our Results (see Figures S4-S6 and Tables S5-S6 in Supplementary Materials).

The lower accuracy of VTC on other child input is especially concerning given its large correlation with target child vocalizations, which would occur if target child vocalizations were misclassified as other child vocalizations. This has previously been documented in other studies using VTC (Cristia et al., 2023), as well as in studies using LENA (Cristia et al., 2021). While it is possible that speaker misclassification might contribute to the size of the association between other child vocalizations and target child vocalizations and to the overall frequency of speech from other children, we do not believe this compromises our findings. First, correlations between human annotated and VTC vocalization counts are fairly high, suggesting that our measures are still sensitive to differences across individuals. Second, other child speech is still significantly associated with target child vocalizations after correcting for misclassification. Third, verbal input from other children is also associated with the number of siblings in the home, suggesting that this measure is sensitive to differences in household composition. Fourth, VTC finds lower amounts of other child speech in the urban U.S.-based Cougar corpus, consistent with comparative studies of speech input from direct observation (Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Vogt et al., 2015). Finally, a reanalysis of our data with a newer version of VTC finds lower but still large correlations between target child and other child vocalizations (see Supplementary Materials). Thus, we believe our analysis captures a genuine and important aspect of early language socialization in Timor-Leste in the form of vocalizations from other children.

Another important limitation is that there is no open-source classifier for addressee type, so as to distinguish between child-directed speech and overheard speech. Bang et al. (2023) have proposed an algorithm based on English LENA data, which is thus not available for the VTC approach. This limitation is particularly pressing for the use of LFRs to study predictors of language development, as many studies have failed to find that individual differences in overheard speech exposure predict language development (Shneidman & Goldin-Meadow, 2012; Shneidman et al., 2013; Weisleder & Fernald, 2013).

Although LFR outputs are still broadly associated with language outcomes despite failing to make this distinction (Wang et al., 2020), being able to identify child-directed vocalizations is desirable for many researchers. Some studies randomly sample from LFRs and use human annotation to identify periods of child-directed vs. overheard speech (Casillas et al., 2020 (Casillas et al., , 2021;; Bunce et al., 2024). However, this process is extremely labor-intensive, limiting the amount of data that can be analyzed, and requires annotators who can understand the languages spoken (Bergelson et al., 2023). In our study, we attempted to approximate child-directed vocalizations using conversational turns as a proxy. While this approach gets us closer, it is also confounded with our measures of target child vocalizations, preventing us from using it as a predictor of language outcomes. Lastly, our study uses child vocalizations as a proxy for children's verbal development. While many studies have found vocalization rates are positively associated with children's age and performance on language testing (Gilkerson et al., 2017; see Wang et al., 2020 for meta-analysis), we cannot associate vocalizations with any particular domain of language development, such as vocabulary or phonology. In addition, the data presently available only allowed us to compute correlations between input and outcomes taken at the same time, rather than longitudinally. Thus, we are limited in the causal claims we can make about the impact of speaker input on development. Some more recent studies have attempted to use LFRs to derive more exact measures of child's language development. For instance, Mitsven et al. (2022) found that while child vocalizations were associated with concurrent performance on language testing, machine-learning-annotated phonemic diversity of child vocalizations was a better predictor of children's language ability (Mitsven et al., 2022). There has also been some success employing "citizen science" platforms to organize quick human annotation of large amounts of long-form audio data for indicators of vocal maturity, such as proportion of canonical syllables produced (Cychosz et al., 2021; Semenzin et al., 2021). These types of developmental metrics would be highly valuable for educational interventions. Because they are derived from naturalistic audio recordings, they do not require trained surveyors to administer. They may also provide a more ecologically valid measure of language development than a direct child assessment or a caregiver reported measure, which may not be contextually or culturally appropriate for the populations tested. They would also allow researchers to examine potential relationships between children's language development and caregivers' verbal responsiveness via conversational turns, which are derived from and therefore confounded with child vocalization rates (Romeo et al., 2018).

Conclusion

Our study uses new advances in long-form recording methods to investigate differences in children's verbal input in Timor-Leste. This method allowed us to examine thousands of hours of audio collected from 277 children living in different regions of the country. We found that the Timorese children in our sample were exposed to a large amount of verbal input throughout the day. Our findings highlight the importance of other children as sources of input. Other children were found to be the most common source of input in our recordings, particularly in conversation. Input from other children was also the greatest predictor of target child vocalizations. Children heard comparable amounts of verbal input as similarly-aged children in a U.S. dataset, but heard relatively more input from other children and less from male adults. We found no overall relationship between parent education and the verbal environment at home, but children living in more urban communities (whose parents were more educated on average) heard less adult input and vocalized less than children living in rural communities. These findings suggest that the impact of education on the early speech environment is complex and potentially dependent on how education affects the presence of adult caregivers in the home. Our work also demonstrates that VTC is sensitive to differences in children's language learning environments in a country where speech input has not previously been studied, supporting its use as a tool for large-scale data collection and analysis in novel settings. This research is the product of long-term interdisciplinary collaboration between psychologists, linguists, and economists, united by a shared desire to better understand and support children's language development globally.
