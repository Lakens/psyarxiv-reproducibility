No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction

The aims and purpose of inpatient care

There has been surprisingly little definition or discussion of the role, function and design of acute inpatient mental healthcare. An interview study with multidisciplinary staff found that patients were admitted because they appear likely to harm themselves or others, and because they are suffering from severe mental illness, and/or because they or their family/community require respite, and/or because they have insufficient support and supervision available to them . The tasks of acute inpatient care are then to keep patients safe, assess their problems, treat their mental illness, meet their basic care needs, and provide physical healthcare. These tasks are completed through 24-hour staff presence, containment, treatment provision, and complex organisation and management.

Later, Bowers et al., developed a conceptual model of inpatient care that identified the primary admission tasks may include any or all of assessment, treatment of acute illness, providing safe and highly tolerant accommodation, rehabilitation, and the resolution of personal distress. However, patients also bring with them other life and health problems. While not a cause of admission, these problems are also managed by inpatient staff and, where they can be resolved, represent an 'admission bonus'.

Inpatient wards are uniquely able to enforce treatment, provide regular observation to contain risks, and tolerate behaviour which would be unmanageable or unacceptable in the community. Inpatient admission also offers respite from and space to address stressors in the person's home environment, and the potential, through 24-hour care, to provide high levels of interpersonal contact and therapeutic engagement, often through a range of ward activities .

In England, the importance of activities has been recognised in the new Culture of Care standards for mental health inpatient services.

Psychiatric inpatient care is most often unpopular -with both patients and many staffand can be traumatising, re-traumatising and coercive . Tensions exist between keeping severely mentally distressed people safe whilst trying to build and sustain engaging, accepting, therapeutic relationships and milieu, often within health care systems and organisations that are inadequately funded and woefully understaffed.

Yet those staff that commit their time and energies to providing inpatient care often do so with great skill and humanity. A cross-national comparative case study reported positive practice within acute inpatient wards, with evidence of safe, respectful, compassionate care.

Patients are at significant risk of relapse and suicide after discharge from inpatient care with lack of social connectedness likely to be a prime factor . The National Confidential Inquiry into Suicide and Safety in Mental Health (2024) reported that 13% of all patient suicides were completed within 3 months of hospital discharge, with the highest number of deaths occurring within 48 hours post discharge. This highlights the importance of providing patients in hospital with coping skills before discharge.

Providing effective care and support at these crucial times is of huge importance to patients, their families and mental health staff to maintain safety and stability following discharge.

Understanding activities and their value Bowers et al (2005) investigated and described the purpose and philosophy of inpatient mental healthcare and found, alongside the use of medication and therapeutic engagement by staff, a 'diverse and rather idiosyncratic range of psychosocial treatments on offer' (p.20). Interspersed with these were a range of other organised or self-directed activities that may serve a range of purposes including minimising boredom, providing relaxation, promoting interaction, or maintaining physical health. Such activities may include art, music, computer games, social media, gardening, exercise and discussion groups.

Evidence suggests engagement in activity is important and may be beneficial to patient outcomes. The City128 study found that the provision of a comprehensive programme of activities was associated with lower rates of self-harm, reduced more serious selfharm and was highly valued by patients . The provision of activities has also been linked with reduced levels of restraint , increased levels of recovery and improved patient experience.

Concern about the lack of activities on mental health inpatient wards, with a lack of therapeutic direction on the wards with little for patients to do, is long-standing . Prior to this study, we published a systematic review of the literature on service users' views on ward activities . Key findings included: • Boredom is a big issue on inpatient mental health wards that is linked to poor patient satisfaction, feelings of frustration and increased incidents of self-harm and aggression.

This is even more so for people detained under mental health legislation.

• Where wards have a good range of activities, for example art, music, computer games, gardening and exercise, patients felt less bored and had improved well-being.

• Over many years, studies have reported a lack of activities, especially during evenings and weekends. Little is known about what activities service users want, or how activities might be meaningful for them.

• Few studies have asked patients about what activities would be useful and why.

The lack of activity provision and the boredom experienced by patients remains a prominent issue, as highlighted by a recent scoping review on boredom, meaningful activities and the occupational therapist's role on inpatient wards . They concluded that research into new models need to be developed to address the persistent problem of boredom and its negative consequences.

Activities and people with psychosis

It is important to ensure the therapeutic needs and priorities for inpatient care, including activities, are obtained from people with psychosis . People with schizophrenia or other psychoses have the second highest number of admissions experiencing psychosis who are currently receiving care from an acute mental health inpatient ward using a mixed-methods Q-methodology approach.

Analysis revealed four distinct themes relating to factors that promote recovery in the acute mental health inpatient setting: i) stability, independence and 'keeping a roof over your head'; ii) hope, optimism and enhancing well-being; iii) personal change, self-management and social support; and iv) symptom reduction through mental health support.

They concluded that staff on acute mental health wards need to ensure that they are considering the personal recovery needs of inpatients. Symptom reduction was valued by some, but broad psychosocial factors were also a priority.

Those with psychosis face additional barriers to accessing community activities that may benefit their recovery. People living with psychosis are far more likely than other service users to experience stigma, discrimination, marginalisation within families, and exclusion from community organisations . Black people and people of colour are disproportionately affected by racism and show increased rates of psychosis.

Furthermore, socio-economic inequalities and poor life skills of people experiencing psychosis are associated with higher rates of health-risk behaviours that are further exacerbated by illness factors such as lack of motivation or the adverse effects of medication.

It is therefore important that research exploring activities and their benefits to recovery consider patients who experience psychosis as they are often overlooked within research but are likely to benefit considerably from engaging with meaningful activities. Aligning with this, a few patients rejected the idea of connection on the ward as helpful to their recovery as they were being cautious of others in relation to safety concerns, disingenuous relationships and high turnover of patients, which lead to a lack of motivation to engage with others and group activities.

What's the point in doing community activities to get to know each other, then all of a sudden you've got to go? That's causing a relationship breakdown and that's one of the things that's making us mentally unwell. Rejection, and having a sense of nothing. (a28, patient, non-binary)

Staff and patient connection

Many patients also noted that activities allow for a space to break down barriers between staff and patients and create a connection between the two. Activities can help interactions with staff by improving communication in an informal setting that allows staff and patients to get to know each other. Staff also suggested that activities can be beneficial for patient-staff relationships.

When we play football with the staff and the patients it's really good. Playing sports in that way means you get to be friendly with people you wouldn't normally and breaks down a bit of that barrier, so you get to see the staff better. (b14, patient, male)

There's been Zumba classes, or sessions on the ward and staff will get into that.... there's something about that kind of community cohesion on a ward that breaks down people's professions and hierarchy and everybody's doing the same thing.

That's definitely helped staff and patients to get better and share something on an equal level with them. It's not teaching this thing, it's just all learning at the same time. (c23, manager: staff, female)

Activities coordinators were identified by patients as staff with a good relationship with patients due to their interactions with patients during activities. Patients emphasised that having good facilitators, especially those with lived experience and external facilitators, can create a positive connection and trust.

Volunteers help as well because they're in between staff and a patient. Usually they have lived experience, so it makes it a little bit less awkward because they can engage with it, act silly and that will bring people out of their shells. We had volunteers on the old ward, and they helped bridge the gap sometimes. (b11, patient, female)

It was recognised by staff that having groups where staff attend or where patients and staff run them together, provided the potential to break down barriers and level the power dynamics. A need for staff to get involved and be open and willing to share with patients was also highlighted to improve connection.

And there's one of the interventions -'get to know me' -which is about staff and patients getting to know each other, and they'll build up that rapport. But I think that is so powerful.... And they appreciate it. Because I know so much about them (b1, staff, female)

Hope and Optimism

Patients and staff often described activities as beneficial for creating hope and optimism for many patients.

Positive thinking and aspirations for the future

Interviewees found that activities create a space for patients to think positively and value efforts. Several patients mentioned that activities are enjoyable and create a positive mindset as they are something to look forward to, they can provide respite from the ward and lift the mood on the ward. Patients also valued the facilitators' efforts and the opportunities when they arose for skills development.

I like going for a walk, or the pottery is something to look forward to. (c20, patient, female) I mean, for me, again, activities helping recovery, when I did the Music sessions, I found that beneficial. And, like I said, with my leave, when I go and I do my activities, in the gym, and that sort of stuff, I find it's a nice little respite. (a31, patient, male)

Echoing the above, staff acknowledged that activities also create positive feelings during and after sessions, with activities able to recharge the patients' spirits.

Having at least one activity a day for these patients, I think it's so important to be able to have something to look forward to in the day. Staff recognised the importance of having something to look forward to that can distract from negative thinking and possibly shift the patient's perspective of their time in the hospital to a more positive experience, thereby supporting hope and engagement in their recovery. Activities might help them to see that it's beneficial to come to hospital. Some patients recognised activities as showing patients that there is hope for recovery and the future with some activities helping them find and build a path forward.

The one [activity] about the story, like story of your life, the tree of life group, when are thinking about, trying to chat about what is going on and just think about your life, what is your path, stuff like that.

You can just have a chance to think about it again and think through and like, "Oh yes." (a21, patient, sex not provided) When you are transitioning from that dark point, coming back to myself, the activities are going to help to keep you engaged on something else. (a29, patient, female)

Staff frequently noted that activities can provide hope for recovery and aspirations for the future and a space to plan. They recognised the importance of activities for providing a sense of purpose and achievement, often through the creation of something. Some patients' hopes for the future included a desire to volunteer, work or study, which some activities could help develop the skills for. Patients often wished to continue activities in the community but expressed the need for signposting to relevant community groups. Staff also recognised the need to encourage patients to continue activities in the community when discharged. Activities can help patients tap into their potential, reconnect with past interests and provide them with the hope that they can do things that can make them feel better.

Motivation to change

A few patients noted that activities created motivation to want to change by keeping them focused and on track for recovery, which was seen as achievable through helping change negative thought patterns and building confidence.

is your thoughts that break down, you start not doing things properly, end up doing the wrong thing because of the thoughts in your head where if you are doing something you focus on it doesn't affect you as much. Patients also found that activities could be a source of hope inspiring relationships for some. Activities with lived experience volunteers had the potential to inspire patients, as hearing about other people's success fostered hope and a belief in recovery.

When I saw the volunteers that have lived a life after the hospital it made it seem more real that there's life outside and it's going to be fine. (b10, patient, female)

Creating a positive sense of identity

A handful of patients recognised the role that activities played in providing a positive sense of identity. Activities achieved this by allowing for reconnection with previous interests which provided a sense of normality or feeling like themselves again and reminded the patient of happier times. Trying new activities assisted with a positive sense of identity as it provided a source of confidence for patients.

The thing I noticed with activities is people with identity issues, that I've suffered with myself, is it brings you back together. It makes you a whole person. You're building yourself. (a8, patient, male) Having the opportunity through activities to reconnect with one's body was noted by one patient as important for a positive sense of self, another patient noted that when you are on the ward there is a disconnect from self as you can look different which negatively impacts identity. Yoga was a lot of slow movement, just calming down. A lot of calming down for anxiety if you're feeling distressed. Just stretching and stuff like that. Activities were also identified by some patients as helping with regaining a positive sense of identity through tackling stigma. This was achieved by providing a space to discuss and make sense of their diagnosis with others.

It takes the stigma out of it. A lot of people still stigmatise, at least we can laugh about it, that's helpful. (b15, patient, female)

Staff also acknowledged that activities could allow for a reconnection with something the patient enjoys, a sense of accomplishment, expression of creativity and reconnection with one's body, which is all recognised as contributing to a positive sense of self. Community support and social connection also were noted as helpful for regaining a positive sense of self.

I think a lot of the activities there can be a sense of achievement, there can be a better sense of self, there can be interpersonal connections. I think there are so many things that doing these activities bring about and they are helpful for wellbeing. (c1, staff, female)

Providing meaning

Meaning in mental health experience

Activities for some patients are helpful for giving meaning to their mental health experience. Patients highlighted the need to provide a space to share their experiences and diagnosis with other patients or staff, with it being noted that there is often limited time on the ward to do this or limited information provided to assist with understanding their diagnosis and experiences.

But psychosis effects everyone different, some people see that someone is after them, or something else, activities is actually going out there and talking to them about what their symptoms are and what they are going through. So maybe having a group where you can sit and discuss your psychosis. It affects everyone very differently. (a3, patient, male)

Staff also recognised the need to provide a space through activities for patients to share about their experiences and discuss their diagnosis, which some suggested could be achieved through creative outlets or discussion groups with other patients.

think a chance to express feelings, challenging emotions in different art forms, different activities, different spaces, I think is really important. That chance to recover in different ways. (c21, community staff, female)

To assist with recovery, patients and staff identified the need to discuss personal visions for wellness, to understand recovery in their own terms, and work with their diagnosis rather than against it. Patients identified activities as helpful for focusing and understanding their mind and some activities helped with grounding and accepting their circumstances and even limitations.

But I feel like, instead of that, it's like we need to find a way for people to work with what they've got because some things can't be changed. In terms of activities, like I say, play, imagination. Just working with that within the arts therapy rather than being scared and shying away from it, I think, yes, it is really important. (c1, staff, female)

Meaningful life and social goals

Numerous staff and patients indicated activities as helpful for building a meaningful life which consisted of having access to education and employment supports, giving responsibility (e.g., caring for a plant) and providing skills for independent living so they can have a positive social role when discharged.

When patients are discharged, if they could be encouraged to participate in work, kind of a job to do, if you are idle, most people are idle they turn to substance misuses, if when they are discharged and they have nothing to do what do they do?

Other activities that consist of long-term projects and provide a sense of purpose and achievement were identified as helpful for a meaningful life, as were activities that involve the patients in running them and activities that are meaningful to the patients rather than based on assumptions.

Cultural and Spiritual Meaning

When trying to find meaning in their experience some patients identified faith and spirituality as helpful for creating a safe space and understanding of self and their mental health. These patients noted that wards need to facilitate patients to practice different faiths, spiritual beliefs, and multiple cultural activities.

Staff also identified faith and spirituality as helpful for creating a safe space and understanding of oneself and experiences.

Focus on strengths and preparing for discharge

Staff and patients identified activities as helping patients focus on and realise their strengths. Activities can provide a space where patients can try new things and develop new skills or tap into old interests to build existing skills. Focusing on strengths can help patients gain confidence, self-esteem and self-worth. Meaningful activities that have an end product or provide a sense of accomplishment and when run by good facilitators, including those with lived experience, assist this.

And it is important for them to feel like you don't have to be good at this, just do it because you're trying to express yourself. And then once people do that, they realise, "Oh, I'm actually not bad at this," and that's instilling their confidence. (a8, patient, male)

In preparation for discharge, providing employment and educational support is recognised by staff and patients as important for equipping people to take control.

Involving patients in running activities and giving space for them to have control or freedom within the activity is identified by staff and patients as helpful for empowering patients. Staff further noted that activities provide a space to celebrate patients' wins and foster hope for the future.

And I think there would be more people who would be open to leading activities, if they were asked, and they felt they would be supported in doing it. (b15, patient, female)

We recruited 41 patients diagnosed with psychosis currently on inpatient wards. The recruitment and inclusion of this population is important as people with psychosis are frequently excluded from research studies , which may contribute to health care inequalities seen in this population . Common reasons for excluding this patient group include concerns about the atient's capacity to provide informed consent, and the potential for high-risk adverse events and safety issues. These concerns may reflect a misunderstanding of the nature of psychosis and perhaps an element of stigma towards this patient group.

A previous study reported that about two-thirds of patients with psychotic disorders questioned were willing to be contacted about participation in research . In this study, even during inpatient admissions, we were able to obtain informed consent and conduct detailed interviews where patients described their experiences and views.

The inclusion of people with lived experience in our research team perhaps aided this process, helping us establish trust with potential participants on the wards . The importance of support at this crucial stage was highlighted. Some found help in faith or a spiritual dimension, but this was possibly under-explored in the interviews. Finally, patients experienced empowerment by learning to take on more responsibility for themselves within and through the activities, with some co-leading activities. Building skills, strengths and confidence in preparation for discharge were seen as important aspects of ward activities that need to be nurtured and supported at and beyond discharge.

Skilled facilitation and the involvement of people from wider community groups, including those with lived experience of mental illness and hospital use is valued. This indicates the importance of ensuring wards can employ experienced occupational therapists and activities coordinators, while also finding ways to support the involvement of community organisations that may bring helpful activities onto the wards while also helping build connections that can be maintained and nurtured once the person leaves the ward. These human, social connections are essential in helping maintain recovery and avoid relapse, re-admission or worse. Hearing the voices of people with lived experience who are in recovery also shows the value of involving peer workers in ward activities and supporting people through and beyond the transition to community living.

The clear desire of most patients with psychosis interviewed to connect with, get to know and develop closer relationships with other patients and staff is important. The closer relationships and recognition of shared experiences reduce the fear and anxiety (47, 48 ) frequently reported on mental health wards and can reduce the risk of tensions which too often lead to aggression, self-harm or absconding. Acute mental health wards can be challenging settings not always conducive to recovery, yet participants spoke of activities providing something more positive and hopeful in environments characterised by containment and risk management. People with psychosis admitted to inpatient wards may well be struggling with an illness that can shatter a clear understanding of self and others, with resulting raised levels of anxiety and suspicion.

They and other patients feel vulnerable and are often subject to the same stigmatising views of psychiatric hospitals and patients prevalent in wider society. Similarly, helping patients and staff to see the individual person and to share moments of light relief and connection can help build therapeutic relationships and ward cultures that are conducive to positive experiences and outcomes, including recovery . Such approaches echo the 'Knowing Each Other' intervention in the successful Safewards model designed to reduce conflict and containment on wards and are most beneficial when all staff are encouraged to contribute . However, we must be mindful of the smaller number of people interviewed who remained cautious and less keen on closer relationships with others on the wards through activities. We need to work with sensitivity to recognise that for various reasons, including awareness of the oftenfleeting connections made on wards, and previous experiences of trauma and/or neurodivergence, other approaches and options may be desirable to provide personcentred, trauma-informed care.

Conclusion

The importance of activities in providing safe effective inpatient care has been recognised in key national policy reports and this study further emphasises the importance of activities in the experiences and recovery of patients with psychosis in otherwise restrictive environments. The findings also demonstrate that the provision of activities on wards can stimulate, motivate, aid and support the recovery of people with psychosis and help prepare them for discharge and re-connection with the wider community. Building and maintaining connections with external agencies is essential and the active involvement of people with lived experience who can draw on their mutual experiences and act as role models is desirable.
