How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Abstract

The R package llmClassificR is available at https://github.com/hfgolino/llmClassificR. The replication script, the GoEmotions subset, and the cached results of all analyses are available in the supplementary materials.

Introduction

Classifying texts into psychologically meaningful categories is one of the oldest problems in the quantitative study of language, and one of the most consequential, since the categories assigned to open-ended survey responses, diaries, clinical notes, social media posts, or interview transcripts frequently become the variables that enter every subsequent statistical model, so that errors in the classification step propagate, often silently, to the substantive conclusions of a study (Feuerriegel et al., 2025; O. N. E. Kjell, Kjell, Garcia, & Sikström, 2019). For several decades, the dominant approaches relied either on dictionaries, in which a text receives a score for each category based on the words it contains (Boyd, Ashokkumar, Seraj, & Pennebaker, 2022; Mohammad & Turney, 2013), or on supervised models fitted to word counts, in which a classifier learns, from texts coded by trained human raters, which words are associated with each category (Feinerer, Hornik, & Meyer, 2008; Salton & Buckley, 1988). The last decade changed this landscape in successive waves: contextual encoder models such as BERT produced representations in which the meaning of a word depends on the words around it (Devlin, Chang, Lee, & Toutanova, 2019; Vaswani et al., 2017), sentence encoders turned these representations into vectors in which texts with similar meanings lie close to each other (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), and generative large language models (LLMs) made it possible to classify texts from nothing more than an instruction and a list of categories (Brown et al., 2020), a possibility that was rapidly adopted in psychology and the social sciences (Abdurahman et al., 2024; Demszky et al., 2023; Gilardi, Alizadeh, & Kubli, 2023; Rathje et al., 2024; Ziems et al., 2024). A further step was taken in September 2026, when the company TypeSafe released Jev, a general-purpose classification model that does not generate text but returns probabilities for typed questions about a text (choosing one of several options, answering yes or no, or rating the text on an ordinal rubric), described by Raschka (2026) as "the ChatGPT moment for classification", since it can classify many kinds of text inputs cheaply without the need to fine-tune a custom classifier for each task (TypeSafe, 2026).

The multiplication of strategies created new possibilities, but also new problems for researchers who want to use text classification as a measurement procedure. The first problem is that most applications assume that the categories are known in advance, while many research questions in psychology start one step earlier, asking whether the texts organize themselves into categories at all, how many categories are supported by the data, and whether these categories correspond to the constructs proposed by a theory. Network psychometrics offers a principled answer to this kind of question for questionnaire data, by estimating a network of partial associations between variables and detecting its communities with exploratory graph analysis (EGA; Golino & Epskamp, 2017; Borsboom et al., 2021; Golino et al., 2020), and the same logic can be applied to texts once each text is represented by an embedding vector, treating texts as nodes and their co-variation across embedding dimensions as edges. Transformers, however, do not produce a single representation of a text, but one representation per layer, and the information that distinguishes psychological categories is distributed unevenly across layers (Jawahar, Sagot, & Seddah, 2019; Rogers, Kovaleva, & Rumshisky, 2020; Tenney, Das, & Pavlick, 2019), which turns the representation of a text into a multiplex network, that is, a set of networks defined on the same nodes. Complexity science provides tools for this situation, and one of them, the prime-weight encoding of multiplex networks proposed by Santoro and Nicosia (2020) and adapted to psychological networks by Golino, Nesselroade, and Christensen (2025), is used in the present paper to combine the networks estimated from every layer of an encoder (and from embedding services) into a single network whose communities can be compared with known categories.

The second problem concerns the quality of the probabilities attached to a classification. A classifier that is right 80% of the time but reports 99% confidence for every decision, or one that cannot say which of its decisions deserve a second look, is of limited use when the classifications are treated as data (Geng et al., 2024; Guo, Pleiss, Sun, & Weinberger, 2017). Probabilities returned by neural networks are frequently miscalibrated (Desai & Durrett, 2020; Guo et al., 2017; Minderer et al., 2021), the confidences verbalized by generative LLMs are only partially informative (Lin, Hilton, & Evans, 2022; Tian et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2024), and the methods that repair or certify uncertainty, such as temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017), Platt scaling (Platt, 2000), and conformal prediction (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023; Vovk, Gammerman, & Shafer, 2005), are rarely part of the text classification software used by applied researchers. The third problem is practical and could be called LLM engineering: generative models answer in free text, must be prompted in batches to keep costs and rate limits under control, are sensitive to the order of the texts and to the wording of the prompt (Lu, Bartolo, Moore, Riedel, & Stenetorp, 2022; Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2024; Sclar, Choi, Tsvetkov, & Suhr, 2024; Zhao, Wallace, Feng, Klein, & Singh, 2021), occasionally return labels that do not exist, change or are retired by their providers, and send data to external servers, all of which affect reproducibility and the ethics of a study (Abdurahman et al., 2024; Sainz et al., 2023; Ziems et al., 2024).

The package llmClassificR (Golino, 2026) for R (R Core Team, 2026) was developed to address these three problems within a single, consistent interface written for applied researchers, and was used in the 2026 edition of a course on text classification with LLMs in R. The package currently exports 75 functions and ships seven data sets. It covers bag-of-words baselines; embeddings from local encoders, decoder LLMs, sentence encoders, and two embedding services (Voyage AI and Jina AI); unsupervised classification with EGA and prime-weight encoding; supervised classification heads; prompted classification with generative LLMs served by Groq, including strict JSON Schema outputs, ensembles, self-consistency, and LLM-as-judge; retrieval-augmented generation (RAG; Lewis et al., 2020) and topic discovery; Jev-based classification; do-it-yourself Jev-like classifiers; and a set of tools for calibration and uncertainty quantification. The package relies on EGAnet (Golino & Christensen, 2026) for network estimation, on glmnet (Friedman, Hastie, & Tibshirani, 2010) for penalized regression, on reticulate (Kalinowski, Ushey, Allaire, & Tang, 2026) for local transformer models (Paszke et al., 2019; Wolf et al., 2020), and on httr2 (Wickham, 2026) for the APIs that do not require Python.

The present paper is both a description of the software and a tutorial on the methods, written to dissect a set of questions that, in the experience of the author, recur whenever LLMs are used to classify text in psychological research: whether unsupervised network methods recover known categories from the embeddings of local and commercial models, and how the layers of a transformer can be combined (Section ); how much supervised classification heads gain over unsupervised methods when labels are available (Section ); how prompted LLMs should be engineered for classification (Section ); how topics can be discovered automatically, or with a human in the loop, when the categories are unknown (Section ); how a general-purpose classifier such as Jev is used, and how a Jev-like classifier can be built from local or API embeddings (Sections and ); what temperature scaling does, and does not do, to the probabilities of an LLM-based classifier (Section ); how uncertainty can be quantified, calibrated, and certified with conformal prediction (Section ); and how the reliability of the labels limits what any of these classifiers can be shown to achieve (Section ). Every analysis uses three kinds of data: synthetic texts generated by LLMs, which were used in the course and ship with the package; descriptions of emotional situations from EmpatheticDialogues (Rashkin, Smith, Li, & Boureau, 2019), labeled with the emotion their writers chose, whose emotions mirror those of one of the synthetic data sets; and Reddit comments from GoEmotions (Demszky et al., 2020) with the same emotions, labeled by several raters who read each comment out of context. The contrast between the three kinds of data turns out to be one of the main lessons of the paper: methods that look nearly perfect on synthetic texts lose part of their accuracy on real situations and much more of it on rater-labeled comments, where the agreement between raters, rather than the classifier, becomes the limiting factor.

Related software

Several R packages support parts of the workflow described here. The tm (Feinerer et al., 2008), quanteda (Benoit et al., 2018), and tidytext (Silge & Robinson, 2016) packages provide infrastructure for bag-of-words text mining; topicmodels (Grün & Hornik, 2011) and stm (Roberts, Stewart, & Tingley, 2019) implement probabilistic topic models such as latent Dirichlet allocation (Blei, Ng, & Jordan, 2003); and the text package (O. In Python, the transformers library (Wolf et al., 2020) and sentence encoders (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) are the standard tools for embeddings, SetFit (Tunstall et al., 2022) trains few-shot classifiers on sentence embeddings, BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022) clusters embeddings to discover topics, and TopicGPT (Pham, Hoyle, Sun, Resnik, & Iyyer, 2024) uses prompted LLMs for the same purpose. Texts as a network: exploratory graph analysis

EGA estimates the number and composition of dimensions in multivariate data by modeling the variables as nodes of a network and detecting the communities of densely connected nodes (Golino & Epskamp, 2017; Golino et al., 2020). To classify texts without labels, the roles of rows and columns are swapped: each text is a node, the p embedding dimensions play the role of observations, and the edge between two texts reflects how strongly their embedding vectors co-vary across dimensions. In llmClassificR, ega_classify() estimates the network with the triangulated maximally filtered graph (TMFG; Massara, Di Matteo, & Aste, 2017), which retains 3n -6 edges, chosen greedily to maximize the sum of the retained correlations under the constraint that the graph remains planar, and detects communities with the walktrap algorithm (Pons & Latapy, 2006), which merges nodes that are close in terms of short random walks on the network (Fortunato, 2010). The number of steps of the random walks controls the granularity of the solution, and the function EGA.fit() of EGAnet chooses it by minimizing the total entropy fit index (TEFI; Golino et al., 2021), an information-theoretic fit measure that does not use the labels. Each community is a candidate category, and texts that EGA cannot assign to any community are left unclassified.

The layers of a transformer as a multiplex network

Running EGA on each layer of a transformer produces L + 1 networks defined on the same n texts, that is, a multiplex network. Averaging or concatenating the layers before estimating a single network is one way of using all of them, but it mixes the representations before the networks are estimated. Santoro and Nicosia (2020) proposed an encoding of multiplex networks in which each layer receives a distinct prime number, and Golino et al. Evaluating partitions against known categories

When labels are available, the partition found without them can be compared with the true classes with the normalized mutual information (NMI; Danon, Díaz-Guilera, Duch, & Arenas, 2005; Vinh, Epps, & Bailey, 2010), which is 1 when the partition reproduces the classes exactly and close to 0 when it is unrelated to them, and which does not require the communities to be named or to be as many as the classes. The package computes NMI = I(Y ; C)/ max{H(Y ), H(C)}, where I(Y ; C) is the mutual information between the classes Y and the communities C and H denotes entropy, which is the most conservative of the usual normalizations (Vinh et al., 2010); the arithmetic-mean normalization of Danon et al. (2005) is available through the argument variant of compute_nmi(). Accuracy, macro-averaged F 1 , and Cohen's κ (Cohen, 1960) are more familiar to applied researchers, but they require each community to be named after a class.

The function match_communities() does this with the Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn, 1955), as implemented in clue (Hornik, 2005), which assigns a different class to each community so that the number of texts whose community is named after their own class is as large as possible; communities left over when there are more communities than classes are named "Extra 1", "Extra 2", and so on, and their texts count as errors. The labels are used only after the communities were found, so the resulting matched accuracy is a legitimate summary of an unsupervised solution, but choosing the number of walktrap steps with the highest NMI is not, because it selects the solution with the labels; the package reports such values as optimistic diagnostics only. Confidence intervals for accuracies are computed with the exact binomial method (Clopper & Pearson, 1934).

Supervised classification heads

When labeled texts are available, a classification head is a small model fitted on top of frozen embeddings, in the spirit of the linear probes used to study what each layer of a neural network encodes (Alain & Bengio, 2017; Hewitt & Liang, 2019). The function cv_logistic() fits an elastic-net multinomial logistic regression (Friedman et al., 2010; Zou & Hastie, 2005) with stratified k-fold cross-validation, choosing the penalty within each training fold, so that every text is predicted by a model that never saw it (Bates, Hastie, & Tibshirani, 2024; Nadeau & Bengio, 2003); probe_layers() repeats this for every layer of an encoder, and learning_curve() estimates how accuracy grows with the number of labeled texts per class, which indicates whether collecting more labels is likely to pay off.

Prompted generative LLMs

Generative LLMs classify texts when prompted with an instruction, the list of categories, and, optionally, a few labeled examples (few-shot prompting; Brown et al., 2020; Min et al., 2022) or a request to reason before answering (chain-of-thought; Wei et al., 2022; Kojima, Gu, Reid, Matsuo, & Iwasawa, 2022). Their answers are free text, which creates engineering problems that the package handles inside every classify_*() function: texts are sent in batches (40 per request by default) to control costs and rate limits, but batches are shuffled, since an ordered batch allows the model to exploit the sequence of labels rather than the content of each text (Lu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2021); answers are mapped to the allowed classes with normalize_labels(), and anything else becomes a missing value counted as an error; and results are returned in the original order of the texts. Structured outputs constrain the answer to a JSON object, and in strict mode to a JSON Schema whose category field is an enumeration of the allowed classes, so that invalid labels become impossible (Groq, 2026; Willard & Louf, 2023). Confidence can be requested verbally (Lin et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2024), scores can be requested for every class, several models can vote (ensembles; classify_ensemble()), the same model can be asked several times at a positive sampling temperature, with the share of votes for each class used as a probability (self-consistency; X. Wang et al., 2023), and a model can be asked to act as an expert judge that cites evidence for its decisions (LLM-as-judge; Zheng et al., 2023).

Retrieval-augmented generation and topic discovery

RAG retrieves the documents most similar to a query, usually by cosine similarity between embeddings, and asks an LLM to answer using only those documents as context (Lewis et al., 2020). In llmClassificR, retrieve_similar() and rag_generate() implement the two steps, discover_topics() asks for a fixed number of topics (each with a label and a description) grounded in representative documents, and classify_with_rag() chains embedding, topic discovery, and classification of every text into the discovered topics. The same goal can be pursued with a human in the loop and without generative models: EGA finds the communities, the most typical words of each community (those whose share of texts in the community most exceeds their share of the other texts) are listed, the most central texts of each community are read by the analyst, and the analyst's names and descriptions of the communities define a zero-shot classifier (next subsection). This human-guided route is related to embedding-based topic models such as BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022), and its LLM-based counterpart to TopicGPT (Pham et al., 2024).

Jev and Jev-like classifiers

Jev answers three types of typed questions about a text (TypeSafe, 2026): a Choice question picks one of up to 255 options and returns a probability for each option, a Noul question returns the probability that a yes/no statement is true (so that several Noul questions about the same text form a multi-label classifier whose probabilities need not sum to 1), and a Score question rates the text on an ordinal rubric with 2 to 10 levels and returns the probability-weighted level. Each question has instructions and criteria, the descriptions of when each option applies, which play the role of the items of a measurement instrument. The architecture, training algorithm, and training data of Jev have not been disclosed; according to its developers, as reported by Raschka (2026), the model was trained only on curated synthetic data with a proprietary procedure called reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions, and its documentation describes its probabilities as well calibrated.

Raschka (2026) sketched how a classifier of this kind could work. A transformer reads the text together with each candidate option c and produces a vector h c per candidate, a shared head computes a score s c = w ⊤ h c + b, and a softmax over the candidates turns the scores into probabilities. Because w and b are shared, the number of candidates can change from task to task without retraining, and the bias b cancels in the softmax (it matters only for yes/no questions). The package implements a bi-encoder version of this idea that runs on a laptop: text i and the description of class c are embedded separately, with any of the embedding functions, and their cosine similarity s ic is the score, a strategy known as dataless classification (Chang, Ratinov, Roth, & Srikumar, 2008) and related to entailment-based zero-shot classification (Yin, Hay, & Roth, 2019).

The function classify_by_description() returns softmax(s ic /T ) with a temperature T , which is equivalent to a head w whose elements all equal 1/T applied to h ic = x i ⊙ d c (the element-wise product of the unit-length text and description embeddings), and train_shared_head() learns w from labeled tasks to test whether such a head transfers to tasks with new labels. The difference from the classifier sketched by Raschka (2026) is that a bi-encoder never reads the text and the description together, while a cross-encoder does, which usually improves accuracy at a higher computational cost (Humeau, Shuster, Lachaux, & Weston, 2020).

Calibration and uncertainty

A classifier is calibrated when, among the texts to which it assigns confidence q (the largest probability), the proportion classified correctly is close to q (Guo et al., 2017). The package reports the expected calibration error (ECE; Pakdaman Naeini, Cooper, & Hauskrecht, 2015), which averages the absolute gap between accuracy and confidence across 10 confidence bins weighted by their number of texts; the Brier score (Brier, 1950), the mean squared distance between the probability vector and the one-hot vector of the true class; and the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the true class. The Brier score and the NLL are strictly proper scoring rules, which means that their expected value is optimized only by the true probabilities (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007), a property that recent work exploited to train reasoning models to state calibrated confidences with the reward c -(q -c) 2 , where c indicates a correct answer (Damani et al., 2026); the function rlcr_reward() implements this reward for teaching purposes. Reliability diagrams (reliability_table() and plot_reliability()) display accuracy against confidence by bin.

Temperature scaling divides the scores (logits) by a single positive number T before the softmax, with T chosen to minimize the NLL on a calibration set (Guo et al., 2017; Hinton, Vinyals, & Dean, 2015); since all scores are divided by the same number, the ranking of the classes, and therefore the accuracy, does not change, and only the confidence is repaired (T > 1 softens over-confident probabilities, T < 1 sharpens under-confident ones). This post hoc temperature must not be confused with the sampling temperature of generative LLMs, which controls the randomness of the generated tokens (Holtzman, Buys, Du, Forbes, & Choi, 2020) and affects classification only indirectly, for example through the diversity of the votes in self-consistency. Platt scaling fits p = logit -1 (as + b) to the score s of a binary question (Platt, 2000) and is the natural method for yes/no (Noul-like) questions, where the bias matters; richer alternatives exist (Kull et al., 2019). Calibration is not the only aspect of uncertainty: selective classification lets a classifier abstain on the texts it is least confident about, trading coverage for accuracy (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017), and split conformal prediction turns any probabilistic classifier into a set-valued classifier with a finite-sample guarantee (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023; Vovk et al., 2005). With a calibration set of size n c and nonconformity scores e i = 1 -pi,y i (one minus the probability of the true class), the threshold q is the ⌈(n c + 1)(1 -α)⌉-th smallest score, and the prediction set of a new text contains every class c with pc ≥ 1 -q (Sadinle, Lei, & Wasserman, 2019). If the calibration and new texts are exchangeable, the set contains the true class with probability at least 1 -α, whatever the classifier, and set sizes become a direct, interpretable measure of uncertainty (Kumar et al., 2023; Quach et al., 2024; Romano, Sesia, & Candès, 2020).

In the present paper, this distribution-free guarantee is what is meant by certification, and its main assumption, exchangeability between calibration and new texts, is precisely what is violated when a model calibrated on synthetic texts is applied to real ones. Design principles

Four principles guided the design of the package. The first is one function per step: each step of the workflows described in Section corresponds to one function with a small number of arguments and sensible defaults, so that a complete analysis reads as a sequence of named steps (Table 2). The second is a common output format: every classifier returns a data frame with one row per text, in the original order, containing the predicted class (prediction), the confidence, and, when the method produces them, one column of probabilities per class, so that evaluate_classification(), calibration_metrics(), reliability_table(), and compare_methods() accept the output of any classifier, from a bag-of-words baseline to Jev. The third is explicit treatment of failures: invalid or missing answers are returned as NA and counted as errors (and reported in the column n_invalid), never silently replaced by a class, and texts that EGA does not assign to any community are treated as singleton clusters when the NMI is computed. The fourth is reproducibility: random operations (folds, shuffling, layouts) take a seed argument and restore the global random state afterwards, API functions retry rate-limited requests with exponential backoff, and Jev functions report the tokens used and their cost with jev_usage().

Data used in the illustrations

Three synthetic corpora are used in most illustrations, namely the Big Five bios, the first-person extraversion facets, and the emotion texts (data sets personality_descriptions, extraversion, and neuroticism); the two other extraversion corpora are used to study transfer across generators and to train Jev-like heads. Two real corpora complement them, and they were chosen because they differ in who decided the label of each text, a difference that turns out to matter as much as the classifier.

The first real corpus comes from EmpatheticDialogues (Rashkin et al., 2019), a data set of 24,850 conversations, each grounded in a short situation that a crowdworker described after selecting an emotion word, writing, in one to three sentences, about a situation in which they had felt that way. The label of a situation is therefore the emotion its writer intended to convey, much as the label of a synthetic text is the category the LLM was asked to write about, with the difference that the writers are people describing events of their own lives. Four of its 32 emotions mirror those of the synthetic neuroticism corpus: angry (Angry), sad (Sad), excited (Excited), and caring (Friendly, the least exact correspondence). From the situations labeled with these four emotions (the data set is distributed under the CC BY-NC 4.0 license), exact duplicates and situations with fewer than 4 or more than 60 words were removed, and 150 situations were sampled at random for each emotion, giving 600 situations (median length 15 words) that were split, within each emotion, into a calibration half and a test half (300 situations each). These situations are the real texts of every illustration that follows.

The second real corpus is GoEmotions (Demszky et al., 2020), about 58,000 English Reddit comments annotated with any number of 27 emotion categories (or neutral) by several raters, whose rater-level annotations are public (the documentation of the data set lists the Apache License 2.0). Inside the prime-weight encoding

The prime-weight encoding is easier to understand on a toy example before it is applied to real layers. Without labels, the package chooses the first number of steps that produces the most frequent number of communities across the 24 solutions, 5 steps for the original encoding (5 communities) and 2 steps for the floor-free network (7 communities); the NMI with the true traits, computed for every number of steps, is a diagnostic that uses the labels and is not used to choose the solution (Figure 3, bottom). Topic discovery: automated and human-guided

Topic discovery addresses the situation in which the categories are not known in advance, or in which a researcher wants to check whether the categories proposed by a theory emerge from the texts. The package supports two routes, which can be combined.

In the human-guided route, EGA finds communities of texts in an embedding space, the analyst inspects the most typical words and the most central texts of each community, names the communities, writes one description per name, and the descriptions become a zero-shot classifier for the whole corpus. Jev-based classification

The Jev functions are written in pure R and send one request per text, in parallel (10 at a time by default), with automatic retries; failed requests are returned as missing values, and every batch reports its duration, failures, tokens, and cost. A question is built with jev_choice(), jev_noul(), or jev_score(), several questions can be asked about the same text in a single request with jev_ask() and jev_ask_many(), and the wrappers jev_classify(), jev_multilabel(), and jev_rate() return data frames in the common output format.

The criteria are the measurement instrument: the same descriptions used by the Jev-like classifier of Section define the four emotions, which makes the two approaches directly comparable. The Jev results are reported for all 600 situations, as for the Jev-like classifier, and for the 300 test situations, as for the prompted LLMs.

Jev step by step

A Jev request contains a text, called the state, and one or more named questions about it. The first step builds four questions about one situation from the calibration half, chosen because it involves two emotions: its writer chose caring, but the situation also involves the sadness of a friend. This section addresses the first two questions with the situations, whose calibration half is used to fit every quantity and whose test half is used to evaluate it; the third is addressed with the rater-labeled comments in Section.

Discussion

The present paper introduced llmClassificR and used it to dissect eight questions that arise whenever LLMs are used to classify texts in psychological research. The analyses combined synthetic corpora generated by LLMs, descriptions of emotional situations labeled by their writers, and Reddit comments labeled by several raters, and they point to a small number of conclusions that are likely to generalize beyond the data used here.

Figure 17 condenses the main pattern, by applying the same four classifiers, with the same embeddings and the same emotion descriptions, to the three kinds of data, and Figure 18 sets the main classifiers of the paper side by side on the same 300 test situations.

# The same four classifiers on synthetic texts, author-labeled situations, # and rater-labeled comments (MPNet embeddings; heads cross-validated) summary_corpora

The first conclusion is that synthetic corpora are excellent for teaching and for prototyping methods, but they are a poor guide to the accuracy of a method on real text.

Every method of Figure 17 was less accurate on the real situations than on the synthetic texts, and all but the bag-of-words head, whose accuracy was essentially unchanged, were less accurate still on the rater-labeled comments; the prime-weight encodings that recovered the synthetic traits and facets with accuracies above 90% (Table 4) recovered the emotions of the situations only partially, and EGA.fit() on MPNet embeddings, the one unsupervised method applied to all three kinds of data, fell from 66.7% on the synthetic texts to 34.7% on the situations and 23.5% on the comments.

Synthetic texts are generated one category at a time, are prototypical, and repeat the same vocabulary, while real texts serve many purposes at once (Long et al., 2024; Shumailov et al., 2024). Synthetic texts can nevertheless serve as training data when the representation generalizes: a head trained agreement between raters, which is rarely reported in text classification studies, should be estimated, and the task defined to match what the raters did, before classifiers are compared (Chen, 2022; Uma et al., 2021).

The third conclusion concerns the role of network psychometrics and complexity science in LLM-based classification. When the categories of interest are the dominant structure of the texts, EGA on embeddings recovers them without labels, and the prime-weight encoding of the per-layer networks, which treats a transformer as a multiplex network, was the most accurate label-free method on the synthetic corpora and on the real situations, outperforming, for BERT and the decoder LLM, the more common strategies of averaging or concatenating the layers; its advantage comes from the pairs of texts that are linked consistently across layers (Section ). When the categories are only one of several organizing principles of the texts, as for the situations, the communities reflect kinds of situations, and the human-guided route of Section shows how they can be turned into a classifier whose categories are defined by the researcher. The layer-wise comparison of supervised probes and unsupervised EGA (Figure 7) makes the same point from a different angle: information can be linearly readable with labels and still not form communities without them. The embedding services do not change this conclusion: added to the BERT layers, Voyage AI and Jina AI left the label-free solutions practically where they were (Table 5), while the Voyage AI embeddings gave the most accurate supervised head and, with label descriptions, the most accurate description-based classifier.

The fourth conclusion is that the probabilities attached to LLM-based classifications must be treated as estimates to be checked and, when needed, repaired. The cosine similarities that drive a Jev-like classifier are on an arbitrary scale, so their softmax at an arbitrary temperature is badly calibrated, and temperature scaling, fitted on a calibration set with a single parameter, repaired the confidence without changing the accuracy, as it did for the under-confident probabilities of a penalized logistic head and for the over-confident probabilities of Jev and of self-consistency, which, once calibrated, had the lowest negative log-likelihood of all classifiers. Conformal prediction went one step further, turning the probabilities of any classifier into prediction sets with a distribution-free coverage guarantee, which held on average over repeated splits and whose sizes are an interpretable measure of uncertainty, although, on the rater-labeled comments, they were only weakly related to the disagreement between raters, and the multi-label Noul probabilities of Jev, which tracked that disagreement better than the probabilities of the two classifiers forced to choose one emotion, still did so only weakly (Table 18). Both procedures depend on the exchangeability between the calibration texts and the texts to be classified, which means that a temperature or a conformal threshold estimated on synthetic texts, or on a different population, offers no guarantee for real texts; the calibration set must come from the population of interest (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023).

The fifth conclusion concerns the choice among the families of classifiers (Figure 18).

On the same 300 test situations, the classifiers that saw no labeled situation (apart from the eight examples of the few-shot prompt) and were reached through an API (the prompted LLMs, Jev, and the description-based classifiers on Voyage AI and Jina AI embeddings) were correct for 82.3% to 88.3% of the situations, the supervised heads on the embeddings of local models for 78.7% to 84.3%, and the head on Voyage AI embeddings for 87.7%, with confidence intervals that overlap between the most accurate members of each family; the zero-shot description-based classifier on local MPNet embeddings, which needs neither labels nor an Internet connection, reached 82.7%, within 6 points of the most accurate classifier, and the label-free solutions, at most 53.0%, were far behind. For a task whose categories can be described in a sentence, the choice among the strong classifiers therefore depends less on accuracy than on cost, on whether the texts may leave the researcher's computer, on whether labeled texts exist, and on the quality of the probabilities, which differed much more than the accuracies (Sections and ). The sixth conclusion is practical. Prompted LLMs, embedding services, and Jev offer remarkable convenience, but they come with engineering obligations that are part of the measurement procedure: randomizing the order of texts in batches, constraining outputs to the allowed classes, reporting prompts, criteria, model versions and dates verbatim, pinning model versions when the provider allows it, estimating costs, and respecting the conditions under which participants' texts can leave the researcher's computer. Table 19 summarizes when each family of methods is appropriate.

The present study has limitations. The real data were two corpora of short English texts, the emotions were chosen to mirror one synthetic data set, and caring is only an approximate counterpart of the friendly texts of the synthetic corpus; both subsets retained only texts with a single emotion, which makes the task easier than the multi-label problems of the original corpora; the EmpatheticDialogues writers selected an emotion before describing a situation, which makes their texts more prototypical than spontaneous descriptions of emotional events; and both corpora have been public for years, so they may be part of the training data of the LLMs and embedding models used here (Sainz et al., 2023). The results of API-based models depend on model versions that will change, and the results of Jev in particular depend on a proprietary model whose architecture and training data are unknown; one of the models planned for the ensemble was retired before the API analyses were run, so that the cached results distributed with the replication materials are the only exact record of the API-based analyses, and a new run of the script should be expected to differ from them in some predictions. The human-guided descriptions of Section were written by an analyst with access to the labels and should be replaced by descriptions written blind to them in any substantive application.

The package will continue to evolve. Planned developments include the submission to CRAN, functions that wrap the conformal and selective classification procedures of Section , local cross-encoder Jev-like classifiers that read the text and the description together, multi-label calibration for Noul-like questions, and tools to compare the uncertainty of models with the disagreement of human coders.

The raters of the 600 comments agreed only moderately on whether a comment expressed its gold emotion: on average, 67% of the raters of a comment chose it, only 20% of the comments were labeled unanimously, and a held-out rater chose the emotion preferred by the other raters in 64.1% of the ratings. The disagreement, however, concerned mostly whether a comment expressed one of the four emotions at all: 33.5% of the individual ratings included none of them (the rater chose another category, such as annoyance or approval, or neutral), and when a rater did choose one of the four emotions, it was the emotion preferred by the other raters in 96.5% of the ratings. The raters, in other words, rarely confused the four emotions with each other, but often did not see any of them in a comment that other raters labeled with one. A classifier forced to choose one of the four emotions cannot express this second kind of disagreement, and its accuracy mixes comments whose emotion is clear to every rater with comments that a third or more of the raters did not read as expressing the gold emotion.

The classifiers of Table 17 were far less accurate on the comments than on the situations: the MPNet head reached 72.7%, the bag-of-words head 64.7%, and the zero-shot Jev-like classifier 61.7%, 20 points below its accuracy on the situations with the same descriptions.
