Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Materials
The forms and questionnaires used in this project are listed below:

c. Demographics Form
Participants will be asked about their demographic information, including age, gender, ethnic background, native language(s), other languages spoken, country of birth, country of residence, length of residence in the country of current residence, educational level, and socioeconomic status. There will also be specific questions regarding their diagnoses: whether they have received a diagnosis, the nature of the diagnosis, and their age at diagnosis. Individuals who self-identify as autistic will also be included in the study. A diagnosis option will be provided for these individuals, and data analyses will be conducted both including and excluding these participants, with results compared. If the results do not significantly differ statistically, the data will be analyzed and reported as a whole.

d. Autism-Related Language Preferences Form
The original form used by Keating et al. (2023) consists of a total of 9 questions, including both multiple-choice and open-ended questions. The 8 multiple-choice questions with an “Other” option asking autism-related language preferences are as follows:
(1) When talking about autism,
(2) When addressing someone diagnosed with autism,
(3) When talking about the identity of someone diagnosed with autism,
(4) When talking more broadly about autism,
(5) When discussing challenges related to autism,
(6) When talking about the strengths of autism,
(7) When addressing individuals who are not diagnosed with autism,
(8) Whether autism can be conceptually separated from autistic individuals (yes, no, other).
Relatively new terms related to autism, such as “profound autism,” will be included in the present project. For multiple-choice questions, participants will be prompted with: “Please select the terms you are comfortable using when [discussing autism]. You can choose as many terms as you like.” Additionally, there will be one open-ended question:
(9) Participants can share any further thoughts on language preferences without time or word limits.

e. Autism-Related Knowledge and Awareness Questionnaire
Originally developed by Flood et al. (2013) and adapted into Turkish as the “Social Attitudes Toward Autism Spectrum Disorders Questionnaire” (OYTTÖ) by Batum and Aydın (2021), this scale is used to measure social attitudes toward people with autism. It contains 26 items, such as “Individuals with autism should not have children.” Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 – Strongly Disagree, 4 – Strongly Agree). In a study conducted in Australia (Bury et al., 2023), researchers used a similar questionnaire, the “Autism Awareness Scale (AAS),” which has not yet been adapted into Turkish. Therefore, we will use a similar questionnaire described above (OYTTÖ) which is available in Turkish at the moment.

f. Autism-Related Internalized Stigma Questionnaire
The original questionnaire, the Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness Inventory (ISMI; Ritsher et al., 2003), has been adapted into Turkish as the “Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness Scale” (RHİDÖ; Ersoy & Varan, 2007). This questionnaire has been adapted for use with various health conditions (e.g., Boyd et al., 2014). Following the method of Bury et al. (2023), the term “mental illness” will be replaced with “autism.” In Turkish. This scale contains items such as “People discriminate against me because I am autistic.” and is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 – Strongly Disagree, 4 – Strongly Agree).

g. Autism Related Social Identity Questionnaire
The social identity questionnaire, originally the “In-group Identification Scale” (Leach et al., 2008), will be used for autism. The original questionnaire has been adapted for use in Turkish in various research domains (e.g., Balaban, 2013). In the original questionnaire, the target concept section is left blank. This section will be filled with autism-related items. The questionnaire will include statements such as “I feel a connection with other [autistic people].” and “I feel solidarity with [autistic people].” rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 – Strongly Disagree, 7 – Strongly Agree).

h. Adult Autism Spectrum Questionnaire (AQ–50)
This questionnaire, originally developed by Baron-Cohen et al. (2001) as the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ), was adapted into Turkish by Kose et al. (2010). It covers areas such as social skills, attention shift, attention to detail, communication, and imagination, as outlined in the diagnostic criteria for autism. The self-report questionnaire consists of 50 items and is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 – Strongly Agree, 4 – Strongly Disagree).

To ensure statistical power for the analyses in this study, the necessary sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software. Power analysis was conducted separately for ANOVA, bivariate correlation, and multiple regression analyses. The required sample sizes to achieve 80% statistical power (α = 0.05) for each analysis are as follows:

• One-way ANOVA: Used to test differences between language preference groups. Based on previous studies (Keating et al., 2023), power analysis was performed with an effect size of Cohen’s f² = 0.40. The minimum required sample size for this analysis is 66 participants.
• Bivariate correlation analysis: Used to test the linear relationships between language preferences and other variables, such as levels of autism-related knowledge, social identity, and internalized stigma. Power analysis was performed with an effect size of Cohen’s r = 0.25. The minimum required sample size is 123 participants.
• Multiple regression analysis: Used to analyze predictor variables. Based on similar previous studies (Bury et al., 2023), an effect size of Cohen’s f² = 0.15 was assumed. The required minimum sample size is 127 participants.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Autism-related terminology is contested, and preferences vary across linguistic and cultural contexts. However, evidence from Turkish-speaking autistic communities is currently limited. This mixed-methods study examined terminology preferences among 127 native Turkishspeaking autistic participants aged 15-66 years. Participants selected all terms they felt comfortable using across seven categories of autism-related language. Eighty-one participants also provided open-ended responses, which were analysed using inductive reflexive thematic analysis. Participants most frequently endorsed otizm ("autism"; 79.30%) as the nomenclature of autism and nörolojik/beyin farklılığı ("neurological/brain difference"; 77.49%) as a broader conceptualisation. Farklılıklar ("differences") was the most frequently endorsed term for both autism-related difficulties (82.45%) and strengths (71.19%). No single expression for an autistic person was endorsed by majority: otistik ("autistic"; 49.33%), nöroçeşitli kişi ("neurodivergent person"; 47.40%), and otizmli ("[person] with autism"; 45.48%) received similar support. Individual-difference measures showed few consistent associations with terminology endorsement after correction for multiple comparisons. Six qualitative themes concerned autonomy, social impact, identity, accuracy, context, and respect. Turkish-speaking autistic adults generally favoured direct and non-deficit-oriented language while emphasising individual choice. Researchers and professionals should therefore ask about personal preferences whenever possible and avoid imposing a single terminology on the wider community.

Introduction

Recent years have seen scholarly, and at times political, debate around the terminology used when talking about autism 1 and autistic people 2 . Central to this debate is the distinction between identity-first language (IFL; e.g., "autistic person") and person-first language (PFL; e.g., "person with autism"). In a large multinational survey of English-speaking autistic adults, primarily from Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, the United Kingdom (UK), and the United States (US), "autistic person" (79.5%) and "is autistic" (85.0%) were endorsed by more participants than "person with autism" (23.9%) and "has autism" (39.1%; Keating et al., 2023). Similar support for IFL has been reported in Australian (Bury, Jellett, Spoor, et al., 2023) and North American samples (Robertson et al., 2025). Methods

This study was pre-registered prior to data collection (https://osf.io/x2vf3) and approved by the Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics Ethical Review Committee at [blinded] (Verification Code: [blinded]).

Participants

Sample size and effect size calculations

The preregistered power analyses were based on an earlier analysis plan involving oneway Analysis of Variances (ANOVA), bivariate correlations, and multiple regression. The largest resulting target was 127 participants, providing 80% power to detect small-moderate effect sizes (f² = 0.15-40) at α = .05. After inspecting the distribution and measurement properties of the primary outcome, we replaced the planned linear analyses with binomial generalised linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs), which were more appropriate for repeated dichotomous responses (0 = not endorsed,1 = endorsed). Given N = 127 and dichotomous endorsement outcomes, we conducted a sensitivity analysis for logistic regression. Across plausible baseline endorsement rates, the design had adequate power to detect small-tomoderate effects at α = .05.

Sample

The sample comprised 127 native Turkish-speakers aged 15-66 years (M = 25.17, SD = 8.79), recruited through in-person (N = 54) or online (N = 73). See Table 1.

Seventy-five participants completed the Social Attitudes Toward Autism Spectrum Disorders Questionnaire (Flood et al., 2013; M = 46.20; SD = 9.43), 74 completed adapted versions of Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness Inventory (Ritsher et al., 2003; M = 61.40; SD = 14.19) and In-group Identification Scale (Leach et al., 2008; M = 47.40; SD = 11.23), and only 32 participants completed the Autism Spectrum Quotient (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001; M = 31.20; SD = 7.12).

Materials

Language Preference Survey

Autism-related language preferences were assessed using a mixed-methods survey developed by Keating et al. (2023) and previously adapted into other languages (e.g., Geelhand et al., 2023). Seven multiple-response categories assessed preferences for autism-related terminology across different communicative contexts: (1) the nomenclature of autism, (2) referring to someone with an autism diagnosis, (3) talking about someone's autistic identity, (4) talking about autism more broadly, (5) talking about challenges related to autism, (6) talking about strengths related to autism, and (7) referring to someone without an autism diagnosis.

Each category included an "Other" option presented with a free-text field. See Appendix 1.

In the present study, the item pool was expanded to include relatively recent terminology in autism discourse (e.g., profound autism). Where helpful, response options were accompanied by an example sentence from everyday use. For instance, for the term "autistic person," the option was accompanied by a sentence of "I met an autistic person today."

Options were presented alphabetically, and participants were instructed: "Please select the terms you feel comfortable using when [talking about autism]. You may choose as many terms as you like." A final open-ended question invited participants to share additional thoughts or perspectives on language use without a time or word limit.

Autistic Traits

Autistic traits were assessed using the 50-item Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ; Baron-Cohen et al., 2001), which was adapted into Turkish by Köse et al. (2010).

Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Agree) to 4 (Strongly Disagree). Higher total scores indicate greater levels of autistic traits. The McDonald's Omega value (Hayes & Coutts, 2020) of the current sample indicated internal reliability was [ω = .816].

Negative attitudes towards autism were measured using the 26-item Social Attitudes

The ω value of the current sample indicated internal reliability was [.838].

Internalized stigma was assessed using an autism-adapted version of the Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness Inventory (ISMI; Ritsher et al., 2003), which has been validated in Turkish as the Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness Scale (RHİDÖ; Ersoy & Varan, 2007).

Data Analysis

Quantitative Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R. Quantitative analyses examined endorsement of autism-related terminology using GLMMs with a binomial link function, given the dichotomous nature of endorsement responses (0 = not endorsed,1 = endorsed), using the glmmTMB package. To assess whether the probability of endorsement differed across autismrelated terms, a series of GLMMs, one for each category of terminology, was fitted with endorsement as the dependent variable, terminology entered as a categorical fixed effect, and participant ID as a random intercept [glmmTMB(Endorsement ~ terminology + (1 | ID))].

To examine individual-difference associations, while retaining the maximum available data, two additional sets of category-specific GLMMs were fitted. First, a combined model included negative attitudes towards autism, internalised stigma, and autism in-group identification, together with the interaction between terminology and each predictor Models were estimated using maximum likelihood estimation, and continuous predictors were z-standardised across all available respondents before model-specific complete-case samples were selected. Sum-to-zero contrasts were used for terminology so that predictor main effects represented associations averaged equally across terms. Overall effects of terminology and terminology × predictor interactions were evaluated using Type III Wald chi-square tests via car package. Average predictor trends and term-specific slopes were estimated using emmeans package. Pairwise comparisons between terms were conducted with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate correction, since this adjustment maintains greater power while controlling Type I Error, and is recommended for scenarios where there are numerous tests (e.g., 16-64 pairwise comparisons) (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995; Chen et al., 2017; Glickman et al., 2014; Hollestein et al., 2021; Lydersen, 2024; McDonald, 2014).

Estimated marginal means and pairwise comparisons for the terminology-only models were calculated on the response scale, with the same correction applied to pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. Given the smaller complete-case samples for the predictor models, particularly AQ analyses, these individual-difference findings were interpreted cautiously.

Qualitative Data Analysis

Open-ended responses were analysed in ATLAS.ti using inductive reflexive thematic analysis (Braun et al., 2019; Braun & Clarke, 2006).

[blinded] conducted initial familiarisation and generated initial semantic codes across the dataset.

[blinded] subsequently reviewed the coded data with [blinded] at 25%, 50%, and 100% of the analysis, prompting reflection on code boundaries, assumptions, and alternative interpretations. Based on participatory research guidelines (Baştan, 2025; Fletcher-Watson & Happé, 2019; Keating, 2021), community involvement was embedded throughout the study. A consultancy committee including autistic members [blinded] was consulted at multiple stages.

Qualitative Results

Eighty-three participants responded to the open-ended question on autism-related terminology preferences. Two responses were excluded because they did not provide sufficient information for analysis. The final dataset for qualitative analysis comprised 81 responses ( 22collected in person and 59 online), ranging from 4 to 255 words in length (M = 44 words).

Reflexivity Statement

Reflexivity was embedded throughout the analytic process. The analysis was conducted from a critical realist ontological position and a contextualist epistemological stance, recognising participants' accounts as grounded in lived experience while acknowledging that these accounts are shaped by broader social, cultural, and linguistic contexts (Braun & Clarke, 2013). The first researcher [blinded] is a developmental scientist who has experience in analysing qualitative data, while the second researcher [blinded] is a developmental scientist/psychologist whose expertise is in autism research. Neither researcher is autistic, and this positionality was considered throughout the analysis.

Thematic Analysis

Six themes were identified and are presented in order of prevalence: ( 1) Don't Decide for Us What We Should Be Called (Autonomy; N = 63, 78%), (2) Words Matter Because of How People Use Them (Social Impact; N = 57, 70%), (3) This Is Part of Who I Am, But It Doesn't Define Me Completely (Identity; N = 57, 70%), (4) Call It What It Is -Don't Soften or Oversimplify It (Accuracy; N = 34, 42%), (5) It Depends, I Use Different Words in Different Situations (Context; N = 21, 26%), and (6) Just Respect How People Want to Describe Themselves (Respect; N = 12, 15%). Themes were not mutually exclusive, and many responses contributed to multiple themes. Collectively, they illustrate that autism-related terminology extends beyond word choice to reflect broader autonomy, identity, stigma, accuracy, and social context.

Theme 1: Don't Decide for Us What We Should Be Called (Autonomy).

Autonomy emerged as the most prevalent theme, appearing in 78% of responses. Participants consistently emphasised that autistic people should have the right to decide how they describe themselves, rejecting the idea that there is a single correct way to talk about autism. Terminology was framed as a personal choice shaped by lived experience rather than something to be prescribed by clinicians, researchers, advocacy organisations, or wider society.

Many participants expressed frustration at having their preferred terminology questioned or corrected by others: "Whatever I say, I'm the one living this -being corrected by someone else saying 'that's not the right word' makes me angry."

(P65)

Others extended this critique beyond interpersonal interactions, arguing that autism terminology is often shaped by institutional and professional interests rather than autistic voices: "Most of the concepts preferred and imposed by medical, academic, and civil society actors serve to reinforce discourses determined by people who profit from how autism is discussed."

(P29)

Although participants often held strong personal preferences, many simultaneously emphasised that no single terminology should be imposed on everyone. Instead, they framed self-identification as an individual right, recognising that different autistic people may legitimately choose different ways of describing themselves.

"I dislike person-with-autism type expressions that, as recommended by ridiculous organisations abroad, portray autism as something separate from the individual -a disease to be eliminated rather than a part of who they are. But it is everyone's own choice." (P19) Subtheme: Rejection of externally imposed terminology

Participants frequently rejected terminology that they perceived as imposed through medical, historical, or normative frameworks rather than emerging from autistic people's own understanding of themselves. Person-first language, deficit-oriented terminology, and historically contested labels were all criticised because they were seen as reflecting external assumptions about autism.

The rejection of person-first language often centred on the idea that it separates autism from the individual and frames it as an illness or problem to be removed: "Saying 'person with autism' sounds like saying 'leper' -as though it were an acquired illness.

But it is simply a difference." (P53) Several participants also rejected the continued use of the Asperger label, linking it to its historical origins and the hierarchical distinctions embedded within it: "I dislike Asperger syndrome and the terms derived from it, because they were invented by the Nazi doctor Hans Asperger to separate individuals on the autism spectrum into those who would and would not be useful to the Nazi regime. For this reason, I believe these terms imply -even if unintentionally -being 'superior' to other individuals on the autism spectrum." (P9) "I strongly reject being referred to by this name -I wish my doctor had known this too. The screening work … aimed to separate autistic people into those deemed 'useful' -it is horrifying." (P92)

Theme 2: Words Matter Because of How People Use Them (Social Impact). The social impact of autism-related terminology emerged in 70% of responses. Participants consistently described the language as acquiring meaning through how it is used within society rather than through the words themselves. Many explained that terms such as autistic had become associated with bullying, mockery, and ableist attitudes, shaping how they felt about using these labels to describe themselves. Beyond describing the effects of stigma, some participants argued that the problem lay in ableist attitudes rather than in the terminology itself. They suggested that replacing one label with another would do little to improve autistic people's experiences unless broader social perceptions also changed: "The reason the word autistic is seen as problematic is because autistic people are subjected to stigma.

We do not need to change the word autistic -we need to change the stigma and ableist ideology." (P71)

Others actively reclaimed the word autistic as a way of challenging prejudice rather than avoiding it: Identity emerged as a central influence on participants' terminology preferences, appearing in 70% of responses. Many described autism as an integral part of who they are, rejecting language that positioned it as something separate from themselves. For these participants, terminology was not simply descriptive but reflected how they understood their own identity.

"I accept the word autistic as part of my existence." (P71) "Because I see autism as an important part of a person's identity, I do not find expressions such as 'person with autism' or 'has autism' appropriate, as these expressions portray autism as a condition that exists separately from identity." (P34) At the same time, participants emphasised that autism represented only one aspect of a broader, multidimensional identity. While recognising its importance, they resisted being reduced to a diagnostic label. "No matter how much ASD affects my life, I do not think a psychiatric diagnosis captures all of my characteristics. It is one aspect of me, but an important one that influences all other aspects of my life." (P108)

A smaller number of participants distanced themselves from autism as an identity category altogether, preferring to describe themselves more broadly in terms of difference:

"I don't see myself as autistic -I'm just different." (P62) Theme 4: Call It What It Is -Don't Soften or Oversimplify it (Accuracy).

Accuracy emerged as an important consideration in participants' terminology preferences, appearing in 42% of responses. Participants consistently favoured language that they felt reflected autistic experience honestly, rejecting terminology they perceived as euphemistic, minimising, or overly simplistic.

Subtheme 1: Rejection of euphemistic and minimising language

Many participants critiqued language that appeared to soften autism in an attempt to sound more respectful. Rather than reducing stigma, they argued that these expressions implied that autism was something negative that required linguistic cushioning. "Expressions like 'child with autism' and 'special child' are among my least favourite, because they feel like words produced by neurotypicals who think the situation is negative and pitiful and are trying to soften it." (P65) "When non-autistic people talk about autistic people, they tend to censor, sanitise, and overcompensate -as though there is something shameful that needs to be hidden."

Subtheme 2: Critique of functioning labels and rigid classification

Participants also questioned terminology that categorised autistic people according to perceived levels of functioning. These labels were viewed as overly simplistic, and, in some cases, harmful because they obscured individual differences and support needs.

"I don't like being called 'mild' or 'severe' because these labels constantly minimised the difficulties I experience, my barriers are overlooked, and I cannot get support." (P16) "I am against labels such as Asperger and high-functioning because they create an artificial divide among autistic people based on criteria determined by neurotypicals." (P4) Subtheme 3: Preference for specificity and needs-based terminology Rather than broad or hierarchical labels, many participants preferred terminology that more accurately reflected individual circumstances and support requirements. "Rather than high/low functioning, I prefer high/low support needs." (P33) "I am uncomfortable with autism being seen as a disability... I would prefer autistic people to be seen as individuals who need support." (P106) Theme 5: It Depends, I Use Different Words in Different Situations (Context). Context shaped terminology preferences for around one-quarter of participants ( 26%). Rather than expressing a single preferred label, many described adapting their language according to their audience, communicative goals, language, or anticipated social reactions.

Some participants used the terms they personally considered imperfect because they were more familiar or attracted less stigma in particular situations: "Asperger feels like a safer way to express it."

(P49) "High functioning isn't ethically ideal, but it's necessary to use so people understand I don't need support." (P49)

Others prioritised communication over ideological consistency, choosing whichever terminology they believed would be most easily understood:

"I prefer to use whichever is most understandable to the general public" (P39)

Language itself also shaped terminology preferences. Several participants described using different terms across Turkish and English because some concepts felt more natural or established in one language than the other.

"I can use the term allistic in English, but in Turkish it remains a very foreign-sounding word … the word I prefer changes depending on the language I am speaking." (P33)

Discussion

This study investigated autism-related terminology preferences among Turkishspeaking autistic adults and examined the individual, social, and contextual considerations underlying these preferences. Overall, participants showed a strong preference for terminology that conceptualised autism as a neurological difference rather than a disease or deficit.

However, no single way of referring to an autistic person was endorsed by a clear majority. The qualitative findings helped contextualise the variability in participants' responses. Participants viewed terminology as an issue of autonomy, identity, social impact, accuracy, context, and respect. The individual-difference hypotheses received mixed support. Negative attitudes towards autism, internalised stigma, and autism in-group identification showed several selective associations with endorsement, whereas autistic traits were not significantly associated with terminology preferences in the smaller AQ sub-sample. The findings highlight both a broad movement away from deficit-oriented terminology and substantial diversity in how Turkish-speaking autistic adults wish to describe themselves.

The most clearly preferred name for autism was the direct term otizm ("autism"), while otizm spektrumu ("autism spectrum") was the second most frequently endorsed expression. By contrast, ağır otizm ("profound autism") received little support. When autism was conceptualised more broadly, nörolojik/beyin farklılığı "neurological/brain difference" was strongly preferred, whereas hastalık "disease" and eksiklik "deficit" were endorsed by very few participants. Similarly, farklılıklar "differences" was the most frequently endorsed term when discussing both difficulties and strengths. These findings do not support our expectation that the medicalised and deficit-based discourse historically prominent in Turkish clinical and educational contexts would translate into a predominant preference for medical-model terminology (Doenyas et al., 2023; Dursun et al., 2022). Instead, the results suggest that many Turkish-speaking autistic people understand autism through a neurodiversity-informed framework that recognises meaningful challenges without defining autism primarily as pathology.

This pattern is broadly consistent with findings from several other linguistic contexts.

French-speaking autistic adults have shown preferences for direct, identity-oriented, and difference-based expressions, including "autism," "autistic person," and "neurological difference" (Geelhand et al., 2023). Polish-speaking autistic people have similarly favoured "autism spectrum" and "on the autism spectrum" over more medicalised descriptions (Niedźwiecka & Pisula, 2024). In Chinese-speaking samples, neurodiversity-and spectrumrelated terminology was among the most preferred, although preferences also varied across identity groups and culturally specific terms (Lao et al., 2026). Thus, although the preferred linguistic form differs across languages, the present findings contribute to a growing crosscultural pattern in which many autistic people favour terminology that is direct, noneuphemistic, and less explicitly deficit-oriented.

At the same time, the findings cannot be reduced to a straightforward preference for identity-first over person-first language. When referring to an autistic person, the highest endorsement rates were observed for otistik ("autistic"), nöroçeşitli kişi ("neurodivergent person"), and otizmli ("[person] with autism"), with relatively small differences between them.

Similarly, when referring to someone's autistic identity, Otistiktir. "is autistic," Nöroçeşitlidir.

Because participants could select multiple options, these similar endorsement rates should be understood as evidence of the group-level acceptability of several expressions rather than as evidence that individual participants were indifferent between them.

This distribution differs from the clearer preference for identity-first terminology frequently reported in English-speaking samples (Bury, Jellett, Spoor, et al., 2023; Keating et al., 2023; Robertson et al., 2025) and the person-first preference found in some Dutch-speaking samples (Buijsman et al., 2023; De Laet et al., 2023). A recent systematic review likewise concluded that identity-first language was preferred in most included studies but also found considerable endorsement of person-first language and substantial variation both within and between languages and cultures (Schuck et al., 2025). The present findings extend this conclusion by demonstrating that the English identity-first/person-first distinction may be particularly difficult to transfer directly into Turkish. Both otistik kişi and otizmli kişi place the autism-related descriptor before the noun, while the contrast between them is conveyed through morphology and conceptual meaning rather than word order. Moreover, the more frequently endorsed shortened forms, otistik and otizmli, can refer to a person without an explicit noun.

Consequently, preferences expressed in Turkish should not be interpreted solely through categories developed around English syntax.

The qualitative findings further demonstrate why a binary distinction between identityfirst and person-first language is insufficient. Autonomy was the most prevalent theme, with participants emphasising that autistic people should determine how they describe themselves.

Participants resisted terminology imposed by clinicians, researchers, organisations, or nonautistic people, even when such terminology was presented as more respectful. This concern reflects broader critiques of epistemic authority in autism research, whereby non-autistic institutions have historically retained disproportionate influence over how autism and autistic people are represented (Botha, 2021; Botha et al., 2023). Participants' accounts therefore suggest that respectful language depends not only on the selected term but also on who selects it and whether autistic people have meaningful control over that choice.

Identity was also central to participants' reasoning. Many described autism as an inseparable and valued part of who they were, thereby supporting identity-oriented terminology. Nevertheless, participants often simultaneously emphasised that autism did not constitute the entirety of their identity. These accounts challenge the assumption that identityfirst language necessarily reduces an individual to a diagnosis. Instead, participants appeared able to understand autism as an integral aspect of identity while maintaining a broader and multidimensional sense of self. Similar associations between autistic identity and identity-first language have been observed in Australian samples (Bury, Jellett, Haschek, et al., 2023), while recent research across developmental neurodivergence has likewise found that people may value identity-first terminology because it is concise, accurate, and reflective of lived experience (Pearson et al., 2026).

Participants also distinguished between the inherent meaning of a term and the social meanings it acquires through use. Some experienced otistik as uncomfortable because it had been used as an insult or a form of mockery, whereas others deliberately reclaimed the same term to challenge ableism. Similarly, some participants reported using Asperger or functioning-related terminology strategically because they expected these expressions to attract less stigma or to be more readily understood. Such preferences should not necessarily be interpreted as endorsement of the conceptual or historical assumptions underlying these labels.

Rather, they may represent pragmatic attempts to manage anticipated prejudice. This finding illustrates Dwyer's (2022) concept of incommensurability: The same expression may communicate identity, pathology, insult, familiarity, or resistance depending on the speaker, audience, and setting.

The coexistence of quantitative endorsement and qualitative criticism was particularly apparent for Asperger terminology. Although Asperger-related expressions received moderate quantitative endorsement, several qualitative responses strongly rejected the label because of its historical associations and its perceived role in creating hierarchies among autistic people.

Some participants may continue to use the term because it formed part of their original diagnosis, is familiar to other people, or is perceived as less stigmatising than otistik. Therefore, aggregate endorsement rates may conceal substantially different motivations for using the same term. This underscores the value of the mixed-methods design: preference frequencies indicate which terms participants consider usable, whereas qualitative accounts reveal the sometimesconflicting identity-related, historical, and pragmatic considerations underlying their choices.

Participants' criticism of functioning labels provides a related example. Although "high-functioning" and "low-functioning" received some endorsement, qualitative responses described these labels as overly simplistic, divisive, and potentially harmful because they can obscure fluctuating abilities and support needs. Several participants preferred support-needs terminology, consistent with previous findings that many autistic people view functioning labels as reductive and favour descriptions that more specifically communicate the type or level of support required (Keates et al., 2025). Nevertheless, support-needs terminology should not itself become a fixed substitute for functioning labels. Support requirements can differ across domains and contexts, and the present results indicate that even needs-based expressions are not universally preferred. Wherever possible, specific descriptions of an individual's needs, strengths, and circumstances may therefore be more informative than global categorisations.

In our analyses considering the contribution of individual differences to endorsement, higher negative attitudes towards autism were associated with lower endorsement of terms, on average, for referring to an autistic person, describing autistic identity, and referring to a nonautistic person. Negative attitudes about autism also predicted lower endorsement of alistik ("allistic"), Nörotipik ("Neurotypical") and Nörotipik kişi ("Neurotypical person"), terms particularly associated with neurodiversity discourse. By contrast, in-group identification showed a different pattern: those feeling stronger in-group identification tended to endorse alistik ("allistic") and Otizmi olmayan kişi ("Person who does not have autism"). In-group identification was also associated with lower average endorsement of terms to refer to someone's diagnostic status/autistic identity, while internalised stigma was associated with greater average endorsement of terms for referring to an autistic person. These interpretations remain tentative because the cross-sectional design cannot establish whether attitudes, stigma, and identification shape terminology preferences or are themselves influenced by engagement with particular forms of language and community discourse.

The findings have several implications for researchers, clinicians, educators, media professionals, and advocacy organisations. First, professionals should ask autistic people which terminology they prefer whenever individual communication is possible. A person's preferred terminology should not be corrected simply because another expression is considered more professionally conventional or theoretically appropriate. Second, when writing about groups and individual preferences cannot be obtained, the present findings support the use of direct and non-deficit-oriented Turkish terminology, particularly otizm, while recognising the acceptability of identity-first, person-first, spectrum-based, and neurodiversity-related expressions. Terms such as "disease," "deficit," "profound autism," and global functioning labels should be used cautiously and only when their meaning is necessary, clearly defined, and acceptable to the people concerned. Third, terminology should be accompanied by accurate and non-stigmatising representations. As participants emphasised, replacing one label with another will have limited benefit if underlying ableist attitudes remain unchanged. Language reform should therefore occur alongside changes in how autistic people are included, supported, and represented.

The study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first mixed-methods investigation focused specifically on the terminology preferences of Turkish-speaking autistic adults. The study examined a broad range of expressions rather than limiting the inquiry to identity-first and person-first language. Recruitment across online and clinical settings and several regions of Türkiye increased the range of perspectives represented, while autistic community consultation contributed to the linguistic and cultural relevance of the materials.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Schuck et al. (2025) emphasize that language preferences are shaped by linguistic, cultural, and methodological factors, rather than reflecting a global consensus. They note that preferences vary not only across countries and languages but also according to recruitment strategies, diagnostic status, gender distribution, and exposure to neurodiversity discourse (Schuck et al., 2025). This study was conducted via convenience sampling and primarily online, which may exclude participants with intellectual disabilities or lower literacy. Online recruitment may have particularly attracted people already engaged with autism-related or neurodiversity-oriented discussions. The findings may consequently underrepresent autistic people with intellectual disabilities, nonspeaking autistic people, people with limited internet access, and those who are less connected to autistic communities. Formal diagnosis and self-identification were also combined, and future adequately powered research should examine whether diagnostic history, age at diagnosis, and mode of identification relate to language preferences. In addition, only a subset of participants completed the open-ended question. Although these responses revealed substantial conceptual richness, interviews or focus groups could provide a more detailed examination of how terminology changes across clinical, family, educational, occupational, and online settings.

Future research should prioritise accessible and participatory methods to include autistic people who remain underrepresented in terminology studies. Comparisons among autistic people, family members, clinicians, educators, and the general public would help identify where differences in terminology may contribute to misunderstanding or unequal power relations. Research should also investigate how preferences vary according to audience and context, particularly when disclosing a diagnosis, seeking accommodations, communicating with healthcare professionals, or participating in autistic communities. Coproduced qualitative research could further examine the cultural meanings attached to otistik, otizmli, Asperger, nöroçeşitli, and emerging Turkish terminology. Such work could inform community-led guidance that remains flexible enough to respect individual choice.

Conclusions

Turkish-speaking autistic people generally preferred direct, difference-based, and nondeficit-oriented language, but there was no universally endorsed way to refer to an autistic person. Participants' preferences reflected identity and accuracy as well as experiences of stigma, audience expectations, and communicative context. Most importantly, participants emphasised their right to determine how they are described. Researchers, clinicians, and other professionals should therefore ask about individual preferences whenever possible and avoid imposing a single terminology on the wider autistic community.
