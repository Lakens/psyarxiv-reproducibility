What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

In many jurisdictions, police are periodically granted new powers intended to reduce crime and enhance community safety, raising questions about the conditions under which the public supports expanded police authority. The Gangs Act 2024 granted the New Zealand Police new powers to restrict gang activity, including the authority to arrest individuals for displaying gang insignia in public and to disperse gang gatherings in public spaces. Drawing on survey data from a non-representative sample of 766 adult residents of Aotearoa New Zealand, we examine normative (procedural justice and legitimacy), instrumental (police effectiveness and fear of crime), and affective predictors of public support for these powers.

Affective responses (feeling safer at the prospect of the new powers) were the strongest predictor of support, and accounted for much of the association between the other predictors and support. Procedural justice was associated with support directly and indirectly, mainly via affective responses. Right-wing political orientation and fear of crime were associated with higher support, and interpersonal contact with gang-affiliated individuals predicted lower support. Police effectiveness was positively correlated with support but negatively associated with it once fairness was accounted for. This study extends the police empowerment literature to the context of powers targeted at a specific, socially stigmatised group. Our findings suggest that support for such powers is more closely tied to how they make people feel than to judgements about police effectiveness, and that those with personal contact with gangaffiliated individuals are less supportive, raising questions about public opinion as a basis for justifying targeted police powers.

Introduction

In many jurisdictions, police rely on the voluntary cooperation and compliance of the public to effectively uphold and enforce the law. Securing public support becomes especially important when police adopt new powers or technologies that have the potential to significantly affect the communities they serve. Powers introduced without sufficient public backing risk provoking backlash, undermining police legitimacy, and proving difficult to enforce in practice, given that compliance rests largely on consent rather than coercion (Sunshine & Tyler, 2003; Tyler & Fagan, 2008). Questions of public support have been central to debates over the expansion of police powers during the COVID-19 pandemic (Yesberg et al., 2024), police militarisation (Moule et al., 2019), and the adoption of live facial recognition technology (Bradford et al., 2020). The introduction of the Gangs Act 2024 presents a recent such case in Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ). Under the Act, displaying gang insignia in public places became an offence, and police were given powers to enforce this ban and to disperse gang gatherings in public spaces (Gangs Act 2024). As with new powers introduced in other contexts, the long-term effectiveness of these powers depends, in part, on the level of public support they receive.

Research on public support for police empowerment has commonly distinguished between normative motivations-grounded in trust that police authority is legitimate and will be used responsibly-and instrumental motivations, grounded in outcome-based judgements about whether new powers will be effective in reducing crime and improving public safety (Bradford et al., 2020; Fox et al., 2021; Sargeant et al., 2024). Both involve relatively deliberate evaluations, of either the police or the likely outcomes of their powers. However, support may also depend on people's more immediate affective responses to new powers, which do not map neatly onto either motivation. Unlike trust or outcome-based judgements, affective reactions may function as a heuristic: a quicker, less deliberative response to a policy (Slovic et al., 2002 (Slovic et al., , 2004)). Although evidence is limited, a small number of studies have found significant associations between affect and support for police empowerment (Yesberg et al., 2024; Yesberg & Bradford, 2019).

This study draws on the police empowerment literature to examine support for the Gangs Act 2024 in NZ. However, the Gangs Act differs from most prior instances of police empowerment studied in the literature in two important ways. First, unlike powers directed at the general public, those granted under the Gangs Act target a specific and socially stigmatised subset of the population: gang-affiliated individuals. Second, unlike prior instances of police empowerment, which have often been initiated by police services (e.g., live facial recognition), the powers granted under the Gangs Act originated as government legislation that was introduced as part of a coalition commitment to "restoring law and order" (NZ Government, 2024). As a result, public support for the Act may reflect not only perceptions of police, but also broader punitive orientations toward gang-affiliated individuals. We therefore examine the normative, instrumental, and affective predictors of support identified in the police empowerment literature alongside factors known to shape punitive attitudes, such as political orientation and interpersonal contact with gang-affiliated individuals (Hirschfield & Piquero, 2010; Rade et al., 2016).

Policing Gangs in Aotearoa New Zealand

Gangs have long been a part of the social landscape of NZ, often emerging within communities affected by colonisation, poverty, and alienation. Modern gangs began to form in the 1950s and 1960s, comprising predominantly young Pākehā (European) men drawn to a nomadic lifestyle centred on motorcycling and the rejection of mainstream cultural norms. This period also saw the rise of the Hells Angels, the first clearly patched and organised outlaw motorcycle gang in NZ, and with it a shift toward more organised and violent activity (Gilbert, 2013). By the mid-1970s, the makeup of gangs in NZ had shifted markedly to include several street gangs and increasing membership of Māori (Indigenous people of NZ): a change reflecting adverse intergenerational impacts of colonial policies (e.g., Native Land Acts 1862 and 1865; Tohunga Suppression Act 1907), abuse in state care facilities, urban migration, and exclusion from social and economic opportunities (Abuse in Care Royal Commission of Inquiry, 2024; Gilbert, 2013). During this period, gangs began recruiting disenfranchised young Māori seeking belonging and identity, and a means of resisting the structures that had excluded them (Gilbert, 2013; Taonui & Newbold, 2012; Webb, 2017).

As gangs expanded their presence across NZ, their relationship with the police became increasingly strained. From the 1970s onwards, both the Government and Police shifted toward more overt suppression strategies, with parliament introducing numerous legislative amendments aimed at criminalising gang membership, restricting association, and expanding police powers to intervene in gang spaces (Gilbert, 2013). During this same period, however, governments also recognised the social drivers of gang formation and partnered with gangs to deliver employment and social initiatives, such as the Community Initiative Scheme (1981) and Group Employment Liaison Scheme (1982). Although these initiatives were seen by some as effective in reducing gang-related harm, they were nonetheless abandoned in a subsequent shift away from addressing structural causes and toward measures that symbolically targeted gang identity, including a regional ban of gang were granted new search powers, including authority to enter the homes of gang-affiliated individuals who repeatedly breach the ban to seize prohibited items. Second, the Act empowers police to issue dispersal notices to groups of three or more gang-affiliated individuals observed associating in public; once a notice is issued, those individuals must leave the area and refrain from associating with one another in public for seven days. 1The introduction of the Gangs Act 2024 has been met with considerable criticism from community organisations, experts, and public sector officials, because the legislation does not address the socioeconomic drivers of gang formation or the structural disadvantages faced by gang-affected communities (Mana Mokopuna -Children and Young People's Commission, 2024; Ministry of Justice, 2024; New Zealand Council for Civil Liberties, 2024; New Zealand Human Rights Commission, 2024) and focusses on suppressing the visibility of gangs rather than preventing crime. Yet, little is known about how the wider NZ public views the Act. Policing in NZ operates on the principle of policing by consent (NZ Police, 2020); hence, understanding public support for the Act is important because it may partly determine the Act's implementation, effectiveness, and broader social impact. Public reactions to the Gangs Act likely reflect two related dimensions of opinion: willingness to grant police greater authority in general, and punitive attitudes toward gang-affiliated individuals specifically. We first turn to the literature on police empowerment to understand why members of the public might support or oppose the new police powers relating to gangs.

Police Empowerment

Police empowerment refers to the extent to which the public supports increases in police use of discretionary power, and accepts the limits this increased power may place on people's rights and freedoms (Fox et al., 2021; Yesberg et al., 2024). Research on police empowerment has tended to focus on visible or controversial forms of policing, such as police militarisation (Moule et al., 2019), and largely comes from the US and UK. Studies have examined public support for police use of surplus military gear (Fox et al., 2021) and the routine arming of police (Yesberg & Bradford, 2019), along with attitudes toward new technologies, such as police use of facial recognition technology (Bradford et al., 2020), body-worn cameras (Crow et al., 2017; Miethe et al., 2019), and drones (Heen et al., 2017).

Research has also examined police empowerment during the COVID-19 pandemic, when police in many countries were granted expanded powers to enforce public health restrictions (Moule et al., 2024; Sargeant et al., 2024; Yesberg et al., 2024). Across these contexts, research consistently distinguishes between normative and instrumental motivations underlying public support for expanding police authority (Kuen et al., 2025; Sargeant et al., 2024).

Normative Motivations

Normative motivations for supporting police empowerment are grounded in perceptions of procedural justice: the fairness with which police engage with members of the public (Sunshine & Tyler, 2003; Tyler, 2006). When people believe that officers treat the public fairly and respectfully, they infer that police can be trusted to use new powers appropriately, and are therefore more willing to support them. Sunshine and Tyler (2003) found that perceptions of procedural justice were associated with greater public support for giving police discretion over how to fight neighbourhood crime (e.g., whether they should have the power to stop and question citizens on the street). Other studies have shown that perceived fairness predicts support for specific powers and technologies, including the use of body-worn cameras (Miethe et al., 2019) and expansion of DNA databases (Kuen et al., 2025). During the COVID-19 pandemic, perceptions that police enforced rules fairly predicted support for the enforcement of social distancing rules in the US (Nix et al., 2021), Australia (Sargeant et al., 2024), and the UK (Yesberg et al., 2024).

This pathway between normative motivations and support for police empowerment is thought to operate, at least in part, through police legitimacy: the belief that an institution has the right to exercise power, the authority to govern, and is morally justified in expecting cooperation and compliance (Jackson, 2018; Tyler, 2006). Although there is no consensus about how legitimacy should be conceptualised and measured (Jackson & Bradford, 2019; Pósch et al., 2021), one common approach separates the construct along two dimensions: normative alignment and obligation to obey (Jackson et al., 2023). Normative alignment reflects the belief that police officers act in ways that accord with widely shared values and broader social norms of appropriate conduct, whereas obligation to obey captures an internalised sense of moral duty: the belief that one should follow police instructions even if they disagree with them or if doing so goes against their own self-interest (Jackson et al., 2023; Pósch et al., 2021). Police legitimacy has been shown to predict support for police empowerment directly-for example, Moule et al. (2019) linked legitimacy to support for police militarisation in the US-and to mediate the effect of procedural justice on support. Bradford et al. (2020) found that both obligation to obey and normative alignment mediated all of the modelled association between procedural justice and acceptance of police use of live facial recognition, and Yesberg et al. (2024) found that obligation to obey mediated part of the association between procedural justice and support for police powers during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Instrumental Motivations

Instrumental motivations for supporting police empowerment, by contrast, are grounded in outcome-based assessments of whether expanded police powers will reduce crime and improve public safety, rather than in beliefs about whether police can be trusted with them. Perceptions of police effectiveness are one such assessment. Sunshine and Tyler (2003) found that perceived effectiveness predicted support for greater use of police discretion. Similar associations have been observed between perceptions of police effectiveness and support for armed policing in the UK (Yesberg & Bradford, 2019), and police militarisation in the US (Moule et al., 2019). Individuals who view the police as effective at fighting crime are more likely to support expanded police authority, seeing it as a practical means to that end (i.e., it will make police even more effective at fighting crime). Not all studies find this effect, however: net of trust in procedural fairness, Bradford et al. (2020) found that trust in police effectiveness was not significantly associated with either legitimacy or support for police use of live facial recognition. Miethe et al. (2019) found that assessments of police effectiveness had no significant effect on support for body-worn cameras, while procedural fairness did. This pattern aligns with the broader literature on police legitimacy, which typically finds that procedural justice plays a more prominent role than perceived police effectiveness in promoting legitimacy and cooperation with police (Jackson et al., 2026; Sunshine & Tyler, 2003).

Fear of crime represents a related but distinct instrumental pathway explaining support for police empowerment: individuals who are more worried about crime are more likely to support developments that grant police more power, to the extent that these developments are perceived to increase safety. For example, Fox et al. (2021) found that fear of crime predicted public support for police acquisition and use of surplus military equipment. Worry about crime has also been linked to support for police use of live facial recognition (Bradford et al., 2020) and body-worn cameras (Crow et al., 2017). Legitimacy has also been shown to mediate part of the association between fear of crime and support for police empowerment (Bradford et al., 2020). For example, Yesberg et al. (2024) found that fear of crime was indirectly associated with support for police powers during the COVID-19 pandemic, through an obligation to obey police.

Affective Responses

Alongside normative and instrumental motivations, people's affective responses to proposed powers may represent another important predictor of support for police empowerment. Affect refers to a person's spontaneous positive or negative feeling toward an object, issue, or decision, which can shape judgements independently of deliberate reasoning about its costs or benefits (Slovic et al., 2004). Research suggests that when people lack detailed knowledge about a topic or new development, they rely on such affective judgements -intuitive feelings about whether something is "good" or "bad"-to guide decisions (Slovic et al., 2002 (Slovic et al., , 2004)). This pattern has been observed across a range of unfamiliar risks and technologies (e.g., Merk & Pönitzsch, 2017). Known as the affect heuristic (Slovic et al., 2002), this process allows people to form rapid evaluative judgements using feelings as a proxy for more effortful deliberate analysis of risks and benefits.

Within the police empowerment literature specifically, affect has received comparatively little attention relative to trust and legitimacy. Affect has typically been operationalised through items capturing people's emotional and behavioural responses to a particular power; for example, whether the presence of an armed officer makes a person feel safer and more comfortable approaching them (Yesberg & Bradford, 2019). Affect and trust are closely linked: affective responses to an unfamiliar policy or technology are shaped, in part, by the extent to which people trust the actors responsible for implementing it (Midden & Huijts, 2009). In the context of the routine arming of police in London, Yesberg and Bradford (2019) found that affect predicted support for giving police more weapons, and that this association was among the strongest of the predictors tested. Similarly, in the context of expanded police powers during the COVID-19 pandemic, Yesberg et al. (2024) found that people who felt safer at the prospect of police having additional powers to enforce lockdown restrictions were more likely to judge the powers as justified. In both studies, affect mediated the relationship between procedural justice, effectiveness, and support for the new powers.

Predicting Punitive Attitudes

The factors reviewed above help explain why people support expanding police authority in general, but the Gangs Act 2024 differs from most police empowerment cases in targeting a specific, socially stigmatised group. Public support for the Act may therefore also reflect punitive attitudes toward gang-affiliated individuals, independent of views about police authority. We next consider two factors relevant to these attitudes: political orientation and interpersonal contact with gang-affiliated individuals.

Political orientation, typically measured on a left-right or liberal-conservative scale, is one of the most consistent predictors of punitive attitudes toward offenders (Rade et al., 2016). Jost et al. (2003) argue that political conservatism is, in part, a form of motivated social cognition: a response to a psychological need to manage uncertainty and threat, reflected in traits such as intolerance of ambiguity, resistance to change, and heightened sensitivity to perceived danger. This threat-management account offers a plausible mechanism for why political conservatism is linked to more punitive attitudes toward groups seen as socially disruptive or threatening, such as gang-affiliated individuals. More broadly, scholars have noted that punitive policies directed at socially marginalised groups frequently feature in populist 'law and order' political strategies that emphasise social threat and disorder (Pratt & Clark, 2005; Wacquant, 2009). Such strategies may resonate with individuals who prioritise safety and social stability, particularly when the targeted group is already stigmatised. The Gangs Act may therefore represent a context in which broader ideological orientations are closely tied to willingness to expand police authority.

A meta-analysis by Rade et al. (2016) found that individuals with more conservative political orientations held more negative attitudes toward people with criminal convictions, while Hirschfield and Piquero (2010) found that conservative ideology was associated with more negative evaluations of formerly incarcerated individuals. In a survey of NZ adults, those who self-identified as right-wing were more likely to disagree with arguments for less punitive sentences (Oldfield & Mills, 2022). Relatedly, Gerber and Jackson (2016) found that individuals high in right-wing authoritarianism-a belief system characterised by deference to authority, adherence to traditional norms, and intolerance of perceived social deviancewere more sensitive to social threats and more supportive of harsh or coercive criminal justice responses. Most directly relevant to police empowerment, Bradford et al. (2020) found that participants who placed themselves further to the right politically were more likely to grant police legitimacy and reported lower privacy concerns regarding a new police technology, both of which were, in turn, associated with greater support for its use. Together, these findings suggest that a right-wing political orientation may be associated with greater support for expanding police powers, particularly when directed toward groups perceived as threatening the existing social order, such as gang-affiliated individuals.

Intergroup Contact Theory (ICT), first proposed by Allport (1954), suggests that the frequency, quality, and context of contact between members of different social groups shape the attitudes they hold toward one another. According to the theory, positive social contact reduces prejudice and fosters more favourable perceptions across group boundaries. Research has identified the mechanisms through which contact reduces prejudice, including reduced intergroup anxiety, increased empathy and perspective taking, and greater knowledge of the outgroup (Pettigrew et al., 2011). Studies consistently show that individuals who know someone with a criminal conviction, or who have had meaningful interactions with formerly incarcerated people, tend to hold less stigmatising and more supportive attitudes toward this population (Hirschfield & Piquero, 2010; Rade et al., 2016). Conversely, those with little or no previous contact often express more negative, stereotyped, or punitive views. Taken together, these findings suggest that contact with gang-affiliated individuals may foster more favourable attitudes and reduce support for new police powers that could negatively impact on them.

Current Study

The literature reviewed above identifies a well-established set of predictors of support for police empowerment, but this research focuses on powers affecting the general public (e.g., militarisation, surveillance, pandemic enforcement) and is largely concentrated in the US and UK. It is unclear whether these predictors will operate in the same way when powers target a specific, stigmatised group, and no research on police empowerment has been conducted in NZ. The Gangs Act 2024 provides a novel opportunity to address these gaps.

We draw on the police empowerment literature-normative motivations (procedural justice, legitimacy), instrumental motivations (police effectiveness, fear of crime), and affective responses-alongside the literature on punitive attitudes (political orientation and interpersonal contact with gang-affiliated individuals) to examine predictors of support for the new policing powers (see Figure 1). We test the following hypotheses: Hypothesis 1: Perceptions of police legitimacy and affective responses will be positively associated with support for the Gangs Act.

Hypothesis 2: Perceptions of procedural justice, police effectiveness, fear of crime, and right-wing political orientation will be positively associated with support for the Gangs Act, while interpersonal contact with gang-affiliated individuals will be negatively associated with support for the Gangs Act.

Hypothesis 3: Perceptions of procedural justice, police effectiveness, fear of crime, right-wing political orientation, and interpersonal contact with gang-affiliated individuals will be indirectly associated with support for the Gangs Act via police legitimacy and affect.

Constructs and Measures

Unless otherwise stated, constructs were measured using Likert-type scales, with higher scores indicating greater levels of the construct (see Supplementary Appendix for all items).

Endogenous variables

Police empowerment was operationalised as participants' support for expanded police powers to restrict gangs. We assessed support for three new powers introduced under the Gangs Act 2024: the police's ability to (1) arrest individuals for wearing gang patches in public; (2) search the homes of individuals who repeatedly breach the ban on gang insignia; and (3) issue dispersal notices to stop gang members from gathering in public spaces.

Participants were asked to rate their support for each power on a 5-point scale from 'strongly oppose' to 'strongly support' (α = .90).

Affective responses were measured with one item asking participants whether the new gang-related powers made them feel more or less safe, consistent with how prior studies have operationalised affect (Yesberg et al., 2024; Yesberg & Bradford, 2019). Participants were asked: "Does the NZ Police having extra powers to deal with gangs make you feel more safe, less safe or does it make no difference?" Participants responded on a 5-point scale from 'a lot less safe' to 'a lot more safe'.

Police legitimacy was measured as two related but distinct constructs (Jackson et al., 2023). Normative alignment captured the extent to which participants perceived police values and behaviour as consistent with their own, using three items: (1) "I support the way the police usually act", (2) "the police usually act in ways that are consistent with my own ideas about what is right and wrong", and (3) "the police stand up for values that are important for people like me" (α = .90). Obligation to obey captured the perceived moral duty to comply with police directives, also measured with three items : "To what extent do you agree or disagree it is your moral duty to… (1) obey the police; (2) support the decisions of police officers, even if you disagree with them; (3) do what the police tell you even if you don't understand or agree with the reasons" (α = .82).

Participants responded to both sets of items on a 5-point scale from 'strongly disagree' to 'strongly agree'.

Exogenous variables

Procedural justice was measured using four items assessing perceptions of police fairness and quality of interpersonal treatment, including whether police (1) treat people fairly, (2) treat people with dignity and respect, (3) listen before making decisions, and (4) explain their decisions (Sunshine & Tyler, 2003). Participants responded on a 5-point scale from 'strongly disagree' to 'strongly agree' (α = .87). Perceived police effectiveness was assessed using three items capturing participants' evaluations of whether the NZ Police are effective at (1) solving crime, (2) preventing crime, and (3) maintaining order. Participants responded on a 5-point scale ranging from 'not at all effective' to 'very effective' (α = .79).

Fear of crime was measured with three items assessing participants' level of worry about crime, antisocial behaviour, and gang activity in their local area. Participants responded on a 5-point scale from 'not at all worried' to 'very worried' (α = .82). Interpersonal contact was measured by asking participants whether they personally knew someone who is, or had been, involved in a gang (1 = contact,0 = no contact). Political orientation was measured using a single item on a 7-point scale, ranging from very left-wing to very right-wing (Bradford et al., 2020).

Control variables

Additional demographic and experiential variables were included as controls to account for confounding factors that could also be associated with support for police powers.

Demographic variables included age (continuous variable), gender identity (1 = male,0 = female or non-binary), ethnicity (five non-exclusive binary variables: European, Māori, Pacific Peoples, Asian, and MELAA), country of birth (1 = NZ,0 = not-NZ), education (1 = university degree,0 = no degree), and employment status (three non-exclusive binary variables: 'employed', 'student', 'unemployed'). We also accounted for whether participants had recent contact with police in the previous two years (1 = police contact,0 = no contact), and whether they had exposure to gangs in the past 12 months (how often they had seen someone in public wearing a gang patch; 5-point scale, ranging from never to very often).

Missing Data

Three items were added to the survey after data collection had begun, so they were not presented to an initial group of participants: worry about gang activity (n = 38 missing), exposure to gangs (n = 39), and interpersonal contact with gang-affiliated individuals (n = 73). For the worry item, we imputed the mean of participants' responses to the other two fear of crime items.

Analytic Approach

We first used Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) in the R package lavaan to assess the scaling properties and empirical distinctiveness of the latent constructs. All observed indicators were set to ordinal, except the fear of crime item with imputed values, which was treated as continuous. The model was estimated using diagonally weighted least squares estimation with robust standard errors. Scaled fit statistics indicated the measurement model was a good fit for the data (N = 766; CFI = 0.979; TLI = 0.974; RMSEA = 0.046). Factor scores from the CFA were saved and used to compute the bivariate correlations reported in Table 2. We then estimated a full structural equation model (SEM) using the same estimator to test direct and indirect associations between the exogenous variables, the three mediators, and support for police empowerment, controlling for the demographic and experiential variables described above. Participants with missing data on any model variable, including exposure to gangs and interpersonal contact, were excluded through listwise deletion, giving an analytic sample of 661.

Discussion

This study examined public support for the new police powers introduced under the Gangs Act 2024 in Aotearoa New Zealand. In doing so, it extends the police empowerment literature to powers directed at a specific and highly stigmatised group, rather than the general population. Support for the Gangs Act varied across the three powers examined. The highest proportion of participants supported dispersal notices preventing gang members from gathering in public and the lowest proportion supported arrest powers for the display of gang insignia, suggesting the public may be more willing to endorse powers perceived as addressing immediate public safety concerns and less willing to support powers that criminalise identity or expression.

Affective responses emerged as the strongest overall predictor of support, and accounted for much of the association between procedural justice, political orientation, fear of crime, and interpersonal contact and support for the Gangs Act. Participants who felt safer at the prospect of new powers were significantly more likely to support them. Although our measure of affect could overlap with support itself, supplementary analyses indicated the two constructs were empirically distinct (see Supplementary Appendix). This finding is consistent with the affect heuristic, which suggests that individuals often rely on emotional responses rather than systematic evaluation when forming judgements about complex or unfamiliar issues (Slovic et al., 2002 (Slovic et al., , 2004)). In the present context, the prospect of new gang-related powers may evoke particularly strong emotional reactions because gangs are highly stigmatised and frequently portrayed in public and political discourse as being associated with danger, disorder, and harm (Bull, 2017; Lewis et al., 2020). Consequently, participants may have relied substantially on how safe the powers made them feel, rather than on a detailed evaluation of the policies. This pattern is consistent with prior police empowerment research in which affective responses to police powers have emerged as a key predictor of support (Yesberg et al., 2024; Yesberg & Bradford, 2019).

Procedural justice also emerged as a consistent predictor of support for the new powers. Participants who viewed the police as fair and respectful were significantly more likely to endorse the powers introduced by the Gangs Act. Procedural justice was associated with support both directly and indirectly, through obligation to obey police, and, more strongly, through affect. These findings align with procedural justice theory and prior research on police empowerment, which shows that perceptions of police fairness are associated with greater support for police powers, partly by enhancing police legitimacy (Sargeant et al., 2024; Sunshine & Tyler, 2003; Yesberg et al., 2024). In the present study, the indirect association between procedural justice and support for police empowerment via obligation to obey was modest, and procedural justice was associated with support mainly through how safe people felt about the new powers. Obligation to obey was significantly associated with support, while normative alignment was not; however, the two coefficients were identical in size and did not differ significantly from one another. Although the constructs were empirically distinct, the model may not have been able to separate the unique contributions of procedural justice and normative alignment because they were strongly correlated. Hence, both obligation to obey and normative alignment may be relevant for understanding support for the new powers.

By contrast, instrumental motivations played a more limited role in explaining support for police empowerment. Police effectiveness was positively correlated with support for police empowerment at the bivariate level. However, the SEM revealed a suppression effect, with the direct path between police effectiveness and support turning negative once other factors, particularly perceptions of fairness, were accounted for. In other words, believing the police are effective did not contribute positively to support for these powers beyond perceptions of fairness. One plausible explanation lies in the Gangs Act's focus on suppressing the visibility of gangs, rather than preventing crime, which may make judgements about police effectiveness less relevant to support than for powers framed more directly around outcomes. In the context of COVID-19 enforcement, for example, perceived police effectiveness remained positively associated with support for police empowerment after accounting for procedural justice, and concerns about health risk were the strongest predictor (Sargeant et al., 2024). By contrast, fear of crime was only a weak direct predictor of support for the Gangs Act. These findings are consistent with prior research showing that perceptions of fairness outweigh instrumental concerns in predicting support for police empowerment (Bradford et al., 2020; Kuen et al., 2025; Miethe et al., 2019), and underscore the importance of perceived fairness relative to judgements about police effectiveness.

Right-wing political orientation was also associated with support, both directly and via affect. This finding is consistent with Jost et al.'s (2003) account of conservatism as motivated social cognition in response to uncertainty and threat. Gangs, frequently framed as disruptive and threatening, are the kind of group toward which this account would predict greater support for coercive responses among those on the political right. This interpretation aligns with prior research linking conservative or right-wing orientation to more punitive and stigmatising attitudes toward people who offend (Gerber & Jackson, 2016; Hirschfield & Piquero, 2010; Oldfield & Mills, 2022; Rade et al., 2016). That this association was partly accounted for by affect suggests that ideological differences may be expressed, in part, through how the new powers make people feel. Oldfield and Mills (2022) found that right-wing participants agreed more often with arguments for less punitive sentences when those arguments used populist rhetoric, suggesting that how criminal justice issues are framed can influence the link between political orientation and punitive attitudes. By the same logic, discourse framing gangs as a threat may similarly have strengthened the association between right-wing orientation and feelings of safety under the new powers. Future research could test this directly, for example by experimentally varying how the powers are framed.

Interpersonal contact with gang-affiliated individuals was associated with lower support for the new powers, both directly and via affect: participants who knew someone involved in a gang felt less safe, not more, at the prospect of police having these powers. For people with these connections, the powers may be less likely to evoke an abstract threat and more likely to evoke thoughts of the people they know, and how the powers could affect those people. This interpretation is consistent with Intergroup Contact Theory (Allport, 1954) and with research showing that knowing someone with a criminal conviction is associated with less stigmatising attitudes toward that group (Hirschfield & Piquero, 2010; Rade et al., 2016), as personal contact replaces stereotyped associations with more individualised knowledge (Pettigrew et al., 2011). However, our measure of interpersonal contact was a single binary item asking whether participants knew someone who is, or had been, involved in a gang. It did not capture the closeness, frequency, or quality of contact, all of which are central to Intergroup Contact Theory, and negative contact may have quite different effects.

In addition, because the data are cross-sectional, alternative explanations for the findings cannot be ruled out. People who know someone affiliated with a gang may differ from those who do not in ways not fully captured by our controls, such as living in communities more heavily affected by gangs or by over-or under-policing, or having more negative personal or family experiences with police. The association may also run in the opposite direction: people who already hold less punitive attitudes may be more open to relationships with, or more willing to acknowledge knowing, gang-affiliated individuals.

Future research using longitudinal designs and more detailed measures of contact would help clarify the mechanism. Nevertheless, regardless of its cause, the association between interpersonal contact and lower support has practical implications. People who know gang-affiliated individuals, and who may also be more likely to be affected by these powers, were less supportive of them. If this lower support extends to reduced cooperation with enforcement, it could undermine the powers' implementation and effectiveness.

Analysis of the control variables showed that Māori participants reported lower normative alignment (i.e., the belief that police share their values) and felt less safe than non-Māori participants at the prospect of the new police powers. Given the central role of affect in explaining support, these findings suggest the powers may be met with greater resistance among Māori communities, in which they are most likely to be applied. Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be noted. First, the use of a nonprobability sample, comprising predominantly young university students, limits the generalisability of the findings to the wider NZ population. Compared with the 2023 Census, European participants were over-represented and Pacific peoples were under-represented, and our recruitment strategy did not specifically target the communities most likely to be directly affected by the new powers. That said, a substantial minority of participants (41%) knew someone involved in a gang, so the sample was not entirely removed from those communities. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although our findings are consistent with theoretical expectations, the temporal direction of relationships between procedural justice, legitimacy, affect, interpersonal contact, and support for the new powers cannot be established with the current data. Recent longitudinal research suggests the relationship between procedural justice and police legitimacy may be weaker or more mixed when examined over time (Kuen & Weisburd, 2025; Pina-Sánchez & Brunton-Smith, 2020).

This limitation reflects broader challenges in the field, where few experimental or longitudinal studies have been conducted to isolate the causal impact of procedural justice on attitudes toward police (Nagin & Telep, 2020).

Finally, some of our measures were not optimal because they relied on simplified or narrow indicators that did not fully capture the underlying psychological constructs they were intended to represent. Affect was measured using only a single item about feelings of safety, which does not capture the full range of emotions the new powers might elicit (e.g., anger, anxiety, distrust). Moreover, because the item asked whether the powers made participants feel safer, it may partly capture perceived benefits of the powers rather than affect alone.

Police effectiveness was measured in general terms rather than in relation to the Gangs Act powers themselves, so we cannot assess whether perceptions of the powers' likely effectiveness shaped support. Lastly, political orientation was measured with a single leftright item, which collapses a multidimensional construct (e.g., economic, social, and authoritarian attitudes) and lacks the nuance of established constructs such as right-wing authoritarianism.

Conclusion

This study examined public support for the Gangs Act 2024 and the factors associated with willingness to grant police new, targeted powers in NZ. Drawing on a survey of 766 adults, it extends the police empowerment literature to powers aimed at a specific, stigmatised subgroup of the population. Affective responses-feeling safer at the prospect of the new powers-were the strongest predictor of support, and accounted for much of the association between procedural justice, political orientation, fear of crime, and interpersonal contact and support. Procedural justice was also a significant direct predictor, and perceptions of police fairness mattered more than perceptions of police effectiveness, consistent with prior research on police empowerment (Bradford et al., 2020; Kuen et al., 2025; Miethe et al., 2019; Sargeant et al., 2024). However, procedural justice was associated with support more through affect than through police legitimacy. Right-wing political orientation was also associated with greater support, largely via affect.

The centrality of affective responses raises a concern about the basis on which new powers are justified. If public support reflects how safe new powers make people feel, rather than a considered evaluation of their costs, benefits, and evidence, then public opinion alone is a limited basis for introducing new, targeted police powers. This concern is reinforced by our finding on interpersonal contact: participants who knew someone involved in a gang, and who may be more likely to be affected by the powers, were less supportive of them and felt less safe at the prospect of them than those without such contact. Māori participants, who are among those most likely to be subject to the new powers, also felt less safe at the prospect of the new powers than non-Māori participants. For police, demonstrating fairness to the general public may help secure broad support for expanded powers, but earning the trust of the communities closest to those powers is likely to be the more meaningful test of whether this authority can be exercised legitimately and effectively.
