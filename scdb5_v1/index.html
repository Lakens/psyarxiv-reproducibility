Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

Background: University students can use guided digital mental health interventions without having immediate access to a coach. Fixed programme content does not always answer questions about choosing a programme or applying an exercise to a personal situation. This study examined whether purpose-specific large language model chatbots can address this gap within an existing platform, MoodLift. The study focused on how students judged their usefulness, trustworthiness, and safety.

Assistant and an AI Coach. Ten first-year students used both tools. They completed usability and acceptability measures before joining one of two focus groups. Questionnaire results were summarized descriptively. Focus-group transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.

: Mean Chatbot Usability Questionnaire scores were 70.3 for the Program Selection Assistant and 70.8 for the AI Coach. Acceptability ratings were generally positive. Five themes were developed: (1) from general self-help to personal support; (2) providing direction while preserving autonomy; (3) students trusted the chatbots conditionally, not automatically; (4) embedding made AI more relevant and legitimate; and (5) safety required a bounded, complementary role. Situational fit emerged as a cross-cutting principle: preferences varied between students and depended on the task and context. Conclusions: Purpose-specific chatbots can help students use guided digital self-help at the moment questions arise. Their value depends on reliable responses and an explicit scope. Providers should explain how data are handled and who remains responsible, while ensuring that human support is available

Introduction

Mental health problems are common among university students. In the WHO World Mental Health International College Student initiative, approximately one third of first-year students screened positive for a common 12-month mental disorder (Auerbach et al., 2018). Only a minority of affected students had received treatment (Bruffaerts et al., 2019). Treatment capacity is one part of this gap. Students also report that they prefer to manage problems themselves or are unsure where to seek help. Lack of time, stigma, and doubts about treatment create further barriers (Ebert et al., 2019). Universities can respond by offering support that is available early and can be used privately, with a route to professional care when needed.

Guided digital self-help can make such support available at scale. Internet-based interventions have shown small-to-moderate effects on student mental health outcomes (Harrer et al., 2019). Their public health value is nevertheless determined partly by use: students must start an intervention, continue with it, and apply what they learn. Reach and sustained engagement vary considerably (Taylor et al., 2024). Students have also expressed preferences concerning personalization and usability and privacy and access to a person remain important (Oti & Pitt, 2021). MoodLift was developed within the Dutch Caring Universities consortium and combines evidence-informed self-help programmes with asynchronous e-coaching (Struijs et al., in press). A coach is not continuously available, and fixed content cannot anticipate every question. Additional support may therefore be useful when students are choosing a programme or working through the programme content and exercises. It may also help them translate general advice into something they can do next.

Conversational agents offer one possible form of immediate support. Most research has concerned standalone mental health chatbots. These systems provide psychoeducation or exercises, and some are intended to offer emotional or treatment-related support. Meta-analytic findings show benefits for some mental health outcomes, with substantial differences between systems and studies (Li et al., 2023). Large language models allow a less scripted exchange versus traditional rule-based chatbots. Clinical evidence for these newer applications is still limited. A review of 160 mental health chatbot studies documented rapid growth in LLM applications and identified inaccurate responses and privacy concerns. It also found therapeutic claims that had not been adequately evaluated (Hua et al., 2025). More importantly, evidence on standalone chatbots cannot readily answer how a chatbot functions inside already established interventions.

A chatbot embedded in an intervention has a narrower task. It can help users select, understand, or apply established material between contacts with a coach. It does not have to deliver a complete intervention. The small body of relevant research has produced mixed findings. For example, adding an automated chatbot to internet-based cognitive behavioural therapy increased programme completion among workers with subthreshold depression (Yasukawa et al., 2024). In another study, natural-language feedback increased weekly participation in a self-guided intervention. The primary depression outcome did not improve however (So et al., 2026). Users of a generative-AI CBT application engaged more often and for longer than users of digital CBT workbooks; reductions in anxiety and depression were comparable (McFadyen et al., 2026). These studies indicate that conversation can change how intervention content is used, even when clinical effects do not differ. The systems, however, vary in technology and intended function. Their level of integration also differs.

Clinical outcomes provide only part of the evaluation. An embedded chatbot must be useful for its assigned task, and users must be able to decide how much confidence to place in an answer. Qualitative research among students and other stakeholders has drawn attention to evidence-based content and user autonomy (Bartali et al., 2026; Reichenpfader et al., 2025).

Participants in those studies also wanted understandable information about data and emergency procedures. They generally described chatbots as complements to human support. Work on responsible health applications adds questions about the sources used by a model, human oversight, and organizational responsibility (Olisaeloka et al., 2026; World Health Organization, 2024). A university platform could offer relevant content and recognizable human backup.

Whether students trust the platform and its provider remains an empirical question.

Few studies have examined purpose-specific LLM assistants within an existing guided intervention. We therefore studied two MoodLift prototypes. The Program Selection Assistant helped students choose a programme from the platform; the AI Coach supported them while they used programme content. Neither was intended to replace human coaching or professional care.

The research question was: How do students experience the use of AI chatbots within the MoodLift platform, and under which conditions do they consider such applications useful, safe, and trustworthy?

Methods

Study Design

An exploratory qualitative study with two focus groups was conducted. Usability and acceptability questionnaires provided descriptive information about each prototype.

Setting and MoodLift Platform

MoodLift is the guided digital mental health platform of Caring Universities. Its structured programmes address, among others, low mood and worry, as well as stress, procrastination, perfectionism, sleep, self-esteem, relationships, and substance use. Trained ecoaches support students while they work through a programme (Struijs et al., in press). The prototypes were designed to help with programme selection and questions that arose between coaching contacts. During the study, students accessed them separately alongside MoodLift; technical integration had not yet taken place.

Chatbot Prototypes

Both prototypes ran on Anthropic's Claude Sonnet 4 (claude-sonnet-4-20250514). The system prompts instructed the model to ask questions collaboratively and to respect student autonomy. They also defined professional boundaries and described how to handle uncertainty.

Crisis procedures were included. The model had not been fine-tuned on MoodLift data. At inference, it received platform information from the prompts and searchable knowledge bases.

Program Selection Assistant

The Program Selection Assistant was called the "Choice Tool" during testing. It asked about the student's main concerns and explored possible connections between problems before proposing a starting programme from 12 options. Matching rules and programme descriptions were included in the prompt. A further 256 indexed fragments from MoodLift materials were available. Students could ask for an alternative or an explanation and could correct the tool. The instructions prohibited diagnosis and treatment. They also prohibited pressure and unsupported inferences. Messages indicating acute risk led to a referral to professional or emergency support.

AI Coach

Students used the "AI Coach" while working within a self-selected programme. The Coach used retrieval-augmented generation: it retrieved relevant passages from a knowledge base containing 1,290 fragments, taken from the programme manuals. It could, for example, explain an exercise, generate examples, or help a student apply the material to their personal situation or experience. Retrieved content was given priority when it addressed the question. The instructions stated that the Coach was not a therapist and should not diagnose or give medical advice. It was also told not to make important decisions for students. Acute-risk messages activated crisis guidance and referral. Each prototype responded in the language used by the student.

Participants and Recruitment

Ten Dutch-speaking first-year psychology students took part. Five women and five men; their approximate age was 19 years. They were recruited through the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam SONA pool. Eligibility required current student status and a minimum age of 18 years. Participants also needed sufficient Dutch or English proficiency. They received course research credits and a €20 bol.com gift card. Three students attended one focus group and seven attended the other.

Procedure

Participants first provided digital informed consent and demographic information. They then received a test account and used the Choice Tool once. They were asked to enter a real or realistic situation involving several concerns. The test accounts already required a MoodLift programme to be selected. This placed the programme recommendation by the Choice Tool within a partly artificial sequence, which was taken into account during the analysis.

Participants next used a selected MoodLift programme and the AI Coach for approximately one week. Daily login was encouraged. They could ask for clarification, request examples, or discuss a difficulty they encountered in the programme. Private reflection notes were encouraged but were not collected.

Participants completed the questionnaires immediately before their focus group. The groups took place in person at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam on June 17 and 19, 2026, and lasted 89 and 81 minutes. The author moderated both sessions. They were conducted in Dutch and audio-recorded for transcription. English quotations were checked against the Dutch transcripts and then verified from the recordings.

Questionnaire Measures

Chatbot Usability Questionnaire

The 16-item Chatbot Usability Questionnaire was administered for each prototype (CUQ; Holmes et al., 2019). Items are presented in positive and negative pairs and use a five-point response scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The questionnaire covers the chatbot's purpose and navigation. Other items address understanding, response quality, error handling, and ease of use. After reversing negative items, total scores were transformed to a 0-100 scale.

Higher values indicate better usability. No validated cutoff is available, so the scores were interpreted descriptively (Pavão et al., 2026).

Additional Evaluation Items

Four additional items were rated on five-point scales. They asked about added value and intended use within MoodLift, followed by trust in the information or advice and willingness to recommend the prototype. "Agree" and "strongly agree" were classified as positive responses.

Focus Group Guide and Data Collection

The semistructured guide first asked about the overall experience and each prototype.

Participants then compared the tools with general-purpose AI. Later questions concerned trust and safety, possible use in practice, and desired improvements. The moderator used follow-up questions when students discussed relevance or personalization. Further prompts covered programme content, motivation, privacy, the provider, and possible human intervention.

Questions about data use and confidence in the advice were also included.

Data Analysis

Quantitative Analysis

We calculated the mean, standard deviation, and range of each CUQ score. Additional items were summarized as positive-response counts with percentages. The sample was not used for inferential tests or formal comparisons between prototypes.

Qualitative Analysis

A reflexive thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke (2006, 2021).

Coding was largely inductive and semantic: initial codes described participants' accounts in relatively concrete terms. Theme development was more interpretive and was informed by the research question, particularly what participants' accounts implied for the usefulness, trustworthiness, and safety of the chatbots.

The transcripts were read repeatedly and divided into data extracts, which were assigned fine-grained codes. Coding was iterative: codes were compared and refined first within and subsequently across the two focus groups. Related codes were then grouped into candidate themes. These themes were reviewed against both the coded extracts and the complete transcripts, with attention to their internal coherence, boundaries, and potentially contradictory cases. Themes were revised until each captured a distinct and meaningful pattern in relation to the research question. Differences between people, tasks, and situations occurred across the themes and were therefore conceptualized as a cross-cutting principle rather than as a separate sixth theme. Illustrative quotations were selected after reviewing the extracts associated with each theme, considering their clarity, relevance, and ability to represent variation within the data.

Researcher Reflexivity and Use of Generative AI

The moderator and analyst is a clinical psychologist and researcher. He leads work on Caring Universities and MoodLift and created the prototypes. This background made it easier to interpret references to the platform. It also meant that favourable findings could be personally and professionally welcome. Before data collection, the researcher expected relatively critical responses because students could compare the prototypes with polished general-purpose chatbots. Several steps were used to keep the analysis open to other interpretations. Codes remained linked to their source fragments, uncertainties and counterexamples were documented, and all harmonized codes were reviewed. Empirical themes were kept separate from suggestions for future design. Concerns about privacy and security were considered alongside positive accounts of MoodLift. The analysis also retained criticism of the technical integration and the limits of AI.

OpenAI's ChatGPT was used as a support tool during parts of the qualitative analysis, including code harmonisation, and the exploration of candidate thematic groupings. AI-assisted outputs were treated as suggestions. The author reviewed the analysis against the transcript data and made all final coding, thematic, and interpretative decisions.

Ethics and Data Protection

Ethical approval was obtained by the ethics committee of the Faculty of Behavioural and Movement Sciences of the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam (VCWE-2026-055R1). Participants received written study information before giving digital consent. Participation was voluntary, and withdrawal remained possible. Audio recordings were deleted after transcription; pseudonymized data were stored according to the privacy statement. Messages passed through Anthropic's Claude service and were not used to train the model. For security and quality control, they could be retained for approximately seven days. Ipportunities B.V. managed the platform.

Anthropic's Data Processing Addendum covered transfers outside the European Economic Area through the European Union Standard Contractual Clauses (Anthropic, 2025).

Qualitative Findings

Five themes were developed from the focus-group data. They concerned personal support, guidance that left room for choice, how trust was formed, the effect of embedding, and the limited role students assigned to AI. The themes together described students' views of usefulness, trustworthiness, and safety. Preferences did not apply uniformly. Situational fit was used to describe differences between students and between moments of use.

Theme 1 From General Self Help to Personal Support

Students valued the Coach when it made static programme material relevant to their immediate situation. It adapted examples and explained exercises in different words. Follow-up questions could bring structure to an experience that was still unclear. One participant compared the fixed examples in the module with those supplied by the Coach: Continuity increased the practical value of the Coach.

If it seemed to know where a student was in the programme, the conversation could continue without extensive repetition.

Without memory, students had to reconstruct earlier information and the exchange became less personal. Direct availability was another benefit. Some participants rarely needed this however, a clear module already gave them enough support.

Theme 2 Providing Direction While Preserving Autonomy

The Choice Tool was most helpful when it explored a student's concerns before offering direction. It could connect problems and ask which concern was most disruptive. This reduced complexity. Students accepted a recommendation more readily when the preceding questions made sense. A quick recommendation, by comparison, appeared overly certain. Students worried that people unfamiliar with AI might assume that all relevant programmes and problems had been considered: "Someone who does not really understand how a chatbot works might think it has genuinely weighed all the options, even though it has not discussed the other problems with you at all."

(FG1, Participant 2)

The concern became more salient when participants imagined using the Choice Tool while distressed or uncertain. An unsuitable recommendation might feel confronting or send a vulnerable student "down the wrong path" (FG2, Participant 5). They wanted the tool to explore the problem before narrowing the options. A brief explanation and the opportunity to correct the recommendation were also important.

Several participants preferred to receive two or three plausible programmes. The chatbot would still reduce complexity, but the student could compare options. One participant said: "It felt as though it had already made the decision for you" (FG2, Participant 5). A single recommendation was acceptable to others, especially when it confirmed what they already expected. Students' tolerance for direction depended on how the recommendation had been reached and whether they retained the final choice.

Theme 3 Students Trusted the Chatbot Conditionally Not Automatically

Students assessed trust response by response rather than extending it to AI in general.

They looked for competence and realism. Restraint, consistency with the programme, and an identifiable basis for the information also affected their judgement. Questions about data handling introduced another consideration. A confident tone could make a favourable first impression. Uncertainty about the source could reverse it quickly.

Some participants regarded the MoodLift Coach as more objective than general-purpose AI and less inclined to agree with the user. They contrasted its professional restraint with chatbots that reassured people too readily. An uncomfortable answer could therefore remain credible. One participant said: "It may have been a harsh truth, but it felt realistic" (FG2, Participant 5). Advice that seemed to have been constructed outside the programme contend weakened trust in the programme grounding. For example, one participant received a usefullooking breathing exercise that was absent from the programme and questioned the source of other information: The breathing exercise itself was not considered harmful. Its unclear origin was the problem. The participant could not tell whether the Coach had retrieved the exercise from MoodLift or generated it independently. This made it harder to know which other answers needed checking. Students wanted enough source information to evaluate an answer. They did not want a technical explanation to interrupt every exchange.

Disclosure also reflected students' beliefs about access to their conversations. When considering a possible future implementation, one participant said that they would prefer general-purpose ChatGPT if researchers could access conversations with the embedded chatbot (FG1, Participant 1). Others placed more weight on convenience or were less worried about a data leak. Across these views, students wanted information about the chatbot to be presented in a concise and layered format. Key information about its purpose and limitations should be visible upfront, without requiring users to work through lengthy disclaimers or legalistic text. More detailed information should remain easy to find under clear and relevant headings, including how long data were retained, who could access them, and who was responsible for the system.

Theme 4 Embedding Made AI More Relevant and Legitimate

Embedding changed how participants understood the prototypes. Because students accessed them separately (the prototypes were not yet integrated in the platform), several initially missed the connection with MoodLift. That connection became clearer through use. When the chatbot drew on the content of the MoodLift programmes, students found its responses more relevant to what they were working on and saw less need to consult a separate, general-purpose chatbot. Provision through a university also gave the chatbot credibility because the university was associated with expertise, student wellbeing, and non-commercial interests. However, university provision alone was not sufficient: students still wanted assurance that their data would be protected and that the chatbot would provide reliable responses.

Programme grounding distinguished the Coach from a generic chatbot. Participants knew that some answers could also be found through an internet search. The benefit was receiving an answer while they were already working in MoodLift. The Coach could connect a broad question to programme theory and continue with a relevant follow-up question: "If I asked anything related to self-confidence, it immediately brought in the theory from the programme.

The answer was realistic and applicable, and it asked follow-up questions to help solve it further." (FG2, Participant 5)

University or psychologically informed provision increased confidence for some participants. One student preferred MoodLift because "there is a university behind it and psychology behind it" (FG1, Participant 1). Participants expected a non-commercial provider to give wellbeing greater priority than monetization. University affiliation was not sufficient by itself. Memories of university data breaches led them back to the need for demonstrated privacy and technical performance.

Theme 5 Safety Required a Bounded Complementary Role

Students described the chatbots as immediate additions to a structured programme and its human support. They did not regard them as replacements for professional or relational care.

Scalable support was attractive when the chatbot's limits were visible. People were still expected to take responsibility for monitoring and escalation.

Participants saw a possible benefit if repeated difficulties raised in a chat became visible to an e-coach. Follow-up and responsibility would remain with the person. They also wanted to distinguish a coach's messages from text generated by AI: Participants also asked what would happen if a conversation revealed severe negative thoughts or an acute crisis. Intervention seemed acceptable in high-risk situations. It nevertheless raised questions about who could access messages and when confidentiality might be breached.

One participant accepted intervention "only when someone is genuinely tending toward suicide or harming themselves" (FG1, Participant 2). Students expected any monitoring to be proportionate and disclosed. Data access should be restricted, and people should remain responsible for the crisis procedure.

The shortened focus-group guide contained fewer direct questions about safety. The findings show that students cared about role boundaries and access to human help. They cannot establish that these were the only safety concerns.

Cross Cutting Principle Situational Fit

Preferences changed across students and situations. Some wanted a warm style with emoticons. Others accepted a robotic tone as long as the information was useful. The Coach had less value when the programme content was already clear; it became useful when a student was stuck or wanted to apply an exercise personally. Willingness to disclose information varied with the sensitivity of the topic and beliefs about privacy. Ideas about authenticity played a part as well. No single conversational style or level of support suited every person and moment.

Discussion

Principal Findings

Students responded positively to two prototype, purpose-specific chatbots-a programme selection assistant and an in-programme coach-embedded in MoodLift, a guided digital mental health platform for university students. The Coach was particularly useful when it helped students apply programme content to their own situations. Students welcomed guidance but wanted to retain control over their decisions. Trust was conditional on both the quality of individual responses and what students knew about the system, including its information sources, data practices, limitations, and provider. Students viewed the chatbots as complements to structured self-help and human support rather than as replacements for either.

Personalization and Autonomy

The AI Coach was valued mainly for translating general material into a personal example or feasible next step. Qualitative work on LLM-based mental health support has likewise found that users look for information that applies to their circumstances and for practical guidance alongside validation (Yoo et al., 2026). A longitudinal diary study linked engagement to perceived effectiveness, continuity, and preferred interaction style (Xu et al., 2025). In MoodLift, this support remained tied to existing programme content. Students did not expect the Coach to become a separate intervention or therapist.

Personalization required information about the student. Conversational memory could create continuity, yet storing this information raised privacy concerns. People with lived experience of depression have described a similar tension (Yoo et al., 2026). The amount of direction also required care. Students wanted help with reducing complexity and comparing alternatives; the final decision remained theirs. Digital shared decision making is based on the same distinction between supporting understanding and replacing a person's judgement (Vitger et al., 2021). Concerns about autonomy and dependence have also appeared in student chatbot research (Bartali et al., 2026).

A personalized answer is not necessarily an autonomy-supportive answer. Students need ways to question the reasoning, correct an assumption, or choose another option. They should also be able to stop. These opportunities to revise the exchange deserve attention alongside personal fit.

Conditional Trust and Transparency

Trust changed during the interaction. Students attended to whether an answer was relevant and realistic, and whether its basis was apparent. This fits the concept of calibrated trust: confidence should reflect actual performance, while uncertainty remains visible when human help may be needed (Lee & See, 2004). Reviews of AI in health care similarly describe trust as dependent on performance and risk, with context and user characteristics shaping the judgement (Choudhury & Shamszare, 2026). The questionnaire scores in this study reflect perception. They provide no evidence that the prototypes were accurate or safe.

Students also needed information outside the answer itself. They wanted to know where the chatbot obtained its information and what it could not do.

Data handling, responsibility, and routes to human support helped them decide how seriously to take a response. Boundary warnings may reduce overreliance. In a randomized vignette study, such warnings increased boundary awareness among college students. Participants also reported less intended overreliance and a stronger intention to seek professional help, although effects on behaviour were not tested (Zhang et al., 2026).

Transparency is useful when it changes how a person interprets an answer. Simply making technical information available is not enough.

Institutional Embedding and Governance

MoodLift content gave the Coach a relevant knowledge base. The provider also offered a positive signal. Students associated university or psychologically informed provision with expertise and accountability. They also expected fewer commercial incentives. Higher-education students have expressed similar preferences for university provision (Bartali et al., 2026). In a small feasibility study of a generative-AI student chatbot, participants reported high privacy trust. The authors cautiously linked this finding to university affiliation and a policy of not retaining conversations (Pinna et al., 2026).

Affiliation, however, cannot guarantee quality or privacy, as participants themselves noted by recalling data breaches at universities. Credible institutional embedding therefore requires clear responsibility, privacy procedures, quality assurance, and transparency about relationships with external model providers.

Safety Through a Bounded Complementary Role

Participants placed the chatbots beside structured programmes and human support.

Lived-experience research has also described LLM chatbots as sources of information or validation that remain complementary to human care (Yoo et al., 2026). The distinction should be stated clearly. Otherwise, users may attribute therapeutic abilities to a chatbot that cannot provide therapy or respond adequately to clinical needs (Khawaja & Bélisle-Pipon, 2023). A clear description of scope helps people recognize when another form of support is required.

The model is only one part of safety. A scoping review of purpose-built generative-AI mental health chatbots found more reporting of technical safeguards than of human oversight or crisis procedures. Adverse-event monitoring was also rarely described (Olisaeloka et al., 2026).

The World Health Organization calls for privacy, human oversight, transparency, and accountability in health applications of generative AI (World Health Organization, 2024).

Evaluations of embedded chatbots need to describe monitoring and escalation. They should identify who is responsible and how the role of the tool is limited.

Safety received less attention in the focus groups after the guide was shortened. Students' comments support visible limits and access to human help, but they do not show that the prototypes were safe. Acceptability research needs to be accompanied by technical tests and monitoring during use. High-risk scenarios require direct evaluation. The preferred design will not be the same for every user. Young people value the accessibility of mental health chatbots and also describe them as impersonal or repetitive (Kostenius et al., 2024). Some users want more direction or conversational depth than others (Xu et al., 2025). Future work should specify who benefits from a particular form of guidance, for which task, and at what point in the intervention.

Implications for Research and Design

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this study is that students used two working prototypes in relation to an The sample was small and came from one university pool. All participants were first-year psychology students, which limits transferability. The focus groups also differed in size. Group interaction may have strengthened shared positions or made some students less willing to disagree. The prototypes were not yet integrated technically into MoodLift, and the programmeselection task followed a partly artificial sequence.

The moderator is the principal investigator of MoodLift, created the prototypes and also conducted the primary analysis. This experience supported contextual interpretation. It could have influenced the questions asked and the subsequent analytic judgements. To make that influence visible, uncertainties and counterexamples were documented and codes remained linked to the source fragments.

Conclusion

Purpose-specific chatbots helped students use online structured self-help when they needed a personal example or an immediate next step. Students did not hand over final decisions, and their trust changed with the responses they received. They also wanted to know where information came from, what happened to their data, and who was responsible for the service.

University provision increased confidence for some participants. It did not remove doubts about privacy or quality. The chatbots were acceptable as useful additions to programme content and human support.

Research on embedded chatbots should first examine whether conversational support improves the use of intervention content. Different ways of eliciting context or adapting an explanation can be compared directly. Studies could also test how a chatbot turns advice into feasible actions. Autonomy should be assessed at the same time: users need to correct assumptions and retain control of decisions. If conversational memory is introduced, evaluations should state what is stored. Any gain in continuity must then be considered against the privacy cost. How boundaries are communicated is a second practical question. Users need an understandable account of purpose and evidence, together with the main limitations. Information about data practices and human support should be accessible without dominating the conversation. Studies can test the effects of showing sources or acknowledging uncertainty. Accepting correction and responding consistently may also matter. Outcomes should include whether users know when to rely on an answer and when to seek a person. Comparisons of commercial, university, and health-care providers could isolate the effect of reputation from actual differences in privacy or oversight.
