Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Creative engagement is associated with distorted experiences of time, yet the cognitive basis remains unknown. In particular, it is unclear how balancing broad associative exploration with task-relevant constraints during idea generation shapes prospective duration perception and subjective passage of time. Here, 31 participants generated spoken sentences from prompt word triplets under conventional, creative, or unconventional instructions while producing target intervals (10 or 20 s). Electrodermal activity and cardiac photoplethysmography were recorded throughout. After each trial, participants completed ratings assessing their subjective temporal accuracy and passage of time. Results showed that creative generation was characterized by broader within-response semantic range and reduced contextual uncertainty, reflecting a balance between conceptual divergence and structural coherence. Creativity was also associated with greater temporal overproduction and absolute proportional error, whereas unconventionality improved temporal precision without a directional bias. The coefficient of variation showed no significant condition effect, indicating no detectable differences in relative trial-to-trial variability.

Exploratory multilevel mediation revealed an indirect association between semantic range and temporal overproduction through speech duration, implicating verbal production dynamics in the relationship between semantic exploration and timing. Subjective passage of time was faster in creative and unconventional conditions compared to conventional, dissociating subjective temporal experience from prospective interval production. Physiologically, creative generation reduced phasic electrodermal activity, whereas unconventionality increased tonic skin conductance. Across trials, higher heart rate and phasic electrodermal activity were associated with shorter produced intervals. Overall, these findings reveal that temporal experience during creative thought reflects distinct contributions from embodied speech production dynamics and internal autonomic pacing.

1. Introduction

Creative generation depends on the ability to move beyond dominant ideas while maintaining relevance to the current goal (Abraham, 2018; Runco & Jaeger, 2012; Sawyer & Henriksen, 2024).

Generating a creative idea therefore requires a dynamic balance between broad associative exploration, inhibition of dominant responses, and evaluation of task-relevant constraints (Beaty et al., 2014; Bowden et al., 2005; Mednick, 1962). These processes may also interact with how people monitor and experience the passage of time, as prospective temporal processing is sensitive to concurrent attentional and executive demands (Brown & Boltz, 2002; Polti et al., 2018). Indeed, creative engagement has been associated with systematic distortions in the subjective experience of time (Mainemelis, 2001 (Mainemelis, , 2002;; Thomson & Jaque, 2023), yet the cognitive basis of these distortions remains poorly understood. Understanding why creativity alters temporal experience may, thus, provide insight into the cognitive processes engaged during creative generation. Here, we combine instructed sentence generation with prospective temporal production to investigate how the semantic demands of creativity relate to the processing and experience of time.

Creative generation involves balancing broad exploration of semantic space with the selection of contextually relevant representations (Beaty et al., 2023; Kenett, 2019; Ovando-Tellez et al., 2022). Novelty requires moving beyond dominant semantic associations to access more remote possibilities, whereas appropriateness requires evaluating and selecting responses that remain relevant to the current context. Behavioural measures such as semantic distance provide an objective index of semantic exploration by quantifying how far a response moves from dominant associations. Complementary lexical and contextual measures can further characterize the accessibility and predictability of retrieved concepts (Benedek et al., 2017; Forthmann et al., 2016; Kenett & Faust, 2019; Weinstein et al., 2022). At the same time, generating more remote responses involves overcoming highly accessible concepts and selecting weaker semantic representations (Beaty et al., 2017; Beaty & Kenett, 2023; Cassotti et al., 2016; Chrysikou, 2019; Luft et al., 2018; Zioga, Kenett, et al., 2024). Thus, creative generation involves coordinating associative exploration and controlled selection to move beyond dominant responses while preserving taskrelevant constraints.

Creative engagement can also alter how the passage of time is experienced. Experimental work on flow has shown that moderate attentional demands can produce a subjective sense that time passes more quickly (Im & Varma, 2018), while others suggest that reduced attention to the passage of time contributes to this temporal acceleration (Lemmens et al., 2025; Rutrecht et al., 2021). Similar distortions have been reported in creative and immersive activities, including musical performance, where performers describe losing awareness of time and where greater engagement in flow has been associated with altered temporal experience (Chirico et al., 2015; Droit-Volet et al., 2013; Gibbs & Schiavio, 2025; Wrigley & Emmerson, 2013; Zielke et al., 2023).

These findings suggest that altered temporal experience may emerge when attention becomes strongly engaged with an ongoing creative activity, but they do not establish the cognitive processes responsible for this experience. In particular, it remains unclear whether temporal distortions are related to specific properties of the generative process itself, such as the expansion of semantic space or the demands associated with producing a response. Furthermore, it is unclear whether any temporal effects are accompanied by autonomic changes that could provide an alternative explanation based on physiological arousal. Moreover, temporal experience encompasses distinct components: online prospective duration tracking and post-trial subjective evaluations of time passage. Whether these components are similarly affected by the demands of creative generation remains unclear.

This coordination can be experimentally probed by manipulating the instructions under which participants generate ideas. Previous research has shown that explicit instructions to "be creative" enhance the originality of generated responses (Chen et al., 2005; Harrington, 1975; Niu & Liu, 2009; Nusbaum et al., 2014; Zioga et al., 2024), leading to distinct behavioural and physiological profiles relative to conventional or unconventional goals across expressive domains (language: Heinen & Johnson, 2018; music: Zioga et al., 2026). Building on this approach, the present study uses instructed sentence generation to manipulate the demands of creative semantic processing. Participants generated sentences in response to noun triplets under three instruction conditions: conventional, creative, and unconventional. Conventionality encourages accessible and contextually-related responses. Unconventionality instead encourages participants to depart from dominant associations and produce atypical responses. Creativity combines these demands, requiring participants to move beyond typical associations while producing responses that remain meaningful and appropriate to the task. To examine how these different configurations of cognitive demands relate to temporal processing, we combined instructed idea generation with prospective temporal production. Participants generated sentences while concurrently tracking duration to produce a specified target interval (10 s or 20 s), executing a keypress when they judged the duration had elapsed. Following each trial, they completed post-trial ratings assessing their subjective temporal accuracy and subjective passage of time.

Following the aforementioned literature, we hypothesize that the instruction conditions will produce dissociable semantic and contextual profiles reflecting the given demands.

Specifically, we used two planned orthogonal contrasts to distinguish a creative-specific effect from an effect of increasing unconventionality from conventional to creative to unconventional generation. We expected semantic distance between prompts and responses to increase progressively from conventional to creative to unconventional conditions, reflecting increasing departure from the semantic context provided by the prompts. Compared to conventional and unconventional conditions, we further expected creativity to exhibit greater within-response semantic range, reflecting a broader semantic space traversed during sentence construction.

Finally, we expected the conditions to differ in contextual uncertainty, indexed by entropy and surprisal, reflecting differences in the predictability of lexical choices.

We further hypothesized that creative generation would alter both prospective and subjective temporal processing. Specifically, we expected creative generation to be associated with greater signed proportional error (i.e., temporal overproduction, where produced intervals exceed the target duration), consistent with reduced temporal pulse accumulation as executive resources are diverted away from temporal monitoring. We also expected elevated absolute proportional error, indexing diminished temporal precision. Crucially, we evaluated the coefficient of variation, predicting that proportional variance would remain preserved across conditions. Concurrently, we hypothesized that creative generation would accelerate the post-trial subjective passage of time.

Finally, we hypothesized that greater within-response semantic range would be associated with greater signed proportional error, consistent with the possibility that broader semantic exploration contributes to variation in temporal processing. We additionally examined whether creative generation, semantic trajectory dynamics, and temporal production were accompanied by modulations in autonomic activity, operationalized via heart rate and phasic electrodermal activity.

2.1 Participants

Thirty-one right-handed adults (25 female, aged 18-42 years, M ± SD = 23.26 ± 5.34) with normal hearing and reading abilities were recruited. The final number of participants involved in the present study exceeded the a priori sample size (N = 28) estimated by means of a power analysis using G*Power (Faul et al., 2009; repeated-measures ANOVA, within-factors, f = 0.25, α = 0.05, 1-β = 0.80, 3 measurements, correlation among repeated measures = 0.50). Additional participants were recruited to account for potential signal noise and data loss common in physiological recordings. Two participants were excluded from all analyses due to data recording failures (N = 29). An additional three participants were excluded specifically from the linguistic analyses due to missing audio recordings (N = 26).

Participation was voluntary with written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics and Deontology Research Board of the Panteion University of Social and Political Sciences (approval no. 80/19-12-2025) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.2 Experimental task and design

We combined instructed story generation adapted from Rastelli and colleagues (2025) with prospective temporal production in a dual-task paradigm (Figure 1A). On each trial, participants were presented with a prompt word triplet and instructed to generate and articulate a single sentence (a short story) according to one of three experimental instructions: 1. Conventional: Form a common, typical sentence using common semantic links and the first association that comes to mind; 2. Creative: Produce an original, imaginative, and evocative sentence (akin to a poetic story) connecting all three words coherently; and 3. Unconventional: Form an unorthodox sentence combining the words in an atypical manner that avoids standard semantic relationships.

Standardized examples were provided during initial instruction (e.g., blacksmith-boat-lemons; airplane-field-newspaper). Prompt words could be inflected into any grammatical form.

The experiment comprised 6 blocks of 6 trials (36 trials total), preceded by a practice block.

Each trial presented the word triplet, the condition instruction, and a designated target interval to produce (10 s or 20 s to prevent expectancy effects; counterbalanced across conditions). While generating and articulating their sentence, participants prospectively monitored time and pressed a designated response key when they judged the target duration had elapsed. 2.3 Construction of prompt word triplets

A standardized battery of 45 Greek word triplets (135 unique lemmas; 36 experimental, 9 reserve set; Supplementary Table S1) was adapted from Rastelli and colleagues (2025). Twenty-four triplets were translated and adapted from Rastelli et al., while 21 were novel triplets. Stimuli were sampled across six categories (plants, occupations, artifacts, animals, locations, vehicles), with each triplet containing at most one word per category to prevent automatic categorical priming.

Stimuli were matched across four criteria: 1) Lexical frequency: Selected from the upper quartile (> 75th percentile) of log-transformed relative word frequencies in SUBTLEX-GR (Dimitropoulou et al., 2010); 2) Concreteness: Verified in the upper quartile (> 75th percentile) of normative concreteness ratings (Brysbaert et al., 2014); 3) Word length: Constrained between 2 and 5 syllables; and 4) Semantic distance: 300-dimensional static word embeddings confirmed that pairwise cosine similarities between prompt words within each triplet (word1-word2, word1-word3, word2-word3) did not differ significantly. The 36 experimental triplets were distributed equally across the three instruction conditions (12 items per condition), with 6 practice triplets sampled at random per participant from the reserve set.

2.4 Physiological data acquisition

EDA and cardiac PPG were recorded continuously using an EmotiBit wireless sensor module (Montgomery et al., 2024) placed on the participant's non-dominant (left) hand. Cardiac pulse waveforms were recorded via a three-wavelength reflective PPG sensor at 25 Hz, while EDA was sampled at 15 Hz from the same module. Raw signals were logged locally to an onboard micro-SD card for offline artifact rejection and signal processing. 2.5 Data preprocessing and metric extraction 2.5.1 Physiological signal processing Electrodermal Activity (EDA). 2.6.3 Exploratory analyses: Predictors of temporal distortion

Physiology was analyzed across a three-part LMM battery examining continuous EDA (tonic SCL, phasic EDA) and PPG (mean HR, RMSSD) over the full trial window (prompt onset to button press). In addition to trial order, target duration (10 s vs. 20 s) was included as a covariate across all models: 1. Discussion In this study, we investigated how different modes of verbal generation relate to the processing and experience of time. Participants generated sentences under conventional, creative, and unconventional instructions while electrodermal and cardiac signals were recorded. Our findings reveal four key results: (1) Creative generation was characterized by broader semantic exploration while preserving contextual constraint; (2) Creative generation induced prospective interval overproduction, a distortion primarily mediated by the demands of prolonged speech articulation; (3) The subjective passage of time accelerated across both creative and unconventional trials, decoupling the perceived speed of time from interval overproduction (signed proportional error); and (4) Autonomic activity provided distinct signatures of generative effort and temporal pacing: tonic sympathetic effort indexed unconventional rule-breaking, while transient autonomic arousal (phasic EDA and heart rate) independently predicted prospective interval underproduction, consistent with an accelerated internal pacemaker.

Together, these findings indicate that temporal estimation during idea generation is shaped by both embodied articulatory demands and internal autonomic pacing.

Creative generation expands semantic exploration while constraining contextual uncertainty

One of our central findings was that creative generation increased the range of semantic distances within utterances and relative to prompt triads, without increasing average semantic distance.

Thus, creative responses spanned a wider range of semantic relations without becoming uniformly remote. This distinction is theoretically important: while semantic distance captures the remoteness of an idea, creativity requires original elements to remain integrated within taskappropriate structural constraints (Beaty et al., 2023; Orwig et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

Contemporary frameworks conceptualize creative cognition not as unguided divergence, but as flexible associative search coupled with effective executive selection and semantic organization (Beaty et al., 2023; Rastelli et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). Our findings support this balance: creative generation reflects broad conceptual exploration that preserves both proximal and distal semantic relations.

Computational language modeling provided a complementary information-theoretic perspective on this dissociation. Creative generation reduced contextual entropy, demonstrating that creative utterances preserved predictability and syntactic-contextual coherence despite their conceptual breadth. Conversely, unconventional generation sharply increased both entropy and surprisal, reflecting heightened contextual uncertainty and local lexical unpredictability (Slaats & Martin, 2025). Crucially, the absence of condition differences in lexical frequency confirms that these effects were not consequences of selecting rarer words. These findings, thus, demonstrate that creative generation achieves divergence through structured, low-uncertainty integration.

Balancing generative novelty with structural coherence has roots in empirical aesthetics and predictive processing accounts of cognition. In particular, in Berlyne's (1971) classic psychobiological framework and the Wundt curve, aesthetic preference follows an inverted-U function where stimuli of intermediate novelty or complexity are preferred, whereas the extremely expected is experienced as dull and the extremely unexpected as jarring. A similar principle plays also a big role in predictive processing models, in which cognitive engagement and aesthetic pleasure emerge from the dynamic tension between expectation and surprise (Cheung et al., 2019; Huron, 2006). Extending this to creative production, Zioga and colleagues (2020) demonstrated that human evaluations of musical creativity follow an inverted-U relationship with statistical predictability: musical compositions that balance expected and unexpected musical intervals are judged as more creative than musical compositions that are either highly predictable or unpredictable.

These differing cognitive strategies were directly reflected in the dynamics of speech production. Creative generation was marked by prolonged speech onset latencies and extended vocalization durations, whereas increasing unconventionality was accompanied by shorter vocalization duration. Longer initiation and execution times are consistent with the heightened cognitive demands of retrieving, evaluating, and synthesizing distant semantic concepts into a coherent story (Acar et al., 2019), reflecting the increased control demands required during ruleguided word production (Zioga, Zhou, et al., 2024).

Semantic exploration and speech production dynamics drive prospective temporal overproduction

Creative generation induced an overproduction of target intervals alongside degradation in timing precision (elevated absolute temporal error). These findings align with prospective timing accounts proposing that concurrent cognitive operations compete for limited executive resources (Balta et al., 2024; Brown, 2006; Brown & Merchant, 2007; Matthews & Meck, 2016). When attention is directed toward an effortful generative task rather than temporal monitoring, the accumulation of internal temporal units may be slowed, resulting in longer physical intervals before the subjective target criterion is reached (Im & Varma, 2018; Lemmens et al., 2025; Rutrecht et al., 2021).

Crucially, this prospective distortion was not a generic byproduct of divergent thinking: while creative generation produced pronounced overproduction and absolute proportional error, unconventional generation showed no signed proportional error and actually enhanced temporal precision. Divergence achieved through rule-breaking, therefore, permitted stable temporal monitoring, whereas creative synthesis severely compromised prospective control.

Crucially, the observed elevation in trial-level absolute error was not accompanied by detectable differences in relative temporal variability. Analysis of the coefficient of variation revealed no significant condition effect. This distinction is informative because absolute proportional error indexes departure from the physical target duration, whereas the coefficient of variation indexes trial-to-trial dispersion relative to the participant's own mean production. These findings are therefore compatible with creative generation inducing systematic overproduction rather than an increase in proportional variability. These results indicate that the reduction in target precision during creative production was driven by consistent overproduction rather than noisy or disorganized timing.

Although broader within-response semantic exploration was positively associated with temporal overproduction in isolation, this relationship entirely disappeared once speech production dynamics were considered. Speech onset latency and, most prominently, speech duration accounted for substantially more variance in temporal production and precision than semantic or contextual metrics alone. Our within-person mediation model clarified the direction of this shared variance: trials requiring broader conceptual search demanded longer vocalization durations, which carried prospective productions past the target duration. Because these dynamics were observational at the trial level, this pathway reflects a process rather than purely causal mediation: the executive overhead of retrieving and organizing distant semantic concepts manifests directly in prolonged motor articulation, which in turn stretches the produced temporal interval.

Importantly, speech execution did not fully exhaust the relationship between creativity and time: the creative contrast retained a significant direct effect on interval overproduction even after controlling for semantic distance, contextual entropy, onset latency, and speech duration. This residual effect indicates that creative synthesis entails cognitive or mnemonic processes, such as ongoing evaluative monitoring, selection among competing candidates, or post-retrieval verification, that divert executive attention from temporal tracking independently of overt speech output. Conversely, the precision-enhancing effect of unconventionality survived adjustment for speech dynamics, demonstrating that departing from dominant prompt-response associations does not compromise prospective interval tracking the way creative synthesis does.

Creative and unconventional generation dissociate time production from subjective time passage

Our findings demonstrate a clear dissociation between prospective duration production and the subjective passage of time. While creative generation produced pronounced temporal overproduction and elevated absolute proportional error, unconventional generation yielded no signed proportional error and actually sharpened precision. Nevertheless, participants reported that time passed significantly faster during both creative and unconventional trials than during conventional ones, an acceleration that was particularly prominent during creative generation. This dissociation aligns with contemporary accounts distinguishing prospective duration production from subjective passage of time judgments, which can be differentially influenced by the ongoing cognitive and affective context (Lamprou et al., 2024; Martinelli & Droit-Volet, 2022). Whereas prospective interval production depends on continuous attentional monitoring to determine when a chronometric criterion has elapsed, passage of time evaluations are post-trial, subjective appraisals shaped primarily by task engagement, absorption, and perceived cognitive effort. Our results extend this distinction to active language generation, showing that the subjective impression of time "flying" can occur independently of directional distortions in interval production.

This decoupling was further corroborated at the trial level by our continuous predictor models. While semantic exploration metrics and speech production dynamics were strong predictors of directional temporal overproduction, neither accounted for variation in subjective passage of time. The unconventional condition provides a compelling illustration of this divergence: despite exhibiting narrower internal semantic range, greater local surprisal, and significantly shorter speech production than creative trials, unconventional generation accelerated subjective passage just as robustly, without biasing interval production. This pattern indicates that subjective acceleration reflects absorption in the act of generative problem-solving rather than the specific articulatory or attentional bottlenecks that distort timing. Consistent with flow and absorption accounts (Rutrecht et al., 2021), high immersion in an effortful generative task reduces meta-awareness of time's passage, speeding its perceived transit even while the underlying interval-production mechanisms remain accurately calibrated. Prospective chronometric timing and subjective temporal experience, thus, capture functionally independent dimensions of creative cognition.

Autonomic physiology provides dissociable signatures of instruction condition and timing

Peripheral autonomic dynamics revealed distinct physiological signatures for creative versus unconventional generation. Creative generation was characterized by attenuated phasic EDA, and greater within-response mean semantic distance was similarly associated with lower phasic EDA.

Electrophysiological studies have demonstrated that accessing remote semantic concepts requires the active inhibition of obvious, habitual associations, a process supported by temporal alpha band oscillations (Luft et al., 2018; Zioga, Kenett, et al., 2024). As phasic EDA typically reflects externally directed attention and orienting responses, engaging in internally focused semantic retrieval likely attenuates these transient sympathetic responses. In contrast, increasing unconventionality was accompanied by elevated tonic SCL. Given that tonic SCL indexes sustained sympathetic tone associated with cognitive effort and preparatory mobilization, this elevation aligns with the greater executive control required to actively suppress dominant associations (Beaty et al., 2014 (Beaty et al., , 2017;; Zioga, Kenett, et al., 2024). Autonomic dynamics, thus, suggest a double dissociation: creative generation relies on internally directed associative expansion, whereas unconventionality demands sustained executive inhibition.

Furthermore, autonomic arousal served as a direct pacemaker for prospective interval production. Both elevated phasic EDA and higher heart rate were associated with shorter produced intervals. This pattern is consistent with pacemaker-accumulator models of timing (Arslanova et al., 2023; Cellini et al., 2015; Fung et al., 2017; Sadeghi et al., 2023; Volodina et al., 2025).

According to these frameworks, heightened sympathetic tone and elevated cardiac chronotropy accelerate the emission rate of an internal pacemaker. Because temporal pulses accumulate more rapidly during states of higher autonomic arousal, the subjective criterion representing the target interval is reached in less physical time, resulting in compressed interval productions. These findings converge with physiological timing paradigms demonstrating that elevated sympathetic tone and physical exertion consistently produce relative interval underproduction (Davis et al., 2026; Fung et al., 2017; Lamprou et al., 2025; Volodina et al., 2025).

Critically, neither phasic EDA nor heart rate predicted the subjective passage of time, reinforcing the dissociation of prospective production and subjective temporal experience.

Whereas prospective production is an online chronometric task directly modulated by visceral arousal and pulse accumulation, subjective passage of time evaluations are higher-order appraisals shaped primarily by task absorption, valence, and cognitive engagement (Micillo et al., 2026; Ogden et al., 2022). Our findings align with ambulatory and laboratory evidence showing that autonomic fluctuations exert negligible influence on subjective passage of time judgments in the absence of explicit affective threat cues (Micillo et al., 2026; Ogden et al., 2019 Ogden et al., , 2022;; Piovesan et al., 2019). Autonomic arousal, thus, acts specifically as a somatic pacemaker for metric interval tracking, leaving the phenomenological feeling of time's passage unaffected.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that creative generation leads to prospective temporal overproduction and impaired temporal precision, while accelerating the subjective passage of time. Importantly, creative generation elicits a distinct computational and physiological profile, characterized by high within-response semantic range alongside low contextual uncertainty, reflecting a balance between conceptual divergence and structural coherence, as well as reduced phasic electrodermal activity.

The results further demonstrate that creative overproduction is mediated by prolonged speech duration, indicating that semantic search shapes prospective timing through the embodied demands of speech production, while, in line with internal pacemaker accounts, elevated heart rate and phasic EDA predict shorter produced intervals. Together, these findings demonstrate that temporal experience during creative thought is governed by a dynamic interplay between embodied speech production demands and autonomic state.
