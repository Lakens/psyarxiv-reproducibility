Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Background. Acquired brain injury (ABI) is a leading cause of long-term disability in adults. In Canada, while advances in acute care and inpatient rehabilitation have improved survival, many people living with ABI experience persistent challenges following discharge. This can include reduced self-efficacy, mental health difficulties, and decreased participation in daily life. Community-based supports remain fragmented and difficult to access. Peer support, or mentorship provided by people with lived experience of ABI, has shown promise in improving outcomes in neurological and chronic conditions. However, peer support interventions for ABI are rarely described in sufficient detail to enable replication or implementation. Objective. To describe the development, theoretical foundations, core components, and delivery of Peers Actively Involved in Rehabilitation (PAIR), a structured, community-based peer support intervention for adults living with ABI. Methods. This paper provides a detailed description of PAIR using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR), informed by principles from the Guidance for Reporting Intervention Development studies (GUIDED). Data sources include co-creation with people with lived experience and community partners, feasibility research, intervention manuals, and operational procedures. Discussion. PAIR integrates lived experience knowledge into rehabilitation through structured peer support worker training, individualized matching, and flexible delivery embedded within community organizations. By transparently documenting development and delivery, this paper supports replication, adaptation, and future evaluation, and contributes to strengthening person-centred, community-based rehabilitation for adults living with ABI.

Introduction

Acquired brain injury (ABI), including traumatic and non-traumatic etiologies such as traumatic brain injury, stroke, anoxic injury, and infection, is a leading cause of long-term disability among adults worldwide [1]. Advances in acute medical management and inpatient rehabilitation have improved survival and early recovery.

However, many people living with ABI experience persistent physical, cognitive, emotional, and psychosocial challenges that extend well beyond discharge from formal rehabilitation services [2]. Given these long-term consequences, ABI has been conceptualized as a chronic health condition requiring ongoing, community-based support rather than time-limited impairment-focused care [3].

In many countries, health services for ABI remains disproportionately concentrated in acute and specialized inpatient settings, with limited continuity of rehabilitation support following discharge. Research indicates substantial gaps in care once people with ABI return to the community [4, 5]. People with ABI describe inadequate access to psychosocial supports, difficulties navigating health and social services, and limited opportunities for individualized rehabilitation aligned with personal goals and priorities [6]. These gaps in supports are associated with reduced self-efficacy, poorer mental health, decreased participation, and lower quality of life for people living with ABI [4, 5].

Peer support has emerged as a promising approach to address these persistent gaps in care [7]. Peer support involves mentorship provided by people with lived experience of a condition to others who are earlier in their journey with the condition [8, 9]. In neurological and chronic conditions (e.g., spinal cord injury, stroke), peer support has been associated with improvements in self-efficacy, emotional wellbeing, coping, and quality of life, and may complement clinician-led rehabilitation by addressing experiential and relational dimensions of recovery [9, 10]. For adults living with ABI specifically, qualitative and mixed-methods studies suggest that peer support facilitates normalization of experience, reduces social isolation, supports adjustment to life after injury [8]. Emerging evidence also suggests potential indirect benefits for family members and caregivers through improvements in self-efficacy and access of support beyond the family [8].

There is substantial heterogeneity in reporting peer support interventions for ABI, with limited description of core components, peer support worker training, fidelity, and theoretical foundations [9]. As such, peer support interventions are difficult to replicate, implement, and scale-up. Additionally, given the diversity of outcomes included, it is difficult to compare and synthesize outcomes across studies [9, 11]. Standardization of reporting, not necessarily of intervention content, has been identified as a key priority for advancing peer support as an evidence-informed component of ABI rehabilitation [8] [9] [10].

There has been increasing recognition that effective rehabilitation interventions, particularly those addressing long-term disability, must be grounded in person-centred and patient-partnered approaches [12, 13]. Co-creation and co-design are processes that meaningfully engage people with lived experience as partners across intervention development, implementation, and evaluation; they are associated with improved relevance, acceptability, and sustainability of rehabilitation programs. Involving people with lived experience of ABI is particularly important given the heterogeneity of rehabilitation trajectories and life contexts, and the historically limited involvement of this population in shaping the services intended to support them [14].

To address the discrepancy between the long-term challenges experienced after ABI, the time-limited nature of traditional rehabilitation services, as well as the limited availability of structured peer support opportunities in the community, we co-developed Peers Actively Involved in Rehabilitation (PAIR), with community partners and people with lived experience of brain injury [10, 15, 16]. The feasibility of PAIR has been previously described in a case-series pre-post study [10], indicating good recruitment rate, excellent retention, full adherence to protocol, high implementation fidelity, but limited pre-to post-intervention change on psychosocial-related outcomes (e.g., selfefficacy, resiliency).

The working alliance between the peer support worker and person receiving peer support was also reported [15], indicating that the alliance was characterised by a strong sense of mutual understanding and validation ('feeling understood'), conversations that extended beyond the injury to broader life experiences ('expanding conversations beyond injury'), and a dynamic of reciprocal growth.

The purpose of this paper is to describe PAIR in detail, including its development, theoretical foundations, core components, and delivery, using established reporting guidelines. By describing PAIR, we aim to support replication, adaptation, and implementation of peer support as a meaningful component of community-based ABI rehabilitation.

Methods

This paper describes PAIR using the Template for Intervention Description and

Replication (TIDieR); Table 1 describes the aspects of PAIR intervention according to the TIDieR checklist. The reporting is informed by the Guidance for Reporting Intervention Development studies (GUIDED) to enhance transparency regarding how and why PAIR was developed, refined, and operationalized.

Development of the Intervention

PAIR builds on previous research and community-based peer support initiatives. Our community partner, Nanaimo Brain Injury Society, established a hospital-based brain injury peer support program in 2015, which was informed by approaches in amputee care, spinal cord injury, and stroke recovery. This longstanding program offered the foundation for a research-community partnership to adapt, evaluate, and extend the intervention. The development of PAIR was grounded in a patient-partnered, community-engaged approach that positioned people with lived experience of ABI and community members as experts and co-creators [17]. The intervention was developed iteratively through collaboration among people living with ABI, peer support workers (people who have provided peer support), leaders from community brain injury organizations, clinicians in ABI rehabilitation, and researchers.

Early development activities were informed by qualitative research exploring experiences of living with ABI [18], perceived gaps in post-discharge services [19], and reported need for peer support [6]. These findings highlighted a strong desire for peer-based support that was relational, individualized, and grounded in lived experience, particularly during the subacute and early community integration phases [5, 8]. We (JS and JG) observed the work of community partners and people with lived experience who conducted integrated peer support programs in their local community for 8 years (Nanaimo Brain Injury Society). During three onsite sessions and four virtual meetings, researchers and community partners had structured discussions about program operations, sustainability processes, and how to strengthen the researchcommunity partnership. These discussions emphasized the need for a peer support model that could be expanded and integrated into other communities; meeting outcomes highlighted the need for formal training and clear operational structure, with flexibility to respond to individual needs.

Building on this foundation, PAIR was co-developed with community brain injury organizations responsible for peer support delivery (BC Brain Injury Association and Nanaimo Brain Injury Society). These partnerships were critical in shaping the structure, training, oversight and facilitation processes, and delivery modalities of the intervention.

We co-developed and tested intervention materials, including peer support worker training content and operational procedures. We refined these materials based on multiple rounds of feedback. For example, JG met with two peer support workers from the community partner (Nanaimo Brain Injury Society) who provided recommendations on providing training within an online platform and offered input on the relevant health outcomes from receiving peer support. To further refine materials, a person who previously received peer support indicated important program features to help facilitate connection with the peer support worker, including flexibility in delivery and alignment with goals and life stage. Last, we (JG and JS) met with two community partners who were currently facilitating peer support intervention. The community partners provided practical guidance on peer support program delivery, including oversight needs for peer support workers and strategies to ensure sustainability and communication.

Findings from our early feasibility research informed the development of recruitment and retention processes, acceptability, and perceived impact [10, 15]. Further feedback of the feasibility research was obtained from peer support workers, participants, and community partners, which led to refinements in peer support worker training content, clarification of roles, adjustments in session frequency and duration, and development of a comprehensive standard operating procedure to facilitate consistent delivery. For example, session frequency and duration was adjusted from eight 30-minute sessions to twelve 60-minute sessions to allow more time for relationship building, deeper conversations, and sharing of lived experiences. Peer support worker training was expanded to include additional content on communication strategies, boundary setting, responding to emotional or complex situations, and sharing lived experience safely and effectively. Training materials were adapted (e.g., presentation slides, written material) to be aphasia friendly with the assistance of a speech language pathologist and a person with aphasia.

Role descriptions were clarified to better distinguish the responsibilities of peer support workers, coordinators, and clinical partners, including guidance around communication and oversight. Aligned with our earlier findings on pairing participants with peer support workers, recruitment and matching processes were refined by incorporating participant goals, shared lived experience characteristics, and logistical considerations (e.g., availability) [15]. Finally, a detailed standard operating procedure was developed to support program fidelity while allowing continued changes, such as safety plans and communication protocols.

Description of Intervention

Theoretical Rationale

PAIR is grounded in social learning theory, which posits that people learn new behaviours, coping strategies, and perspectives through observing and interacting with others [20]. Peer support workers serve as credible role models, demonstrating strategies for navigating life with ABI. They acknowledge the ongoing challenges individuals face, while also offering a sense of hope.

The intervention also draws on theories of resiliency, which is understood as a dynamic process involving cognitive, emotional, and behavioral capacities that support adaptation in the face of adversity [21]. For people living with ABI, resiliency has been associated with improved mental health, participation, and quality of life [22]. PAIR is designed to promote resiliency peer support that models adaptive coping strategies, validates lived experiences, and fosters hope for life after ABI.

Principles of person-centred care have further informed PAIR [23] [24] [25]. These principles emphasize expertise of lived experience and collaborative relationships. Within PAIR, this is operationalized through the inclusion of peer support workers with brain injury and partnerships with leaders from community brain injury programs, whose experiential knowledge offers a credible form of expertise complementing clinical perspectives.

Evidence suggests that incorporating programs including shared lived experience can foster trust, engagement, and hope. Rather than focusing on remediating an impairment, PAIR prioritizes participant-identified goals and values. This focus is important because person-centred goal-setting has been associated with improved engagement and more meaningful rehabilitation outcomes by aligning interventions with content that matters to the individual. In contrast, a sole emphasis on impairment remediation may not translate into improved participation or quality of life, particularly in ABI where long-term adaptation and identity reconstruction are central [26]. As such, PAIR focuses on goals of the individual with peer-delivered support.

Core Components and Intervention Materials

Key materials supporting PAIR were co-created and tailored for various partners involved in program delivery. Descriptions are as follows.

For peer support workers, we co-developed tools with community partners and people with lived experience to promote intervention fidelity. Fidelity indicators were aligned with PAIR's theoretical framework, as described above, such as offering hope and sharing lived experience (see Appendix A). We also co-created standardized peer support worker training curriculum, co-developed with community partners and aligned with Peer Support Canada core competencies [27] (see Appendix B for description of peer support session content). We created infographics to assist peer support workers to initiate conversations, comply with safety protocols, and understand their role (see Appendix C for safety plan).

For research staff, we created a comprehensive standard operating procedure. This document guides the intervention processes such as eligibility screening and recruitment, participant matching processes, risk management considerations (e.g., safety plan), and communication processes (e.g., email templates). The document also outlines research practices such as outcome assessment, data collection and management, fidelity monitoring, and adherence to ethical requirements.

For clinical partners involved in screening of potential participants, we created eligibility checklists for ease of screening participants in clinical settings and infographics to share with potential participants, if needed. We also created a secure online data sharing system for partners to share the contact information of potential participants.

For potential participants, we developed infographics to explain the peer support intervention and established a visual consent process to describe the research study (see Appendix D). The infographic included a one-page document with a visual description of the study. A structured, written script for researchers was developed to use during consent. We created the process of consenting participants using visual aids, as opposed to the traditional written consent documentations, based on participant feedback gathered during our co-creation process. Our visual documentation included relevant icons, simplified text, and step-by-step diagrams. Participants indicated that they preferred visual materials because dense, text-heavy documents were often overwhelming and difficult to process in the context of cognitive challenges (e.g., reduced attention, memory difficulties, and slowed information processing).

To further support accessibility for participants, we created accompanying visual slides to administer outcome measures. These visual slides displayed each question and response options with accompanying visual icons and key information bolded. These slides were designed to reduce cognitive load by presenting one question at a time, using clear language, consistent formatting, and visual cues. In response to additional co-creation feedback, an adapted version of the slides was developed for participants with speech and language challenges (e.g., aphasia), incorporating strategies such as simplified wording, pictorial scales, and opportunities for supported communication.

Overall, these adaptations reflect the importance of input of those with lived experience and our commitment to reducing barriers to participation and ensuring that study procedures are responsive to the diverse cognitive and communication needs of people with ABI.

Intervention Procedures

PAIR consists of one-to-one peer support sessions between an adult with recent experience of ABI and a trained peer support worker with long-term lived experience of ABI. Sessions are relational and tailored to the needs and challenges of the participants. Common areas of discussion include adjustment to injury, coping strategies, emotional wellbeing, self-management, navigating health services, participation in meaningful activities, and rebuilding identity following injury [15].

A key component of the PAIR intervention is the process of matching participants with peer support workers, which is undertaken collaboratively by researchers (JG), PAIR Research Coordinator, and PAIR Community Facilitator, who provides support and encouragement to peer support workers. Matching is guided by principles derived from co-design and feasibility work, with the aim of fostering connection and engagement between individuals [10]. Qualitative findings from our feasibility work examined working alliance between the participant and peer support worker within PAIR. Findings emphasized that the success of peer support relationships is not solely determined by connecting people with similar clinical and demographic characteristics, but also ensuring participants feel understood, experience mutual openness, and engage in conversations that extend beyond the injury itself. As such, the matching process considers participant's life stage and circumstances (e.g., living alone, caring for children), demographics (e.g., age, sex, gender), goals for rehabilitation (e.g., work, social), and challenges after ABI (e.g., physical, cognitive). Additionally, similar personal interests and relevant social or cultural factors are considered, as these can help to create rapport and connection on experiences unrelated to ABI. The PAIR Research Coordinator and PAIR Community Facilitator also support rematching, if there is not a mutual connection after two peer support sessions; this is based on private discussions with participant and peer support worker.

Content of the peer support sessions are based on the co-created fidelity indicators, as mentioned above. The peer support sessions follow a semi-structured approach, instead of a protocolized format. A semi-structured format allows peer support workers to be responsive to participant needs in the moment, but have a guideline to follow during sessions. There are 11 key aspects that are recommended in every peer support session; these relate to fostering hope, promoting self-compassion, and sharing lived experience. There are five aspects that are not required in every session, but are recommended to be addressed at least once during the intervention; these broadly relate to discussions of the rehabilitation journey, health outcomes, and navigating health services. Rather than prescribing session-by-session content, fidelity within PAIR adheres to core relational and ethical principles of peer support. This approach reflects emerging consensus that effective peer support requires standardization of processes and values, rather than rigid curricular content, particularly in heterogeneous populations such as ABI [9]. Overall, peer support workers are encouraged to draw on their lived experience in a reflective and intentional manner to emphasize mutual understanding.

Peer support worker Role

Peer support workers are adults with lived experience of ABI who are more than two years post-injury. Selection criteria emphasize readiness to support others, communication skills (can include peer support workers with aphasia), and ability to engage reflectively with lived experience. Peer support workers were referred by local community organisations. Peer support workers complete structured training by a research team member (JG) and receive ongoing oversight and support from the PAIR Community Facilitator. Peer support worker training comprises of three sessions, approximately two-hours for each session. The training was designed to prepare peer support workers with ABI to deliver the PAIR intervention within a structured and accessible approach. Content includes an introduction to peer support with a focus on the value of lived experience as expertise and the role of the peer support worker in fostering hope, connection, and empowerment. Peer support workers are oriented to the structure and key components of PAIR, such as tailoring conversations and sharing experiences. Training also includes instruction on communication skills such as active listening, validation, shared storytelling, and managing conversational balance.

Additionally, strategies for adapting communication to accommodate cognitive and communication challenges are discussed (e.g., managing slowed processing and aphasia). Peer support workers also receive instruction on role definition, maintaining confidentiality, and navigating the development of a connection while maintaining boundaries. Content of training is delivered through multiple modes such as role-playing scenarios, reviewing case examples, and group discussion. There are discussions through training to ensure safety for the peer support worker and participant. For example, when to seek additional support or how to address an uncomfortable situation (e.g., how to create conversations). Peer support workers are taught procedures for documenting sessions and maintaining fidelity to the intervention, while still allowing flexibility to respond to individual participant needs. See Appendices A and B for the fidelity checklist and description of session content.

Participants Receiving Peer Support

Participants receiving peer support within the PAIR intervention are adults living with ABI who are within one year post-injury and are transitioning from hospital-based rehabilitation to community living. This timeframe was selected due to the increased need for psychosocial support and life re-construction. Participants need to have the cognitive and behavioural ability to engage in peer support (with or without communication aids), the ability to participate in a conversation even for short periods, express preferences or goals (verbally, nonverbally, or with support), and regulate behaviour to engage respectfully and safely in one-on-one interactions. For instance, participants can experience memory challenges, but would be appropriate for PAIR if they are able to retain key information with repetition or external supports. Additionally, individuals with communication impairments such as aphasia can engage in PAIR if they can use adapted strategies (e.g., visual supports, communication devices, communication partner).

Participants are excluded if they were involved in other structured peer support programs. Additional screening considerations, such as behavioural stability and readiness to engage in peer support, were applied pragmatically through clinical assessment by the collaborators to ensure participant safety and appropriateness for the intervention. As such, PAIR is designed to accommodate heterogeneity in ABI experiences, with flexibility in pacing, content, and delivery to support meaningful engagement across a range of functional abilities and life contexts.

Administration Team

The PAIR Research Coordinator is part of the research team and has a central role in the day-to-day management of the intervention. They have training in accessible communication with people with disability, administration, research tools, and project management. The PAIR Research Coordinator is responsible for scheduling peer support sessions, data management, and ensuring that study procedures are delivered consistently and according to protocol. The PAIR Research Coordinator manages the informed consent process and monitors participant engagement and retention throughout the study. They are responsible for overseeing intervention fidelity, including coordinating session recordings and maintaining documentation. This role serves as a primary point of contact for troubleshooting logistical or participant-related concerns.

The Research Coordinator oversees research assistants who may assist with PAIR for duties such as recruitment, consent, data collection, and fidelity monitoring.

The PAIR Community Facilitator has a central role in providing oversight and encouragement for the peer support workers. The person selected for this role has extensive experience working within community-based brain injury services and with people with disabilities; they also have lived experience as a caregiver of a child with an ABI. In past roles, they developed expertise in facilitating peer connections through the community brain injury peer support program (Nanaimo Brain Injury Society). The role of the Community Facilitator is to oversee the peer support workers in their role, such as meeting regularly with peer support workers (e.g., at least three times during the twelveweek session) to provide practical guidance and emotional support to facilitate confidence in the peer support worker. The Community Facilitator debriefs experiences, addresses challenges, and reinforces key principles of PAIR. In addition, the Community Facilitator supports the research team to ensure that the intervention remains grounded in the realities of living with ABI through adapting communication and study protocols.

Multiple research trainees are involved in this project and are responsible for components of PAIR with corresponding research studies supervised by the Principal Investigator. For example, one trainee oversees the implementation study including participant recruitment, data collection, and fidelity of intervention. Another trainee is responsible for data collection on the peer support workers. Yet another trainee oversees the involvement of the caregiver participants within PAIR as well as supporting the implementation of PAIR. During the initial design and feasibility testing of PAIR, trainees were also involved. For example, JG led the initial co-creation and feasibility of PAIR, JG and JW provided insight into the health outcomes measures used in the implementation study, and JG helped to develop accessible materials to support participation in PAIR. During the delivery of PAIR, trainees are responsible for data collection, intervention implementation, and data management and analysis. They also lead research team meetings and peer support worker meetings to foster connection, relationship, and best-practices within the implementation process. Throughout their involvement, trainees gain experience in person-centred and co-created research approaches, while contributing to the rigorous and ethical conduct of the study.

Mode of delivery

PAIR is delivered through synchronous, individual interactions. Sessions were planned to occur virtually through secured videoconference, but could be conducted in alternate modalities (e.g., telephone, in-person) depending on participant preference, accessibility, and contextual constraints. Flexibility in delivery mode is a core feature to support inclusion and access.

Location

PAIR is embedded within community settings. Recruitment is initiated as participants are preparing for discharge from hospital settings, or shortly after returning home. This design supports continuity of health services beyond the health institutes. We first initiated PAIR within the rehabilitation hospital associated with our university and the hospital that we initially partnered with (Nanaimo Regional General Hospital). We then expanded to six hospitals which are across four out of five provincial health authorities.

Dose and Duration

PAIR is delivered over 12 weeks, with one session per week. However, depending on participant needs, schedules, and goals, the dose and duration can be adapted at any point during the intervention. Session duration is up to 60 minutes, and also tailored to participant needs (e.g., can be reduced in length if participants are fatigued or have reduced attention). Typically, when a match between a participant and peer support worker is made, they meet at a consistent day and time each week for 12 weeks. The dose and duration of PAIR was determined through observation of existing peer support programs (e.g., Nanaimo Brain Injury Society), previous research [9 -11], and discussion with people with lived experience and community partners of existing programs.

Tailoring and Adaptations

As described above in Intervention Procedures, PAIR adheres to core principles of peer support and aligns to Peer Support Canada Competencies. PAIR is intentionally individualized and continually adapted based on participant needs and goals. On an individual level, peer support workers respond and adapt conversations based on participant issues in the moment. While the core relational and theoretical elements remain consistent, the intervention is designed to accommodate heterogeneity in postinjury challenges and personal context, as is often seen in this population [28, 29].

Fidelity and Monitoring

We conceptualize fidelity in PAIR as adherence to core principles of peer support, rather than particular session content delivered in uniform patterns. PAIR supports fidelity through standardized training, use of a procedure manual, reflective documentation, and ongoing mentorship and discussion with peer support workers. A fidelity checklist is completed by peer support workers after each session, encouraging them to reflect on their session and the content of the conversation. The fidelity checklist also includes an open-ended text box for the peer support worker to indicate issues within the session or notes to be followed up. To assess intervention fidelity, 20% of all sessions are audio-recorded, as per previous research [30, 31]. A member of the research team with no previous connection or relationship to participants or peer support workers independently reviews these recordings and evaluates adherence to the intervention using the standardized fidelity checklist.

In terms of monitoring, the PAIR Community Facilitator meets with peer support workers to understand their lived experience of ABI, their capacity for peer support, personal interests and leisure activities, and relevant cultural or social factors. This conversation occurs after receiving training and prior to conducting the intervention, helping to inform the matching processes. Once matched, the PAIR Community Facilitator meets with peer support workers at three occasions during the intervention: after the first session, after the sixth session, and after the tenth session. These meetings aim to provide support, direction, and encouragement to the peer support worker, discuss steps to manage the completion of the sessions (for the last meeting) and build sustainability for future peer support engagements.

The research team also meets regularly with implementation partners to support intervention delivery, monitor fidelity, and address challenges. These meetings include regular meetings with hospital collaborators approximately every six weeks to discuss recruitment, referrals, and participant flow; monthly meetings with peer support workers to provide ongoing support, facilitate shared learning, and identify training needs; and quarterly meetings (four per year) with community partners to review implementation experiences, discuss opportunities for program improvement, and support sustainability and integration within local service systems.

Discussion

This paper describes Peers Actively Involved in Rehabilitation (PAIR), a peer support program for people with ABI; it outlines the development, theoretical foundations, and delivery. Given the growing calls for improved reporting of complex rehabilitation interventions [32], this paper contributes practical guidance for replication and implementation.

Detailed documentation of intervention development and implementation is essential, particularly in light of calls for greater standardization and reporting of programs to facilitate replication and broader adoption [9, 11]. This paper provides a comprehensive intervention description aligned with TIDieR and informed by GUIDED principles. As such, this paper supports replication, adaptation, and broader implementation of PAIR across diverse service contexts. It also contributes to the growing evidence base on the development of peer support interventions by increasing transparency around the theoretical, participatory, and practical processes that shaped the intervention and its delivery. Notably, previous reviews of peer support interventions for people with ABI have identified a small and heterogeneous evidence base, with considerable variation in intervention characteristics and limited standardization across programs, making it difficult to compare, replicate, and scale interventions across settings [33]. Detailed reporting of intervention content, delivery procedures, training, and implementation processes may help address these limitations and facilitate the translation of effective peer support programs into routine practice.

PAIR is aligned with previous published evidence on peer support interventions for people living with chronic neurological conditions [9,11]. As key tenants, PAIR, along with other peer support interventions, highlights the importance of lived experience as a guiding principle and focus on psychosocial outcomes rather than impairment remediation [9]. PAIR builds on previously reported interventions by integrating structured training, tailored procedures, and scaling to multiple sites. As such, PAIR addresses concerns from previous systematic reviews and qualitative studies [7] [8] [9] 11] by preparing the peer support worker and maintaining high quality assurance of the program.

There has been a growing shift in rehabilitation paradigms from traditional clinician-driven models toward more relational, co-created practices [34, 35]. Historically, rehabilitation has been grounded in biomedical and expert-led frameworks in which clinicians assess deficits, set goals, and direct intervention [34]. This often positions patients as passive recipients of care. While this model has contributed to important advances in clinical outcomes, it has been increasingly critiqued for its limited attention to the lived experience of individuals, the social context of disability, and the importance of autonomy, identity, and meaning in recovery [34, 36]. In response, contemporary approaches consider social participation, personal meaning, lived experience, and community inclusion in the design and delivery of rehabilitation programs. There is an emphasis on collaborative partnerships, lived experience, and person-defined goals [35, 37].

Peer support aligns with contemporary approaches by positioning peer support workers, who are people living with ABI, as experts by experience [8, 9]. Peer support workers offer unique forms of understanding, validation, and role modeling that are often inaccessible within traditional clinician-led approaches. Through the reciprocal exchange experience, peer relationships can help normalize daily challenges, reduce feelings of stigma, and improve self-efficacy of those receiving support [11, 15]. This relational dynamic may be particularly important in the context of ABI, where identity reconstruction and adaptation to long-term changes are central components of recovery [26].

The growing recognition of lived experience as expertise has also contributed to increased interest in co-creation approaches within rehabilitation research and practice [38, 39]. Incorporating multiple perspectives and experiential knowledge can enhance the relevance, acceptability, and feasibility of interventions; importantly, they ensure that interventions address priorities identified by the communities they are intended to serve [35, 40]. Editorials and empirical studies in rehabilitation increasingly emphasize that co-creation can reduce research waste, improve alignment with real-world needs, and facilitate successful implementation in community settings. In PAIR, co-creation with people with lived experience and community partners was embedded at all stages of the program, including initial concept development, feasibility, refinement of training, and development of operational procedures, supporting iterative learning and adaptation [10, 15].
