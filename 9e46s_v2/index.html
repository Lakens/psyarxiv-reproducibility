What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Effective help-seeking is essential for young children's adaptation in early childhood education settings, yet little is known about how they selectively seek help based on different social cues. This study examined how young children integrate teachers' familiarity and response valence when predicting a protagonist's help-seeking behavior. Across two studies, 90 Han Chinese children aged 4-6 years (45 boys) completed a third-party task simulating teacher-child interactions. In Study 1, familiarity was manipulated using photographs of each child's real teachers, whereas Study 2 employed standardized cartoon figures and verbal cues to indicate familiarity. The results showed that children tended to expect that the protagonist would seek help from familiar teachers, especially those who responded positively. Meanwhile, they hesitated to expect that the protagonist would seek help from unfamiliar teachers, regardless of their response valence. This pattern indicates that familiarity consistently exerted a stronger influence than response valence, as children consistently prioritized familiarity when anticipating support. Together, these findings highlight how young children flexibly integrate multiple social cues when forming help-seeking expectations and offer practical implications for fostering secure, responsive teacher-child relationships in early education.

young children often hesitate to ask for assistance, especially when caregivers are unfamiliar and caregivers' responses are inconsistent (Ahnert et al., 2006; Ritchie & Howes, 2003). Such hesitation can hinder both learning and social-emotional development, underscoring the need to understand the emphasizing the social-cognitive processes that guide children's help-seeking in early education contexts.

Due to their limited problem-solving abilities, young children mainly rely on supportive adults to navigate everyday challenges (Benenson & Koulnazarian, 2008; Bowlby, 1969 Bowlby, /1982;; Rose & Asher, 2004). Recognizing when and from whom to seek help is a key developmental milestone (Nelson-Le Gall, 1985). As children grow, their reliance gradually extends beyond primary caregivers to include teachers, peers, and other significant adults (Henrich & Broesch, 2011; Newman, 2000). In ECE settings, preschool teachers often serve as important secondary caregivers, providing both emotional and instrumental support (Hamre, 2014). Examining how young children form expectations about teachers' availability and responsiveness can shed light on how early attachment-based representations are applied within institutional caregiving contexts.

Importantly, children do not seek help indiscriminately but rather selectively (Bridgers et al., 2023; Corriveau & Harris, 2009). Previous research on help-seeking has mainly focused on school-aged children's academic situations (Albrecht & Karabenick, 2018; Karabenick & Newman, 2009) or on preschoolers' informational requests for knowledge (Jaswal & Neely, 2006; Koenig, 2012; Mills, 2013). However, in ECE settings, children primarily encounter social-emotional and everyday challenges. Little is known about how young children form expectations of teachers' support based on multiple social cues. To fill this gap, the present study investigated how young children integrate teachers' familiarity and response valence when forming help-seeking expectations. The findings may extend attachment and selective trust frameworks to ECE settings, providing insight into how relationship-based reasoning supports children's adaptation in early education.

Young Children's Help-Seeking in Teacher-Child Interactions

Children seeking help from teachers is common in classroom environments (Karabenick & Newman, 2009; Newman, 2000). For school-age children, help-seeking has traditionally been conceptualized as a self-regulated learning strategy within academic contexts (Karabenick & Gonida, 2018; Karabenick & Newman, 2006; Nelson-Le Gall, 1985). In contrast, in ECE settings, help-seeking functions less as a learning strategy and more as a coping strategy for obtaining emotional reassurance and practical assistance (Rickwood & Thomas, 2012). Although coping-oriented help-seeking has been widely discussed in mental health research (Schomerus et al., 2013; Shea & Yeh, 2008), relatively few studies have examined how young children seek or anticipate help in daily educational contexts.

Within ECE settings, teachers serve as primary sources of both emotional and instrumental support. Consistent with this role, the Teaching Through Interactions framework identifies emotional support, classroom organization, and instructional support as core dimensions of teacher-child interactions that promote development (Hamre, 2014; Hamre & Pianta, 2007). Among these, emotional and instructional support align most closely with children's help-seeking behaviors, which aim to obtain comfort and guidance. Through repeated help-seeking experiences, children not only resolve immediate difficulties but also internalize patterns of responsiveness, gradually forming expectations that guide later interactions with supportive adults (Dykas & Cassidy, 2011; Thompson, 2021).

These expectations are theorized to contribute to children's developing internal working models (i.e., mental representations of others' availability and responsiveness, Bowlby, 1969 Bowlby, /1982;; Dykas & Cassidy, 2011; Waters & Waters, 2006). Such models help children anticipate whether adults will respond helpfully when they are in need.

Previous studies have shown that caregivers' responsiveness shapes young children's expectations about support (Jia, Shen, Fang et al., 2024; Jia, Zhou et al., 2023). However, it remains unclear how these expectations are constructed within teacher-child interactions, where multiple social cues must be evaluated simultaneously. The present study, therefore, focused on the process of social-cognitive integration, examining how young children combine relational (i.e., familiarity) and behavioral (i.e., response valence) information to form help-seeking expectations in early educational contexts.

The Role of Teachers' Familiarity and Response Valence

Teachers' familiarity plays an important role in shaping young children's interactions within teacher-child relationships. Familiarity provides the foundation for high-quality care and supportive engagement, fostering a sense of security and emotional comfort (Datler et al., 2012). Empirical studies show that young children are more likely to seek assistance from familiar teachers, as repeated interactions with known adults reduce uncertainty and promote approach behaviors (Corriveau & Harris, 2009). This tendency reflects young children's reliance on relational cues to guide social reasoning during early development.

Beyond familiarity, the valence of teachers' responses, whether positive or negative, also influences children's help-seeking expectations. According to the Teaching Through Interactions framework, positive emotional climates characterized by warm, supportive responses enhance engagement and encourage help-seeking (Hamre, 2014; Hamre & Pianta, 2007). Conversely, negative or dismissive responses can undermine children's expectations of support, discouraging future help-seeking (Jia, Wu et al., 2023). Together, these findings highlight the importance of teachers' emotional responsiveness in guiding young children's expectations of teacher support within ECE settings.

However, in everyday classrooms, social situations rarely involve a single clear cue. Children often face ambiguous or conflicting signals. For example, a familiar teacher who responds negatively, or an unfamiliar teacher who responds positively.

Interpreting such mixed cues requires advanced social-cognitive processing, as children must evaluate and weigh multiple dimensions of information when anticipating others' support.

By around 5 to 6 years of age, children begin to show increasing sophistication in integrating multiple social cues when reasoning about others' behavior (Corriveau & Harris, 2009; Corriveau et al., 2009; Williams & Danovitch, 2019). For example, Corriveau and Harris (2009) found that while younger children (aged 3-4 years) mainly relied on familiarity, 5-year-olds flexibly prioritized informant accuracy over familiarity when evaluating knowledge sources. These findings suggest that children at this age can balance competing cues and adjust their expectations accordingly.

However, most previous studies of cue integration have focused on selective social learning contexts, in which children evaluate the accuracy of others' knowledge, rather than supportive situations that involve emotional or instrumental assistance (Corriveau & Harris, 2009; Williams & Danovitch, 2019). Moreover, these studies often use decontextualized, low-stakes paradigms, which limit ecological validity. To address these limitations, the present study introduces a third-party help-seeking expectation task that simulates realistic teacher-child interactions. This paradigm allows for a direct examination of how children weigh familiarity and response valence cues when anticipating supportive behavior. By situating these judgments within emotionally meaningful scenarios, the study provides a new perspective on the development of social cue integration and the early formation of interpersonal expectations in ECE settings.

The Current Study

The current study examined how young children integrate teachers' familiarity and response valence when forming help-seeking expectations. To achieve this, we employed a third-party expectation task that simulated everyday teacher-child interactions. In each trial, participants were asked to predict whether a child protagonist would seek help from a familiar teacher or an unfamiliar teacher who displayed either a positive or a negative response. Teachers' response valence was manipulated through facial expressions and tone of voice. Specifically, positive responses featured a smiling face and a warm tone, whereas negative responses showed an angry face and a disapproving tone.

To enhance ecological validity, Study 1 used photographs of real teachers. Each participant's own lead teacher served as the familiar teacher. Study 2 followed the same task structure but replaced real teachers with cartoon figures and conveyed familiarity verbally through the experimenter. This modification allowed us to examine whether the effects of familiarity and response valence would remain consistent when familiarity was presented symbolically rather than based on direct experience.

We examined three main hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that young children would predict higher help-seeking toward familiar than unfamiliar teachers based on previous findings (Corriveau & Harris, 2009; Jia, Shen, Zhu et al., 2024). Second, drawing on evidence that preschoolers anticipate greater support from responsive caregivers (Jia, Wu et al., 2023), we predicted higher expectations toward teachers displaying positive rather than negative responses. Third, given that children about 5 years of age can integrate multiple social cues in social reasoning judgments (Corriveau & Harris, 2009; Williams & Danovitch, 2019), we predicted an interaction effect such that positive responses from familiar teachers would elicit the strongest help-seeking expectations.

Study 1 Participants

Forty-five Han Chinese children aged 5-6 years (range = 5.17-6.17 years, M = 5.81, SD = 0.28, 22 boys) were recruited from two preschools in Hangzhou, an eastern city in P. R.

China. The sample size was determined via a Monte Carlo simulation method based on a pilot study (N = 16), focusing on the interaction between familiarity and response valence. Using a conservative effect size value (β = 2.5) smaller than the observed effect size from the pilot study (β = 2.79) and α = 0.05, simulations conducted with the simr package (version 1.0.7, Green & Macleod, 2016) indicated that a sample of 44 participants would yield a statistical power of 85.6% (95% CI [82.21, 88.56]). The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from the children's guardians, and oral assent was obtained from the participants.

Task and Procedure

Materials

To manipulate teacher familiarity and response valence, we developed customized visual and auditory stimuli for each participant. In the familiar condition, a photograph of each child's own lead teacher was presented; in the unfamiliar condition, a photograph of a female research assistant who was unknown to the participants was used. All photographs were taken under standardized conditions (white background, consistent lighting) and were edited to ensure uniform brightness, hue, and vibrance. Each teacher's face was combined onto an identical female cartoon body to maintain consistency in clothing and posture.

Response valence was manipulated through both facial expression and speaking tone. Positive responses featured smiling faces with crescent eyebrows and warm, friendly tones, whereas negative responses featured angry faces with slightly raised (10 degrees) eyebrows and disapproving tones. These auditory stimuli were pretested with 34 undergraduate students majoring in early childhood education, confirming a significant perceived difference between positive (M = 4.24, SD = 1.05) and negative tones (M = 1.85, SD = 0.99) on a 5-point Likert scale (t (33) = 9.40, p < .001, Cohen's d = 2.35).

All teachers depicted in the stimuli were female, ensuring both methodological control and ecological validity. In China, the ECE workforce is predominantly female, with women accounting for 97.78% of all full-time teachers in 2020 (Ministry of Education of the People's Republic of China, 2021). Using only female teacher images thus reflected real classroom demographics and avoided gender-related confounds.

Help-Seeking Expectation Task

A computerized help-seeking expectation task was created in PsychoPy 3 (Peirce et al., 2022). The task followed a 2 (familiarity: familiar vs. unfamiliar) × 2 (response valence: positive vs. negative) within-subjects design, resulting in four conditions. Each condition included four randomized scenarios depicting common preschool challenges: (1) difficulty in doing origami, (2) inability to cut paper shapes, (3) conflict during block play, and (4) conflict while reading a picture book.

At the beginning of the task, participants were introduced to a child protagonist and two teacher figures (see Figure 1). They were told that one teacher had taught the child protagonist since the first year of preschool (familiar teacher), while the other worked at a different preschool and had never met the child (unfamiliar teacher). The position of the two teachers was counterbalanced across participants. Each condition included four trials presented in random order. In each trial, the child protagonist encountered a problem and cried, accompanied by a 1.5-second audio clip of a real child's cry. A teacher then appeared from the bottom right corner of the screen and stopped on the right side. Meanwhile, the experimenter narrated the scenario. For example, in the familiar-positive condition, the experimenter said, "This child is trying to fold a small frog but cannot and cries sadly. Then, their teacher comes over and asks 'What happened?' gently." After hearing the story, participants were asked to predict whether the child protagonist would seek help from the teacher. If they expected the child to seek help, the response was coded as a "1"; otherwise, it was coded as "0".

The order of conditions was counterbalanced across participants. Each child completed the task individually in a quiet room within their preschool. Upon completion, children received stickers as small rewards.

Statistical Analysis

To examine the effects of teacher familiarity and response valence on young children's help-seeking expectations, we applied a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) analysis using the glmer function from the lmerTest package (version 3.1-3, Kuznetsova et al., 2017) in R (version 4.0.5), which called functions from the lme4 package (version 1.1-37, Bates et al., 2015). Fixed factors included familiarity (familiar, unfamiliar), response valence (positive response, negative response), their interaction, and age (continuous). Familiarity and response valence were treated as categorical variables, coded with a simple contrast. To control for potential effects of age differences, age was grand-mean centered before inclusion in the model. Participant was included as a random factor, with random intercepts and random slopes for withinparticipant variables.

According to Barr et al (2013), we fitted the maximal model first. If the maximal model failed to converge, we simplified the model to the zero-correlation-parameter (ZCP) model. If the ZCP model failed to converge, the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) method was used to optimize the model (Bates et al., 2015). Post-hoc analyses were conducted using the emmeans package (Lenth et al., 2018).

To further examine the tendency of children's help-seeking expectations, we calculated the probability that young children expected that the protagonist would seek help in each condition and then compared them with the chance level using the one-sample t-test.

The main effect of age was not significant (β = 1.35, SE = 1.97, 2 = 0.65, p = .494). We found a significant interaction between familiarity and response valence (β = -2.72, SE = 0.83, 2 = 10.60, p = .001, OR = 0.07, 95% CI [0.01, 0.34]). There were no other significant interactions (ps > .05).

Discussion

The results indicate that within ECE settings, young children tended to expect help from familiar teachers, particularly when those teachers displayed positive responsiveness. This pattern underscores the important role of familiarity and perceived responsiveness in shaping children's expectations of social support. Although positive responsiveness from unfamiliar teachers modestly increased children's help-seeking expectations, they remained generally reluctant to anticipate help-seeking from unfamiliar figures. This finding highlights the enduring influence of familiarity as a primary social cue in young children's social reasoning about whom to approach for help. These results were in line with our hypotheses and consistent with previous research showing that young children anticipate greater support from familiar and responsive caregivers than from unfamiliar or negatively responsive individuals (Jia, Shen, Zhu et al., 2024; Jia, Zhou et al., 2023). Extending these findings, the present study found that by 5 to 6 years of age, children are capable of evaluating and integrating multiple social cues simultaneously when forming help-seeking expectations, reflecting emerging flexibility in their social-cognitive processing.

Study 2

The results of Study 1 showed that young children could integrate teachers' familiarity and response valence when forming help-seeking expectations in ECE settings.

Study 2 was designed to test the robustness and generalizability of these findings while addressing potential confounds. Specifically, we replaced real teacher photographs with cartoon teacher figures to control for the possible influence of participants' direct interpersonal experiences with their own teachers. In this way, Study 2 examined whether children could apply the same cue-integration processes when familiarity was conveyed symbolically rather than experientially.

To ensure that familiarity cues were understood purely as communicated social information, the experimenter provided explicit verbal descriptions of whether each teacher was familiar or unfamiliar to the child protagonist. This design allowed us to assess whether children's help-seeking expectations were grounded in their interpretation of social cues, independent of any prior personal relationships.

Participants

Based on the sample size calculation from Study 1, an additional 45 Han Chinese children aged 4-7 years (range = 4.58-7.08 years, M = 6.24; SD = 0.68, 23 boys) were recruited from the same two preschools in Hangzhou, China. The participant age range was slightly broader than in Study 1, reflecting the natural age composition of the participating classrooms. Written informed consent was obtained from participants' guardians, and oral consent was obtained from all the children.

Task and Procedure

The overall task structure in Study 2 was identical to that of Study 1, except for the presentation of teacher stimuli. Instead of photographs of real teachers, standardized cartoon teacher figures were used to depict the familiar and unfamiliar teachers (see Figure 3). Familiarity was manipulated through explicit verbal instructions provided by the experimenter. At the beginning of the task, participants were introduced to two cartoon teachers and were told which figure represented the child protagonist's familiar teacher (e.g., "This is the teacher who has been with the child since the first year of preschool") and which represented an unfamiliar teacher (i.e., "This is a teacher from another preschool whom the child has never met"). To ensure comprehension, participants were then asked to recall which teacher was familiar and which was unfamiliar. If their responses were incorrect, the experimenter repeated the explanation until the child demonstrated clear understanding. Only children who fully understood the distinction proceeded to the main task.

Apart from this modification, all other aspects, including the manipulation of response valence, scenario content, trial structure, randomization, and testing environment, were identical to those used in Study 1, ensuring methodological consistency across studies.

Statistical Analysis

First, a GLMM was conducted, including all participants to examine the effects of teachers' familiarity and response valence on children's help-seeking expectations. To account for potential age effects, age (grand-mean centered) and its interactions with familiarity and response valence were included as fixed factors in the model. Second, because the age range in Study 2 was broader than in Study 1, we re-ran the analysis, restricting the sample to 5-6-year-olds to ensure that the main findings were robust and not driven by the inclusion of younger participants. Third, one-sample t-tests were performed to determine whether children's help-seeking expectations in each condition differed from the chance level.

Finally, a cross-study analysis was conducted to assess the stability of results across the two studies and to examine potential differences associated with variations in the familiarity manipulation. In this model, Study (Study 1 vs. Study 2) and its interactions with the other fixed factors were added as additional fixed predictors. The model specification and optimization procedures were identical to those used in Study 1.

Results

The GLMM showed a significant main effect of familiarity (β = 5.46, SE = 1.37, 2 = 15.95, p < .001, OR = 235.10, 95% CI [16.04, 3446.80]). The post-hoc analysis showed that children's help-seeking expectations of the familiar teacher were significantly higher than those of the unfamiliar one (see Figure 4, z = 3.79, p < .001). The main effect of response valence was also significant (β = 4.49, SE = 1.27, 2 = 12.42, p < .001, OR = 132.95, 95% CI [11.03, 1602.31]). The post-hoc analysis showed that children's expectations of the positive response teacher were significantly higher than those of the negative response teacher (z = 3.32, p < .001). There were no other significant main or interaction effects (ps > .05). These results suggested that young children tended to expect that the protagonist would seek help from familiar teachers and positive-response teachers. We also re-ran the analyses for Study 2, restricting the sample to 5-6-year-olds to examine the robustness of the findings. The results remained consistent with those obtained from the full sample, indicating that the inclusion of 4-year-olds did not significantly affect the overall pattern of results. Detailed results were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

One-sample t-test showed that children's help-seeking expectations were significantly above the chance level for the familiar teacher with positive responses (t (44) = 12.14, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.81) and significantly below the chance level for the unfamiliar teacher with negative response (t (44) = -4.93, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.74). In contrast, children's expectations were at chance level for both the familiar teacher with negative responses and the unfamiliar teacher with positive responses (ps > .05). These results suggested that children showed a tendency to expect the protagonist to seek help from familiar positive response teachers but not from unfamiliar negative response teachers, while they did not show a clear bias for familiar negative response and unfamiliar positive response teachers.

Discussion

General Discussion

Young children actively seek help from competent others when encountering difficulties (Colman & Thompson, 2002). Across two studies, we found that children aged 5-6 years integrated teachers' familiarity and response valence when forming help-seeking expectations in ECE settings. Consistently, they expected that the protagonist would be most likely to seek help from familiar teachers who responded positively and least likely to seek help from unfamiliar teachers who responded negatively. These patterns, replicated across both studies, confirm the robust influence of familiarity and response valence on young children's social expectations. Notably, familiarity exerted a stronger influence, as children continued to expect support from familiar teachers even following negative responses. These findings highlight young children's capacity to process multiple social cues and their pronounced tendency to prioritize relationship-based information when navigating supportive interactions.

Our results extend previous research by showing that familiarity is not merely a basis of informational trust (Danovitch & Mills, 2014; Reyes-Jaquez & Echols, 2013; Schieler et al., 2018) but a central factor of support expectations in problem-solving situations. Previous studies suggested that children's trust in familiar adults might arise from generalized beliefs that teachers are reliable help providers (Corriveau & Harris, 2009). Yet, our results do not support this assumption. Even when unfamiliar teachers responded positively, children did not show higher help-seeking expectations toward them. This pattern indicates that the social role of "teacher" alone is insufficient to elicit expectations of support. Instead, an established interaction history is crucial for shaping help-seeking expectations in early childhood.

Our findings also show that the strength of the familiarity effect depends on how familiarity is represented. When grounded in daily interactions (Study 1), familiarity exerted stronger effects than when conveyed symbolically through verbal information (Study 2). The cross-study analysis confirmed this pattern. Specifically, children relied on familiarity cues across both studies, but overall help-seeking expectations were lower when teachers were represented by cartoon figures accompanied by verbal cues. This difference may reflect reduced experiential salience, as familiarity remained the dominant cue, but its influence weakened when direct experience was absent.

Furthermore, our results suggest that young children flexibly integrate social cues rather than following fixed rules. In contrast to selective trust paradigms where the effects of behavioral accuracy can override familiarity (Corriveau & Harris, 2009), familiarity in our study remained a powerful predictor even under negative response conditions. This suggests that strong relational priors built through repeated positive teacher-child interactions may buffer the effects of short-term negative cues. Children's expectations are therefore not purely reactive to immediate behavior but are guided by stable, experience-based representations that support adaptive help-seeking in everyday social environments.

Two complementary mechanisms may explain how children integrate familiarity and response valence when forming help-seeking expectations. One possibility is a sequential process, in which children first assess familiarity and then evaluate responsiveness. Unfamiliar teachers may trigger early disengagement due to perceived social risk (Gu et al., 2009). Alternatively, children may engage in parallel integration, processing both cues simultaneously but weighting them differently according to the situation. Evidence from selective trust research supports this latter view, showing that children as young as 4 or 5 years can coordinate perceptual and relational cues simultaneously when making social reasoning (Corriveau et al., 2009). The present findings are consistent with this parallel integration account, showing that familiarity generally carried more weight, although its influence was weaker when represented symbolically rather than experienced directly, as in Study 2. This pattern suggests that the richness of prior interaction experience modulates how children combine social information, a mechanism extending beyond informational trust to emotionally grounded supportive interactions.

Differences between the two studies further clarify this mechanism. Children in Study 1 generally showed higher help-seeking expectations than those in Study 2. This pattern was particularly visible in the negative response conditions, though the interaction was not statistically significant. This contrast likely reflects experience-based versus knowledge-based representations (Dykas & Cassidy, 2011). Photographs of real teachers in Study 1 may have activated children's episodic memories and well-established relational schemas, sustaining positive expectations even after negative cues (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2016). In contrast, the abstract, verbally conveyed familiarity in Study 2 lacked experiential depth, making children's expectations more sensitive to immediate response valence. Together, these results indicate that not all familiarity cues are equivalent; direct relational experience provides affective and mnemonic grounding that strengthens children's confidence in social support.

Most importantly, our study moves beyond simply adapting help-seeking paradigms to new contexts. It provides a theoretically grounded account of how familiarity depth and response valence jointly shape social expectation, linking developmental mechanisms of cue integration with applied understanding of teacher-child relationships in early education. Our findings also contribute to theoretical refinement with attachment and social expectation frameworks. Building on hierarchical models of attachment representations (Fraley, 2007; Waters & Waters, 2006), our results suggest that children flexibly draw on both specific representations (formed through direct interactions with a teacher) and generalized representations (derived from broader social expectations).

Rather than relying on fixed schemas, children dynamically integrate these levels of representation when anticipating supportive behavior. This dynamic integration may mark a developmental advance of the ability to flexibly weigh relational and behavioral cues when predicting others' responsiveness.

From a methodological perspective, the present study advances ongoing debates about stimulus realism in developmental research (Packer & Moreno-Dulcey, 2022 , 2023; Paulus & Caporaso, 2024; Wellman & Yu, 2022). Consistent effects across both photo-based and cartoon-based paradigms confirm the robustness of the findings, while differences in magnitude highlight how stimulus format influences the activation of relational representations. Photo-based stimuli engage lived social experiences and elicit more ecologically grounded judgments, whereas simplified or symbolic stimuli provide experimental control but may attenuate affective engagement. These results argue for the value of multi-method approaches that integrate ecological validity with experimental rigor, allowing researchers to triangulate findings across representational levels of social cognition.

Practically, the findings carry important implications for early education. They highlight the important role of familiarity and consistent positive responsiveness in supporting children's adaptive help-seeking behaviors. For educators, this underscores the importance of establishing and maintaining trusting relationships, particularly during transitional periods such as preschool entry or classroom reassignment (Howes & Ritchie, 2002). Ensuring continuity in teacher assignments and fostering warm, responsive interactions can encourage children to seek assistance when facing challenges, thereby enhancing both emotional security and problem-solving capacity (Birch & Ladd, 1997).

Moreover, children's tendency to prioritize familiarity over immediate behavioral cues presents a double-edged pattern. While familiarity can buffer against occasional negative interactions, persistent unresponsiveness from familiar teachers may normalize inadequate support. This calls for teacher training programs to emphasize not only relationship-building but also the sustained provision of positive, consistent responsiveness over time (Rimm-Kaufman & Hamre, 2010).

These insights are particularly relevant in educational situations with high teacher turnover or frequent exposure to unfamiliar caregivers, where relationship stability is fragile and children's access to reliable support may be compromised (LoCasale-Crouch et al., 2008). Intervention and policy efforts aimed at enhancing children's help-seeking competence should incorporate strategies that promote teacher stability, emotional attunement, and responsive caregiving. Creating secure and consistent environments may help young children feel confident and safe when seeking help.

While this study provides new insights into how teachers' familiarity and response valence shape young children's help-seeking expectations, several questions remain. First, previous studies on selective social learning have shown age-related changes in how children weigh relationship-related and behavioral cues (Corriveau & Harris, 2009; Corriveau et al., 2009; Williams & Danovitch, 2019). The current study's narrow age range limited the examination of such developmental differences. Future research should adopt broader age spans to test how the integration of familiarity and response valence evolves across early childhood.

Second, the distinction between experience-based and knowledge-based familiarity warrants further investigation. Direct interactional familiarity may rely on episodic memory and person-specific schemas, whereas verbally conveyed familiarity may depend on semantic processing and abstract social knowledge. Future studies could combine neurocognitive measures (e.g., fNIRs or EEG) with behavioral paradigms to examine whether these forms of familiarity engage distinct cognitive and neural systems.

Third, although the third-party paradigm provided strong experimental control, it may not fully capture children's spontaneous help-seeking behaviors in real-world teacher-child interactions (Paulus & Caporaso, 2024). Future research should incorporate naturalistic observations or interactive paradigms to examine whether experimentally observed patterns translate into daily behavior in authentic ECE settings.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that young children integrated both teachers' familiarity and response valence when forming help-seeking expectations.

Familiarity consistently emerged as the dominant cue, highlighting its foundational role in shaping children's representations of social support. By revealing how young children flexibly coordinate relational and behavioral information, this work deepens our understanding of the social-cognitive mechanism of the formation of young children's help-seeking expectations and underscores the enduring importance of secure, responsive teacher-child relationships in early education.
