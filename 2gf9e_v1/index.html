To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 2 imprecise p values out of 10 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

p < .05 Although the interaction was marginally significant (Model 6: β = -.15, p = .05) it emerged as significant in the overall model (Model 7; β = -.18, p < .05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

Bart Wille obtained his PhD from Ghent University in 2013. His research focuses on individual differences in the work context. Specific topics include the role of personality in career choice, job attitudes, work performance, and leader effectiveness.

[div-01]

The concept of leaders acting as servants was first introduced by Greenleaf (1977) and has since attracted considerable scholarly attention (e.g., Lee, Lyubovnikova, et al., 2020; Lemoine et al., 2019). Servant leadership is commonly defined as "an other-oriented approach to leadership manifested through one-on-one prioritizing of follower individual needs and interests and outward reorienting of their concern for self towards concern for others within the organization and the larger community" (Eva et al., 2019, p. 114). Research has linked servant leadership to a range of positive outcomes at the individual, team, and organizational levels (e.g., Eva et al., 2019; Hoch et al., 2018). Importantly, many of these relationships remain significant after controlling for other leadership styles, such as transformational, ethical, and authentic leadership (Hoch et al., 2018), highlighting the unique value of servant leadership in organizations. Although recent years have seen growing interest in understanding why leaders engage in servant leadership behaviors (Eva et al., 2019), important questions remain regarding the individual characteristics that drive leaders to serve others. The current study addressed this gap by focusing on antecedents of servant leadership and using supervisor-subordinate dyads to answer the question: "What drives leaders to serve?" Individual differences in leader personality are important predictors of their leadership styles (e.g., transformational leadership, Judge & Bono, 2000; authentic leadership, Malik et al., 2023), including servant leadership (Hunter et al., 2013; Washington et al., 2006). Yet, prior research on the role of individual differences in servant leadership has focused predominantly on explicit traits (Eva et al., 2019). Personality scholars have long argued that a comprehensive understanding of personality requires consideration of both explicit and implicit predispositions (e.g., Kehr, 2004; Winter et al., 1998). Implicit motives are emotional responses that drive individuals towards rewards and away from punishments (Schultheiss & Köllner, 2021). Three implicit motives have received particular attention (the "Big Three"): the inner desire or need for power, affiliation, and achievement.

In the present study, we propose that these motives explain unique variance in servant leadership beyond explicit personality traits. Furthermore, drawing on the channeling hypothesis (Lang et al., 2012), we argue that implicit motives interact with explicit traits to predict servant leadership behavior.

This study aims to extend the literature on servant leadership antecedents in three ways.

First, it replicates and extends prior research on personality traits and servant leadership (Eva et al., 2019) by adopting the HEXACO model (Ashton & Lee, 2007). In doing so, it examines the role of Honesty-Humility, a trait that has been theorized to be central to servant leadership (e.g., Sousa & van Dierendonck, 2017; van Dierendonck, 2011) but has received limited empirical attention. Second, it revisits the role of implicit motives in leadership. Scholars have argued that existing theories linking implicit motives and effective leadership may no longer fully reflect contemporary organizational structures and leader expectations (Steinmann et al., 2020). Moreover, advances in implicit motive assessment, such as the development of the Motive Self-Categorization Test (Runge & Lang, 2019), enable a more rigorous examination of these processes. Finally, by jointly examining implicit and explicit motives, this study tests the channeling hypothesis (Lang et al., 2012; Winter et al., 1998) and responds to calls for a better understanding of how personality shapes motive expression and how they both relate to effective leadership (Steinmann et al., 2020).

Servant Leadership and Explicit Traits

Compared to other leadership styles (e.g., transformational; Judge & Bono, 2000), research on the antecedents of servant leadership remains relatively limited (Eva et al., 2019).

Existing studies suggest that leaders who are more agreeable (Sun & Shang, 2019; Washington et al., 2006) and less extraverted (Hunter et al., 2013) are more likely to exhibit servant leadership behavior. However, this line of research has largely relied on the Five-Factor Model of personality (McCrae & John, 1992), thereby overlooking Honesty-Humility, a core dimension of the HEXACO model (Ashton & Lee, 2007). Honesty-Humility reflects a tendency to follow the rules, be honest and cooperative, and avoid manipulating others (Ashton & Lee, 2007). These characteristics align closely with the moral nature of servant leadership (Lemoine et al., 2019). Moreover, humility is widely considered a defining characteristic of servant leadership and is included as a key dimension in contemporary conceptualizations of the construct (Sousa & van Dierendonck, 2017; van Dierendonck, 2011). Consistent with this reasoning, recent research has shown that Honesty-Humility positively predicts humble leadership (Hassan et al., 2023). Taken together, these findings suggest that Honesty-Humility may represent a particularly important antecedent of servant leadership beyond the Big Five traits: Hypothesis 1: Honesty-Humility is positively related to servant leadership after controlling for the other five HEXACO personality traits.

Servant Leadership and Implicit Motives

While explicit motives are conscious, self-attributed needs, implicit motives are unconscious drives to seek certain incentives and avoid disincentives (McClelland et al., 1989; Schultheiss & Köllner, 2021). The two show low convergence because they operate as separate systems and therefore predict different types of behavior (Köllner & Schultheiss, 2014; McClelland et al., 1989). Specifically, explicit motives are related to deliberate, socially influenced behaviors, whereas implicit motives are linked to spontaneous behavior and intrinsic incentives.

Research suggests that implicit motives improve the prediction of spontaneous behaviors that are less susceptible to self-presentation biases (e.g., Asendorpf et al., 2002).

Extending this argument to morality, Perugini and Leone (2009) proposed that whereas explicit measures of morality (e.g., honesty) better predict self-reported morality, implicit measures may better predict actual (im)moral behavior.

Servant leadership is commonly regarded as a moral leadership style (e.g., Lee, Lyubovnikova, et al., 2020; Lemoine et al., 2019). Because servant leadership behaviors may stem from both spontaneous (e.g., on-the-spot decisions) and deliberate (e.g., careful decisionmaking) processes (Perugini & Leone, 2009), we propose that implicit motives may provide predictive value beyond personality traits alone. This leads us to our second hypothesis: Hypothesis 2: Implicit motives explain variance in servant leadership beyond HEXACO personality traits.

Servant Leadership and the Power Motive

High-power motivated individuals enjoy having impact over others and try to reduce others' impact over them (Schultheiss & Köllner, 2021). Power motivation has long been recognized as a key driver of leadership emergence and effectiveness (e.g., House et al., 1991).

Specifically, power-motivated people are more likely to reach the top of an organization (McClelland & Boyatzis, 1982), help their team achieve pre-established goals (Steinmann et al., 2015) and have a more successful career (Peterson & Stewart, 1996). However, findings on the relationship between power and specific leadership styles are mixed. Although power has been related to both bright (e.g., transformational; Steinmann et al., 2020) and dark (e.g., autocratic; Fodor & Smith, 1982) leadership styles, research suggests that implicit power motivation is often expressed through a desire to exert a positive influence on others and society. For instance, power-motivated individuals display greater social responsibility over time (Winter et al., 1981) and wish to contribute more to the welfare of others, the next generation, and society as a whole (Peterson & Stewart, 1996). These tendencies align closely with servant leadership, which emphasizes creating value for the community and fostering follower growth and success (Liden et al., 2008). Moreover, servant leaders might offer unsolicited help to followers, which is an indicator of the need for power (Winter, 1991). Based on these findings, we advance the following hypothesis: Hypothesis 3a: Leader power motivation is positively related to servant leadership.

Servant Leadership and the Affiliation Motive

Affiliation-oriented individuals have a deep inner desire to be accepted and loved by others (Winter et al., 1998). They obtain high satisfaction from their social relationships, which is why they focus on establishing, maintaining, and restoring positive relationships with others (Koestner & McClelland, 1992). Because affiliation-oriented individuals seem to prioritize harmonious interactions at all costs, affiliation motive was initially thought to impede effective leadership (McClelland, 1975). However, later research has found either no relationship (House et al., 1991) or a positive one (Steinmann et al., 2016 (Steinmann et al., , 2020)).

Steinmann and colleagues (2020) investigated the role of implicit affiliation motivation in transformational leadership. They argued that affiliation-oriented leaders should exhibit greater individualized consideration because they are more sensitive to followers' needs and care about their development. Consistent with this reasoning, their study showed that individualized consideration was one of the mechanisms through which affiliation motivation predicted positive leadership outcomes. Given that individualized consideration closely overlaps with servant leadership's emphasis on follower care and development (Eva et al., 2019; van Dierendonck, 2011), we propose that affiliation motivation is likely to energize various behaviors characteristic to servant leadership: Hypothesis 3b: Leader affiliation motivation is positively related to servant leadership.

Servant Leadership and the Achievement Motive

Achievement-oriented individuals have a strong desire to meet standards of excellence and master challenging tasks (Schultheiss & Köllner, 2021). Research linking achievement orientation to leadership styles has found that implicit achievement was unrelated (De Hoogh et al., 2005) or negatively related to charisma (House et al., 1991), and positively related to passive leadership (Steinmann et al., 2016). One explanation is that achievement-motivated individuals aim to attain exceptional outcomes individually and are generally less interested in leading and empowering others (McClelland & Boyatzis, 1982).

In their vignette study, Steinmann and colleagues (2020) argued that achievement motivation should be negatively related to transformational leadership. Specifically, they suggested that such leaders may be less likely to delegate responsibility (i.e., the intellectual stimulation dimension) and may pay less attention to interpersonal aspects (i.e., the individualized consideration dimension). These tendencies stand in contrast to servant leadership, which emphasizes follower growth, well-being, and long-term development (Stone et al., 2004). Because achievement-motivated leaders are primarily driven to attain excellence through their own efforts (McClelland & Boyatzis, 1982), investing heavily in followers' development may be perceived as secondary or even counterproductive to their goals. Channeling Effects of Implicit and Explicit Motives

Scholars have long argued that implicit motives and explicit traits should be studied jointly because they may interact in predicting behavior (e.g., Winter et al., 1998). Building on this idea, the channeling hypothesis (Lang et al., 2012; Winter et al., 1998) proposes that explicit traits operate as channels through which implicit motives manifest in behavior.

Consistent with this view, studies have found motive-trait interactions predicting outcomes such as relationship and career success (Winter et al., 1998), task performance (Lang et al., 2012), and counterproductive work behavior (Fatfouta & Schwarzinger, 2024). Overall, research suggests that a high implicit motive disposition tends to result in stronger behavioral expression when it is matched with high levels of a corresponding explicit trait (Lang et al., 2012). In the following section, we propose three channeling hypotheses that are conceptually relevant to servant leadership.

Implicit Affiliation Motive × Extraversion

Extraversion is characterized by sociability and positive emotionality (John & Srivastava, 1999). These traits facilitate the expression of a strong affiliation motive by enabling individuals to initiate and maintain social interactions, thereby fostering successful relationships and performance (Winter et al., 1998; Lang et al., 2012). We argue that extraversion similarly channels the expression of affiliation in the leadership context and thus promotes servant leadership. Specifically, affiliation-motivated extraverts likely use the social opportunities in their workplace to build deep relationships with their subordinates (Lang et al., 2012), allowing them to better understand the needs, values, and interests of their followers-a key characteristic of servant leadership (Eva et al., 2019). In contrast, affiliationmotivated introverts may be less able to translate their relational motives into behavior because they engage less readily in social interactions. Likewise, extraverts low in affiliation may establish social contact but lack the motivation to develop caring, follower-oriented relationships, potentially disappointing followers (Lang et al., 2012). Accordingly, we propose that leaders who possess both the initial resources to form (i.e., extraversion) and the motivation to maintain (i.e., affiliation) positive relationships should be more likely to exhibit servant behavior: Hypothesis 4a: Extraversion channels the expression of the affiliation motive such that affiliation-oriented leaders will display more servant leadership behaviors when they score high on extraversion, in comparison to when they score low on extraversion.

Implicit Power Motive × Extraversion

Research suggests that extraversion channels not only the expression of affiliation motives but also power motives. Specifically, power-oriented extraverts were found to have a more successful career than power-oriented introverts (Winter et al., 1998). These leaders are more likely to display servant behavior because their desire to influence others is matched with their extraverted behavior (e.g., assertiveness, dominance). In contrast, power-motivated introverts desire to influence others but are less likely to translate this desire into broad social impact. Likewise, extraverts with low power motivation may have the facilities to engage in social activities but fail to use these interactions to influence others and achieve collective goals. Hence, we posit that leaders who possess both the initial affordances (i.e., extraversion) and the motivation (i.e., power) to influence others should be more likely to display servant behavior: Hypothesis 4b: Extraversion channels the expression of the power motive such that power-oriented leaders will display more servant leadership behaviors when they score high on extraversion, in comparison to when they score low on extraversion.

Implicit Affiliation Motive × Agreeableness

Agreeableness reflects a tendency to be warm, empathetic, trustful, and cooperative John & Srivastava, 1999). Because these characteristics facilitate the establishment and maintenance of positive relationships, agreeableness has been identified as an important trait for the realization of affiliation-related goals (Hofer et al., 2015). Indeed, highly agreeable individuals are more likely than less agreeable individuals to achieve affiliation goals over time.

We argue that agreeableness similarly channels the expression of the affiliation motive in the leadership context. First, focusing on others' well-being requires an altruistic orientation, which is more characteristic of highly agreeable than less agreeable individuals (John & Srivastava, 1999). Second, servant leadership requires understanding followers' needs, interests, and goals (Eva et al., 2019), a capacity closely related to empathy and more prevalent among agreeable individuals (John & Srivastava, 1999). Third, empowering followers to make decisions and take risks requires a certain level of trust (Eva et al., 2019). Yet, people scoring low on agreeableness tend to be more skeptical and distrustful toward others (John & Srivastava, 1999). In contrast, highly agreeable leaders with a low affiliation motive may display considerate and supportive behaviors but lack the motivation to cultivate and maintain deep relationships with followers over time. Consequently, we posit that leaders who possess both the motivation (implicit affiliation motive) and assets (trait agreeableness) to maintain and establish positive relationships should be more likely to exhibit servant behavior than their counterparts: Hypothesis 4c: Agreeableness channels the expression of the affiliation motive such that affiliation-oriented leaders will display more servant leadership behaviors when they score high on agreeableness, in comparison to when they score low on agreeableness.

Method Participants and Procedure

Participants were Belgian subordinates and supervisors working across diverse sectors and organizations. Dyads were recruited through the personal networks of bachelor's and master's psychology students. To be eligible, participants had to (a) work at least 4/5 time, (b) be at least 21 years old, (c) have at least two years of work experience, and (d) occupy either a subordinate or supervisor role. Two recruitment procedures were used. In the first procedure, subordinates received an information letter inviting them to participate in a study on motivation and leadership. The letter included a brief study description, information about the survey, a personal code to link dyadic responses, and a link to the online survey. Approximately one week later, researchers invited supervisors by email after subordinates had provided their contact information. To protect privacy, subordinates were required to obtain their supervisors' consent before sharing their email addresses. The second procedure followed the same logic but started with supervisors. Supervisors were asked to provide the email address of the subordinate with whom they had worked the longest. Researchers then invited these subordinates after collecting their contact information through the supervisor survey. The research project received ethics approval from XXX (blinded for peer review) ethical committee (2020/163).

The supervisor survey consisted of two modules: (a) a traditional survey with demographic questions and explicit measures and (b) the implicit measures. Given the intensity of the design, supervisors could complete both modules separately within a two-week period.

We employed varying incentives during the data collection process. Depending on the recruitment wave, participants received either the chance to win a gift voucher or immediate personalized feedback as compensation.

A total of 273 subordinates and 260 supervisors completed the questionnaires. After excluding participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 229 dyads remained. To ensure data quality, we further excluded participants who failed the control items that were placed at various points in the surveys (e.g., "Please respond with "Disagree" for this item"). The final sample included 219 complete dyads. Subordinates (62.1% female) were between 21 and 64 years old (M = 42.36, SD = 11.40) and had worked in their organization for an average of 12.93 years (SD = 10.38). Most were highly educated, with 73.5% holding a bachelor's or master's degree. Supervisors (50.2% female) were between 21 and 66 years old (M = 46.12, SD = 9.96) and reported an average organizational tenure of 16.43 years (SD = 10.94). Similar to subordinates, most supervisors were highly educated (86.8% held a bachelor's or master's degree). The average dyadic tenure between subordinates and their supervisor was 6.15 years (SD = 6.18), and approximately half of the subordinates (49.3%) reported being in contact daily with their supervisor. Finally, participants in this study worked in diverse industries such as trades and services (21.9%), engineering, production, and construction (21.5%), healthcare and well-being (16.0%), education, culture, and science (15.1%), and law, security, and public administration (14.1%).

Measures

Explicit traits

Supervisors provided self-ratings of their personality using the 60-item Dutch language version of the HEXACO Personality Inventory (Ashton & Lee, 2009; de Vries et al., 2008).

Items were rated using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Internal consistency was acceptable to good across the six dimensions: Honesty-Humility (α = .74), Emotionality (α = .78), Extraversion (α = .72), Agreeableness (α = .79), Conscientiousness (α = .70), and Openness to Experience (α = .78).

Implicit motives

We measured implicit motives using the Motive Self-Categorization Test (MSC; Runge & Lang, 2019), a more recent instrument based on the Operant Motive Test (Kuhl & Scheffer, 2001). In this test, participants are presented with 15 pictures depicting ambiguous social situations and are asked to answer three questions: (1) "What is important for the person in this situation, and what is the person doing?" (2) "How does the person feel?", and (3) "Why does the person feel this way?". After responding to these questions, participants are presented with four to six statements and asked to select the one that best matches the content of their story. This self-coding method offers several advantages over traditional coding procedures involving expert raters (Runge & Lang, 2019), including greater proximity to participants' own interpretations, increased scoring objectivity, and reduced time and resource requirements because expert coders are no longer needed. Moreover, Runge and Lang (2019) demonstrated strong correlations between the implicit motive scores of the MSC and expert-coded motive scores, supporting the validity of the instrument. Figure 1 presents an example item of the MSC.

Servant Leadership

Servant leadership was rated by subordinates using the SL-7, a short form of the Servant Leadership Scale (Liden et al., 2008; 2015). Participants responded on a scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Sample items include "My leader puts my best interests ahead of his/her own." and "My leader emphasizes the importance of giving back to the community." Internal consistency was good (α = .79).

Control Variables

We included gender, age, and supervisor tenure as control variables. Gender is shown to be related to implicit motives (McClelland et al., 1989; Pang & Schultheiss, 2005) and servant leadership behavior (Beck, 2014). Age was included because research shows that personality traits (Roberts et al., 2006) and implicit motives (McClelland et al., 1989) may evolve over the life course. Finally, we controlled for supervisor tenure because it is associated with a tendency to display servant leadership behavior more frequently (Beck, 2014).

Results

Descriptive statistics and intercorrelations are provided in Table 1. Consistent with previous findings, servant leadership was significantly and positively related to supervisors' gender (Beck, 2014). Neither age nor supervisor tenure was significantly related to servant leadership. Correlations between explicit traits and implicit motives were low, which is also consistent with prior research (Köllner & Schultheiss, 2014). Likewise, correlations among implicit motives were weak or negative, replicating earlier MSC research (e.g., Runge et al., 2020). Finally, implicit power showed a higher mean than affiliation and achievement motives, consistent with findings from Western samples (e.g., Lang et al., 2012; Runge et al., 2020).

Table 2 presents the multiple regression analyses used to test the hypotheses. Analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2023). We first estimated a model with control variables only (Model 1), followed by personality traits (Model 2) and implicit motives (Model 3). Next, the hypothesized interactions were tested separately (Model 4 to Model 6) and together in an overall model (Model 7). Following Aiken and West (1991), all predictors were standardized prior to the moderated regression analyses.

Hypothesis 1 predicted that Honesty-Humility would be positively related to servant leadership after controlling for the other five HEXACO personality traits. As shown in Table 2 (Model 2), Honesty-Humility did not emerge as a significant predictor of servant leadership.

Thus, Hypothesis 1 was not supported.

Hypothesis 2 predicted that implicit motives would explain variance in servant leadership beyond HEXACO personality traits. However, adding implicit motives did not significantly improve explained variance (R² = .03; p = 0.19). Therefore, Hypothesis 2 could not be supported.

Hypotheses 3a-c predicted that power and affiliation motives would be positively related to servant leadership, whereas the achievement motive would be negatively related.

However, after controlling for HEXACO personality traits, none of the implicit motives significantly predicted servant leadership (power: b = 0.04, p = .17; affiliation: b = 0.03, p = .27; achievement: b = -0.02, p = .47). Therefore, Hypotheses 3a-c were not supported.

Hypotheses 4a-c investigated the channeling effects of implicit and explicit motives on servant leadership. Interactions were first tested separately (Models 4-6) and then together in a pooled model (Model 7). Consistent with hypothesis 4a, affiliation motive interacted positively with extraversion in predicting servant leadership (Model 4: β = .15, p < .05). As shown in Figure 2 (Panel A), the positive relationship between leaders' affiliation motive and servant leadership was indeed stronger at higher levels of extraversion. In contrast, Hypothesis 4b was not supported, as the interaction between power motive and extraversion was not significant (β = -.06, p = .40). Finally, Hypothesis 4c proposed that agreeableness would positively channel the implicit affiliation motive in the prediction of servant leadership.

Although the interaction was marginally significant (Model 6: β = -.15, p = .05) it emerged as significant in the overall model (Model 7; β = -.18, p < .05). As shown in Figure 2 (Panel B), the interaction was opposite to expectations: leaders scoring low on agreeableness and high on implicit affiliation displayed the highest levels of servant leadership.

Overall, including the channeling effects over HEXACO personality traits (Model 2) significantly increased the amount of explained variance in servant leadership (R² = .07).

Only including implicit measures led to smaller and non-significant changes in R² values (.03), indicating that the additional explained variance mostly came from the interacting/channeling effects.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to advance the servant leadership literature by examining leaders' explicit traits, implicit motives, and their interplay as antecedents of servant leadership. Specifically, we assessed leaders' self-rated explicit personality traits using the HEXACO personality framework (Ashton & Lee, 2009), which enabled us to include Honesty-Humility alongside traits that have previously been linked to servant leadership (e.g., Hunter et al., 2013). In addition, we investigated whether leaders' implicit motives (i.e., the power, affiliation, and achievement motive) explained variance in servant leadership beyond these explicit traits. Drawing on the channeling hypothesis (Lang et al., 2012; Winter et al., 1998), we further examined whether explicit traits interact with (corresponding) implicit motives and whether these interactions improve the prediction of servant leadership.

Contrary to our expectations, neither the HEXACO personality traits nor the implicit motives emerged as significant predictors of servant leadership. However, incorporating the channeling effects significantly increased the explained variance in servant leadership.

Consistent with our hypothesis, we found support for a channeling effect of extraversion on the relationship between leaders' affiliation motive and servant leadership, such that affiliationoriented leaders displayed higher levels of servant leadership only when they were also highly extraverted. In contrast, no support was found for the hypothesized channeling effect of extraversion on implicit power. Finally, agreeableness significantly moderated the relationship between implicit affiliation and servant leadership, although not in the hypothesized direction.

Specifically, affiliation-oriented leaders exhibited higher levels of servant leadership when they were lower rather than higher in agreeableness. One possible explanation is that leaders who are relatively low in agreeableness, yet strongly motivated to develop and maintain close interpersonal relationships, may be more willing to address problems, confront difficult issues, and provide candid feedback when necessary. Although such behaviors may sometimes be perceived as less accommodating, they may ultimately promote followers' growth and development. In this regard, Heine et al. (2018) described a combination of support and challenge as "tough love" within the servant leadership context. Alternatively, servant behaviors displayed by these leaders may be perceived as more genuine, thereby strengthening followers' perceptions of servant leadership.

Theoretical Implications

The non-significant relationships between explicit traits and servant leadership in the present study add nuance to prior research on these associations. Overall, research on the relationship between servant leadership and personality traits using dyadic designs is relatively limited. Specifically, studies using similar designs have shown that leaders' agreeableness is positively associated with followers' perceptions of servant leadership (Sun & Shang, 2019; Washington et al., 2006), whereas leaders' extraversion is negatively associated with such perceptions (Hunter et al., 2013). One potential explanation for the non-significant relationship between agreeableness and servant leadership concerns differences in measurement. Whereas prior studies relied on Big Five conceptualizations of agreeableness, we used HEXACO Agreeableness, which differs conceptually by excluding sentimentality-related content and incorporating anger-related tendencies typically captured by Neuroticism in the Big Five framework (Ashton et al., 2014).

The non-significant relationships between implicit motives and servant leadership are also noteworthy. Although we are not aware of prior research directly examining these associations, significant links have been found between the affiliation motive and related leadership constructs such as transformational leadership (Steinmann et al., 2015 (Steinmann et al., , 2020)).

Contrary to these expectations, implicit motives did not exhibit direct relationships with servant leadership in the present study. However, the significant interaction effects suggest that affiliation motives may not influence servant leadership in isolation but rather in combination with corresponding explicit personality traits (i.e., agreeableness and extraversion). The emergence of these effects across raters is particularly noteworthy because it reduces concerns that the findings are driven by common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2024) and suggests that the proposed channeling mechanisms are reflected in how leaders are actually perceived by their followers.

Taken together, these findings advance understanding of how implicit and explicit personality jointly influence servant leadership. Hunter and colleagues (2013, p. 327) proposed that the different impact of agreeableness and extraversion on servant leadership may stem from "underlying motivations and values (communion striving and status striving) that drive servant leader behavior" and called for future research to examine this possibility. Likewise, Steinmann and colleagues (2020) argued that additional research is needed to understand how personality traits shape the relationship between motives and effective leadership. Our findings suggest that leaders were perceived as more servant-oriented only when their extraverted behavior aligned with a strong implicit need for affiliation. This channeling effect is consistent with prior research in other domains (e.g., Lang et al., 2012; Winter et al., 1998) and may help explain the previously observed negative association between extraversion and servant leadership (Hunter et al., 2013). As Lang et al. (2012) argued, extraverted behavior may facilitate the initiation of relationships, but without a strong affiliation motive it may be insufficient to sustain them over time.

Our findings may also help explain inconsistent results regarding the role of implicit affiliation in positive leadership styles (House et al., 1991; McClelland, 1975; Steinmann et al., 2016 Steinmann et al., , 2020)). Specifically, a strong need for affiliation was associated with higher servant leadership only when combined with high extraversion and low agreeableness. This pattern suggests that affiliation motives may need to be complemented by more dominant and assertive attributes to translate into effective leadership. Such an interpretation is consistent with research showing that the combination of communal (i.e., caring) and agentic (i.e., assertive, harsh) attributes can enhance leader effectiveness (e.g., Do & Minbashian, 2014; Micevski et al., 2017).

Practical Implications

The current findings encourage organizations to consider both explicit personality traits and implicit motives in talent management practices. Compared to explicit personality assessments, implicit motives have traditionally been underutilized because their assessment often requires substantial time and trained coders. By eliminating the need for expert coders, the MSC (Runge & Lang, 2019) offers a practical and promising alternative.

First, organizations may benefit from incorporating both implicit and explicit personality assessments into leadership selection and promotion decisions. Doing so could help identify individuals who possess not only the traits but also the underlying motivational drives associated with servant leadership. Assessing both explicit and implicit traits may also support leadership training and development by providing leaders with insight into their strengths and development needs, thereby enabling more targeted interventions. For example, introverted leaders who are strongly motivated to build and maintain close relationships may benefit from training aimed at enhancing social and assertive behaviors (e.g., Haehner et al., 2024).

Likewise, highly affiliation-motivated leaders who score very high on agreeableness may benefit from conflict management training to offset potential drawbacks associated with excessive agreeableness. Finally, because servant leadership is linked to a range of positive individual, team, and organizational outcomes (Eva et al., 2019; Hoch et al., 2018; Lee, Lyubovnikova, et al., 2020), a better understanding of its antecedents may ultimately help organizations foster more effective leadership and become more competitive.

Strengths, Limitations and Future Directions

The present study possesses several notable strengths. First, it contributes to a relatively underdeveloped area of research by examining leaders' implicit motives, which are rarely included in leadership studies due to the resource-intensive nature of their assessment. Given the substantial time commitment required from participants (approximately 60 minutes in the present study), obtaining a relatively large sample of supervisors represents a significant strength. Second, the study employed a multi-informant leader-follower design, with leaders reporting their own personality characteristics and followers rating servant leadership. This design reduces concerns regarding common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2024) and provides a particularly rigorous test of the proposed relationships. Finally, participants were drawn from a wide range of organizations and occupational backgrounds, increasing confidence that the observed relationships generalize across different organizational contexts.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered. First, the crosssectional design prevents causal inference. Recent reviews have emphasized the value of research designs that allow for stronger causal conclusions in servant leadership research (Schowalter & Volmer, 2023). Although implicit motives and personality traits tend to be relatively stable, the possibility that servant leadership itself contributes to personality development over time cannot be excluded. Additionally, our design provides only a snapshot of leaders' implicit and explicit personality characteristics and perceived leadership style at a single point in time. As a result, it remains unclear how and when followers infer and use information about their leaders' underlying motives.

Second, it is important to note that the overall amount of variance explained in servant leadership was relatively modest. This finding is consistent with prior leadership research, in which personality traits generally show only modest associations with leadership styles (e.g., Bono & Judge, 2004). As noted by De Vries (2012), such effects may be attenuated by the limited correspondence between self-rated personality and other-rated leadership. At the same time, the modest explained variance indicates that servant leadership is likely influenced by a broader constellation of individual and contextual factors than those included in the present study.

Drawing on these limitations, we believe this study presents potential opportunities for future research. First, prospective studies could examine other potentially relevant variables (e.g., contextual variables, follower attributes) to improve the prediction of servant leadership.

In particular, more attention should be devoted to factors that moderate the relationship between leader characteristics and servant leadership. For instance, followers' expectations toward their leaders (e.g., ideal leader prototype; Lacroix & Verdorfer, 2017) and sensitivity to favorable treatment (Wu et al., 2013) may provide valuable insights into earlier stages of the leadership process. Second, future research could extend our knowledge of implicit motives by examining their visibility to others. While personality research routinely incorporates self-and otherratings of explicit traits (Connelly & Ones, 2010) little is known about whether implicit motives can be accurately perceived by followers. The leadership arena-reputation-identity (LARI) model (Vergauwe et al., 2022) offers a useful framework for investigating this issue.

Specifically, comparing leaders' self-rated implicit motives with followers' perceptions of these motives could help determine whether implicit motives are observable and the extent to which they shape leadership perceptions.

Finally, future research could employ longitudinal designs to examine how implicit motives and explicit traits jointly influence perceptions of servant leadership over time. While explicit traits may be readily observable, implicit motives may only become visible through repeated interactions, allowing followers to revise or confirm their initial impressions of leaders. For instance, low agreeableness may be perceived less negatively when followers start recognizing an underlying motivation to build and maintain meaningful relationships. Such reevaluations may strengthen perceptions of servant leadership, particularly when leaders' behavior is perceived as sincere (Ma et al., 2020). Studying these temporal dynamics could enhance our understanding of how servant leadership perceptions emerge and unfold over time.

Conclusion

Despite the surge of interest in servant leadership over the past decades, its antecedents remain relatively underexplored. Our research addresses this gap and highlights the benefits of considering the joint and interactive effects of implicit and explicit motives in the (servant) leadership field. Overall, the findings suggest that a combination of specific traits-high implicit affiliation, high extraversion, and low agreeableness-can help gain a better understanding of what drives leaders to serve.
