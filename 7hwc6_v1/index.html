Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Full text

Abstract

Daily context is widely recognized as shaping emotion regulation (ER) strategy use, yet empirical work has largely focused on situational context, with limited evidence on temporal context, despite the fact that cognitive and affective resources relevant to ER fluctuate systematically across the day. The present study used ecological momentary assessment to examine how temporal rhythms and situational contexts shape ER strategy use and whether their contributions are independent. Strategies were organized into approach-and avoidance-oriented orientations (e.g., reappraisal vs. distraction) to capture theoretically meaningful differences in regulatory goals and cognitive demands. Over 21 days, 155 adults completed 4 daily prompts assessing situations and ER strategy use (12,011 observations). Multilevel models indicated that participants endorsed fewer approach-and avoidance-oriented ER strategies as the day progressed and on weekends than on weekdays. Multilevel latent profile analyses identified four situational profiles (Positivity-engagement, Low-engagement, Negativity-engagement, and Structured-engagement contexts) that were associated with ER strategy use. Approach-oriented strategies were most frequent in Structured-engagement contexts, although their difference from Negativity-engagement contexts depended on savoring. Avoidance-oriented strategies were used more in Negativity-engagement and Low-engagement contexts and less in Positivity-engagement contexts than in Structured-engagement contexts, a pattern that held across alternative strategy groupings in sensitivity analyses. Accounting for situational context improved model fit and attenuated the temporal differences by 45% to 79%, indicating substantial overlap between temporal patterning and differences in the situations encountered across the day, while a smaller evening decline in approach-oriented strategies remained after accounting for situational context.

These findings provide initial evidence to ground context-sensitive models of ER flexibility and suggest temporal and situational windows for future research on ER-focused intervention.

INTRODUCTION

Emotion regulation (ER) is broadly defined as the process by which people consciously or automatically use strategies to modulate emotional responses to environmental demands (Gross, 2015). ER unfolds dynamically in daily life and plays a central role in psychological well-being and psychopathology (Aldao et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2025). Building on this foundation, recent theoretical frameworks have emphasized the importance of considering context and flexibility in ER (Aldao, 2013; Aldao et al., 2015; Bonanno et al., 2023). Evaluating such flexibility, however, requires first understanding the naturally occurring patterns of strategy use in daily life. Many studies examined only situations as context and focused on a limited set of situational characteristics (Chen et al., 2024 (Chen et al., , 2026;; Springstein & English, 2023; Willroth et al., 2025), without simultaneously considering how the time of day could shape ER. Addressing this gap can potentially improve our ability to explain why emotional crises tend to peak at night (Bu et al., 2025; Tubbs et al., 2022; Valtonen et al., 2006), to evaluate whether certain ER-context covariations constitute adaptive fit (Chen & Joormann, 2026), and to identify when and where ER-focused interventions are most needed (Bettis et al., 2022; Southward et al., 2021).

Understanding ER strategy use in daily contexts thus requires examining two fundamental contextual dimensions of daily life: the temporal context of the day and the situational context that arises alongside it.

Temporal Context and ER Strategy Use

One fundamental contextual dimension shaping ER strategy use in daily life is time.

Cognitive and affective resources relevant to ER fluctuate systematically across the day, shaped by circadian rhythms and daily routines. Experience-sampling studies show reliable diurnal patterns in affective valence and arousal (Bu et al., 2025; Owens et al., 2000), and ER is typically engaged in response to affect (Gross, 2015). It follows that the propensity to use different ER strategies may likewise vary as the day unfolds. Consistent with this view, prior work has documented diurnal shifts in specific strategies, with reappraisal reported more frequently earlier in the day and rumination more commonly reported later (Moberly & Watkins, 2008; Takano & Tanno, 2011). Despite these insights, it remains unclear whether the temporal dynamics of ER strategy use extend to broader motivational orientations, such as approach-versus avoidanceoriented strategies.

Situational Context and ER Strategy Use

In addition to time, situational context plays a central role in shaping ER strategy use.

Situations can be understood as a fleeting set of external circumstances comprising cues that people perceive and interpret to form psychological meaning (Rauthmann & Sherman, 2020).

These situational characteristics signal environmental demands and guide individuals in selecting strategies that best fit those demands (Guillaume et al., 2016; Rauthmann et al., 2014). To systematically capture the psychological meaning of situations, Rauthmann and colleagues (2014) developed the DIAMONDS taxonomy, which identifies eight core situational characteristics: Duty, Intellect, Adversity, Mating, pOsitivity, Negativity, Deception, and Sociality. More recent extensions have incorporated Controllability, given its central relevance to ER (Chen et al., 2024). This taxonomy provides a comprehensive framework for quantifying moment-to-moment situational context and examining how situational characteristics influence ER strategy use and emotions (Chen et al., 2024; Sherman et al., 2015). For example, social features of situations such as the presence of others and interpersonal demands are associated with differential strategy use, with suppression more likely when non-close others are present and when people pursue interpersonal goals (English et al., 2017). However, prior work has examined individual DIAMONDS and Controllability dimensions in isolation. Because situational characteristics tend to co-occur, examining them individually may not fully capture how situations are experienced as configurations of multiple psychological features. Profilebased approaches provide a complementary way to represent such multivariate patterns by characterizing recurring combinations of features at the momentary level (Lee & Beck, 2026). A profile-based approach therefore allows us to examine whether ER differs across recurring configurations of situational characteristics, rather than estimating associations with each dimension separately.

Temporal and Situational Contexts in ER

Although temporal and situational contexts each shape ER strategy use, they may not be separable influences in daily life. Daily experiences are often structured such that situations and their associated psychological characteristics tend to occur at particular times. For example, Duty and Intellect demands are more common earlier in the day, whereas Sociality and pOsitivity situations more often emerge in evenings and on weekends (Bu et al., 2025; Marchand et al., 2016; Ryan et al., 2010). This raises a key question: are temporal differences in ER strategy use fully explainable by momentary situational context? They may jointly contribute to ER patterns: systematic shifts in situations across the day, and time-linked processes (e.g., circadian variation in cognitive control, fatigue) that operate independently of situational context. Examining the degree of attenuation when situational context is added to temporal models may allow us to characterize the extent to which each source of context independently contributes to ER.

Organizing ER Strategies Along an Approach-Avoidance Dimension

To examine how ER strategy use varies across temporal and situational contexts in a theoretically meaningful and statistically tractable way, we organize strategies into broad motivational orientations rather than examining them independently. This categorization is particularly suited to ecological momentary assessment (EMA) designs. First, individuals may report using none, one, or multiple strategies across assessments (Ford et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2026), which could lead to extremely high or low base rates at any given prompt, a problem that reduces statistical precision and inflates the number of simultaneous comparisons. Aggregating strategies into orientations produces more stable count outcomes while preserving theoretically meaningful distinctions. Second, motivational orientation captures a theoretically coherent dimension that is likely more sensitive to broad contextual pressures, such as the availability of cognitive resources or the nature of situational demands, than any individual strategy. A longstanding distinction in ER and motivation literatures differentiates approach-oriented strategies (e.g., problem solving, reappraisal), which involve engaging with or modifying emotional experiences, from avoidance-oriented strategies (e.g., distraction, suppression), which involve disengaging from or attenuating emotional responses (Daniel et al., 2023; Kashdan et al., 2008). This distinction is not intended to evaluate strategy effectiveness, but rather to capture differences in regulatory goals and cognitive demands that are likely sensitive to contextual constraints. Organizing strategies by motivational orientation thus provides a theoretically grounded perspective for examining how temporal and situational contexts shape patterns of ER strategy use in daily life.

The Present Study

The present study used EMA over 21 days to examine how temporal and situational contexts shape the use of approach-and avoidance-oriented ER strategies in daily life.

Specifically, we aimed to address three main questions: a) How does the temporal context relate to approach-and avoidance-oriented ER strategy use? b) How does situational context relate to these strategies? c) Does accounting for situational context improve model fit and change observed temporal patterns in ER strategy use? All three questions were exploratory. For temporal context, individual ER strategies may follow different diurnal patterns because they rely on different cognitive, affective, and motivational processes (Moberly & Watkins, 2008; Takano & Tanno, 2011). Aggregating strategies into broader approach-and avoidance-oriented groups therefore does not imply a single directional pattern across the day. For situational context, the profiles represent combinations of multiple situational characteristics, and different characteristics within a profile may point toward different regulatory responses. We therefore examined how strategy orientations varied across these naturally occurring configurations rather than specifying directional predictions for particular profiles. Finally, temporal and situational contexts may overlap while also contributing independently to ER strategy use. We therefore examined how much the temporal associations changed after accounting for situational context, without specifying a particular magnitude of attenuation. Together, these analyses characterize how ER strategy use is organized across the temporal and situational structure of daily life.

METHODS

Transparency and Openness

Data were processed using R 4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2024) and Mplus Version 8.3 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). This study is a secondary analysis of a larger parent study and the research questions and analysis plan for the present analyses were not preregistered. All data, materials, and code used to conduct the primary analyses are available online at OSF (deidentified for masked review). Below, we report how we determined our sample size, describe all data inclusions, exclusions, and transformations, and list all measures used in the study.

Participants and Procedure

This study draws from a larger project examining ER flexibility, whose sample size was determined by its primary aims (Chen et al., 2024). A total of 173 participants from the United States were initially enrolled and were recruited through social media platforms (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, Reddit). Each participant attended an orientation meeting with a trained research assistant, during which the EMA procedure was explained and a practice survey was completed.

Fifteen participants withdrew before or during the EMA phase, and three participants were excluded because they completed the EMA protocol outside the 21-day window due to scheduling conflicts, which would have introduced inconsistency in the temporal structure of the data, resulting in a final analytic sample of 155 (M age = 35.73, SD = 12.34). The sample was predominantly female (72.9%), with 3.9% identifying as non-binary or gender non-conforming.

Most participants identified as Caucasian American (49.0%), followed by Asian American (27.7%), Black or African American (12.9%), American Indian, Alaska Native, or Native Hawaiian (2.6%), and multiracial (5.2%), and 10.3% identified as Hispanic or Latino.

Demographic details are presented in Table 1.

During the 21-day EMA period, participants received four daily prompts at approximately 12 PM, 3 PM, 6 PM, and 9 PM, with up to 90 minutes to respond. At each prompt, participants were asked to recall the most emotionally salient situation they had experienced in the past three hours, which anchored all subsequent ratings. Across the four prompts, the recall windows therefore spanned approximately 9 AM to 9 PM. This schedule was established by the parent study to provide evenly spaced coverage across daytime and evening hours while limiting participant burden over the 21-day EMA period and avoiding later-night assessments. Because the parent study was designed to assess ER flexibility across everyday situations rather than circadian variation across the full day, early-morning and late-night periods were not sampled (see Limitations). Participants completed 12,011 of 13,020 scheduled prompts (92.3%; perparticipant range = 62-84 of 84; median = 80). Participants were compensated US$40 for completing at least 80% of the surveys and US$60 for completing at least 95%. Full methodological details are available in Chen et al. (2024).

Measures

EMA Measures.

Perceived Situational Characteristics. Following procedures used in prior research (Rauthmann et al., 2014), participants rated the applicability of nine situational characteristics to their recalled situation on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (entirely). Eight characteristics were drawn from the DIAMONDS taxonomy (Rauthmann et al., 2014): "Work has to be done" for Duty, "Deep thinking is required" for Intellect, "Someone is being threatened, blamed, or criticized" for Adversity, "The situation is romantically or sexually charged" for Mating, "Situation is enjoyable" for pOsitivity, "Situation includes negative feelings (e.g., stress, anxiety, guilt)" for Negativity, "Someone is being deceived" for Deception, "Social interaction is possible or required" for Sociality. Additionally, Controllability ("I have control over the situation") was added based on its relevance to ER following a prior study (Chen et al., 2024). Of these nine dimensions, Adversity, Mating, and Deception showed low to no variability across observations for most participants (see Figure S1). Analyses focused on the remaining six situational dimensions: Duty, Intellect, pOsitivity, Negativity, Sociality, and Controllability. This decision was made prior to profile estimation to avoid artifacts from nearzero-variance indicators.

Momentary ER Strategy Use. Following the situational ratings, participants indicated whether they used a series of ER strategies as 0 (no) or 1 (yes) based on their response to the situation. Participants could endorse multiple strategies per prompt. Strategies included cognitive reappraisal (i.e., I changed how I thought about the event), expressive suppression (i.e., I made sure to hide how I feel), rumination (i.e., I repeatedly thought about the event and could not stop), worry (i.e., I worried about what may happen), distraction (i.e., I did something else to take my mind off it), problem-solving (i.e., I tried to solve problems or resolve the events), savoring (i.e., I took time to savor or enjoy what happened), planning (i.e., I made plans for the next steps), positive refocusing (i.e., I focused on the positive aspect of the event), soothing (i.e., I looked to others for comfort), and perspective seeking (i.e., I tried to find out what others would do if they were in my situation). Following previous studies (Bischof et al., 2024; Daniel et al., 2019; Daros et al., 2019; Hohensee et al., 2025), strategies were classified into two motivational orientations: approach-oriented ER strategies (i.e., cognitive reappraisal, savoring, problem-solving, planning, and perspective seeking) and avoidance-oriented ER strategies (i.e., distraction, rumination, expressive suppression, soothing, worry, and positive refocusing). These groups broadly correspond to the engagement-oriented and avoidance-oriented groupings used in these studies; we use the approach-avoidance terminology of the motivational framework (Daniel et al., 2023; Kashdan et al., 2008). For each orientation, a composite score was computed by summing the binary endorsements across all items in that orientation at each prompt, yielding a count of approach-and avoidance-oriented strategies used per situation. These counts index how many strategies were endorsed, not the intensity, effort, or effectiveness of regulation. No strategy or situational item was missing at any completed prompt. Because individual strategies are endorsed relatively infrequently at any one prompt, prior EMA studies have similarly operationalized these orientations by counting the number of strategies used within each group at each moment (Bischof et al., 2024; Daniel et al., 2019; Daros et al., 2019; Hohensee et al., 2025), consistent with evidence that people commonly use multiple ER strategies within the same regulatory episode (Ford et al., 2019).

Four strategy classifications need clarification because these strategies have been classified differently across prior studies. Positive refocusing, classified here as an avoidanceoriented strategy, involves shifting attention toward positive aspects of a situation; however, its defining regulatory function is to redirect attention away from the emotionally salient event rather than engage with it (Garnefski et al., 2001). In Southward and Cheavens (2020), by contrast, positive refocusing loaded with the adaptive-engagement strategies. Soothing is similarly classified as an avoidance-oriented strategy because its primary function in the present context is to reduce negative affect through external co-regulation rather than to engage with or modify the eliciting situation (Gökdağ, 2021; Hofmann et al., 2016); we acknowledge that social support seeking can serve approach-oriented functions in other contexts. Rumination, classified here as an avoidance-oriented strategy (Bischof et al., 2024; Hohensee et al., 2025), involves repetitive and passive focus on distressing thoughts and emotions; however, its defining regulatory function is not to engage constructively with the eliciting situation, but to substitute abstract analysis for direct emotional processing and behavioral action (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008; Watkins, 2008). Although rumination maintains attention on negative content, it often perpetuates distress and serves as a form of avoidance of adaptive engagement, such as problemsolving (Hohensee et al., 2025; Southward & Cheavens, 2020), rather than direct cognitive or experiential avoidance. Worry was classified as avoidance-oriented consistent with the contrast avoidance model, which conceptualizes worry as a means of avoiding abrupt shifts into more aversive emotional states (Newman et al., 2013; Newman & Llera, 2011). This approach-and avoidance-oriented classification is theoretically motivated instead of data-driven. Prior work provides some support for an engagement-avoidance distinction at the between-person level, but alternative structures have also been identified, particularly for rumination and worry, and within-person structures vary across samples (McMahon & Naragon-Gainey, 2019; Naragon-Gainey et al., 2017; Southward & Cheavens, 2020). We therefore tested alternative groupings and directly examined the structure of the 11 strategies in the sensitivity analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Temporal Context and ER Strategy Use

To examine how time of day and day of week related to approach-and avoidanceoriented ER strategy use, we fitted linear mixed-effects models (Brown, 2021; Luke, 2017) predicting approach-and avoidance-oriented ER strategy use from time of day (12 PM, 3 PM, 6 PM, 9 PM), day of week (weekday vs. weekend), and their interaction, adjusting for age, gender (man vs. not), and race (White vs. non-White). Fixed effects were evaluated using Type III tests with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (Keselman et al., 1999; P. Li & Redden, 2015). We estimated marginal means and planned contrasts (later prompts vs. 12 PM; weekend vs. weekday) and probed the time-of-day × day-of-week interaction using simple contrasts. Results were reported as model-derived means and contrasts with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Situational Context and ER Strategy Use

To identify naturally occurring situational profiles from the nine DIAMONDS + Controllability dimensions, we conducted multilevel latent profile analyses (MLPA; Henry & Muthén, 2010). MLPA simultaneously models within-person (momentary) situational profiles and between-person (individual-level) classes reflecting individuals' typical distribution of profiles across the study period.

Model selection proceeded in two stages. First, we conducted a standard latent profile analysis ignoring the multilevel structure to determine the optimal number of moment-level profiles (Henry & Muthén, 2010; Nieterau et al., 2025). We then estimated the full MLPA using Momentary situational profiles were then treated as categorical, time-varying predictors of ER strategy use, capturing state-level situational effects at each prompt. Individual-level classes, derived from the MLPA, were included as between-person predictors to capture traitlevel differences in typical situational exposure across the study period. Profiles and classes were assigned based on most-likely membership and then entered as observed predictors in separate linear mixed-effects models. ER strategy use was not modeled as a distal outcome within the mixture model, and classification uncertainty was therefore not propagated into the follow-up analyses. All models adjusted for race (White vs. non-White), age, and gender (man vs. not).

The Overlap Between Temporal and Situational Contexts

To address the third question, we compared models that included temporal predictors only against models that additionally adjusted for momentary situational context. Rather than adopting a causal mediation framework, which would require strong counterfactual assumptions (e.g., the absence of unmeasured time-varying confounding) that are difficult to justify in EMA data (particularly given that situational exposure reflects naturally occurring and categorical contexts rather than manipulable variables; Imai et al., 2010; Maxwell & Cole, 2007), we used an explanatory adjustment approach. This is a model comparison strategy that evaluates whether adding situational context as a covariate improves out-of-sample predictive performance and reduces estimated temporal contrasts, without making causal claims about the mechanism linking time and situation to ER strategy use (Shmueli, 2010).

Given that approach-and avoidance-oriented strategy scores are counts, outcome models were estimated using Bayesian multilevel Poisson regression via the brms package. Models were estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling with four chains and 10,000 iterations per chain, including 5,000 warm-up iterations. Model comparison was conducted using leave-oneout cross-validation (LOO-CV) to evaluate differences in expected log predictive density (ELPD), with higher ELPD indicating superior out-of-sample predictive performance (Vehtari et al., 2017). We additionally computed Bayesian R² to quantify the proportion of variance explained by each model (Vehtari et al., 2017). Improvements in predictive performance following inclusion of situational context were interpreted as evidence that situational exposure provided additional explanatory value beyond temporal factors. Convergence diagnostics (R-hat, effective sample sizes, and divergent transitions), posterior predictive checks, and Pareto-k diagnostics for the LOO-CV analyses are reported in the Supplemental Methods.

To assess attenuation of temporal effects, we compared time-of-day and weekend contrasts between the reduced (temporal-only) and full models. Adjusted contrasts held situational context constant by using the same profile distribution at every time point.

Specifically, predicted ER values for each profile were weighted by the overall observed profile distribution so that differences between time points reflect only the time coefficients. Weighting instead by the model-predicted profile distribution at each time point yields the overall marginal time contrast under the naturally occurring situational composition, because differences in the profile mix across time points are retained; we report this quantity in the Supplemental Materials for comparison (Table S6, Panel A). Because the Poisson models did not include time × profile interactions, the adjusted time contrast is represented directly by the full-model time coefficient on the log scale. We therefore report the reduced-and full-model coefficients as rate ratios for interpretability and quantify attenuation from the corresponding log-scale coefficients as 1 - (Table S6, Panel B; equations in the Supplemental Methods).

Contrasts were calculated relative to the 12 PM weekday prompt and summarized as posterior means with 95% credible intervals.

Sensitivity Analyses

Because the count indexes how many strategies were endorsed rather than whether any was, we also refit the temporal and situational models with a logit link to a binary outcome (1 = at least one strategy of that orientation endorsed). Additionally, given the alternative classifications identified in prior work, we repeated the temporal and situational models under several groupings, each addressing a contested, alternative placement: (1) approach-oriented strategies without savoring, because savoring engages with positive rather than challenging content and may differ from the other approach-oriented strategies (Bryant & Veroff, 2017); (2) avoidance-oriented strategies without positive refocusing and soothing, because both may also serve engagement-oriented functions (Southward & Cheavens, 2020) and ( 5) interpersonal (soothing and perspective seeking) and positive-focus (savoring and positive refocusing) groupings suggested by the factor analysis described below. In addition to these regroupings, we examined the structure of the 11 strategies directly using a multilevel exploratory factor analysis in Mplus. We estimated one-to four-factor solutions at the withinperson level and one-to three-factor solutions at the between-person level, with the other level unrestricted; the four-factor within-person solution was inadmissible (Supplemental Results).

DISCUSSION

The central finding of this study is that temporal patterns in regulatory behavior largely reflect the situations people encounter at different times of day: temporal and situational contexts overlap substantially, and much of the temporal patterning was accounted for by the changing composition of situations across the day and week. Three findings emerged. First, both approachand avoidance-oriented ER strategies declined from daytime to evening and were less frequently used on weekends than on weekdays, indicating that the number of strategies endorsed is temporally patterned. Second, ER strategy use differed across situational profiles. Relative to Structured-engagement contexts, approach-oriented strategies were less frequent in Lowengagement and Positivity-engagement contexts, whereas the difference in Negativityengagement contexts depended on savoring. Avoidance-oriented strategies were more frequent in Negativity-engagement and Low-engagement contexts and less frequent in Positivityengagement contexts, a pattern that remained across alternative strategy groupings. Third, after accounting for situational context, the 9 PM versus 12 PM differences were attenuated by approximately 45% to 66% and the weekend differences by approximately 53% to 79%, indicating substantial overlap between temporal patterning and the changing composition of situations across the day and week. A smaller evening decline in approach-oriented strategy use nevertheless remained after accounting for situational context.

Temporal Context and ER Strategy Use

Both approach-and avoidance-oriented strategy use declined from daytime to evening and was lower on weekends than on weekdays, consistent with prior evidence that affective and cognitive processes vary across the day (e.g., Bu et al., 2025; Owens et al., 2000). The decline concerned how many listed strategies were endorsed rather than whether any listed strategy of either orientation was endorsed: the probability of endorsing at least one strategy of either orientation did not differ by time of day or day of week, and the two orientations declined in parallel rather than one replacing the other. The decline also did not apply to every strategy: savoring and positive refocusing increased rather than decreased across the day and on weekends.

Additionally, rumination declined across the sampled period, in contrast to prior studies that have reported nonlinear diurnal patterns in ruminative self-focus, including increases later in the day (Moberly & Watkins, 2008; Takano & Tanno, 2011). Differences in temporal coverage may partly explain this discrepancy. The present assessments extended to 9 PM, whereas prior work sampled across a broader portion of participants' waking day and captured later-evening periods in which rumination may increase. Differences in measurement may also contribute: we assessed whether participants had used rumination in response to a salient situation during the preceding three hours, whereas prior work assessed the ruminative quality of ongoing thought at the moment of assessment. Temporal patterns in rumination may therefore depend on both the portion of the waking day sampled and the form of rumination being assessed, and sampling across a broader portion of the waking day would be needed to determine whether rumination increases later in the evening.

Two factors may contribute to this overall decline. First, cognitive and task-related demands may be more concentrated earlier in the day (Marchand et al., 2016; Owens et al., 2000; Ryan et al., 2010), such that later situations prompt fewer strategies. Weekend reductions in ER strategy use further support this idea: weekdays may impose greater task demands and interpersonal obligations that call for regulation, whereas weekends may involve less structured demands and situations that prompt fewer strategies (Marchand et al., 2016; Ryan et al., 2010).

The attenuation analysis is consistent with this explanation for a substantial part of the diurnal decline and of the weekend difference. Second, evening reductions may reflect regulatory fatigue (or mental fatigue), depletion of motivational and cognitive resources following sustained demands across the day (Herlambang et al., 2021; Lewczuk et al., 2022). However, fatigue, sleep, chronotype, and cognitive control were not directly assessed, and the sampling window did not extend into late-night hours. These possibilities therefore cannot be distinguished from other time-linked processes in the present data. Future research incorporating momentary measures of fatigue would help clarify the relative contributions of these processes. Differences in how specific forms of regulation relate to context emerge more clearly at the situational level, as discussed below.

Situational Context and ER Strategy Use

MLPA identified four psychologically meaningful situational profiles that were differentially associated with ER strategy use. In the primary classification, approach-oriented strategies were most frequent in Structured-engagement contexts, which were characterized by high Duty, pOsitivity, and Controllability and low Negativity, and were less frequent in Positivity-engagement, Low-engagement, and Negativity-engagement contexts. However, the sensitivity analyses showed that the association with Negativity-engagement contexts depended on savoring. Once savoring was removed, the remaining approach-oriented strategies (problem solving, planning, reappraisal, and perspective seeking), taken together, were more frequent in Negativity-engagement than in Structured-engagement contexts, whereas the reductions in Lowengagement and Positivity-engagement contexts remained. Among the individual strategies, this was driven mainly by problem solving. These findings suggest that the primary approachoriented composite brought together strategies that share an engagement orientation but respond differently to the content of the situation. Problem solving, planning, reappraisal, and perspective seeking involve acting on, modifying, or working through a situation and may therefore be used when situations make salient demands, including negative ones (Ford & Troy, 2019; Troy et al., 2013). Indeed, their greater use in Negativity-engagement contexts suggests that negative situational intensity did not reduce the use of these strategies in the present data, contrasting with experimental evidence that high emotional intensity can shift preferences away from reappraisal toward disengagement (Sheppes et al., 2011). Savoring, by contrast, involves engaging with positive experience and was concentrated in more positive situational contexts (Bryant & Veroff, 2017; Fredrickson, 2004). Thus, the motivational distinction between engaging with and disengaging from a situation captures only part of how strategies differed across contexts; the valence of what is being engaged with also appears important.

Avoidance-oriented strategies showed a more consistent situational pattern. They were more frequent in Negativity-engagement and Low-engagement contexts and less frequent in Positivity-engagement contexts relative to Structured-engagement contexts. The higher number of avoidance-oriented strategies endorsed in Negativity-engagement contexts is broadly consistent with evidence that disengagement-oriented responses become more likely as negative emotional intensity increases (Sheppes et al., 2011). This pattern did not depend on the placement of any single contested strategy. It became stronger when positive refocusing and soothing were removed from the avoidance-oriented grouping and was also evident when distraction and suppression and rumination and worry were examined separately. The situational association for avoidance-oriented strategy use therefore appears comparatively robust across alternative ways of organizing the strategies, although these findings concern the number of strategies endorsed rather than the likelihood of using any strategy of that orientation.

A broader pattern emerged across the sensitivity analyses and the multilevel exploratory factor analysis. Savoring and positive refocusing were concentrated in Positivity-engagement and Structured-engagement contexts, were relatively uncommon in Negativity-engagement and Low-engagement contexts, and formed a distinct positive-focus factor at both levels of the factor analysis. They also differed from most other strategies temporally, increasing rather than decreasing across the day. By comparison, the groupings that exclude savoring and positive refocusing, approach-and avoidance-oriented alike, were more frequent in Negativityengagement than in Positivity-engagement contexts. This suggests that strategy organization in daily life reflects not only whether regulation involves engagement or disengagement but also whether the strategy is directed toward positive or negative emotional content. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that strategies also cluster by the content they engage with, as in the separation of aversive cognitive perseveration from adaptive engagement (Naragon-Gainey et al., 2017), and with factor analyses in which savoring and social support separate from other strategies at the within-person level (McMahon & Naragon-Gainey, 2019; Southward & Cheavens, 2020). The approach-and avoidance-oriented composites therefore remain useful for organizing broad motivational tendencies, but their associations should be interpreted alongside the component strategies and the alternative groupings examined here, and none of these patterns implies that a particular strategy-context combination is more adaptive.

Between-person differences in stable situational exposure showed minimal associations with ER strategy use, consistent with prior work showing that most variance in strategy use occurs within rather than across individuals (Brans et al., 2013; English et al., 2017). The present findings therefore point more strongly to moment-to-moment situational variation than to stable differences in the types of situations people typically encounter. In this sense, momentary context is associated both with how many strategies are endorsed and with which strategies are used.

The Overlap Between Temporal and Situational Contexts

Incorporating situational context substantially improved model fit. Holding situational composition constant reduced the evening decline by about half for approach-oriented strategies and by about two-thirds for avoidance-oriented strategies, and reduced the weekend differences by roughly half to four-fifths. Temporal and situational contexts therefore overlap substantially rather than contributing fully independently. The descriptive data illustrate this overlap:

Negativity-engagement and Structured-engagement contexts became less common and Positivity-engagement contexts more common over the day and on weekends, and these shifts matter because the situational profiles differed systematically in the number and types of strategies endorsed. What remains after adjustment is a smaller evening decline in approachoriented strategies (rate ratio 0.93 at 9 PM) and no reliable residual difference for avoidanceoriented strategies or for weekends. The residual decline suggests that time-linked processes such as fatigue may contribute to approach-oriented regulation even after situational composition is taken into account, but the present design cannot identify them.

Together, these results speak to all three motivating questions raised before: the evening decline in approach-oriented strategies that remained after adjusting for situational composition is consistent with reports that emotional crises peak in the evening (Bu et al., 2025; Tubbs et al., 2022; Valtonen et al., 2006), although our sampling ended at 9 PM; the situational asymmetry between approach-and avoidance-oriented strategy use offers descriptive benchmarks that future work could use to evaluate what constitutes adaptive ER-context fit (Chen & Joormann, 2026); and the finding that avoidance-oriented strategies were most frequent in Negativity-engagement contexts and that approach-oriented strategies were fewest in Low-engagement contexts identifies the contexts where studies measuring regulatory need and outcomes should look first when evaluating when and where ER-focused support might matter (Southward et al., 2021).

Limitations and Future Directions

While our study provides important contributions, it also has limitations. Most fundamentally, the explanatory adjustment framework adopted here does not support causal inference (Maxwell & Cole, 2007). Situational characteristics and ER strategy use were assessed contemporaneously within the same EMA prompt, meaning we cannot establish that situational exposure temporally preceded strategy selection. Future research should employ designs that permit temporal ordering, such as lagged multilevel models or experimental situational manipulations, to better clarify the directionality of these associations. Second, we used four fixed EMA prompts per day, with recall windows spanning approximately 9 AM to 9 PM. This design may have missed rapid shifts in situational experience and emotional responses and did not capture early-morning or late-night periods. In addition, prompts were anchored to clock time, while wake time, chronotype, and work schedules were not assessed. As a result, the same prompt could occur at different points in the waking day for individuals with earlier versus later schedules, and the present design cannot distinguish clock-time patterns from variation associated with individual circadian timing. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as patterns across the sampled clock-time window rather than as a complete circadian course of ER strategy use. Future studies could use burst sampling, random-time prompting, or assessment schedules that extend across a broader portion of the waking day and incorporate wake time, chronotype, and work schedules so that temporal effects can also be examined relative to each person's daily rhythm (Ladis et al., 2023; Marciniak et al., 2024; Schricker et al., 2023). Third, the sample was not demographically representative, with a predominance of White and female participants. Future studies should replicate these findings in more diverse populations and across cultural contexts (Chen et al., 2025; Chen & Soto, 2024). Lastly, situational characteristics and ER strategies were assessed with single-item measures, which may limit psychometric precision. Individuals often engage in multiple ER strategies within a single situation or across time (Ford et al., 2019; Hartmann et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026) and they may also experience mixed emotions in response to a given situation (Larsen et al., 2001; Larsen & McGraw, 2014; S. M. Li et al., 2026). Future research should model within-person variability in ER strategy use and transitions between strategies over time and examine ER in the context of mixed emotional experiences.

Despite these limitations, the present findings offer important implications for both theory and future clinical research. Much of the ER flexibility literature has emphasized adaptive strategy selection in response to situational context, yet descriptive evidence regarding when and under what conditions strategies are actually used in daily life remains limited (Bonanno et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024). Because much of the temporal variation reflected shifts in situational exposure, with only a small residual evening decline, future models of ER flexibility may need to consider how the temporal structure of the day organizes the situations people encounter. The sensitivity and factor analyses further suggest that such models should consider the emotional content a strategy engages with, not only its motivational orientation.

These findings may also have clinical implications for the timing of ER assessment and intervention. The residual evening decline in approach-oriented strategy endorsement suggests that time of day may be relevant to how people regulate their emotions, even after accounting for situational context. However, the present study did not assess fatigue, regulatory goals or resources, strategy effectiveness, subsequent affect, distress, or impairment, so it cannot determine whether this temporal pattern reflects greater regulatory difficulty or clinical vulnerability later in the day. Future research should test whether these patterns are more pronounced in clinical populations and whether they correspond to periods of greater regulatory difficulty. Establishing these links would be necessary before using temporal patterns in ER strategy use to inform when ER-focused interventions are delivered.

Conclusion

This study provides initial evidence that the number of ER strategies people endorse in daily life varies with both temporal and situational context, and that these two sources of context overlap substantially. Participants endorsed fewer approach-and avoidance-oriented strategies later in the day and on weekends, whereas whether any strategy of either orientation was endorsed did not differ. Much of this decline reflected the situations people were in at those times. With situational composition held constant, the evening and weekend differences were smaller by about half or more, and only a small evening decline in approach-oriented strategies remained. Across situations, approach-oriented strategies were most common in Structuredengagement contexts and least common in Low-engagement contexts, whereas avoidanceoriented strategies were most common in Negativity-engagement contexts and least common in Positivity-engagement contexts. The avoidance-oriented pattern held across alternative strategy groupings, whereas the approach-oriented pattern depended on how savoring was grouped.

Together, these findings indicate that temporal patterns in ER strategy use cannot be interpreted without reference to the situations that occupy different times of day, and they provide descriptive benchmarks for future work on when and where people regulate.

Attenuation:

Rationale for the four-profile solution.

Single-level latent profile models with one to six profiles were estimated on the six situational characteristics (Table S2). Information criteria continued to decrease through the six-profile solution, as is common with large numbers of observations, so the number of profiles was chosen on parsimony, class size, and interpretability (Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018). The four-profile solution had entropy of .86 and a smallest profile of 19.9% of prompts, and each profile had a distinct configuration of high and low characteristics (Structured-engagement, Negativity-engagement, Low-engagement, Positivityengagement). The five-profile solution reproduced these four profiles and added an intermediate profile with all six characteristics near the scale midpoint (23.7% of prompts). The six-profile solution split that intermediate profile by valence into a mildly positive variant (pOsitivity near 5, Negativity near 2; 13.6%) and a mildly negative variant (pOsitivity near 2, Negativity near 4; 14.4%). Neither solution introduced a new configuration of high and low characteristics (Figure S2), so the four-profile solution was retained.
