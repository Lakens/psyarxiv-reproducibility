Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE) extends traditional psychoeducation for bipolar disorder by grounding clinical content in the neural mechanisms underlying the illness. This chapter reviews the neuroscience foundation relevant to bipolar disorder, including prefrontal-limbic circuit dysfunction, reward processing abnormalities that shift between mood states, circadian rhythm disruption, autonomic dysregulation, and the neuroplastic changes linked to illness progression and recovery. We translate these mechanisms into accessible psychoeducational content addressing diagnosis, pathogenesis, risk factors, and treatment, and describe brain-based strategies spanning cognitive monitoring, circadian-focused interventions, and emerging digital phenotyping tools. Delivery considerations are addressed with attention to the state-dependent nature of insight and cognitive function across mood episodes, phenotype-specific personalization, and family involvement. We outline mechanisms through which NIPE may improve outcomes, including enhanced awareness, reduced stigma, improved treatment adherence, and greater selfefficacy. While empirical evaluation of NIPE specifically for bipolar disorder remains limited, the chapter identifies priorities for future research, including randomized trials, mechanistic mediation analyses, and precision medicine approaches tailored to bipolar phenotype and illness course.

1. Introduction

Self-awareness represents a critical component of mental health, particularly in recovery (Law, Gee et al. 2020). For individuals with bipolar disorder (BD), achieving and maintaining self-awareness presents unique challenges that distinguish this condition from other psychiatric disorders. BD, characterized by recurrent episodes of mania or hypomania and depression, affects approximately 1-2% of the global population and is associated with substantial functional impairment, elevated suicide risk, and diminished quality of life (Merikangas, Jin et al. 2011) The challenges of impaired self-awareness in BD are multifaceted and statedependent. During manic episodes, grandiosity and diminished recognition of behavioral consequences preclude accurate self-appraisal (Ghaemi, Boiman et al. 2000). During depression, insight is distorted by negative cognitive biases and hopelessness (Dell'Osso, Pini et al. 2000). Even during euthymic periods, residual cognitive impairments can impact metacognitive abilities (Deckersbach, Lund et al. 2008).

Traditional psychoeducation has demonstrated efficacy in reducing relapse rates and improving medication adherence (Colom, Vieta et al. 2003 , Miklowitz, Price et al. 2012). However, conventional approaches may not provide the deeper understanding needed to navigate the illness's complexity. Neuroscienceinformed psychoeducation (NIPE) has emerged as an evolution of conventional psychoeducation, offering patients brain-based knowledge about their condition (Tara, Jamie et al. 2026). See Table 1 for a comparison between traditional psychoeducation vs neuroscience-informed psychoeducation in bipolar disorder.

Recent neuroscience research has elucidated key neural mechanisms underlying BD, including prefrontal-limbic circuit dysfunction, reward processing abnormalities, circadian rhythm disruptions, and autonomic nervous system imbalance (Liloia, Rocca et al. 2026 , Wang, Peng et al. 2026). Digital phenotyping studies using wearable technology have shown that physiological markers can predict mood episodes days to weeks in advance (Ortiz, Halabi et al. 2025 , Ortiz, Halabi et al. 2025).

This chapter provides a comprehensive examination of NIPE specifically designed for BD. We first discuss neuroscientific principles in the context of BD, followed by an exploration of tailored strategies, delivery considerations, efficacy mechanisms, and future research directions. By clarifying neural processes underlying BD and supporting development of personalized strategies, NIPE has potential to meaningfully enhance recovery trajectories.

2. The Principles of Neuroscience in BD 2.1 Neural Circuitry and Systems-Level Dysfunction BD is increasingly conceptualized as a disorder of neural circuits, with particular emphasis on networks involved in emotion regulation, reward processing, and circadian rhythm maintenance (Liloia, Rocca et al. 2026).

Explaining these circuit-level changes to patients gives a physical basis for symptoms that otherwise feel like personal failures of control, and it sets up the specific compensatory strategies discussed in Section 4.

Emotion Regulation Networks: The prefrontal cortex, particularly the ventrolateral and dorsolateral regions, exerts top-down regulatory control over the amygdala, which processes emotional salience. In BD, neuroimaging studies demonstrate reduced prefrontal activation and increased amygdala reactivity during emotional processing, suggesting impaired regulatory control (Steardo, D'Angelo et al. 2025). Structural imaging reveals reduced gray matter volume in prefrontal regions and altered white matter integrity, providing neuroanatomical substrate for these functional abnormalities (Steardo, D'Angelo et al. 2025). The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) integrates cognitive control signals with emotional information. In BD, both structural and functional ACC abnormalities contribute to difficulties in detecting and resolving emotional conflicts (Ogłodek, Vober et al. 2026).

Reward Processing Abnormalities: BD involves fundamental alterations in reward circuits involving the ventral striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex. Individuals show heightened striatal activation during manic phases, explaining increased goal-directed activity and risk-taking (Chen, Wang et al. 2022). During depression, reward circuit function diminishes, with reduced striatal response providing a neural explanation for anhedonia and amotivation (Chen, Wang et al. 2022). These cyclical alterations (i.e., oscillating between hyperactivation and hypoactivation) represent a core neurobiological feature distinguishing bipolar from unipolar depression.

Circadian Rhythm Dysregulation: The circadian system, orchestrated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, regulates 24-hour oscillations of sleep-wake cycles, hormones, and mood. Individuals with BD show alterations in clock gene expression, melatonin patterns, and circadian amplitude (Campbell, Frye et al. 2025). The relationship between circadian disruption and depressive and (hypo)manic episodes is bidirectional: mood episodes disrupt rhythms, while disruption triggers further mood dysregulation (Boivin 2000).

2.2 Neuroplasticity and BD

Neuroplasticity offers the clearest bridge between mechanism and hope in NIPE: the same capacity for change that drives illness progression also supports recovery, and this framing should anchor how the content below is delivered to patients.

The kindling hypothesis suggests repeated mood episodes lead to progressive neural changes lowering thresholds for subsequent episodes (Post 2007).

Clinically, episodes increase in frequency over time. At the neural level, kindling involves long-term potentiation-like mechanisms in emotion circuits. However, neuroplastic capacity offers opportunities for adaptive reorganization. Lithium enhances neuroplasticity by increasing gray matter volume, promoting neurogenesis, and protecting against excitotoxic damage (Machado-Vieira, Soeiro-De-Souza et al. 2014). Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), critical for synaptic plasticity, shows reduced levels during acute episodes with partial normalization during euthymia (Krasner, Ong et al. 2025). Interventions including exercise, sleep optimization, and stress reduction enhance BDNF expression (Hashimoto, Shimizu et al. 2004).

Neuroimaging studies have shown that brain network configurations change substantially between mood states (Chen, Wang et al. 2022). These statedependent reorganizations suggest BD involves dynamic shifts in which neural networks dominate processing, helping individuals recognize that thinking patterns during episodes reflect temporary configurations rather than permanent traits.

2.3 Autonomic Nervous System Dysregulation

Autonomic signals are also the most directly measurable of these systems, making them a practical entry point for the digital phenotyping strategies described in Section 4.3. Beyond central nervous system alterations, BD involves significant autonomic dysfunction. Heart rate variability (HRV), reflecting autonomic flexibility, is reduced in BD throughout different phases of the illness, including euthymia (Faurholt-Jepsen, Brage et al. 2017 , Faurholt-Jepsen, Kessing et al. 2017 , Ortiz, Bradler et al. 2021). Individuals with a history of suicide attempts show substantially greater autonomic dysregulation (Ortiz, Park et al. 2024).

The autonomic nervous system connects bidirectionally with emotion regulation brain regions through pathways like the vagus nerve. In BD, this coupling may become dysregulated, contributing to a disconnect between physiological arousal and subjective experience. During mania, increased sympathetic activation with reduced vagal tone reflects heightened arousal (Gullett, Zajkowska et al. 2023).

3.1 Diagnosis and Symptom Recognition

Translating diagnostic criteria into brain-based language changes how symptoms are experienced, replacing self-blame with a mechanistic explanation patients can act on. Brain-Based Explanations: Manic episodes can be explained as states where reward circuits become hyperactivated, dopamine signaling increases, and prefrontal regulatory control diminishes (Chen, Wang et al. 2022). As a result, symptoms such as elevated mood arise from reward system overactivation; racing thoughts from accelerated processing with reduced filtering; impulsivity from weakened prefrontal inhibition; and reduced need for sleep from arousal system override. A useful metaphor compares the manic brain to a car with stuck accelerator and failing brakes: the reward system overactivates while prefrontal cortex cannot effectively slow things down. Conversely, depressive episodes involve hypoactive reward systems reducing pleasure capacity, reduced prefrontal cortex activity contributing to concentration difficulties, and overactive regions processing negative emotion fueling rumination (Steardo, D'Angelo et al. 2025). NIPE should emphasize that during depression, the brain has a negativity bias coloring perceptions, which is a cognitive distortion arising from altered neural processing.

Mixed Episodes: Mixed states, simultaneous depressive and manic symptoms, can be conceptualized as simultaneous activation of typically opposing systems: reward circuits may be hyperactive (producing agitation) while pleasure circuits are hypoactive (producing anhedonia). NIPE content should validate these experiences as reflecting genuine neural complexity and note that mixed states carry particularly elevated suicide risk (Bartoli, Crocamo et al. 2020).

Cognitive Symptoms: Cognitive impairments affecting attention, memory, and executive functions often persist during euthymia (Hussain, Suraweera et al. 2026). These reflect the same neural disruptions producing mood symptoms (e.g., prefrontal dysfunction, hippocampal abnormalities). Understanding cognitive difficulties as core features rather than mood byproducts reduces self-blame.

3.2 Pathogenesis and Illness Mechanisms

Understanding what drives bipolar disorder at the biological level gives patients a coherent account of why symptoms emerge and recur, rather than a list of unrelated facts. The mechanisms below (i.e., neurotransmitter dysregulation, inflammation, circadian disruption, and kindling) interact and reinforce one another, and each offers a natural entry point for explaining why a specific treatment or lifestyle change matters.

Neurotransmitter Systems: Dopamine shows state-dependent alterations with hyperactivity during mania and hypoactivity during depression (Trouillas, Claustrat et al. 2025). Serotonin dysfunction contributes to impulsivity and depressive symptoms. Norepinephrine regulates arousal, elevated during mania and reduced during depression. For NIPE, neurotransmitters can be explained as chemical messengers enabling brain cell communication: when these systems become imbalanced, symptoms emerge.

Inflammation in BD involves elevated inflammatory markers during acute episodes (Fiori, Lombardo et al. 2026). Neuroinflammation may disrupt neural signaling, impair neuroplasticity, and damage cellular components. Factors known to influence course also affect inflammation: stress increases it, while exercise, sleep, and healthy diet reduce it, providing a mechanistic explanation for lifestyle intervention benefits.

Circadian Biology: Humans evolved to live synchronized with 24-hour lightdark cycles. In BD, clock systems become dysregulated; and sleep architecture is disrupted during both depressive and manic episodes (Geoffroy and Maruani 2025, Fiori, Lombardo et al. 2026). Even during euthymia, sleep abnormalities are present in BD. NIPE should emphasize that maintaining regular sleep-wake schedules is neurobiologically necessary for mood stability.

Kindling Processes: Each mood episode may produce neuroplastic changes increasing future vulnerability (Post 2007). This has critical implications: early intervention becomes especially important because preventing episodes may prevent progressive neural changes.

3.3 Risk Factors and Treatment Mechanisms

Explaining risk and treatment mechanisms together helps patients see that vulnerability is not destiny: the same genetic and stress-related pathways that increase risk are also the targets treatment, including lithium, works to correct. (Ochsner and Gross 2005).

Understanding that thoughts during episodes are products of altered neural processing rather than accurate reality creates psychological distance (e.g., "this is the illness talking" rather than "I am worthless").

Prospective Thinking: Developing individualized strategies, including prodromal symptoms, triggers, and tools to use if/when symptoms start to emerge, engages future-oriented cognition systems. Creating relapse prevention plans during euthymia allows prefrontal executive functions to guide future behavior, even when compromised during episodes.

4.2 Circadian-Focused Interventions

Because circadian disruption is both a trigger and a consequence of mood episodes, interventions that stabilize daily rhythms address the illness at its mechanistic root rather than only managing symptoms after they appear. Social Rhythm Regularity: Regular mealtimes, exercise timing, and social interactions influence circadian systems. IPSRT emphasizes social rhythm regularity based on evidence that social rhythms entrain biological rhythms (Frank 2007). Maintaining regularity may be challenging during transitions, but understanding its neurobiological importance motivates routine preservation. 4.3 Early Warning Signs and Digital Phenotyping:

These strategies extend the autonomic mechanisms discussed in Section 2.3 into daily practice, turning physiological signals patients cannot consciously access into actionable information.

Consumer wearables continuously track sleep, heart rate, HRV, and activity. In BD, analyzing these data streams may reveal patterns predictive of mood changes (Halabi 2026). NIPE should explain that recent studies using wearable technology have shown that changes in sleep and activity arise earlier than changes in mood, prior to a depressive or hypomanic episode (Ortiz, Halabi et al. 2025 , Ortiz, Halabi et al. 2025). While still under development, using these algorithms will help by learning personal baselines and detecting deviations before a full-blown episode.

5. NIPE Structure and Delivery for BD

Rezapour, Berry, Cullum, and colleagues (2026) identify seven structural elements that determine how effectively NIPE content is processed and retained: salience, trustworthiness, intensity, repetition, motivation, multi-media delivery, and interaction. The delivery considerations below for bipolar disorder instantiate these elements within a population defined by state-dependent cognitive function.

Timing decisions primarily govern intensity and motivation, presenting content when cognitive load can be tolerated and when the reward system is not already destabilized by mood state. Phenotype-based personalization governs salience, ensuring content is self-relevant to the individual's specific illness course. Multimodal and digital delivery address multi-media delivery and interaction, while family involvement extends trustworthiness beyond the individual to the caregiving system.

5.1 Timing Considerations

During acute manic episodes, impaired judgment, distractibility, and diminished illness awareness preclude meaningful engagement with educational content. During severe depression, cognitive slowing and impaired concentration compromise information processing. NIPE delivery should ideally occur during euthymic periods when cognitive function and insight are optimally available.

A staged approach aligns content with illness phases. Upon diagnosis, basic information about illness and treatment is appropriate. Following acute episode resolution represents an ideal window for comprehensive NIPE, as motivation is high due to recent episode, but mood stability allows effective processing. During euthymia, NIPE can focus on advanced topics including relapse prevention.

Studies have shown that digital phenotyping can be done during any time of the illness, including active episodes (Ortiz, Park et al. 2023). Euthymia is optimal for comprehensive NIPE delivery when cognitive function approaches pre-illness baseline (Bourne, Aydemir et al. 2013).

5.3 Family Involvement

Including family in NIPE creates shared framework for understanding, improving functioning and support quality (Miklowitz, Price et al. 2012, Miklowitz and Gitlin 2025). When families understand mood episodes reflect neural dysregulation rather than character flaws, it reduces blame (Ahmed, Pilegaard et al. 2025). Educating families about early warning signs empowers non-confrontational concern-raising; and understanding treatment mechanisms helps families support medication adherence. However, family involvement must be balanced with respect for individual autonomy and privacy. 6. Mechanisms of NIPE Efficacy in BD NIPE may improve outcomes through multiple mechanisms: -Mechanistic understanding creates more robust illness awareness, less vulnerable to mood-state distortion (e.g., "when sleep drops below 5 hours for three nights, mood regulation systems destabilize" provides an objective framework resistant to cognitive biases and helps to identify tools needed to modify behavior).

-Reducing negative attitudes about psychiatric illness and self-deprecation, positioning BD as a brain-based medical condition comparable to diabetes rather than a character flaw (Sarı, Şengün İnan et al. 2025).

-Improving adherence through mechanistic rationale extends beyond symptom suppression. For instance, understanding that lithium promotes neuroplasticity and protects neurons can motivate sustained use. For individuals questioning medication need during wellness, explaining that euthymia reflects successful treatment is valuable.

-Transforming episodes from mysterious events to understandable phenomena with identifiable triggers and prodromal signs builds agency.

Knowledge enables better understanding of triggers, which enables preparation. However, NIPE must balance agency emphasis with recognition that despite best efforts, episodes sometimes occur. At the same time, understanding effective treatments exist reduces catastrophic fears about episode consequences.

-Fostering realistic expectations, which involves providing accurate information about illness course. Neuroplasticity content supports optimism (e.g., recovery is possible, treatments promote beneficial brain changes). However, NIPE should acknowledge BD is a chronic illness that requires ongoing management. This balanced approach promotes sustained engagement necessary for long-term recovery.

7. Evidence Base and Future Directions

Traditional psychoeducation for BD has substantial support. A recent study in over 2800 patients with BD found psychoeducation significantly reduced relapses (OR 0.57) (Joas, Bäckman et al. 2020). While NIPE for any psychiatric disorder has received less attention (Rezapour, McLean et al. 2025), to our knowledge, this is the first review on the topic. One preliminary study found significant increases in neuroscience knowledge and modest reductions in negative attitudes (Arbuckle, Ferreira et al. 2024)

Genetic and Epigenetic Factors: BD has strong genetic component (heritability 60-85%), but genes influence vulnerability rather than deterministically causing illness (O'Connell, Koromina et al. 2025). Epigenetics (i.e., changes in gene expression) bridges genetic vulnerability and environmental influences. For instance, substance use can produce epigenetic modifications altering brain function (Post 2016). Importantly, epigenetic changes are potentially reversible, explaining how interventions modify illness course. Stress and HPA Axis: The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis mediates stress responses through cortisol secretion. Chronic stress produces sustained activation with elevated cortisol, damaging the hippocampus, impairing neuroplasticity, and disrupting sleep (Krasner, Ong et al. 2025). Stressful events frequently precede mood episodes, suggesting stress triggers neural cascades leading to destabilization. Lithium's Neuroprotective Effects: Lithium increases gray matter volume in prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, promotes hippocampal neurogenesis, and enhances BDNF expression (Machado-Vieira 2018). It modulates intracellular signaling affecting circadian rhythms and cellular resilience; and demonstrates anti-inflammatory properties. NIPE should emphasize lithium is not merely suppressing symptoms but actively supporting brain health.Psychotherapy: Effective psychotherapy produces measurable brain changes. CBT increases prefrontal activation and reduces limbic hyperactivity, changes mirroring some aspects of successful pharmacotherapy(Yalin, Yildiz et al. 2026). A specific type of therapy focused on social rhythms (Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy, IPSRT) works partly by stabilizing circadian rhythms through regular routines(Frank 2007, Geoffroy andPalagini 2021). Familyfocused therapy may reduce stress and HPA axis activation(Miklowitz and Gitlin 2025). 4. NIPE should explain that monitoring makes implicit neural states explicit, and available to prefrontal cortex regulation. It also helps patients to associate certain behaviors (e.g., exercise) with improvements in mood and energy; and others (e.g., drinking alcohol) with alterations in sleep. Critical research gaps include a lack of RCTs comparing neuroscience-informed versus standard psychoeducation; a lack of research identifying which NIPE components are most effective; uncharacterized individual differences in responsiveness; a lack of studies on delivery parameters; and insufficient longterm outcome data. Future strategies should include precision medicine approaches, involving phenotype-based personalization, tailoring content to clinical presentations, biomarker-informed NIPE incorporating neuroimaging or genetic findings, learning style adaptation, and cultural adaptation. Conclusion Neuroscience-informed psychoeducation represents a promising evolution of traditional psychoeducational approaches for BD, grounded in contemporary understanding of neural circuitry, neuroplasticity, autonomic function, and neurochemistry. The neuroscience foundation is robust: prefrontal-limbic circuit dysfunction, reward processing abnormalities oscillating across mood states, circadian rhythm disruptions destabilizing neural function, and autonomic dysregulation reflected in altered HRV patterns. Understanding neuroplasticity mechanisms, both maladaptive changes contributing to progression and adaptive changes supporting recovery, provides framework for explaining treatment effects and motivating sustained management. NIPE content should address diagnosis, pathogenesis, risk factors, treatment mechanisms, prognosis, and prevention. Neuroscience principles can be translated into accessible language and meaningful metaphors conveying mechanistic understanding without requiring technical expertise. Timing considerations are critical given state-dependent cognitive function and insight variations. Euthymic phases represent optimal windows for comprehensive delivery. Multi-modal delivery through individual sessions, groups, and digital platforms maximizes accessibility. Family involvement creates shared understanding, though must balance with individual autonomy. Efficacy mechanisms likely involve enhancing metacognitive awareness through mechanistic understanding; reducing negative attitudes and bias through biological explanations emphasizing neuroplasticity; improving adherence by clarifying medication mechanisms; building agency through knowledge; fostering realistic expectations; and reducing anxiety by transforming unpredictable episodes into understandable phenomena. While empirical evidence specifically evaluating NIPE for BD remains limited, promising directions include integrating digital health technologies for real-time monitoring, developing precision medicine approaches that personalize content to phenotype and biomarkers, conducting rigorous RCTs with mediation and moderation analyses, implementing qualitative research, and pursuing implementation science to enable real-world translation. NIPE transforms BD from an unpredictable, complex disorder into an understandable brain-based condition with identifiable mechanisms, measurable early warning signals, and actionable intervention strategies, thus empowering individuals to move from passive symptom endurance to active, informed selfmanagement grounded in contemporary understanding of neural circuitry, neuroplasticity, and autonomic function. By effectively providing mechanistic knowledge about BD, NIPE can enhance illness awareness, treatment adherence, and self-efficacy, and improve clinical outcomes.
