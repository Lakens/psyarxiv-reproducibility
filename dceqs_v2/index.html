How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

1. Introduction

Meta-analytic structural equation modeling (MASEM) is a powerful method for research synthesis that combines the cumulative empirical work of meta-analysis with the theory-testing capabilities of structural equation modeling (Cheung & Chan, 2005; Cheung & Cheung, 2016; Jak, 2015; Jak & Cheung, 2020). By synthesizing correlation or covariance matrices across studies, MASEM can be used to evaluate measurement models, including the dimensionality of instruments and measurement invariance across groups, as well as structural models, including mediating pathways, predictive relations, and competing theoretical accounts. In psychometric research, MASEM has become an established tool for evaluating the factor structure, reliability, and cross-cultural generalizability of widely used instruments such as the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (Gnambs et al., 2018), the International Trauma Questionnaire (Kindred et al., 2026), the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (Wedderhoff et al., 2021), and the Self-Compassion Scale (Bourke, 2026). Other studies have used MASEM to examine the measurement precision of creativity tests (Acar et al., 2024) or attitudinal and motivational aspects of students' academic achievement (López-López et al., 2025). More broadly, MASEM has also been increasingly applied to synthesize substantive theory-driven models, such as technology acceptance in education (Scherer et al., 2019), models of the structure of human cognition (Emslander & Scherer, 2022), and the identification of mediating processes (Duzen et al., 2026), including mechanisms underlying intervention effects (Vints et al., 2025). These applications demonstrate the versatility of MASEM as a research synthesis method across fields to address a broad range of research questions.

1.1 Direct and Indirect Data Sources in MASEM

The empirical input for MASEM consists of study-level correlation or covariance matrices among the observed variables included in the measurement or structural model. Ideally, this information is obtained from the primary data themselves, and the increasing availability of openly shared raw data has made such analyses much more feasible in recent years (e.g., Campos & Scherer, 2024; Schroeders et al., 2024). However, data protection concerns sometimes preclude unrestricted data sharing. In these cases, researchers must rely on summary statistics reported in primary studies. These summary statistics can provide direct information, such as correlations or covariances reported in text, tables, or supplements, or indirect information, such as factor loadings and factor correlations from which the implied correlation matrix can be reconstructed under specific model assumptions.

Direct information is preferable because it corresponds most closely to the empirical input required for MASEM. Yet complete correlation matrices are often omitted from the main text because of space constraints and are not consistently provided in online supplements. This is particularly problematic for psychometric applications, where item-level matrices may involve 20 or more variables per instrument (e.g., Schroeders et al., 2024; Wedderhoff et al., 2021).

Consequently, researchers often turn to indirect sources, most notably factor-loading matrices and factor correlations. These quantities are more commonly reported than complete item-level correlation matrices and can approximate the original matrix when the underlying factor model fits adequately (Gnambs & Staufenbiel, 2016). Thus, aggregate-data MASEM often requires extracting either directly reported statistics or model-based quantities used for matrix reconstruction. This extraction process is labor-intensive, error-prone, and complicated by the lack of standardized reporting formats.

1.2 AI-Assisted Data Extraction in Research Synthesis

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) have created new opportunities for extracting structured information from weakly standardized scientific texts (e.g., PDF articles). In meta-analytic research in the social and behavioral sciences, automated extraction was used early on, for example, to retrieve p-values, sample sizes, and hypothesis counts from full-text articles (Nuijten et al., 2016; Teja Lanka et al., 2021).

Recent work in evidence synthesis has focused on LLM-assisted data extraction for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (see Fütterer et al., 2026, for a review). For example, Gartlehner et al. (2024) demonstrated that data elements from randomized controlled trials can be extracted with high accuracy relative to humans, while Konet et al. (2024) showed that performance depends not only on the model, but also on PDF parsing and the quality of the text made available to the model. Broader evaluations of GPT-4 for systematic-review tasks similarly indicate that LLMs can support screening and data extraction across publication types and languages, but that performance remains task-dependent (Khraisha et al., 2024).

Recent benchmarks provide a nuanced picture by comparing LLM performance with human extraction or expert-adjudicated gold standards. In a second-order meta-analysis context, Jansen et al. (2026) compared three LLMs with two human experts for extracting effect sizes, study counts, and participant counts from educational meta-analyses. Against an expertadjudicated benchmark, the LLMs reached intraclass correlations comparable to those of the human experts, although their exact agreement was somewhat lower. Similarly, Li et al. (2026) evaluated several LLMs for extracting statistical results, risk-of-bias assessments, and studylevel characteristics from randomized controlled trials and found high precision but consistently lower recall because models omitted relevant information. Customized prompts helped improve recall, but did not eliminate this limitation completely. Kataoka et al. (2026) also reported useful but imperfect performance for automated extraction systems based on GPT-4o and o3, with stronger performance for detecting relevant variables than for accurately extracting all numeric information. These findings suggest that LLMs can be competitive with human raters for welldefined, extractive tasks, but that their performance is uneven across information types and that omissions remain a recurrent risk. Taken together, these findings indicate that LLM-based extraction is not yet a fully autonomous process, but depends on human oversight and verification (Khraisha et al., 2024).

Extraction quality depends not only on the model, but also on how the source document is represented. Text-based pipelines are efficient when machine-readable PDFs can be reliably parsed into clean text, tables, and metadata, thereby supporting chunking, retrieval, and source linking. Vision-based processing can be useful when relevant information depends on layout, scanned pages, complex tables, figures, or visually encoded content. Recent surveys of document parsing describe a broader shift from modular pipelines based on optical character recognition (OCR), layout detection, and rules toward more unified systems based on large VLMs, especially for visually rich or structurally complex documents (Ke et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). For MASEM, this matters because the required information is often distributed across tables, text, table columns, and notes, including correlation matrices, factor loadings, factor correlations, and descriptive study information. Accordingly, the aim of this Software Focus is to introduce MASEMiner and evaluate its extraction and verification workflow for information required in MASEM.

2. MASEMiner Workflow

Recent evaluations of LLMs and VLMs for evidence synthesis suggest that these systems are best understood not as replacements for expert coding, but as components of a transparent, human-in-the-loop workflow that combines automated extraction with human oversight, verification, and transparent reporting (Gartlehner et al., 2024; Khraisha et al., 2024; Konet et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). Because LLM performance depends strongly on task specification and prompt configuration, specialized workflows are necessary for reliable AI-assisted evidence synthesis rather than generic one-size-fits-all solutions (Adam et al., 2026). MASEMiner provides such a workflow that is specifically designed for extracting information relevant to MASEM. It integrates AI-assisted extraction with subsequent human verification based on the original sources. The software is open-source and can be run locally or accessed through a hosted web application. The source code, installation instructions, and technical documentation are available on GitHub (https://github.com/johanna-einsiedler/maseminer). A hosted browser-based version is available at https://paper.metalens.tech/maseminer, which also includes a short introductory video. The MASEMiner extraction and verification workflow consists of four consecutive steps: (1) model configuration, (2) prompt specification, (3) document upload and extraction, and (4) human verification (see Figure 1). 2.1 Model Configuration

In the first step, users configure the AI model used for extraction. Specifically, they select a provider (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or DeepSeek), choose a specific model version (e.g., GPT-5.6 Luna/Terra/Sol), and enter the corresponding API key. Alternatively, users can connect any model behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, including locally hosted or selfhosted models served with a public address through vLLM, Ollama, or LM Studio. For these custom models, users provide the server URL, the model name or Hugging Face model ID, and an API key placeholder if required by the interface; additional setup details are provided in the online help. For optimal extraction performance, we recommend using the most capable currently available model from the selected provider.

Data privacy depends on where MASEMiner runs and where the model is hosted. In the hosted version, the API key is transmitted only to the selected provider and is not stored in any database 1 . Running MASEMiner locally avoids submitting an API key to an external web server entirely, but the content of the documents is still transmitted to the selected external model provider when a cloud API is used. For workflows that must keep documents within a local computing environment, users can combine a local MASEMiner installation with a self-hosted model with an OpenAI-compatible endpoint as described above. In either case, users should consult the data-retention and privacy policies of their model provider and make sure that uploading copyrighted, confidential, or unpublished material is permitted under their institutional requirements. 1 The API key passes through the server only to run the extraction and is retained in the server memory and job queue for at most 30 minutes. The interface states this explicitly where the API key is entered ("Your API key is not stored: it passes through our server only to run the extraction (in memory and the job queue for at most 30 minutes) and is then discarded.").

2.2 Prompt Specification

In the second step, users specify the extraction task by providing the task-specific information (e.g., target variables). MASEMiner then incorporates this information into a predefined prompt template (see Figure 1, blue-highlighted passages), alongside general extraction logic, the output schema, and review-related instructions. MASEMiner supports two task types, each implemented through a parameterized prompt template tailored to MASEMspecific data structures. The direct-information task is designed to extract correlations and related effect sizes (e.g., odds ratios) that are reported directly, either in the text or in tables.

MASEMiner extracts reported statistics in their source metric. Any effect-size transformation or harmonization required for the subsequent synthesis is performed downstream and remains part of the analyst's meta-analytic workflow. For this task type, users provide canonical variable labels that define the target variables in the correlation matrix that will be subjected to MASEM.

The template uses these labels to map construct-equivalent terms in the article to common matrix cells. In addition to correlations, MASEMiner extracts scale reliabilities and sample characteristics, such as sample size, percentage of female respondents, and mean age, with links to the corresponding source evidence.

The indirect-information task is used to extract factor loadings and factor correlations from factor-analytic results to later reconstruct study-specific correlation matrices. For this task type, users provide the measure's name and abbreviation, the number of items, and the item wording or item labels. The template specifies how items, factors, samples, and factor-analytic solutions are represented in structured loading and factor-correlation matrices. It also includes decision rules for handling multiple samples or competing factor solutions, preserving item order, distinguishing latent factor correlations from observed subscale correlations, and marking unavailable or ambiguous values. In addition to the psychometric information, MASEMiner extracts sample characteristics (e.g., sample size, percentage of female respondents, and mean age), as well as analytic information (e.g., type of factor analysis and number of retained factors).

The user-supplied information is then inserted into the corresponding prompt template (Figure 1, Step 2). Beyond formatting this input, the template adds four components that support extraction accuracy and the human-in-the-loop review workflow. First, the template lists output requirements that define extraction rules identified during pilot testing to improve accuracy (e.g., unreported values must not be imputed, and numeric values must be reported as pre-computed scalars rather than arithmetic expressions). Second, the template specifies a fixed JSON response schema, which the interactive viewer parses to populate the review interface. Third, it includes instructions for providing an AI-assessed confidence rating for each extraction target (e.g., factor loadings, factor correlations, sample metadata), reported on a three-level ordinal scale (low, medium, high). Fourth, it instructs the model to return an evidence snippet for each extracted value, which is displayed by the review interface alongside the corresponding value to enable rapid manual verification. In addition to these guided prompt configurations, users can review the generated prompt (Figure 1, Step 2B) and edit the raw prompt to implement study-specific extraction rules (e.g., prioritize certain factor solutions or adapt the output schema).

2.3 Document Upload and Extraction

In the third step, users upload one or more PDF files for extraction. Before extraction begins, MASEMiner identifies whether each uploaded PDF contains a usable text layer or is a scanned document. If the PDF contains a usable text layer, users can choose between text-based and vision-based extraction. In text-extraction mode, the PDF text layer is parsed as Markdown (using PyMuPDF, 2026) and then sent to an LLM, which is usually faster and less expensive but depends on the quality and completeness of the native PDF text layer. In vision mode, PDF pages are rendered as images (again using PyMuPDF, 2026) and sent to the selected multimodal model; this mode is slower and more expensive but can process scanned PDFs and visually structured tables. In both modes, the model receives the prompt together with the document content, with each page explicitly labeled by its sequential PDF position so that evidence citations resolve back to the correct page. If there is no text layer, extraction requires a visioncapable model. Conversely, if the selected model does not support multimodal input, extraction is only possible when the PDF can be converted via its text layer.

3. Evaluation Design

To evaluate the extraction accuracy of MASEMiner, we used four meta-analytic databases from previous research that provided human-coded reference data. These databases were selected to cover two task types central to MASEM. First, we considered direct information, that is, statistical information explicitly reported in text, tables, or matrices, such as zero-order correlations and related effect sizes. Second, we considered indirect information, that is, information that must be reconstructed from psychometric analyses reported in primary studies, such as factor loading patterns, factor correlations, and factor-analytic metadata. All extractions were conducted with the most recent OpenAI model available at the time of evaluation (GPT-5.6 Sol, accessed September 2026). The prompts used for the extractions, the exact JSON output, the reference databases, and the evaluation scripts are available in the online repository.

The four use cases were selected because each provided a suitable benchmark based on detailed, human-coded extraction protocols. For each information type, we drew on two published meta-analytic databases and sampled a subset of primary studies (k = 10). For each information type, the studies sampled from one database were used during prompt development, whereas the studies sampled from the second database were kept separate and used as a held-out

3.1 Analytical Procedure for Evaluation

Our extraction tasks differed from standard binary classification, since the number of potentially extractable values was not fixed in advance for several of the targets. For example, a correlation-matrix cell for a given sample may contain more than one eligible effect size (e.g., measured with different instruments). Similarly, the number of factor loadings and factor correlations depends on the number of factors identified in a given sample. We therefore evaluated performance using metrics that do not require a predefined set of true negatives (e.g., specificity or conventional cell-level accuracy).

Instead, we evaluated extraction performance against a reference database for each use case: the values coded in the original meta-analysis were double-checked against the primary publications for the present study. Each comparison yields four counts (Table 1). A true positive (TP) is a reference value that was correctly recovered by the LLM. Three conceptually distinct types of errors were differentiated: (1) omissions (OMIT), where the LLM fails to report a value contained in the reference database; (2) overextractions (OVER), where the LLM reports a value for which no reference value exists (e.g., a correlation for a variable pair or a sample that was not a target of the meta-analysis); and (3) wrong values (WRONG), where the LLM reports a value that does not match the reference value. We did not distinguish between wrong-sample and wrong-variable assignments as separate categories, since they are already contained in these counts (e.g., a loading assigned to a wrong factor is counted as two wrong values). Empirically, such misassignments were practically non-existent (6 of 2,397 target values, or 0.25%, all due to three items of one publication that were assigned to a neighboring factor).

These counts map onto false negatives (FN) and false positives (FP) as follows: an omission is a false negative, an overextraction is a false positive, and a wrong value is counted as both-a false positive, because it is an erroneous output, and a false negative, because the true value was not recovered (FN = OMIT + WRONG, FP = OVER + WRONG). A wrong value is therefore penalized twice. We adopted this conservative scoring deliberately, since omissions and overextractions are relatively easy for a human reviewer to spot, whereas wrong values are not immediately obvious and thus an error is more likely to pass unnoticed into the synthesis.

Based on these counts, we computed sensitivity (recall), precision, and Jaccard overlap (Tharwat, 2021); Tables 2 and 3 report these metrics together with the absolute numbers of omissions, overextractions, and wrong values, so that readers can judge the verification burden behind each index. Because multiple extracted values are nested within the same publication and are therefore not independent, the pooled indices should not be interpreted as independent tests of the extraction system. We therefore additionally computed SEN, PRE, and JAC separately for each primary publication; these publication-level results are reported in the online supplement (Tables S1-S4) to show between-document variability in extraction performance.

3.2 Direct Information: Reported Correlations and Other Effect Sizes

The first direct-information use case was based on Gnambs (2013), which served as a prompt-development case. The original meta-analysis examined correlations among self-and other ratings of the Big Five personality traits from a multitrait-multi-informant perspective. For this purpose, the study reconstructed a 10 × 10 correlation matrix comprising the five Big Five traits assessed by self-ratings and peer ratings and conducted separate meta-analyses for the unique correlations in this matrix. This use case was well suited for developing the directinformation extraction prompt because the relevant information consisted of explicitly reported zero-order correlations, but these values had to be selected from a complex literature base in which studies could report multiple samples, multiple instruments, subgroup results, reliabilities, and overlapping correlations. A random sample of 10 primary studies included in the metaanalysis was therefore used to refine the prompt rules for identifying eligible correlations, distinguishing target from non-target effects, and extracting associated study and sample information.

The second direct-information use case was based on Marker et al. (2022) and served as the independent evaluation case. This meta-analysis examined the association between sedentary video gaming and body mass and additionally tested the mediating role of physical activity using MASEM. In contrast to the first use case, this database was not used to develop or revise the prompt. It was included to evaluate whether the generic direct-information extraction prompt generalized to a new substantive domain and a different type of effect-size database. The target information of 10 randomly selected primary studies included zero-order associations between video gaming and body mass as well as additional associations involving physical activity, together with study-level and sample-level information (e.g., sample size). This use case was demanding because the primary studies differed in the operationalization of video gaming and body mass, sometimes reported multiple effect sizes per sample, and provided different eligible statistics (i.e., correlations, odds ratios) for the meta-analysis.

3.3 Indirect Information: Factor-Analytic and Psychometric Model Information

For the first indirect-information use case, we drew on the database from the metaanalysis by Schroeders et al. (2022), which synthesized factor-analytic evidence on the dimensionality of the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (Bagby et al., 1994). A random sample of 10 primary studies was used for prompt development. For the TAS-20 prompt-development set, very long full-text documents were excluded to avoid costs. In contrast to direct effect-size extraction, the task was to extract factor loadings and factor correlations needed to reconstruct the correlation matrix, along with sample characteristics. This use case was well suited for developing the extraction prompt because it required rules for selecting the relevant multifactor solution, mapping item-by-factor loading matrices regardless of their order in the source, and distinguishing substantive factor correlations from other reported associations (e.g., sum score correlations).

The second indirect-information use case was based on the NCS-18 database from Schroeders et al. (2024) and served as the independent evaluation case. This meta-analysis examined the structure of the 18-item Need for Cognition Scale (Cacioppo et al., 1984). A random sample of 10 primary studies was used to test whether the generic indirect-information prompt generalized to a different scale and psychometric context. The target information included the indirect psychometric information required for matrix reconstruction and sample characteristics. Thus, the NCS-18 case tested whether the prompt could be transferred from one factor-analytic extraction task to another without scale-specific revisions.

3.4 Human Verification

To evaluate the complete human-in-the-loop workflow, four coders (student research assistants trained in meta-analytic coding) verified the MASEMiner output in the interface and, where necessary, corrected, deleted, or added values. Each use case was verified by two coders; the two other coders extracted the same publications conventionally into a coding sheet mainly to assess coding time needed per publication.

For the MASEMiner workflow, the time for uploading the documents and running the automated extraction was recorded once per use case and added to the individual verification time.

4.1 Direct Information

For direct information, MASEMiner recovered the target effect sizes of the self-other correlations completely (SEN = 1.00) and without any wrong value (see Table 2). Precision was modest (PRE = 0.54) because MASEMiner also returned 285 correlations that were not target effects of the meta-analysis, primarily correlations among informant ratings or among selfratings, which stem almost entirely from four publications (Table S1). This type of overextraction is manageable in practice because the MASEMiner interface allows users to selectively delete ineligible effects during verification. The sample sizes attached to the effect sizes and the reliabilities were extracted with high sensitivity (0.98 and 0.89); the omissions were confined to one publication each. Descriptive information was extracted less completely: mean age, country, and in particular the percentage of females were missing for several samples, mostly because the reference database and MASEMiner relied on different computation bases (e.g., the sex ratio before vs. after data exclusion, or the country inferred from the authors' affiliations). The second use case on video gaming and body mass replicated this pattern. Effect sizes and sample sizes were recovered completely (SEN = 1.00) with precisions of 0.69 and 0.93, because MASEMiner also extracted related but non-target statistics; 12 of the 15 overextractions stem from a single publication (Table S2). In this case, mean age and country were extracted without error. For the percentage of females, two values were missing and two differed from the reference database, which had computed the percentage from the reported frequencies, whereas MASEMiner extracted the printed value.

4.2 Indirect Information

For indirect information, the extraction of factor loadings and factor correlations showed very high agreement between the MASEMiner-extracted and the human-coded values (see Table 3). In the TAS-20 use case, 979 of the 980 factor loadings were recovered correctly; the single wrong value reproduced a typographical error of the source table. Six factor correlations from two publications were not extracted because MASEMiner treated the reported values as correlations between subscale sum scores, which the prompt excludes by rule. Descriptive information was extracted without error, except for two missing percentages of females. The second use case on the NCS-18 replicated this pattern. Factor loadings were extracted with high accuracy (SEN = 0.92, PRE = 0.98). All 18 omitted loadings, the two omitted factor correlations, and the wrong number of factors stem from one publication for which MASEMiner extracted only two factors of the three-factor solution; the seven wrong loadings stem from one publication in which three items were assigned to a neighboring factor and one loading had the wrong sign.

Descriptive information was extracted with near-perfect accuracy. The publication-level results (Tables S1-S4) thus show that the deviations are concentrated in a few publications, whereas publications with cleanly typeset tables were extracted without error. Overall, the results indicate that MASEMiner can accurately extract not only direct effect sizes but also the indirect information required to reconstruct correlation matrices.

5. Discussion

MASEMiner is part of a broader movement toward using AI in research synthesis (Fütterer et al., 2026). It was designed to support researchers in extracting information required for meta-analytic structural equation modeling, including zero-order correlations reported in tables and text, as well as indirect information that can be used to reconstruct correlation matrices, such as factor loadings and factor correlations. Across four use cases, the system achieved high extraction accuracy, and the verification study showed that automated extraction followed by human verification reduced coding time by 42% relative to conventional manual extraction at comparable accuracy. An open question is whether the extraction accuracy of MASEMiner can be improved further. It should be noted that the current performance is already on a par with that of trained human coders and that a considerable part of the remaining discrepancies stems from contradictory or ambiguous information in the primary studies.

Nevertheless, further improvements are conceivable. First, several agents could code the same source independently, with the final values determined by consensus scoring (e.g., majority voting across runs). Second, additional plausibility checks could be implemented, together with explicit disambiguation rules for cases in which a source offers several admissible values (e.g., multiple samples or factor solutions).

Moreover, the use of AI in research synthesis is not restricted to extracting statistical information from published articles, but may also support literature search and study screening (König et al., 2024 (König et al., , 2026)). It is also possible to integrate downstream analytical functionality, such as pooling reconstructed correlation matrices, transforming extracted statistics into a common effect size metric, or generating draft reports. Tools such as MASEMiner could be extended toward more automated forms of evidence collection and preparation, analogous to large-scale synthesis platforms such as Neurosynth (Yarkoni et al., 2011) and the more recent Neurosynth Compose (Kent et al., 2026), which combine automated data extraction, centralized databases, and expert-guided curation to make cumulative evidence more accessible and reusable. At this stage, however, we intentionally refrain from implementing such end-to-end automation. Instead, MASEMiner focuses on extraction and human verification while keeping analysis as a separate step to preserve transparency and ensure that automation supports rather than replaces expert judgment. Accordingly, the present evaluation targets the accuracy and efficiency of the extraction and verification workflow rather than the downstream MASEM results. Such results depend on additional application-specific decisions, including effect-size transformations, matrix reconstruction, pooling, and model specification.

More generally, the increasing use of AI in research synthesis underscores the importance of reproducibility in AI-assisted research, an issue that has received insufficient attention to date. Schroeders et al. (2026) argue that the level of reproducibility in AI-assisted research should be distinguished according to its role in the research process. When AI is used as a research partner to support brainstorming, editing, or drafting, transparent disclosure may often be sufficient. By contrast, when AI is used as a methodological instrument, its outputs enter the evidential chain and therefore require more detailed documentation. MASEMiner falls into the latter category because the extracted values provide the empirical basis for subsequent meta-analytic analyses.

For this reason, we provide not only the extracted values but also the corresponding human revisions in JSON format. This makes the audit process transparent, allows users to trace how AI-generated outputs were reviewed and modified, and supports the reproducibility of the extraction workflow.

Several limitations need to be considered. First, the default prompts were designed to be as generic as possible, avoiding idiosyncratic features of the validation databases that could lead to prompt overfitting and reduce generalizability to other applications. Our initial validation indicated that extraction performance remains high. Nevertheless, because prompt design is known to substantially affect LLM performance, it is impossible to cover all potential applications with a single universal prompt or a one-size-fits-all approach (Adam et al., 2026; Sahoo et al., 2025). Some degree of prompt adaptation will therefore likely be necessary when applying MASEMiner to new research domains, instruments, or extraction targets (e.g., higherorder factor models). The structured prompt and output schema can in principle be extended to additional summary statistics, such as means and standard deviations, or study characteristics, such as respondents' mean educational level or assessment modes, when these are required for a specific synthesis. However, these targets were not evaluated in the present study, and the extraction accuracy of other information remains to be established. Moreover, very long full-text documents were excluded from the TAS-20 prompt-development set, so that the reported accuracy does not cover unusually long documents, dissertations, or reports with extensive supplementary material, for which context limits may pose additional challenges. Second, given the very high extraction accuracy for most targets, users may be prone to automation bias, placing excessive trust in the AI-generated output and conducting less thorough human verification than is required for a genuinely human-in-the-loop extraction workflow (e.g., Klingbeil et al., 2024). Our verification study is consistent with this concern: coders rarely deleted surplus effect sizes, and wrong values that required consulting the source table were corrected only in part. Finally, the extraction performance reported here is based on the most recent OpenAI model available at the time of validation. We deliberately refrained from benchmarking individual LLMs because our focus was on the tool and its workflow rather than on model comparisons, and because LLM performance may change rapidly with model updates (Chen et al., 2024).

6. Conclusion

MASEMiner provides a workflow for AI-assisted extraction of heterogeneous statistical information required for MASEM, including directly reported correlations and indirect information needed to reconstruct correlation matrices. Across four use cases, factor loadings were recovered almost perfectly, and directly reported effect sizes were recovered completely, although with surplus candidates that users can delete in the interface. In combination with human verification, the workflow reduced coding time by 42% relative to conventional manual extraction at comparable accuracy. Editable outputs, links to the source evidence, and a JSON export that preserves both the original model output and all human corrections keep the extraction process transparent and verifiable.
