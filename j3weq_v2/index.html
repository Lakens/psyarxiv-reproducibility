Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Mean Length Utterance (MLU) is a common measure of syntactic complexity in studies of children's language development. It is often used by the clinical community, including speech-language pathologists in the diagnosis of children's speech disorders (e.g., Rice et al. 2006; Hernandez et al., 2024). In its original formulation, Brown (1973) proposed MLU as the typical number of morphemes of English per utterance in a child's speech as a means of measuring emerging syntactic complexity. His original proposal was to calculate the MLU for a speaker by counting the total number of morphemes in the speech sample and dividing by the number of utterances. The underlying logic is the implication that the number of units occurring together is directly proportional to grammatical complexity. While Brown himself did not specify what constituted an utterance, Miller (1981) proposed that utterance boundaries may be based on intonations and pauses between utterances.

Since this original measure was proposed, MLU has been computed in myriad ways, with different authors using different operationalisations of the measure (e.g., MLU of longest 3 utterances; subsampling from the first 50 utterances; see more below). While all operationalisations aim to capture something about grammatical complexity in early development, each metric reflects different underlying assumptions about properties of the data distribution, and the intended targets of the metric. Furthermore, while this measure has been useful in characterising the grammatical complexity of English-speaking children (e.g., Guo & Eisenberg, 2015; Miller & Chapman, 1981), it is difficult to implement in language communities whose language features do not map on to that of English. This challenge is further compounded when we consider multilingual communities who make use of all their languages variably within and across utterances.

There are three aims of this paper. First, we provide some current examples of how MLU is defined, used and collected in the field of child language development. We then explore measurement characteristics of MLU-like metrics in a large corpus of child-directed speech from parents in multilingual Singapore and explain the challenges of implementing commonly used MLU metrics to our multilingual translanguaging-heavy dataset. Next, using our dataset as an example, we show that these MLU-like metrics are better suited as measures of grammatical complexity and offer suggestions for researchers and clinicians who are looking for new ways to examine grammatical complexity and/or also work in bi/multilingual communities. Finally, to help researchers and clinicians make principled decisions, we simulate a few conditions for selecting the most appropriate MLU-like metrics depending on key factors: the specific linguistic metrics of interest, characteristics of the study population, and practical considerations such as time and manpower. Based on the results of these simulations, we present a decision tree as a reproducible, pre-registerable pathway to aid researchers in selecting the most suitable MLU-like syntactic complexity metric across diverse language data types, including varying sample sizes, transcription depth, and linguistic contexts.

Types of speech elicitation targets.

In designing an MLU study or in clinical applications using MLUlike metrics, the kinds of sample and the context of the sample can vary widely. The method of speech elicitation for children or adult-child dyads includes naturalistic speech with a caregiver such as free play sessions (e.g., Rollins et al., 1996), picture naming tasks (e.g., Goh et al., 2008), wordlesspicture-book sharing (e.g., Woon et al., 2021), and more targeted interactions in a clinical setting with a speech-language pathologist (e.g., Sanz-Torrent et al., 2008). These different elicitation strategies may have different implications for the time available for speech sampling, as well as for the child's level of comfort during the task. In our review of the existing literature, we observed seven different approaches of types of speech elicitation activities and three combinations of speech-target (whose speech is being analysed) and interlocutors (see Figure 1). Each of these approaches and speech-targets results in different amounts of data available for analysis and different resulting counts. We expect that in multilingual communities, there is a greater challenge in selecting suitable speech elicitation tasks or speech-targets for analysis as children and caregivers may not feel comfortable using their "home" language (e.g., native languages, informal varieties, or translanguaged speech) in front of unfamiliar adults or clinicians. One way of overcoming this observer bias is to conduct daylong recordings with small speech recorders. Over the course of the day, participants (typically children) wearing the recorders and their interlocutors forget or pay no attention to the presence of the recorders which then lead to recordings which better reflect language use at home by the child and their caregivers (e.g., Casillas, 2023; Cychosz et al., 2021). However, the nature of daylong recordings generates large volumes of data which then leads to the following issues of transcription costs (see Casillas & Casey, 2024 on processing daylong recordings) and data subsampling and exclusions.

Approach to measurement. Since the origins of the MLU metric, researchers have taken different approaches regarding the participants who would be the target of the research, as well as the measurement unit (e.g., morphemes) and span (e.g., utterances) over which the measure of syntactic complexity was computed. While the majority of studies on grammatical complexity investigate the MLU of children at different stages of development (e.g., de Villiers & de Villiers, 1973; Heilmann et al., 2010), some studies have used MLU to investigate the grammatical complexity of parental speech in child-directed speech (Baker et al., 2015; Bingham et al., 2012; Hoff, 2003; Hoff & Naigles, 2002; Taumoepeau, 2006; Taylor et al., 2009). In our review of the existing literature, we see variability in both the research targets (child, parent) and the languagecontext under investigation (monolingual, code-mixed, etc.), resulting in five research targets reflected in the literature (see Figure 1).

In terms of measuring the unit of speech, strategies include morphemes (e.g., Brown, 1973), words (e.g., Wu, 2020) or counting syllables (e.g., Cheung, 1998), and these strategies were likely chosen to suit different languages or different research contexts. For example, when working with written transcripts, word counts are transparent in transcripts of English, as word boundaries are marked by spaces, while syllable and morpheme counts are more difficult to derive, as morpheme boundaries are not always clear. Hence, a researcher working with written English transcripts may choose to use word counts instead of morpheme counts as their measure of language use or language complexity. On the other hand, syllable counts are transparent in transcripts of Chinese, as each syllable of Chinese is represented by a single written character, while word and morpheme counts are more difficult to derive, as word boundaries are not marked. So, researchers working with Chinese scripts may prefer using syllable counts. For teams working with audio recordings instead of written transcripts, some automated data pipelines have been used to estimate the number of syllables in a span of speech from the raw audio (e.g., de Jong & Wempe, 2009). However, due to differences in language structures, and the baseline data on which an automated pipeline was trained, these pipelines are not a one-size-fits-all solution and manual processing of audio recordings remains necessary. Regardless of language, the measurement of units of speech is further complicated when different automated models disagree on the number of words or morphemes in a particular token, or in a particular variety (see Choo et al., forthcoming for details).

In terms of the span of connected speech over which the units are sampled, some researchers have focused on utterances, which are defined pragmatically (e.g., Miller 1981), while others have focused on sentences or clauses, which are defined syntactically (e.g., Guo & Eisenberg, 2015). Syntactic units such as 'clause' and 'sentence' may be more straightforward to determine in linguistic varieties with well documented subject-prominent grammars such as Standard English (e.g., Li & Thompson, 1976), whereas this metric may be less straightforward in languages which feature topic-comment grammar such as Mandarin Chinese (e.g., Shi, 2000), and informal Singapore English (e.g., Chang, 2009; see also Ng, 2024 for a discussion of topic-comment sentence constructions in parent-child talk in Singapore). In addition to the challenge of parsing different units and spans from transcripts of different languages, it should be noted that typologically distinct languages have different measurement properties across the different metrics. For example, when comparing MLU-words-per-utterance to MLU-morphemes-per-utterance, a highly agglutinatingfoot_0 language such as Tamil would have a much larger difference between the two metrics, while a more isolating language such as English would yield more-similar values in both. Given that the optimal strategy for calculating both units and spans can differ within and across different languages, the selection of metrics becomes even more challenging for speech samples containing more than one language. In our review of the existing literature, we observed five different approaches to the unit of speech and four different approaches to the span of speech (see Figure 1). Each of these approaches will result in wildly different counts for datasets that contain more than one language, especially if those languages are typologically different like the ones we present in this paper. We detail this later in the paper with a worked example (see Figure 2).

Cost of transcriptions.

The high diversity in implementation of MLU across different studies is likely to be driven in part by resource constraints in different research settings. MLU is usually computed from written transcripts of speech behaviour. However, transcription is a labour-intensive task, where one minute of speech may take up to an hour to transcribe, depending on the level of detail in the transcription protocol. For example, in one recent study (see Woon et al., 2021), an in-house transcription protocol was developed (Loh et al., 2022) for transcription of multi-speaker, multilingual speech, with custom markup for linguistic features of interest. It was estimated that transcriptions of parent-child speech took approximately 30 to 45 minutes of transcription per minute of speech, plus an additional 15 to 30 minutes of expert checking. Initial training for new transcribers took 16 hours per transcriber (including practice and error correction), meaning that the true cost of transcription could easily reach one hour per minute of high engagement narrative speech (see Choo et al., forthcoming for more details about labour costs across languages).

As a way to reduce labour costs needed for transcriptions, researchers may look to using commercial transcribers or AI-based transcription tools. However, those methods may not be ideal for transcribing recordings collected for research or clinical use. First, while commercial transcribers working on monolingual speech (without specialist annotations) were often able to transcribe at a faster rate than in-house trained research staff, the transcriptions often differ from the norms of linguistically informed transcription protocols and are better suited for captioning professional speech or media content. For example, commercial transcription services routinely omitted certain speech signals from the transcriptions, including speech errors, repairs, inaudible speech, and fillers such as 'aah' and 'mm'. Since some of these features may be important for detailed linguistic analysis of utterance complexity, these transcriptions may not be well suited to recordings collected to study child language use or child-directed language use. Second, for readily available AI-based transcription tools, there is a risk of privacy breaches since any recordings shared with the companies providing such services may be used to train their AI tools (Bäckström, 2025; Su, 2019). While in-house transcriptions represent best practices ethically and linguistically, the high overhead in staff-costs may make detailed, linguistically informed annotation unaffordable for many (see also Choo et al., forthcoming, for a worked example). Furthermore, in a clinical setting, practitioners may have limited time allocated to each patient, so time for data transcription may be scarce. As a result, many researchers and practitioners in speech and language therapy have historically been interested in metrics of complexity that can be applied to small samples of speech.

Data subsampling and data exclusions.

Given the resource-intensive nature of in-house transcription, few researchers are able to compute metrics across large samples of recorded speech. In our overview of the literature, only four papers reported conducting analysis on their entire recorded sample, while more than twenty papers subsampled from the original recording in a variety of ways. For example, some researchers sampled a reduced number of utterances from each participant, ranging from 3 to 100 utterances (e.g., Casby, 2011; Gavin & Giles, 1996) or from 1 to 35 minutes of speech (Goh et al., 2008; Heilmann et al., 2010). Some researchers implemented a random sampling method (e.g., Sanz-Torrent et al., 2008; Wu, 2020), while others implemented a small window either at the start of the recording (Nóro & Mota, 2019; Rollins et al., 1996) or shortly after a 'settling in period' (Tommerdahl & Kilpatrick, 2014). Finally, some researchers sampled from a small number of longest utterances in the sample (e.g., longest one, three and five utterances : Ezeizabarrena & Garcia Fernandez, 2018; Jackson-Maldonado & Conboy, 2007). This metric may be more labour intensive than others, as the determination of the longest utterance implies a full transcript or another way of processing utterance-length prior to sub-sampling. Depending on the nature of the task and the recording context, adopting a fixed span of analysis can also help to normalize the measure's attributes among participants whose conversations lasted for different durations. Overall, each of these subsampling approaches reduced the amount of speech processed for an individual recording.

Despite the diversity of sampling approaches, few papers report how different subsampling approaches compare, or describe their reasoning for adopting one subsampling approach over another. In our review of the existing literature, we observed eighteen different approaches of subsampling data (see Figure 1) and elaborate on the few that reported on different subsampling approaches. Tommerdahl and Kilpatrick (2014) compared the use of morphosyntactic features in 50, 100, 150 and 200-utterance samples of children's speech. They found that a sample size of 100 utterances showed significant correlations for their study of grammatical features, and was an appropriate sample size for analysis: shorter samples (i.e., a 50-utterance sample) provided insufficient data for analysis, while longer samples (i.e., 150 or 200 utterances) did not provide a significantly larger degree of reliability. A separate study by Guo and Eisenberg (2015) compared several metrics for measuring speech complexity in samples of various durations. They found that the MLU-morphemes-per-utterance did not differ significantly when computed over shorter samples (1, 3, 7 and 10-minute samples) versus the complete 22-minute recording. However, total number of words, and number of different words did vary across these sample sizes. This suggests that MLU is somewhat stable over samples of different durations. In addition, Vandewalle et al. (2012) calculated MLU-words-per-utterance in the five longest utterances and across all utterances in a storybook telling-and-retelling task and found no difference between the two measures. Overall, it appears that there is no gold standard in subsampling methods and researchers also fail to explain rationale for selecting one method over the other.

In addition to the sampling strategy employed, papers in our sample differ in the kinds of vocal behaviours that were excluded. In our review of the existing literature, we observed nine different strategies for exclusion of different speech behaviours (see Figure 1). Some studies excluded exclamations and interjections (Brown, 1973), imitations, repetitions and routines (Bedore et al., 2010; Jiménez et al., 2006; Yip & Matthews, 2006; Yow et al., 2017) or participants who spoke fewer than a set number of utterances (e.g., 10: Yow et al., 2017; 100: Parker & Brorson, 2005). Furthermore, some studies excluded from analysis any spans that contained speech in more than one language (Bedore et al., 2010; Jiménez et al., 2006; Yip & Matthews, 2006; Yow et al., 2017). Excluding stimuli from the non-target language of the study is often thought to make a sample 'cleaner' for subsequent calculations. This strategy may be suitable in contexts where bilinguals use the target language most of the time (resulting in few exclusions), or in contexts where it is typical to use languages discretely in different contexts, with infrequent translanguaging. However, this kind of exclusion may not be suitable in high translanguaging contexts where many utterances contain words or phrases from more than one language (cf. Styles et al., 2022a; 2022b). In speech contexts where there is more than one dominant language, translanguaging is a commonplace phenomenon (Shank Lauwo & Norton, 2023). Hence, excluding translanguaged utterances from parental or child speech may severely under-represent the natural linguistic complexity of the speakers, or overinflate their monolingual characteristics. Furthermore, in dense, translanguaging contexts like those in the present study, utterances containing words from more than one language may be long and complex, meaning the exclusion of translanguaged utterances would underestimates a speaker's actual grammatical complexity. This is similar to how early studies showed a disadvantage for bilingual children's vocabulary when the children were tested on a vocabulary measure in only one language (Doyle et al., 1978; Rosenblum & Pinker, 1983). Excluding translanguaged utterances results in the loss of valuable productions that may be able to tell us about how language is represented in the mind of a multilingual speaker, and how linguistic resources are passed on intergenerationally. Overall, similar to the existing subsampling methods, there is no standard approach for data exclusions and features of naturalistic data collected, especially those from multilingual communities, who are more vulnerable to exclusion, which in turn leads to a misrepresentation of actual language use.

To work around the issue of excluding speech in more than one language, one more type of MLU count we found in our review was to split the languages directly and calculate one MLU per language (Yow et al., 2017). However, this is not ideal either as this may reinforce the potentially flawed assumption that each language is cognitively represented separately in a multilingual person's mind. Recent commentaries challenge this assumption, instead asserting that a person's linguistic knowledge is a single entity comprised of resources that may originate in different languages (cf. Li & García, 2022 on translanguaging as a decolonising project), and that an individual's tendency to integrate or separate multiple languages will be due to the sociolinguistic context and speech environment (cf. Gullifer & Titone, 2020 measure of language entropy). To give a worked example from the featured dataset in this article, Figure 2 shows how different operationalisations of grammatical complexity observed in the literature can have powerful impacts on the measurement of MLU-like metrics in multilingual contexts, resulting in a total morpheme count ranging 7 to 62 morphemes, and an MLU of 7-20.7 morphemes per utterance (for further examples see Supplementary Table 1). Hence, approaches that incorporate flexibility or integrate diversity-oriented metrics are important in multilingual translanguaging communities, as they can highlight the naturalistic structure of conversations. Given that typologically distinct languages can differ in the typical number of morphemes per word, one feature of this approach is to select metrics that highlight linguistic complexity at the level of the morpheme, as each morpheme represents the smallest unit of meaning within a language.

Measurement diversity and the challenge of analytic flexibility. In the sections above we reviewed the diversity of approaches to measurement of MLU-type metrics as an index of grammatical complexity in a speech sample. Some of the diversity of measures arise from different ways of generating a sample, while others arise from different ways of computing metrics from an existing sample. We have observed several decision points at which deriving a metric of MLU has substantial flexibility even after the primary data have been collected. Taken together, a researcher has potentially 7 * 3 * 5 * 4 * 18 * 9 ways to produce a continuous MLU-like metric for a particular research target. From a statistical perspective, this represents extremely high researcher degrees of freedom, also known as a large 'garden of forking paths' (Gelman & Loken, 2013). The lack of clear decision pathways for adopting a particular measure of MLU means that there is substantial analytic flexibility in the field. As summarised by Chambers (2017), where analytic flexibility is high, researchers run the risk of selecting a measure after testing which of multiple strategies produces the desired outcomes. Moreover, in the existing literature, it is not always clear which analyses were based on a priori selection of measures, and which were based on post-hoc decision making after the results are known (see also Gelman & Loken, 2013; Simmons et al., 2011; Šoškić et al., 2022).

While it is not incorrect to investigate measures from different perspectives, best practices are those where the researcher clarifies which of their research process are exploratory (i.e., discussing among various options what patterns might exist in the data) versus hypothesis testing (i.e., evidence is used to test whether a theory predicts a particular outcome).

As mentioned, one of the goals of the current paper is to compare the measurement characteristics of different implementations of MLU on a large sample of speech using different measurements and subsampling approaches, and to use the results to propose a decision framework for preregister their metrics prior to hypothesis testing. Given the variability in the existing literature, in the current paper, we report exploratory analyses designed to compare different implementations of MLU, and to see which measures reliably capture a developmental trajectory -that is to say, metrics that are positively correlated with the age of the child. The end-goal is to present a decision tree that researchers and practitioners can use to decide which measurement of MLU-like syntactic complexity is best for their specific data and purposes. This exploratory investigation serves as a baseline study of measurement properties, so that a single measure of MLU can be selected for a preregistered hypothesis test of an intervention that was conducted between time points.

Methods Participants

Parent-child dyads were recruited as part of the Talk Together Study (Woon et al., 2021). The children ranged in age from 8 months to 40 months (median = 18.7 months, IQR: 12.55 to 27.63 months).

Data collection design

The recordings analysed in the current study are a subset of a larger micro-longitudinal randomized control trial (Talk Together Study, Woon et al., 2021) with three time points, where the first time point represents a 'baseline' of parent-child speech. This dataset contained video recordings of parent-child dyads reading a wordless picture book, Little Orangutan: What A Scary Storm (Styles, 2020) at the first time point (N = 146), with the children aged between 8-40 months. All recordings were conducted over the video conferencing platform Zoom Version 5.0.5 (26213.0602) (Zoom Video Communications, Inc., 2020). Details of the micro-longitudinal study can be found in a separate paper (Woon et al., 2021). In the multilingual context of Singapore, parents in this study were encouraged to use any language or mix of languages they typically used with their child for the book sharing with their child. Book sharing between parent and child was an ideal task to elicit naturalistic speech compared to other forms of speech interactions e.g., text-and-picture book reading (Mathers et al., 2024). Additionally, because the picture book contained no words and followed a simple storyline, parents were unrestricted in the word types and number of utterances they wanted to use for each page, reducing possible biases about using particular languages, speaking formally versus informally, and whether translanguaging would be suitable (Woon et al., 2021). As the focus of the Talk Together Study was an intervention designed to enhance parents' child-directed speech, the research target of the current investigation is parental speech. The booksharing task lasted between 2 and 35 minutes (median = 7.25 minutes), with parents making up most of the speech (median = 66%).

All transcribers, checkers, morpheme coders and data analysts were blind to the intervention allocation during data collection, processing and analysis of the current paper. The intervention allocation has not been linked to transcripts or to data arising from transcripts at the time of writing. All transcribers and checkers were native speakers of the local linguistic varieties (English and one of Mandarin, Malay, Tamil) and were assigned to transcribed recordings that matched their linguistic ability e.g., an English-Malay bilingual transcriber would transcribe a recording from an English-Malay parent-child dyad. The dataset contained child-directed speech with the following language combinations from the respective groups of parents: English-Mandarin (n = 117), English-Malay (n = 15), English-Tamil (n = 10), English-Mandarin-Malay (n = 2) and English-only (n = 2). Hence, 99% of families translanguaged during their book-sharing sessions (Chua & Styles, 2023).

Approach to measurement

In this study, we examined two speech units of interest. First, we selected the morpheme rather than the word due to the high typological diversity of the main languages in our multilingual dataset. We define a morpheme as the smallest unit of speech that conveys a meaning that could be parseable for a child using regular, productive morphological processes. For example, 'quickly' is considered two morphemes, while 'mice' and 'backward' are both considered monomorphemic. Second, we examined the utterance rather than the sentence, as parents did not typically speak in complete sentences to their children, and incomplete sentences can still convey meaningful information. We used the definition of 'utterance' as documented in the BELA transcription protocol (see below; Loh et al., 2022; Sundaray, 2020), and consider complete sentences (that is, sentences that are marked by intonation and pauses between sentences) or, in lieu of complete sentences, "chunks that go together in intonation patterns" as utterances. As the Singapore linguistic context features a median translanguaging rate of around 5% (Range: 0-50%) (Styles et al., 2022a), in the current analysis, we consider all utterances -including mixed language utterances -to be equally valid for the measure of grammatical complexity. For example, a typical utterance looked like "elephant 大象 ok meimei see a 大象" [elephant elephant ok younger-sister see a elephant]. As less than .01% of the corpus included languages 2 outside these four (i.e., English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil), all instances of other languages were excluded from analysis.

Using our dataset, we compared a wide range of implementations of MLU that researchers have used in the literature (as discussed in the previous section and summarised in Figure 1), and simulated how such metrics may change if estimated over small versus large subsets of existing data. These can roughly be grouped into four broad categories: total speech, central tendency of complexity, lower and upper bound extremes of complexity. The full set of metrics for comparison is summarised in Table 1.

Total speech. Following previous work that measured MLU across all utterances and all morphemes (e.g., Vandewalle et al., 2012), we computed both the total number of utterances and total count of morphemes as measures of total speech. However, there are two limitations to using totals. First, they are dependent on the length of the sample, which may be constrained by factors such as time available for recording, or child attention. Second, a straightforward measure cannot differentiate between 100 one-word utterances and 10 ten-word utterances, meaning that there is no way to account for complexity of individual utterances. For this reason, we also include metrics summarising the complexity of utterances (i.e., MLU-like metrics).

Central Tendency of Complexity.

We calculated the Mean Length Utterance (MLU) in each transcript. As the arithmetic mean is most appropriate for normal distributions, the MLU estimate may be influenced by outliers in the high tails of the utterance length distribution. Hence, to accommodate deviations from normality, Median Length Utterance (Med-LU) was computed. The Modal Length Utterance (Mod-LU) was also computed to examine the most frequently occurring length of utterance.

Lower bound extremes of complexity.

As deviations from normality were expected in the current sample, we included metrics that captured the lower bound of complexity, computed as the percentage of utterances consisting of a single morpheme. Monomorphemic utterances typically include exclamations, interjections or routines -features that had been systematically excluded from analyses by Brown (1973) and many other authors. However, some of these features may be important for detailed linguistic analysis of utterance complexity, especially for a young child's burgeoning vocabulary, or parental interactions with young children (children in our sample were aged between 8-40 months). Transcription and data cleaning. The recordings were transcribed by a large team of transcribers from the Brain, Language and Intersensory Perception Lab (BLIP Lab) trained on the in-house BLIP ELAN Language Annotation (BELA) Convention designed for multilingual transcriptions (transcription manual: https://blipntu.github.io/belacon/; Loh et al., 2022). Hence, parental speech during wordless picture-book sharing contained significantly more speech and longer utterances when speaking to older children than to younger children. Both the typical length of an utterance (MLU, Med-LU) and the length of the most extreme long utterances are shown to be longer for parents speaking to older children, with the latter increasing at a much faster rate than the typical length of an utterance. As short utterances are characteristic of child-directed speech (e.g., Lester et al., 2022), by investigating the lower bounds of the distribution, we also observed that the proportion of very short utterances is smaller for parents speaking to older children. The lower bound measure including all utterance types showed equivalent effect size to MLU and 1L metrics, making it as effective for capturing age-related complexity in parental speech as more traditional measures. Med-LU is somewhat more powerful than MLU, and the upper bounds of complexity have an even larger effect size when more long utterances are included in the metric (i.e., 10L). In addition to assessing measures of complexity across the whole sample, we further simulated how these measures compare on smaller subsamples of the corpus of speech. As such, we created subsamples from the beginning of the recorded transcriptions to allow comparison between the full sample (all utterances) and a set of reduced samples (first 100, 90, 80, 70, 60, 50 and 10 utterances). This simulation will allow us to evaluate the outcomes of different sampling approaches and propose a decision pathway for selecting the most appropriate measures for researchers and practitioners to use, according to the metrics of interest, the population of interest and the resources constraints that exist.

Discussion

We set out to investigate the relationship between a child's age and the MLU of parental childdirected speech in Singapore. As most of the previous research on this topic has been conducted in communities of monolingual English speakers, this investigation has a specific focus on measurement of MLU as applied to a multilingual context with frequent translanguaging. In terms of total speech, as expected, we observed a significant positive correlation for child age and quantity of speech, as measured by both total number of utterances and total number of morphemes. In terms of central tendency for complexity, we found a significant positive correlation between child age and MLU-like metrics, as measured by both mean and median length of utterance. In terms of the extreme upper bounds of complexity, child age correlated with various measures. In particular, the mean length of the longest 10 utterances (10L) provided a more stable measure of upper bound extremes than the mean length when calculated over fewer utterances (e.g., the single longest utterance, 1L). For the extreme lower bounds of complexity, we also observed that the percentage of monomorphemic child-directed utterances was inversely correlated with age, and this effect was strongest when all utterance types (including interjections) were included in the data sample. When conducted across the whole dataset, MLU, Med-LU, percent monomorphemic utterances and longest single utterance all correlated with age, at equivalent effect sizes (ρ = .20), suggesting that multiple metrics are valid for a general overview of 'complexity' development, and that the unique characteristics of a particular dataset and research constraints may be used to guide decisions about which metric to select. The only metrics that routinely exceed this effect size are the total morpheme count (how much a parent talks to their child over the course of the book-sharing) and the 10L metric (the average length of the ten longest utterances of a parent to their child). Overall, in multilingual Singapore, where over 99% of the parents in our study used more than one language with their child during a naturalistic book-sharing session, we see that parental speech grew increasingly complex as children grew older. We argue that language-inclusive metrics (including speech in all languages, and all kinds of speech) provide the best estimates of speech complexity in communities where translanguaging is prevalent.

Based on our results, we revisit the broad themes in Figure 1 and propose the following best practices for each of the metrics in Table 3 below. Challenge: Grammatical complexity such as MLU can be measured in myriad ways in a parent/child speech sample, potentially leading to high researcher degrees of freedom (Chambers, 2017)).

In the current paper, we conducted a large-scale exploratory analysis of multiple metrics, with a goal to characterise different measurement properties. To reduce researcher degrees of freedom, best practice is to specify which metrics will be analysed prior to data collection/viewing. An evidence-based decision-pathway may help researchers to select metrics depending on study characteristics and resource context, prior to observing data, as would be suitable for a preregistered protocol.

Action Points: Use of a metric-selection decision pathway allows preregistration of metrics when characteristics of a sample have not yet been observed.

Unit of speech in the measure

Challenge: 'Word' as a unit of analysis comes from a history of English language research which aligns poorly with other linguistic contexts, including languages with different writing systems, typologically diverse languages, and multilingual samples.

As our sample includes data from four languages (English, Mandarin, Malay and Tamil), we elected to run all analyses reported here using morphemes as the basic unit of measurement, and to include data from all languages that make up the linguistic repertoire of the speaker. This approach prioritises inclusivity over linguistic purity, and may be suitable in a variety of multilingual/diglossic contexts. For further discussion about the decision to use morphemes, see Choo et al. (forthcoming).

Action points: Future work should consider language characteristics of the sample before determining the unit of comparison (words versus morphemes). Morphemes may be the most equitable unit of measurement for grammatical complexity, whether they occur in monomorphemic words in isolating languages, or in multimorphemic words in agglutinating languages. Span of speech in the measure Challenge: 'Sentence' as a unit of analysis comes from a history of English language research which aligns poorly with other linguistic contexts, such as languages with topic-comment grammar, and multilingual, translanguaged samples.

Linguistic definitions of what constitutes a well-formed 'sentence' (cf., Brown, 1973) do not align with the ways that caregivers structure their speech for children in all contexts. As some languages are better characterised by topic-comment grammatical structure, selection of utterance over sentence may be most flexible and inclusive. We selected utterance as the span of speech rather than sentence to flexibly account for both linguistic structure and pragmatic factors in parent-child speech.

Action points: Decisions about the span of speech over which complexity is measured should take into account different linguistic structures and the pragmatic realities of parent-child speech. The span of a measure should be guided by the age of the child and the language context. Further research may be needed to validate metrics used for data with a different underlying structure, or with children of different age ranges.

Speech elicitation method

Challenge: Speech recorded in naturalistic parent-child interactions are highly variable, and different elicitation and sub-sampling techniques will highlight different speech features.

Differences in duration or total amount of speech may form part of the developmental trajectory for parent/child speech, meaning that a short time-bound subsample may 'flatten' developmental differences. The metrics reported here come from speech samples which ranged in duration from 2 to 35 minutes with a median duration of 7'15 minutes. In general, the book-sharing sessions were longer for older children (in terms of raw duration, as well as total count of morphemes), and more complex (as demonstrated in the different MLU-like metrics reported). In the current study, we did not set a threshold for the minimum duration or number of utterances for inclusion in the sample for analysis, as we had no a priori reason for determining what constituted insufficient speech. In wordless picture-book sharing recordings in our dataset, many measures of complexity did not show significant developmental trajectories in the shortest subsamples, although the proportion of monomorphemic utterances (including interjections) remained reliable even for very small samples.

Action Points:

The minimum amount of speech recorded or processed for an evaluation of grammatical complexity should be guided by the age of the child, the kind of speech under investigation (e.g., naturalistic storytelling; picture description; task oriented discussion; narrative recall; etc), and the purpose of the evaluation (e.g., norming typical behaviour; inclusion in a developmental battery as a dependent variable in a study; assessment of a child's language skills in a clinical context). Further research may be needed to validate metrics used for data with a different underlying structure to those reported in the current report. For example, speech recorded for clinical evaluation of a child's language skills may be structured differently, and further research with controlled speech samples may be needed to validate the metrics reported here.

Measure of central tendency

Challenge: Underlying data distribution is rarely reported when using common measures of central tendency such as MLU.

Means are often used as a default summary measure, however the mean is a poor measure of central tendency unless the underlying sample is normally distributed. In our sample, we clearly demonstrate non-normal distributions in parents' child-directed speech in our sample. Non-normal distributions are also characteristic of children's utterances, which typically contain many extremely-short utterances and few extremely-long utterances (Brown, 1973). As such, the median is a truer representation of central tendency in grammatical complexity. Choice of mean in a nonnormally distributed sample essentially skews the measure towards the upper bound of utterance length. Researchers may want to consider whether their research target is better construed as an estimate of upper bound complexity.

Note that in correlations with child age, the median length of parental utterance has a lower intercept (i.e., the median is a lower value as it is less skewed by high outliers), however the effect size for median is almost identical to the effect size computed for mean.

Action points: Future studies should check the data distribution of utterance lengths in their sample before deciding whether to select median or mean as a measure of typical linguistic complexity, and consider whether their use-case would benefit from a measure of extreme (upper bound) language use instead. Upper bound metrics: Longest X utterances Challenge: The long tails of data distribution for utterance length mean that the longest utterances are unusual for the individual, however they may contain important evidence of development at its limits.

When sampling from the extreme upper bound of complexity, we found that sampling from a larger number of utterances provides a more stable estimate, with larger effect sizes for 10 longest utterances than for samples including fewer tokens. Computing an upper bound of complexity over the 10 longest parental utterances provides an effect size boost from ρ = .2 to ρ = .3. However, in resource constrained contexts, the 6 longest utterances from a sample of 90 utterances may be a practical minimum sample size. It is notable that in our sample, the upper bound of complexity estimate provided the strongest effect size in correlations with age, suggesting it is a powerful measure of parental speech complexity for parent-child speech interactions with children across this age range (8-40 months).

Action Points:

Researchers and clinicians should use estimates of upper bound complexity instead of the measures of central tendency if their research aims to highlight the most advanced behaviours available. Estimates of upper bounds of complexity benefit from including more utterances in the sample. Detailed recommendations can be found in the decision tree (Figure 6). Lower bound metrics: monomorph emic utterances Challenge: Although often overlooked, the shortest utterances in parentchild speech provide insights into the development of conversational complexity.

In the current study, we demonstrated that lower bound estimates of parental utterance length can be as effective in correlations with age as measures of central tendency (i.e., similar effect size). We also observed the effect of age was stronger when interjections/languageless utterances were included in the metric. This holds true even for the smallest subsample of the first 10 utterances, and for the second smallest subsample of the first 50 utterances -the proportion of monomorphemic utterances has the strongest effect size which is larger than the total or central tendency metric. This lower bound estimate provides an effect size that is almost as strong as traditional counts of MLU, showing how prevalent interjections are in early child-directed speech.

Traditional interpretations tend to treat interjections merely as emotional reflexive outbursts or 'response cries' (cf. Goffman, 1978). However, work by conversation analysis researchers have shown how interjections like hm? assist parents in repair-initiation at MLU Stage 1 (Corrin, 2010), and how interjections like mm hm, huh? and oh may be at the 'heart of language' in allowing us to narrate stories, seek clarification, and exchange information (Dingemanse, 2024). Following this logic, we argue that speech that might be classified as 'languageless' (Loh et al., 2022), or as 'interjections' (Brown, 1973) can meaningfully be included in lower bound metrics of parental speech, and enhances the effect size of lower bound metrics.

Action Points: When sampling from the extreme lower bound of complexity, all parental utterance types should be included, including interjections and utterances that may be considered 'languageless' or 'non-meaning-bearing' (e.g., fillers). Further investigations will be needed to understand whether a similar pattern is observed in metrics of child speech complexity as well as parental child-directed speech.

Choice of lower bound, central tendency or upper bound measures of complexity

Decisions on the metric of interest should be explained clearly in light of specific research targets, theoretical considerations, and/or methodological constraints. Brown's (1973) original conception of MLU provided an estimate of central tendency for a child's speech complexity, averaged across all utterances in a recording. He also noted that "as the mean length of utterance gets longer so does the upper bound or longest utterance" (p. 184). Our illustration of the distributional characteristics of utterance length across a parent-child conversation shows how the extreme upper tails of a distribution essentially skew the mean towards the upper bound. A more parsimonious approach is to consider lower bound, upper bound and central tendency as discrete, measurable characteristics of speech behaviour. Given that the current paper shows all three parts of the complexity distribution (lower bound, central tendency, upper bound) differ significantly across the developmental age range of the current study, researchers can select the metric that best aligns with their specific use-case (i.e., which metric best aligns with their research interests or clinical use) and methodological constraints (i.e., labour available for processing), or report all three metrics for completeness.

Action Points: Future research will benefit from theoretical and methodological justifications for the selection of metrics of speech complexity. Selection of metrics should be clearly stated (with justifications) at the point of preregistration. An example decision tree is provided in Figure 6, as would be appropriate for inclusion in a preregistration or protocol registration.

Choice of data subsamples: first X utterances

Decisions about whether the whole sample of speech or a subsample of speech are included in analysis should be clearly explained with attention to resource constraints such as time available, cost of transcription/annotation, and technical resources available for automated pre-processing.

According to our measures of change over development, sampling from the whole distribution provides the most reliable metrics of complexity. To simulate different recording durations or resource-limits on transcriptions, we compared correlations of age of child with the different measures of complexity calculated over different sample durations, starting from the first utterance (first 10, first 50 to first 100 in intervals of 10, all utterances). When subsampling, there is increasing stabilisation of effect size with increasing sample size. Given this finding, in resourceconstrained contexts, particular approaches to subsampling should have or theoretical or methodological justifications.

Action points: Researchers aiming to use measures of complexity as key metrics in their research should plan for adequate resources for full transcription of the entire sample where possible. However, where resource constraints exist, researchers should clearly state their data subsampling approach (with justifications). Translanguaging and multilingual data

Decisions about handling multilingual data can influence the characterisation of individuals and communities, and should be clearly justified. As translanguaging is normal linguistic behaviour in many intergenerational bilingual communities, translanguaged and nonmajority language content should be included in MLU-like metrics if possible, and its removal requires strong justification.

We have shown elsewhere that within-utterance translanguaging was observed for 80% of parents in the Talk Together Study sample, at a median translanguaging rate of 5% (Styles et al., 2022a), making it a normal part of daily linguistic interaction between parents and children in the local context. To form an accurate representation of everyday speech, translanguaged utterances were included in the metrics reported in this paper, and a holistic MLU is reported (not separated into different languages). To include all languages in the metric, it was necessary to use morphemes as the unit of analysis. In some research contexts, conducting transcription and morpheme-counts may be more resource intensive for some linguistic content than for others. The labour cost of this approach is high, as we outline elsewhere (Choo et al., forthcoming). Semi-automated processes such as use of automated morphemisers can mitigate these costs in some languages, although their use is limited by current biases in the datasets on which they have been trained (Styles et al., 2023.

Action Points:

Researchers working in monolingual majority contexts should think carefully about whether it is possible to include non-majority linguistic content in their metrics, as selective exclusion of some linguistic content may introduce structured biases in their metrics. Researchers working in multilingual communities should plan for multilingual metrics where possible, and plan to use morphemes as the unit of analysis to allow better cross-language coherence among typologically distinct languages.

Based on the above operationalisations, we present a decision tree (Figure 6) as a reproducible, preregisterable pathway for deciding which measurement of MLU-like syntactic complexity to use in a given context. The decision tree allows a researcher to state which of their research features are known (e.g., is the target of the research 'typical speech' or 'unusual extremes'), and which are unknown (e.g., normal vs. non-normal distribution), and subsequently follow the decision tree after data are collected, to finalize the metric for inclusion in analysis. The decision tree provides two tangible benefits. First, in the case of an unexpected outcome (e.g., less labour available for transcribing/morpheme segmentation of speech), the tree provides a reasoned pathway for selection of the next-most-suitable approach. Second, consulting the decision tree may allow a researcher to refine their procedure during research planning to ensure more robust metrics.

Scenario 1) a research team has access to a large corpus of child-directed speech across a wide age range of children that has already been fully transcribed. In line with existing literature, the team expects that older children will be exposed to speech of higher complexity speech than when compared to younger children, and they would like to preregister an analysis plan including a measure of speech complexity, such as MLU. Since both central tendency and upper bound complexity have been shown to correlate strongly with age, the research team might consider including both estimates in their study design.

Recommendations: For a measure of central tendency, both mean and median have been shown to correlate strongly with to child age, but the choice should be determined by the distribution of the underlying data. In particular, for non-normally distributed data, we recommend selecting the median utterance length as the measure of central tendency. Prior to inspecting data from the study, the research team may not be able to estimate data distribution, but they may register the pathway, as it has clear guidance on which should be used once data is seen. For measures of upper bound complexity, the more stable relationships between complexity and age were observed when a larger number of upper bound utterances were included in the metric, meaning that a metric computed over the 10 longest utterances (10L) is indicated. Hence, following the decision points in the flowchart, the research team could elect to focus on central tendency (MLU or Med-LU depending on distribution), upper bound complexity (MLU of the 10 longest utterances in a sample of at least 100 utterances), or both, in a preregistered design with precise operationalization of metrics prior to observing the data.

Decision tree simulation for a researcher with access to full transcriptions of long samples of speech, and/or resources to produce them.

Scenario 2) A clinician has a few minutes to gather a sample of parent-child speech in a context with high resource constraints. In the limited time available, the clinician expects the parent to produce about 50 utterances. In line with existing literature, they expect complexity of speech to increase with child age, meaning that a dyad with unexpectedly low speech complexity may be flagged for further investigation for speech, language, or communication difficulties. Their initial interest may be in evaluating the upper bound of speech complexity, representing the most complex speech the child might be routinely exposed to.

Recommendations: Metrics of upper bound complexity were shown to be unstable for samples of speech with fewer than 90 utterances, meaning that measures of upper bound complexity should be used with caution in a small sample, or an alternative metric used. Since measures of central tendency require transcription of large samples to be effective, they may not be suitable in a resource constrained environment. If the language sample can be increased to include at least 90 utterances, the mean of the 6 longest utterances (6L) would be a suitable metric, although the mean of the 10 longest utterances (10L) in a sample of 100 would be even better. If the language sample cannot be increased, then a measure of lower bound complexity would be more stable, such as the proportion of monomorphemic utterances including interjections. In this way, access to a decision aid during planning allows researchers and clinicians to decide whether to devote resources to collecting more data at the point of evaluation, transcribing/coding more speech after the evaluation, or to use an alternative metric which requires less offline coding.

Conclusion

The aim of this paper was three-fold. First, we provided an overview of how MLU is counted and analysed in literature then explored measurement characteristics of MLU-like metrics in a large corpus of child-directed speech from parents in multilingual Singapore. We compared different methods of computing MLU and showed the challenges of trying to fit a multilingual translanguaging-heavy dataset into commonly used MLU metrics. Next, using our dataset as an example, we showed that these MLU-like metrics are better suited as measures of grammatical complexity and gathered our suggestions for researchers and clinicians who are looking for new ways to examine grammatical complexity and/or also work in bi/multilingual communities. Third, we also simulated conditions where researchers or clinicians may be short on time or labour, and would require a metric that is quicker to administer while still providing viable metrics (see Choo et al., forthcoming for detailed discussion of labour costs).

From our dataset, we observed a general trend for increasing complexity with increasing age of the child, though some metrics showed stronger correlations than others. In our comparison of metrics, we demonstrate that all three parts of the utterance length distribution (lower bound, central tendency, upper bound) are informative for characterising complexity in the speech of parents talking with children of different ages. Although the current dataset focusses on parental speech to typically developing children across a wide age range (8 to 40 months), it is possible that the different measures reported here may have advantages when working with different kinds of data. For example, when working on speech samples from very young children, second language learners, or speakers with language delays and disorders, lower bound estimates may prove to be more informative than measures that rely on longer utterances. By contrast, where the research target is aligned with low frequency utterances of unusually high complexity, upper bound estimates may be more informative for contexts focusing on individual differences. Future research will be needed to investigate how the variety of metrics can best be applied to a variety of research contexts, however, by including less-well-known MLU-like metrics in the review of measures, we have shown that non-traditional measures may be valuable to practitioners working in under-resourced contexts, without compromising the validity of the metrics. To aid in decision making, we present a decision tree as a reproducible, pre-registerable pathway for deciding which measurement of MLU-like syntactic complexity is best for data of different types.

The current paper demonstrates the use of these metrics in a multilingual context where translanguaging is prevalent with a focus on the morpheme as the key unit of analysis (see Choo et al., forthcoming for detailed discussion of how morphemes are better suited for multilingual contexts). While further research will be needed to unpack the nuanced relationship between morphology and complexity in different languages, this paper is a proof-of-concept for approaches that focus on inclusion of all linguistic content in messy, real-world contexts over traditional approaches to classifying and separating speech into discrete named-language categories.
