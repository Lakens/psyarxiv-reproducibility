Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) prevalence has increased markedly over recent decades, yet primary tools for early detection remain focused almost exclusively on observable behavioral signs in children aged 18 months and older. Instruments such as the M-CHAT-R/F, ADOS-2, and ADI-R, while clinically valuable, operate within a narrow temporal and conceptual window that systematically excludes a wide range of prenatal, perinatal, medical, environmental, and psychosocial signals now supported by robust empirical evidence. Objective: This paper presents a theoretically grounded critique of current early detection frameworks for ASD and proposes a Multidomain Early Risk Architecture (MERA). MERA is a detection architecture rather than a causal model of aetiology: it organizes ten empirically supported domains of clinically accessible signals according to the developmental window in which each becomes observable, and specifies through a four-tier taxonomy what inference each class of signal can bear. Approach: A structured narrative synthesis of peer-reviewed literature published between 2006 and 2025 was conducted across PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science. Each retained source was mapped to one of 75 candidate risk factors and classified by study design and journal indexing quartile. The resulting evidence base comprises 152 factor-source pairings drawn from 148 distinct sources, organized into ten thematic domains and four evidential tiers. Findings: The retained literature documents ASD-relevant signals distributed across all ten domains and four developmental windows. Sixty-nine percent of pairings derive from quartile-1 Page 1 journals and 38 from systematic reviews or meta-analyses.

Half of the evidence base corresponds to factors established before any behavioural observation of the child is possible, and which no current screening instrument incorporates. Domains differ systematically in evidential status: some comprise antecedent exposures, others early prodromal markers, and others features of the diagnostic phenotype itself or associated conditions of contested directionality.

Conclusion: A multidomain architecture that stratifies signals by developmental window and by evidential status offers a conceptual foundation for tiered developmental surveillance. The framework has implications for instrument development, primary care protocols, and public health policy-particularly in settings such as Chile where diagnostic delays remain substantial. Its domain structure and clinical utility require prospective validation, which the framework is explicitly designed to enable.

1. Introduction

The global prevalence of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has undergone a profound transformation over recent decades. Where rates once hovered around 1 in 150 children, recent surveillance data from the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimate 1 in 36 children as of 2023, with similar upward trends across Europe, Australia, East Asia, and Latin America. This trajectory reflects not only improved diagnostic sensitivity but a genuine broadening of the phenotypic landscape. In Chile, ASD has been progressively incorporated into national developmental monitoring guidelines, yet diagnostic delays in the public health system range from two to four years from first parental concern to formal diagnosis.

Against this backdrop, the dominant paradigm for early ASD detection rests on a behavioral surveillance model. Tools such as the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers-Revised with Follow-up (M-CHAT-R/F), the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS-2), and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R) have become gold-standard instruments by virtue of their strong psychometric properties and clinical utility. Yet all share a fundamental structural limitation: they are designed to identify behavioral anomalies in children already old enough to display them-typically at 18 months or later. This temporal constraint means detection begins precisely where overt behavioral divergence becomes visible, while ignoring the complex developmental trajectory that precedes it.

A growing body of evidence challenges this behaviorally centered and temporally narrow model.

Prospective longitudinal studies of infant siblings of autistic children document divergence in canonical babbling, motor development, and social orienting within the first year of life (Long et al., 2024; Yankowitz et al., 2022; Gabis et al., 2020; Hatch et al., 2021); large-scale Nordic population registry studies report associations between ASD diagnosis and antenatal and perinatal characteristics established before birth (Persson et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2019; Villamor et al., 2022); meta-analyses of prenatal and perinatal exposures converge on a consistent set of obstetric and pharmacological associations (Garza-Martínez et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Gardener et al., 2011); and experimental neuroimaging and electrophysiological research identifies atypical neural response as early as the first postnatal months (Hazlett et al., 2017; Jones & Klin, 2013). Taken together, these literatures indicate that signals relevant to ASD are distributed across biological, environmental, and psychosocial dimensions, and that a subset of them is detectableand, in the case of certain modifiable exposures, potentially alterable-well before the behavioral phenotype consolidates.

The theoretical implication is substantial. If ASD risk accumulates across ten or more domains spanning the prenatal period through early childhood, instruments evaluating only behavioral signs at 18-36 months are not merely incomplete but structurally misaligned with the developmental process they seek to detect, with clinical consequences: delayed intervention, foreclosed preventive action, and systematic disadvantage for populations with limited specialist access. This paper pursues two interconnected objectives. The first is a theoretically grounded critique of current early detection frameworks, arguing that their behaviorally centered architecture constitutes a structural blind spot rather than a mere empirical gap. The second is the proposal of the Multidomain Early Risk Architecture (MERA)-a conceptual framework integrating ten empirically supported domains of ASD-relevant signals into a developmentally organized structure, and specifying through a four-tier taxonomy of evidential status what inference each class of signal can support.

2. Structural Limitations of Current Behavioral Detection Frameworks

>Structural Limitations of Current Behavioral Detection Frameworks

2.1 The Behavioral Paradigm and Its Origins

The modern architecture of ASD detection was shaped by two converging forces: the progressive refinement of diagnostic criteria from DSM-III through DSM-5, and the observation that specific behavioral anomalies-particularly in social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviors-reliably discriminated ASD from other developmental profiles. The resulting instruments are behaviorally anchored by design. The M-CHAT-R/F evaluates 20 items all requiring observation of specific behaviors-pointing, gaze following, name response-that are developmentally appropriate targets only after approximately 12-18 months. This grounding has genuine virtues: behavioral criteria are observable, relatively standardized across cultural contexts, and reliably assessed by clinicians and parents. The problem is not that behavioral detection is wrong; it is that it is temporally and theoretically incomplete.

A clarification of scope is required. The limitation identified here concerns instruments designed specifically for ASD detection. General developmental surveillance-the Ages and Stages Questionnaire, the Survey of Well-being of Young Children, or periodic surveillance under paediatric guidelines-does operate from the first year and does evaluate motor, language, and social-emotional milestones. The argument is not that such surveillance is temporally late, but that it is milestone-referenced rather than risk-stratified: it evaluates whether expected competencies have been reached at a given age, and incorporates at no point the antecedent biomedical and family-history information already held in the clinical record (see §6.3).

2.2 The Temporal Blind Spot

The behavioral model implicitly assumes that ASD emerges at or around the point at which its symptoms become visible, an assumption inconsistent with available developmental neuroscience.

Neuroimaging studies of infant siblings demonstrate structural and functional brain differences detectable as early as 6 months, preceding behavioral sign consolidation by 6-12 months (Hazlett et al., 2017); electrophysiological studies show atypical cortical responses to social and auditory stimuli at 3-4 months (Jones & Klin, 2013); and prospective studies following high-likelihood infants from birth document characteristic trajectories-reduced canonical babbling, atypical motor development, diminished social orienting-within the first year (Long et al., 2024; Gabis et al., 2020; Yankowitz et al., 2022).

Population cohort studies from Sweden, Denmark, Finland, and Norway involving millions of participants have identified robust associations between ASD diagnosis and factors present at or before birth: advanced parental age, obstetric complications, gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, prematurity, low birth weight, and prenatal exposure to pharmacological and environmental agents (Persson et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2024; Garza-Martínez et al., 2025). These have been replicated across independent datasets and implicate epigenetic, immunological, metabolic, and neurological mechanisms operating during pregnancy and the neonatal period. The behavioral detection model, by virtue of its temporal anchoring, is blind to this pre-behavioral period; the composition of the evidence base across domains is reported in §4.

2.3 The Phenotypic Expansion Problem

A second structural limitation concerns the scope of the behavioral phenotype itself. DSM-5 criteria represent a necessary simplification for diagnostic purposes, but a growing literature documents that the clinical presentation of ASD has expanded substantially beyond this core, and that many individuals who meet criteria present with profiles current instruments were not designed to capture. Individuals previously diagnosed under other categories-social (pragmatic) communication disorder, sensory processing disorder, hyperlexia, pathological demand avoidance, specific learning disabilities-are increasingly recognized as part of the spectrum. Presentations such as the female phenotype characterized by social camouflaging, or late-identified adults whose compensatory strategies masked difficulties, were largely invisible to instruments calibrated on male, clinically referred samples. This expansion creates an additional detection gap: instruments designed around a modal presentation systematically under-identify those who deviate from it.

2.4 What MERA Is and Is Not

MERA is not a causal model of ASD aetiology, and it does not assert that the factors it organizes are causally implicated in ASD pathogenesis. It is a detection architecture: a framework for organizing heterogeneous, clinically accessible signals according to the developmental window in which each becomes observable and the inferential weight each can legitimately bear.

The distinction is consequential. An aetiological model must satisfy temporal precedence, mechanistic plausibility, and freedom from reverse causation. A detection architecture operates under a lower requirement: it must identify signals that, alone or combined, raise the posterior probability that a child warrants comprehensive developmental assessment. Signals that are consequences of an emerging phenotype rather than causes remain informative for detection precisely because they co-occur with it. What they cannot support is causal inference, and MERA makes no such claim.

One qualification applies. The framework does advance mechanistic hypotheses about why particular domains should interact-that neuroinflammatory, microbiome-mediated, and epigenetic pathways provide plausible substrates for cross-domain amplification. These are auxiliary hypotheses whose function is to render the interactive principle testable rather than to constitute an aetiological theory, and Figure 3 should be read accordingly. The clarification has a direct methodological implication, developed in §5.1: because the components differ in what they can be used to infer, they must be classified by evidential status rather than treated as a homogeneous set of risk factors.

3. Approach to the Evidence Synthesis

>Approach to the Evidence Synthesis

3.1 Rationale for a Structured Narrative Approach

The evidence base was assembled through a structured narrative review, selected over a PRISMAconstrained systematic review for reasons that warrant statement. The objective was not to estimate a pooled effect for a single exposure-outcome relationship, for which systematic review and metaanalysis are appropriate, but to construct a conceptual integration across ten heterogeneous domains whose literatures differ substantially in design, measurement, and terminology. A protocol-constrained search optimized for one question would have fragmented rather than integrated this literature. The tradeoff is reduced transparency of selection, revisited in §8.1.

3.2 Sources and Search

Searches were conducted in PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science. Search terms combined ('autism spectrum disorder' OR 'autism' OR 'ASD') with domain-specific terms in each of the ten thematic areas; the search strategy, comprising the core terms and the domain-specific term sets, is documented in Supplementary Material S2. Reference lists of retained reviews and meta-analyses were hand-searched for additional sources. Searches were conducted by a single reviewer without a pre-registered protocol; selection criteria were applied consistently but were not formalized prospectively, and this is stated as a limitation in §8.1.

3.3 Eligibility Criteria

Sources were included if published in peer-reviewed journals between 2006 and 2025; reporting an empirical or synthesized association between a candidate factor and ASD diagnosis, symptom severity, or a prospectively validated ASD-predictive marker; using human samples; and written in English or Spanish. Sources were excluded if they were animal-model studies without human validation, except where cited solely to describe a proposed mechanism and identified as such; case reports or series with fewer than ten participants; reports of general developmental delay without ASD-specific outcome ascertainment; studies of intervention efficacy rather than risk, marker, or mechanism; or conference abstracts, dissertations, or non-peer-reviewed preprints. Where publications reported overlapping samples, the most recent or comprehensive was retained.

3.4 Classification of Sources

Each retained source was mapped to one of 75 candidate risk factors and, through those factors, to a primary MERA domain and an evidential tier as defined in §5.1. Sources were further classified along two dimensions. The first was study design, in six categories: systematic review or meta-analysis; quantitative observational study, encompassing cohort, registry, prospective longitudinal, and case-control designs; theoretical or narrative review; qualitative study; idiographic or singlecase study; and mixed-methods study. The second was journal indexing quartile at retrieval. Where a journal was indexed in more than one database, the quartile from the database applying the more selective inclusion standard was retained, with Web of Science treated as the reference standard; 144 of the 152 pairings were classified from Web of Science and the remainder from Scopus or PubMed. Indexing quartile does not constitute formal risk-of-bias assessment and is not presented as such; it is a coarse but transparent indicator of publication standard, to be weighed alongside the design distribution. Correspondence between each source and the claim it was cited to support was independently verified before inclusion. The classification is reported in Tables 1 and 2 and, at factor level, in Supplementary Table S1.

3.5 Interpretive Scope of the Synthesis

Because sources were not weighted by methodological quality and no pooled effect estimation was performed, the counts reported in §4 index the volume of available evidence per domain rather than the strength of association. They should be read as a map of where evidence has accumulated, not as a ranking of effect magnitude. A domain supported by many cross-sectional studies may carry less inferential weight than a domain supported by a single well-powered registry analysis. Moving from volume to weight would require three steps not undertaken here: formal risk-of-bias appraisal of each source using design-appropriate instruments, extraction of comparable effect estimates with their precision, and random-effects pooling within factors where the underlying constructs are commensurable. These steps constitute the planned second phase of the present research programme, and the domain weights they would yield are a precondition for any scoring algorithm derived from the framework. This distinction governs the interpretation of Figure 1 and is revisited in §8.1.

5. A Multidomain Early Risk Architecture (MERA) for ASD

MERA organizes empirically supported ASD-relevant signals into ten distinct but interrelated domains. This is not a checklist of isolated predictors but a framework grounded in the premise that detection-relevant information is distributed across developmental windows and differs systematically in the inference it supports (Figure 2).

5.1 Evidential Status of MERA Components: A Four-Tier Taxonomy

The components organized within MERA are not epistemologically homogeneous. Some are antecedent exposures that cannot, by temporal logic, be consequences of the condition. Others are early behavioural divergences that prospectively predict later diagnosis. Others still are features of the diagnostic phenotype itself, or associated conditions whose direction of effect remains contested. Treating these as a single category of risk factor would be conceptually imprecise and clinically misleading. A four-tier classification of evidential status is therefore introduced, applied transversally across the ten domains.

Tier I -Antecedent risk markers.

Exposures or characteristics established before the developmental period in which an ASD phenotype could manifest, and which therefore cannot be consequences of the condition. This tier comprises family psychiatric and neurological history and genetic syndromes (D1), obstetric and perinatal conditions (D2), prenatal toxic and pharmacological exposures (D3), congenital and neonatal medical events (part of D4), and antenatal maternal stress and depression (prenatal component of D10). Evidence derives predominantly from population registries, birth cohorts, and genetically informed designs.

Function: risk stratification before any behavioural observation is possible.

Tier II -Early prodromal markers. Behavioural or developmental divergences that emerge before the diagnostic threshold is reached and that prospectively predict later ASD outcome in longitudinal designs with pre-diagnostic measurement. This tier comprises reduced canonical babbling and motor developmental delay (D5), diminished response to name, atypical gaze dynamics, and reduced joint attention (D6), and early repetitive engagement with objects (infant component of D7). Evidence derives from prospective infant-sibling cohorts. Function: extension of the detection window earlier than the eighteen-month anchor of current instruments. These markers are behavioural, not pre-behavioural; their value lies in timing rather than modality.

Tier III -Concurrent clinical features.

Manifestations of the ASD phenotype itself, including features that constitute DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. This tier comprises consolidated restricted and repetitive behaviours (D7) and atypical sensory reactivity (D8, corresponding to DSM-5 criterion B4). Evidence derives from case-control and cross-sectional designs. Function: case identification and phenotypic characterisation. These features are explicitly not risk factors; their inclusion carries no aetiological claim.

Tier IV -Associated conditions of uncertain or bidirectional directionality.

Conditions cooccurring with ASD at elevated rates whose direction of effect is contested, plausibly reciprocal, or confounded by shared antecedents. This tier comprises epilepsy, anaemia, and autoimmune conditions (part of D4), gastrointestinal dysfunction and food selectivity (D9), and postnatal maternal depression, adverse childhood experiences, and early screen exposure (postnatal component of D10). Function: clinical flags that raise the index of suspicion and warrant comprehensive assessment, without supporting causal attribution.

The classification invites an objection that is better stated than left implicit. If the components of Tiers III and IV are not risk factors-if some are diagnostic criteria and others are of contested directionality-what remains of a framework designated as a risk architecture? And is it not circular to detect autism partly by observing autism?

The objection rests on a conflation of two functions that clinical detection instruments routinely combine, and the combination is neither novel nor problematic. The neonatal early-onset sepsis calculator is an instructive precedent: it establishes a prior probability of infection from antenatal and intrapartum maternal factors alone-gestational age, maternal intrapartum temperature, duration of membrane rupture, group B streptococcus status, and intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (Puopolo et al., 2011; Escobar et al., 2014)-and then updates that prior against the infant's concurrent clinical examination, classified as well-appearing, equivocal, or clinically ill, to yield a posterior probability that guides management (Kuzniewicz et al., 2017). No one regards this two-stage structure as circular. The antecedent information identifies which infants warrant closer observation; the concurrent signs determine what that observation finds. The two classes of information answer different questions and are combined by design rather than by conceptual error.

MERA operates on the same logic, and the four-tier taxonomy makes the division of labour explicit. Tiers I and II perform an anticipatory function: they identify elevated probability before a consolidated phenotype exists-Tier I before any behaviour can be observed, and Tier II before the diagnostic threshold is reached. Tiers III and IV perform a confirmatory and characterising function: in a child already flagged by the preceding tiers, they raise posterior probability and describe the presentation. What the framework prohibits-and what an unclassified list of risk factors would fail to prohibit-is the use of Tier III or IV information as evidence of aetiological risk, or its inclusion in a prospective risk calculation as though it were antecedent.

The contribution should therefore be stated precisely.

It does not lie in the individual factors, most of which are separately established. It lies in three things: the temporal stratification of heterogeneous signals according to when each becomes clinically observable; the explicit specification of what inference each class can bear; and the proposal that these signals be aggregated into a single evolving risk profile rather than evaluated independently at one screening point. Whether that aggregation improves detection is an empirical question this paper does not claim to answer.

5.2 Domain Overlap and Cross-Listing

Overlap between domains is genuine and warrants explicit treatment. Several factors appear substantively in more than one domain: oral-motor and swallowing difficulties bridge early medical conditions (D4) and feeding (D9); food texture rejection bridges sensory reactivity (D8) and feeding selectivity (D9); dietary exposure to additives and preserved foods bridges toxic exposure (D3) and feeding (D9); maternal depression bridges perinatal conditions (D2) and psychosocial context (D10); gesture and motor imitation bridge psychomotor development (D5) and social behaviour (D6); and visual fascination with repetitive movement bridges restricted behaviours (D7) and sensory processing (D8).

This overlap is informative rather than a defect of the classification. The principle of interactive amplification set out in §6.1 predicts precisely this pattern: where shared biological pathways link domains, phenotypic expression should cross domain boundaries. Domain overlap is on this reading a corollary of the model rather than an inconsistency within it. It nonetheless carries a measurement consequence the framework must address directly. In any instrument derived from MERA, cross-listed factors must be assigned to a single primary domain to prevent doublecounting and consequent inflation of cumulative risk estimates. Supplementary Table S1 records primary domain assignment for each cross-listed factor, with secondary domains indicated parenthetically. This assignment is provisional: the empirical structure of the domains is a matter for exploratory factor analysis in validation samples, and the factor solution obtained in such samples is expected to depart from the conceptual organization proposed here.

5.3 Specificity: ASD-Specific and Transdiagnostic Signals

A substantial proportion of the antecedent factors organized within MERA are not specific to ASD, and the evidence base assembled here documents this directly. Several of the retained sources report the same exposure against both ASD and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) outcomes: prenatal maternal stress (Manzari et al., 2019), caesarean delivery (Chen et al., 2024), early-onset neonatal infection (Andersen et al., 2025), maternal and early-life antibiotic exposure (Njotto et al., 2023), congenital hypothyroidism (Lin et al., 2024), prenatal paracetamol exposure (Bauer et al., 2018; Patel et al., 2022), and dietary mercury exposure (O' Connor et al., 2025).

Anaemia is associated with ASD, ADHD, and learning disability in the same nationally representative sample (Yang et al., 2021), and diminished response to name-a Tier II markerhas been shown to index risk for attention problems more broadly (Hatch et al., 2021). Presenting such factors as ASD-specific would misrepresent the evidence.

MERA is therefore characterized more precisely as a neurodevelopmental risk architecture with ASD-oriented weighting rather than as an ASD-exclusive model. This is not a concession forced

5.4 Domain 1: Genetic and Family History

Tier I. The components of this domain are antecedent by definition: family history and genetic constitution precede the child's development and cannot be consequences of an emerging phenotype. Evidence derives predominantly from population registries and genetically informed designs; 12 of the 15 sources in this domain are quartile-1 publications.

The heritability of ASD is among the highest of any neurodevelopmental condition, with twin studies estimating monozygotic concordance of 64-91%. Its genetic architecture is highly polygenic, involving hundreds of common variants of small effect alongside rare de novo mutations, and a critical implication is that genetic risk extends well beyond ASD itself to a broad neuropsychiatric phenotype.

Population-based studies demonstrate that family history of ADHD is associated with elevated ASD likelihood, consistent with shared genetic aetiology (Rommelse et al., 2010; van Steijn et al., 2012). Familial aggregation of language disorders (Kiruthika & Tiwari, 2022), anxiety and depression (Duvekot et al., 2016; Ghirardi et al., 2021), and epilepsy is similarly documented. A Swedish registry study covering over two million individuals reported that family history of mental and neurological disorders at multiple degrees of kinship was associated with elevated ASD likelihood, with the association attenuating as genetic relatedness decreased (Xie et al., 2019). For detection purposes, a detailed family psychiatric and neurological history-extending beyond the nuclear family to ADHD, language disorders, epilepsy, affective disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions-is an underutilized, zero-cost risk indicator.

5.5 Domain 2: Prenatal and Perinatal Conditions

Tier I, with one component of mixed status. Obstetric and perinatal conditions are antecedent by temporal logic. Maternal depression is split: antenatal depression is Tier I, whereas postpartum depression is assigned to Tier IV, since an emerging infant phenotype may plausibly contribute to maternal depressive symptoms. This is the largest domain in the evidence base ( 26pairings across 12 factors) and among the most heavily supported by pooled estimates: 11 of its sources are systematic reviews or meta-analyses. Advanced maternal age is among the most replicated findings in this domain. Al-Salihy (2025) reported that maternal age was the dominant predictor in an ecological model of ASD prevalence across two decades of population data; the design is ecological and the estimate should not be read as an individual-level effect. Busque et al. (2022) reported an adjusted odds ratio of 2.22 for maternal age of 35 years or above in a cohort restricted to infants born before 29 weeks of gestation; the association is therefore established within an extremely preterm population and its generalisation to term births is not established by that study. Advanced paternal age independently contributes through accumulation of de novo mutations in spermatogonial cells (Bozkurt et al., 2025), who also reported that paternal age and a history of hypoxia or asphyxia were associated with greater symptom severity rather than diagnosis alone. Song, A. Y. et al. (2022) showed that accelerated parental epigenetic age-biological rather than chronological-was associated with lower cognitive outcomes and elevated ASD likelihood, while decelerated paternal epigenetic ageing was associated with reduced likelihood. Caesarean delivery has received increasing attention. A meta-analysis of 35 studies found a significant association with ASD (OR = 1.25), particularly pronounced for caesarean with general anaesthesia (OR = 1.62), and reported a parallel association with ADHD (Chen et al., 2024). Liu et al. (2022) confirmed this in over 1.4 million low-risk California births, estimating that up to 10% of cases may be attributable to unnecessary caesarean delivery. Proposed mechanisms include microbiome disruption, anaesthetic exposure, and altered stress hormone activation. Gestational hypertension and preeclampsia represent another well-documented area, and the evidence distinguishes between the two more sharply than the domain label suggests. Curran et al. (2018) reported approximately double the odds of ASD in offspring of mothers with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy in the UK Millennium Cohort. Maher et al. (2020), in a Swedish population-based study, reported an elevation of the order of 25 percent for preeclampsia specifically. Separately, and in an experimental rather than epidemiological design, Curran et al. (2018) observed that serum from preeclamptic pregnancies altered neuronal growth in vitro; this is consistent with a neurobiological pathway but does not establish that it accounts for the epidemiological association. Xie, Q. et al. (2020) identified 156 genes common to preeclampsia and ASD, including RPS4Y1, proposed as relevant to male preponderance. The heterogeneity of estimates across hypertensive conditions suggests these may warrant separate treatment in a later iteration (see §8.2).

Gestational diabetes mellitus is associated with a 30-31 percent increase in ASD likelihood across meta-analyses (Garza-Martínez et al., 2025; Guo, D. et al., 2025), with risk amplified when pharmacological treatment was required during pregnancy. Gestational haemorrhage is associated with over a threefold elevation (Arafa et al., 2022) and is identified as a significant perinatal factor in the comprehensive meta-analysis of Gardener et al. (2011). Additional perinatal conditions associated with elevated ASD likelihood include prolonged pregnancy beyond 42 weeks (Persson et al., 2020; Jenabi et al., 2023), low Apgar score at five minutes (Salehi et al., 2024; He et al., 2021), and low birth weight (Guo, B.-Q. et al., 2024). Small size for gestational age is associated with elevated likelihood in the meta-analysis of Jenabi et al. (2021) and in the Finnish register study of Lampi et al. (2012), discussed in §5.3. Villamor et al. (2022) examined defective placentation syndromes in a Swedish cohort of 1,645,455 infants with a sibling-controlled analysis of 1,092,132, reporting adjusted hazard ratios up to 1.9 where small size for gestational age co-occurred with preeclampsia; the sibling design provides partial control for shared familial confounding.

Maternal depression during and after pregnancy is associated with elevated ASD likelihood in the offspring. Tusa et al. (2025) synthesised 12 studies comprising more than 1.6 million mother-child pairs, reporting elevations for pregestational (OR 1.52), antenatal (OR 1.48), and postnatal (OR 1.70) depression. Hagberg et al. (2018) reported elevated risk for both treated and untreated depression (RR 1.72 and 1.50), while antidepressant use for other indications showed none, with a sibling comparison that attenuated but did not eliminate the association-pointing to biological mechanisms rather than medication effects. Peltier et al. (2025), in a cohort of 297,720, reported a hazard ratio of 1.57 for postpartum depression. As set out in §5.1, the antenatal and postnatal components of this factor are assigned to different tiers: an emerging infant phenotype may plausibly contribute to postpartum depressive symptoms, and the postnatal association cannot be read as antecedent. Maternal hypothyroidism during pregnancy has also been implicated, with Kaplan et al. (2024) reviewing the mechanistic and epidemiological evidence linking thyroid hormone deficiency during critical fetal cortical development windows to ASD risk.

5.6 Domain 3: Prenatal Substance and Toxic Exposures

Tier I. Prenatal exposures are antecedent by definition. This domain also contains the components of the framework most plausibly modifiable through public health action, and is therefore of particular relevance to prevention as distinct from detection. Ten of its 21 pairings derive from systematic reviews or meta-analyses, the highest proportion of any domain.

The developing brain is highly sensitive to exogenous chemical exposures during critical periods of prenatal neurogenesis, migration, and synaptogenesis.

5.7 Domain 4: Early Medical Conditions

Mixed: Tier I and Tier IV. Congenital and neonatal events-prematurity, neonatal infection, congenital hypothyroidism-are antecedent and belong to Tier I. Epilepsy, anaemia, and autoimmune conditions are assigned to Tier IV: their temporal relation to ASD is frequently concurrent or subsequent, and reverse or reciprocal pathways are plausible.

Prematurity is one of the most robust medical risk factors.

Chang et al. (2023) demonstrated in a nationwide Taiwanese cohort that infants born before 28 weeks had a fourfold elevated risk of ASD with intellectual disability, rising progressively as gestational age decreased; Guo et al. (2024) confirmed this by meta-analysis. Nagai et al. (2024) documented that primitive reflex persistence in very low birth weight infants was associated with later diagnosis, suggesting a neurological pathway.

Severe bacterial infections with hospitalization have been associated with a hazard ratio of 13.80 for ASD (Hsu et al., 2022). Culture-confirmed early neonatal sepsis and meningitis are associated with a hazard ratio of 1.43 in a Danish nationwide cohort, persisting after sibling-controlled analyses and with a parallel ADHD association (Andersen et al., 2025). Proposed mechanisms involve neuroinflammation, blood-brain barrier disruption, and altered glial function during critical developmental periods.

Early antibiotic use has attracted attention as a candidate exposure mediated by gut microbiome disruption, but the evidence separates by developmental window in a way that constrains Congenital hypothyroidism is associated with a nearly fivefold elevated ASD risk (Lin et al., 2024), consistent with the role of thyroid hormones in fetal cortical development (Uchida & Suzuki, 2021). Anaemia warrants a more careful statement than it has commonly received. Yang et al. (2021), analysing 213,893 children aged 3-17 in the National Health Interview Survey, found that children with a parent-reported physician diagnosis of anaemia had approximately twice the odds of an ASD diagnosis (aOR 2.07, 95% CI 1.39-3.08), with elevated odds of ADHD and learning disability. The design is cross-sectional and both exposure and outcome are parentreported, so the estimate establishes co-occurrence rather than temporal precedence, and the factor is assigned to Tier IV on that basis. The distinct proposition that iron deficiency may alter brain structure before anaemia becomes manifest derives from Fiani et al. (2023), which addresses mechanisms-principally dopamine synthesis and myelination-rather than an ASD-specific effect estimate. Autoimmune conditions are more prevalent in ASD (Villarreal et al., 2024), and the broader neonatal risk landscape is synthesized by Salehi et al. (2024). Swallowing and feeding difficulties in early infancy (Ausderau et al., 2024; Şahan et al., 2021) intersect with Domain 9.

5.8 Domain 5: Psychomotor and Language Development

Tier II, with concurrent components. Motor delay, reduced canonical babbling, and regression are prospectively measured before diagnosis in infant-sibling cohorts and constitute prodromal markers. Echolalia, perseverative speech, and comprehension difficulties are concurrent features that presuppose a developmental level not present in infancy, and belong to Tier III.

The assumption that ASD is primarily a disorder of social communication has obscured the extent to which motor development is systematically atypical from early infancy. Prospective studies using standardized motor assessments have consistently documented delays in sitting, crawling, and walking at rates significantly higher than population norms. Hedgecock et al. (2018) demonstrated that gross motor delay modulates quality of life and behavioral outcomes. Gabis et al. (2020) showed that motor delay is a more frequent early red flag in girls than previously recognized, with implications for the female phenotype, whose social communication deficits are often subtler-making motor atypicality a more accessible early marker in this population. Stachura et al. (2025), using the MABC-2, confirmed coordination, balance, and fine motor deficits in children without co-occurring conditions. Fine motor difficulties are independently associated with severity: Bhat (2023) showed in the SPARK dataset that fine motor performance predicts social communication, language, and functional delay and repetitive behavior severity after controlling for intellectual disability, and Peyre et al. (2024), in the French ELENA cohort, showed longitudinally that motor difficulties predict less favorable social skill trajectories over three years.

Canonical babbling has emerged as one of the most temporally precise prodromal markers available. Long et al. (2024), in a prospective study using monthly all-day recordings from birth to 13 months, found reduced canonical babbling ratios from approximately 9 months in infants later diagnosed with ASD, with the effect concentrated among boys; girls later diagnosed did not show reduced rates relative to neurotypical girls, and the sex-specific character of this marker constrains its use as a universal screening indicator. Yankowitz et al. (2022), in a large IBIS Network sample, found significantly lower canonical babbling ratios at 12 months in infants subsequently diagnosed, while explicitly cautioning that group-level differences do not establish sufficient discriminative accuracy for canonical babbling to function as a stand-alone marker. Both findings are consistent with the framework's cumulative logic: individual Tier II markers are proposed as components of an accumulating profile rather than as independent screening tests.

Language delays are central features of the early phenotype. Reindal et al. (2023) found delayed first words and structural language impairment associated with greater pragmatic communication deficits in children evaluated for ASD. The neurobiological correlates of language ability have been characterised by Lv et al. (2025), who identified specific white-matter differences associated with language ability in Chinese boys with ASD; that study concerns the neural substrate of language ability in already-diagnosed children and does not address the age of first words, and is cited here for the former claim only. Language regression-the loss of previously acquired language and social skills, typically between 15 and 24 months-is observed in approximately 20-30% of children later diagnosed and is associated with greater symptom severity and worse longterm outcomes (Hu et al., 2022; Reyes et al., 2024). Kaba & Arıcı Canlı (2024) identified risk factors for regression including febrile convulsions, family psychiatric history, and caesarean delivery, demonstrating cross-domain interactions between the medical, genetic, and language domains.

Motor imitation intersects motor and social domains. Iao et al. (2024) showed longitudinally in a Taiwanese sample that manual motor imitation predicts concurrent and later language development; Lidstone & Mostofsky (2021) proposed that visual-motor integration deficits underlie both imitation failures and broader social difficulties; Latrèche et al. (2024) showed less face-looking during imitation tasks; and Taylan et al. (2022) found that worse imitation of meaningful gestures predicts greater severity and reduced intention understanding.

Echolalia is present in up to 90% of verbal children with ASD at some stage. McAllister et al.

(2025) documented its prevalence and function through parent, teacher, and clinician reports, and Pruccoli et al. (2021) argued for reconceptualizing it as a phonic stereotypy with adaptive communicative function. Perseverative speech has been studied as an interactional phenomenon (Emborg, 2022), with behavioral treatment approaches documented by Migan-Gandonou Horr & Michael (2021). Difficulty comprehending complex verbal instructions has been documented by Murata et al. (2020) and De Luca et al. ( 2025), who showed improved comprehension with multisensory interfaces.

5.9 Domain 6: Early Social Behavior

Tier II. The markers in this domain are behavioural and therefore observable, but they emerge months before the diagnostic threshold is reached and are measured prospectively in highlikelihood infant cohorts. Their contribution lies in timing rather than in modality. Emotion recognition is an exception, belonging to Tier III. Physical contact avoidance has neurobiological grounding. Kadlaskar et al. (2019) showed that infants at high likelihood for ASD show atypical responses to caregiver touch, with reduced or aversive responses predictive of later social communication difficulties, and Smirni et al. (2019) reviewed tactile defensiveness in neurodevelopmental disorders, placing physical contact avoidance at the intersection of social and sensory domains.

5.10 Domain 7: Restricted and Repetitive Behaviors

Mixed: Tier II and Tier III. Repetitive engagement with objects at nine months is prospectively predictive and belongs to Tier II. Consolidated restricted and repetitive behaviours constitute DSM-5 criterion B and belong to Tier III: they are features of the diagnostic phenotype and are not risk factors. Their function within the framework is confirmatory, not anticipatory.

5.11 Domain 8: Atypical Sensory Processing

Tier III. Sensory hyper-and hyporeactivity corresponds to DSM-5 criterion B4 and is therefore constitutive of the diagnostic phenotype rather than antecedent to it. Its inclusion reflects utility for case identification and phenotypic characterisation, and carries no aetiological claim.

Sensory processing differences are present in an estimated 69-93% of individuals with ASD and, while not included as a diagnostic criterion until DSM-5, are increasingly recognized as a central feature of the condition rather than a secondary symptom. Scheerer et al. (2021) identified five distinct sensory phenotypes through cluster analysis, including a taste-and-smell sensitivity phenotype, supporting the view that sensory differences are heterogeneous across the spectrum and require domain-specific assessment.

Auditory hypersensitivity affects approximately 41% of individuals with ASD at any given time and over 60% across the lifespan (Williams et al., 2021a), and is associated with anxiety, behavioral dysregulation, and social avoidance. Williams et al. (2021b) reviewed three subtypeshyperacusis, misophonia, phonophobia-and their mechanisms. Costa et al. (2022) found that over 60% of children with clinical ASD signs presented parental-reported auditory hypersensitivity to specific sounds, with direct implications for caregiver-report screening. Visual sensitivity to bright or flickering lights is documented through pupillary response studies (Hayakawa et al., 2025; Soker-Elimaliah et al., 2022) and behavioral observation (Nair et al., 2022). Olfactory hypersensitivity has neurobiological grounding:

Johnson et al. (2023) linked altered GABA/glutamate ratios in amygdala-hippocampus and cerebellum with poorer olfactory processing and greater social symptom severity, and Panerai et al. (2020) confirmed elevated taste and smell sensitivity in ASD children with feeding problems.

Tactile defensiveness has been examined in a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study reporting genetic-instrument evidence consistent with a causal relationship between disturbance of skin sensation and ASD liability (Zhong et al., 2023).

Mendelian randomization supports causal inference only under the standard instrumental-variable assumptions of relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction, and the finding should be read within those constraints rather than as demonstration of causation. Baweja et al. (2023) documented high prevalence of exaggerated physical reactions, including self-injury, in ASD compared to ADHD and population controls, while Ferguson et al. (2025) reviewed the mechanisms underlying self-injurious behaviours in ASD with profound intellectual disability. Crucially, sensory hypersensitivity in infants is observable and reportable by caregivers before behavioral and communication signs emerge; the inclusion of sensory markers therefore extends the detection window and captures a dimension of ASD that is both clinically significant and informative about underlying neurocircuitry differences.

5.12 Domain 9: Feeding and Gastrointestinal Functioning

Tier IV. Gastrointestinal dysfunction and food selectivity co-occur with ASD at elevated rates, but the direction of effect is contested and in several instances explicitly reciprocal. These components function as clinical flags warranting comprehensive assessment; they do not support aetiological attribution and should not enter a prospective risk calculation as antecedent factors. With 7 pairings across 3 factors this is the smallest domain in the evidence base.

5.13 Domain 10: Psychosocial and Environmental Factors

Mixed: Tier I and Tier IV. Antenatal maternal stress and depression are antecedent and belong to Tier I. Attachment quality, adverse childhood experiences, and early screen exposure are assigned to Tier IV: for each, reverse causation is plausible, since an emerging phenotype may shape dyadic interaction, vulnerability to adversity, and patterns of screen use.

Maternal prenatal stress is associated with increased ASD risk across studies and meta-analyses, with parallel ADHD associations (Manzari et al., 2019), and effects are most pronounced with severe or chronic first-trimester stress (Varcin et al., 2017; Alamoudi et al., 2023). and in response to stressors, with the direction of deviation varying by stressor type. This is relevant in two respects: it provides a candidate neuroendocrine pathway complementing the three axes in Figure 3, and it illustrates the directionality problem defining Tier IV, since atypical stress-system function in a child already showing the phenotype cannot be read as evidence that stress exposure contributed to it.

Attachment security is disrupted in a significant proportion of children later diagnosed. Martin et al. (2020) reported that insecure-resistant attachment at 12 months was associated with a ninefold elevated probability of subsequent diagnosis, while secure attachment predicts better language and social outcomes among those diagnosed (Rozga et al., 2018), indicating that attachment quality moderates trajectories even within ASD. Papageorgopoulou et al. (2024) showed that parent-infant interaction trajectories-reduced sensitivity and mutuality from as early as 8 months-predict 3year clinical outcomes, making early interaction a potentially modifiable indicator, and Gao et al.

(2024) found a negative dose-response relationship between caregiver-child interactive behavior frequency and autistic-like behavior severity at age three.

Early screen exposure before age two has received increasing empirical attention. Sarfraz et al.

(2023) systematically reviewed the evidence, concluding that greater duration and earlier onset are associated with higher likelihood of diagnosis; Liu et al. (2025) reported a comparable association in meta-analysis; Peng et al. (2024) described dose-response relationships between screen time and symptom severity; and Sadeghi et al. (2023) found that both foreground and background screen time were associated with greater severity in toddlers aged 16 to 36 months. These findings are consistent with two interpretations the available designs cannot distinguish. Screen exposure may amplify neurodevelopmental vulnerability by displacing reciprocal social interaction; alternatively, and equally consistent with the data, an emerging autistic phenotype may itself increase screen engagement, whether through the child's preference for predictable visual stimuli or through caregivers' use of screens to manage behavioural difficulty. This component is accordingly assigned to Tier IV, and no causal reading is advanced.

6. The MERA Model: Theoretical Integration 6.1 Three Organizing Principles as Falsifiable Hypotheses

The ten domains described above are not independent risk factors to be summed in a linear checklist. MERA conceives of ASD vulnerability as an emergent property of cumulative, interactive risk across biological, developmental, environmental, and relational dimensions. Three theoretical principles organize the model, as illustrated in Figure 3.

The principle of cumulative vulnerability holds that the probability of ASD increases non-linearly as risk factors accumulate across domains. A child who experienced prenatal paracetamol exposure, maternal gestational hypertension, perinatal hypoxia, neonatal infection requiring hospitalization, and early microbiome disruption occupies a qualitatively different risk position than a child with any one of these factors in isolation. The principle of temporal stratification recognizes that risk factors operate across distinct developmental windows with differential neurological sensitivity: prenatal toxic exposures during the first trimester affect cortical neurogenesis; third-trimester obstetric complications affect migration and synaptogenesis; neonatal medical events affect early circuit formation; and early psychosocial factors shape attachment and social brain development. The principle of interactive amplification holds that risk factors across domains potentiate each other through shared biological pathwaysneuroinflammation, epigenetic reprogramming, and microbiome-brain signaling-rather than merely through statistical artifact.

The three principles are advanced as testable propositions rather than as established findings. No dataset currently exists in which all ten domains have been measured within a single cohort, and consequently none of the three has been tested in the integrated form proposed here. Each generates a specific and falsifiable prediction. Cumulative vulnerability predicts that a multiplicative or threshold model of cross-domain accumulation will outperform an additive model in predicting ASD outcome in prospective cohorts; the cumulative-risk tradition in developmental psychopathology provides established analytic methods for such model comparison, and if an additive model performs equivalently or better, the principle is refuted. Temporal stratification predicts that the predictive weight of a given factor will vary systematically as a function of the developmental window in which it occurs-that first-trimester and third-trimester exposure to the same agent will carry measurably different effect sizes-and is refuted by the finding that timing does not moderate effect. Interactive amplification predicts statistical interaction terms of specified sign between designated domain pairs: between polygenic risk (D1) and prenatal toxicant exposure (D3); between early antibiotic exposure (D4) and gastrointestinal dysfunction (D9); and between caregiver-infant interaction quality (D10) and sensory reactivity (D8). These are testable as interaction effects in adequately powered cohorts, and their absence refutes the principle. The principles are the model's hypotheses, not its findings, and the framework should be evaluated on the coherence and testability of those hypotheses rather than on evidence it does not yet possess.

6.2 Relationship to Existing Theoretical Frameworks

MERA is theoretically consonant with several existing frameworks in developmental psychopathology. The multiple-hit hypothesis, originally formulated for schizophrenia, holds that neurodevelopmental disorders result from the accumulation of multiple genetic and environmental insults rather than from a single causal event, with an early priming insult increasing vulnerability to a subsequent one (Bayer et al., 1999; Maynard et al., 2001). Applied to ASD, this model predicts the phenotypic heterogeneity observed clinically and the variable penetrance of individual risk factors across individuals. Developmental systems theory, formalized by Gottlieb (2007) as probabilistic epigenesis, emphasizes bidirectional influence within and between levels of an organism's developmental manifold-genetic activity, neural activity, behavior, and the physical, social, and cultural environment-providing a metatheoretical foundation for integrating genetic and environmental domains within a single model. The developmental psychopathology framework of Cicchetti and Cohen (2006) emphasizes that understanding atypical development requires mapping the pathways through which normative development is diverted at specific developmental junctures, which is precisely what the temporal stratification principle formalizes.

6.3 Added Value Relative to Existing Developmental Surveillance

A reasonable objection to MERA is that general developmental surveillance already captures much of what Tier II describes. The Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ-3), the Survey of Well-being of Young Children (SWYC), and the periodic surveillance recommended by paediatric guidelines all evaluate gross and fine motor milestones, expressive and receptive language, and elements of social-emotional development from the first year of life. The added value of MERA must therefore be stated precisely rather than assumed.

Three contributions can be identified. The first, and most substantive, concerns Tier I. These data are already generated in routine perinatal and paediatric care-obstetric records, birth parameters, neonatal event logs, family history-but are not aggregated into any developmental risk profile and are not consulted when a concern subsequently arises. MERA proposes integrating information that already exists in the clinical record, a substantially lower implementation burden than new data collection. The second concerns sequencing: MERA specifies which classes of signal are informative at which window, permitting temporally stratified rather than uniform surveillance, whereas existing instruments are milestone-referenced but not risk-stratified. The third concerns scoring logic: cumulative cross-tier accumulation rather than independent pass-or-fail evaluation of discrete milestones. Where Tier II overlaps with existing surveillance, this is an implementation asset rather than redundancy, indicating that a substantial component can be operationalized through instruments already embedded in primary care-a consideration of importance in health systems, including Chile's, where parallel screening infrastructure is not realistic.

6.4 Implications for Instrument Development and Predictive Modeling

Rather than a single behavioral checklist at 18 months, MERA suggests tiered surveillance in which domains are assessed at windows corresponding to their biological and behavioral salience.

Prenatally and at birth, Domains 1-3 can be assessed through routine antenatal records and family history; during the first year, Domains 4-6, 8, and 10 become assessable through caregiver report and structured surveillance; after 12 months, Domains 6, 7, and 9 become fully assessable through both caregiver report and direct observation. This would enable progressive accumulation of a multidomain risk profile, earlier identification before behavioral signs consolidate, and more targeted use of specialist resources. It also opens the path toward predictive models-weighted risk calculators, machine learning classifiers, or Bayesian networks-trained on prospective multidomain data, moving beyond screening toward genuine risk stratification.

7. Clinical and Public Health Implications

>Clinical and Public Health Implications

7.1 Primary Care Practice

MERA has concrete implications for primary care that can be implemented without waiting for new validated instruments. Paediatricians, midwives, and family practitioners managing prenatal care and well-child visits are positioned to collect multidomain risk information across Domains 1-4 as part of standard documentation. Integrating a brief family neuropsychiatric history, systematic recording of obstetric complications, and standardized neonatal records into a developmental risk profile would cost little clinical time but could substantially advance the detection timeline.

Identification of multiple risk factors across Domains 1-4 could trigger enhanced surveillance in Domains 5-10 during the first two years-a tiered approach analogous to risk-stratified surveillance in neonatology.

7.2 The Chilean Context

In Chile, the epidemiological and policy context makes early ASD detection an urgent public health priority. Diagnostic delays of two to four years from first parental concern to formal diagnosis are common, meaning children who could benefit from early intervention are frequently not identified until school age, when the neuroplasticity window is substantially reduced. The Chilean public health system conducts universal developmental assessments during the first years of life through primary care; these contact points represent existing infrastructure that could be leveraged for multidomain risk surveillance without requiring a parallel specialist system. The Chilean context also introduces locally relevant risk modifiers-industrial contamination in specific regions, high consumption of processed and canned foods, patterns of maternal healthcare access, and psychosocial stressors associated with economic inequality and migration-warranting investigation in Chilean cohort studies.

7.3 Ethical Considerations

Ethical implementation of any MERA-derived instrument must be equity-oriented, avoiding stigmatization of families whose risk circumstances reflect social determinants rather than individual failures. The probabilistic nature of risk must be clearly communicated: multiple factors increase probability but do not determine outcome, and many high-likelihood children will not develop ASD. Premature labeling without careful clinical assessment risks unnecessary parental anxiety and unjustified intervention.

8. Discussion

The framework reorganizes a body of evidence that is largely established but not previously integrated. Its claim is not that the factors are new, but that their temporal stratification, classification by evidential status, and aggregation into one evolving profile constitute a detection architecture current instruments do not provide. This section sets out the limitations qualifying that claim and the agenda following from it.

8.1 Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged transparently. First, the evidence base was organized through a narrative rather than a systematic review with formal PRISMA procedures, which introduces the possibility of selection bias and limits the precision with which differential weights can be assigned across domains. For a theoretical integration across ten heterogeneous domains, a narrative approach permits the conceptual synthesis that a strict PRISMA-constrained review would preclude-a tradeoff that is methodologically defensible at this stage of model development -but searches were conducted by a single reviewer without a pre-registered protocol, and sources that were never retrieved could not be assessed. Future work should formalize the search strategy through prospective registration and apply the meta-analytic procedures described in §3.5.

Second, the empirical literature underlying MERA draws predominantly on population cohort studies from Northern European, North American, and East Asian contexts; the applicability of the proposed domain structure and weights to Latin American populations-including Chile, where this research programme is anchored-remains to be empirically established, and cultural, dietary, and environmental factors specific to these contexts may warrant additional domains or item reformulation.

Third, MERA is at present a conceptual architecture rather than a validated psychometric instrument. While the ten-domain structure is empirically grounded, its clinical utility-including sensitivity, specificity, and predictive value-cannot be confirmed until prospective cohort studies with paired samples test the framework against behavioural outcomes. This limitation is deliberate and methodologically appropriate: conceptual frameworks precede instrumentation, and the framework was developed explicitly as the foundation for a structured validation programme. A multidomain caregiver-report instrument derived from the framework has been constructed and has entered content validation through expert judgement. It includes the factors identified in §4.4 as provisionally supported; this is deliberate, since the expert-judgement procedure and the subsequent factor-analytic stage are designed to evaluate rather than to presuppose the adequacy of each item, and factors that fail to meet retention criteria will be removed before the instrument enters predictive validation.

Fourth, the proposed cross-domain interactions-the amplification pathways mediated by neuroinflammation, the gut-brain microbiome axis, and epigenetic reprogramming-are grounded in converging biological evidence but remain inferential at the level of individual domain combinations; their formal characterisation will require mechanistic studies and computational modelling on prospective datasets. Fifth, the domain structure and factor granularity constitute a conceptual partition whose empirical validity is an open question; a factor solution obtained in validation samples is expected to depart from the present structure. These limitations define rather than undermine the framework's contribution: they constitute the empirical agenda through which a model of this scope is translated into clinical practice.

8.2 Future Research Directions

MERA generates several research priorities: prospective longitudinal studies in Chilean and Latin

American populations to test whether the domain structure and risk weights generalize beyond predominantly Northern European and North American samples; instrument development to operationalize the framework into validated caregiver-report and clinician-administered tools, with particular attention to the prenatal and first-year domains unaddressed by existing instruments; intervention research to determine whether risk-stratified surveillance improves outcomes over standard behavioral screening; and mechanistic research to characterize the pathways through which cross-domain interactions operate. A second class of priority concerns the granularity of the factor structure itself, which is in several places demonstrably coarser than the evidence it organizes. Four refinements are sufficiently well specified to state concretely. Maternal and paternal age are currently treated as a single parentalage factor, but the literature comprises studies examining each independently and studies examining them jointly, with different effect structures; these warrant disaggregation. The syndromic component of Domain 1 is represented at a granularity that the comparative evidence does not support, since symptom profiles differ systematically across fragile X syndrome, Angelman syndrome, tuberous sclerosis complex, and neurofibromatosis type 1 (Lubbers et al., 2024).

Preeclampsia and gestational hypertension are treated as a single hypertensive factor despite effect estimates that differ substantially between them. And atypical primitive reflex profiles in low-birth-weight infants later diagnosed with ASD (Nagai et al., 2024) constitute a candidate Tier I or Tier II marker with no representation in the present structure, notable because primitive reflex assessment is already routine in neonatal examination and would therefore require no new data collection.

A third priority concerns sex differences, which recur across domains in a pattern the present framework does not model. Canonical babbling is reduced in boys later diagnosed with ASD but not in girls (Long et al., 2024); motor delay is a more prevalent early indicator in girls (Gabis et al., 2020); BTEX exposure is associated with differential incidence by sex (Hashemi et al., 2025); and dietary mercury exposure shows sex-dependent association (O'Connor et al., 2025). Whether sex operates as a moderator of individual factor weights, as a determinant of which markers are informative, or both, is a question that a multidomain framework is well positioned to address and that single-factor studies cannot. This is the most substantive extension available to the framework and a principal objective of its next iteration.

8.3 Conclusion

By anchoring detection to the behavioural signs that consolidate at eighteen months and later, current frameworks miss two distinct classes of information. The first is a genuinely prebehavioural set of antecedent risk markers-genetic, obstetric, toxicological, and early medicalthat are established before any behavioural phenotype could be observed, and that are therefore inaccessible to any instrument, however early, that relies on observation of the child. The second is a set of early behavioural divergences in psychomotor, communicative, social, and sensory functioning which are themselves observable, but which emerge months before the diagnostic threshold is reached and are consequently not captured by instruments calibrated to the eighteenmonth window.

The distinction matters for what follows from it. The first class extends detection into a period in which no behavioural observation is possible, and can be addressed only by aggregating biomedical and family-history information already held in the clinical record. The second extends detection into a period in which behaviour is observable but is not currently being observed, and can be addressed by earlier and more targeted use of instruments that already exist. These are different problems with different solutions. At the same time, the phenotypic expansion of ASD underscores that even behavioural detection frameworks may be calibrated too narrowly.

The Multidomain Early Risk Architecture proposed here represents a conceptually grounded response to these limitations. By organizing ten empirically supported domains into a developmentally stratified structure, by classifying their components according to evidential status, and by formulating the principles of cumulative vulnerability, temporal stratification, and interactive amplification as falsifiable hypotheses, MERA provides a foundation for the next generation of ASD early detection research and instrumentation. Its implementation in primary care contexts-particularly in settings such as Chile where diagnostic delays impose substantial individual and social costs-has the potential to reduce the gap between the emergence of detectable signals and access to intervention. The path from this conceptual framework to a validated multidomain instrument is the most urgent priority for the research programme it inaugurates.
