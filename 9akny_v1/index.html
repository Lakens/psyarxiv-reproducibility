Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Code Check works

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Food odours are often used to study anticipatory food reward, yet it remains unclear whether they evoke stronger reward responses than other odours or food cues presented in other sensory modalities. We systematically reviewed the literature to evaluate these assumptions.

Methods: MEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science were searched. Experimental studies investigating behavioural reward responses to orthonasal food odours in healthy participants were included. Two primary contrasts were considered: (1) food vs. non-food odours and (2) food odours vs. food stimuli, as well as the influence of hunger and satiety. Risk of bias was assessed using QualSyst and certainty of evidence using GRADE. A co-occurrence network of odours used in the field was constructed.

Results: Of the 64 included studies, 11 compared food with non-food odours (total N = 363) statistically, while three compared food odours with pictures (total N = 216). Evidence for greater reward responses to food than non-food odours was limited and inconsistent, although positive findings tended to originate from larger studies. Evidence suggesting that food pictures elicit stronger reward responses than food odours was limited due to the small evidence base and methodological heterogeneity. Across studies, odour selection was concentrated on a small number of stimuli.

Conclusions: Few studies have directly examined assumptions underlying the use of food odours as reward stimuli. Greater methodological standardisation and explicit stimulus comparisons are needed to establish under which experimental conditions food odours provide an optimal model of anticipatory food reward. Accordingly, research on food perception or eating behaviour draws on a wide range of stimulus materials, including real foods, images, odours (both orthonasal and retronasal), tastes, and flavours. Likewise, outcomes of interest might cover basic assessments of hedonic features of the stimulus material, intent to buy or consume, the amount consumed, or more abstract concepts like approach/avoidance or parameters estimated from decision-making paradigms. This methodological flexibility presents a challenge to those interested in researching eating behaviours through sensory perception: stimulus choices are not neutral, but can shape the resulting behavioural and hedonic responses, and the sensory modality by which the stimulus is delivered represents one key source of this variation.

A further source of variation in behavioural outcomes stems from the context in which a study is conducted. The perception of food stimuli is dependent on both external factors, such as the research environment, and internal influences, such as the participant's current metabolic state (hunger vs. satiety). For example, in the framework of sensory-specific satiety (SSS), foods will be liked less after having been eaten to satiety than they were before, while non-eaten foods retain their original pleasantness (Rolls et al., 1981). A central aspect of food reward processing is the hedonic and motivational evaluation of food-related cues. Repeated co-exposure of an edible object with its flavour during consumption forms predictive associations that link the appearance before consumption to a favourable outcome, https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=18902780,17255415&pre=&pre=&suf=&suf=&sa=0,0&dbf=0&dbf=0 (Khorisantono & Seubert, 2024; Stevenson, 2024) https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=18902780,17255415&pre=&pre=&suf=&suf=&sa=0,0&dbf=0&dbf=0 food before consumption, both can be well suited to studying reward processes. During the food reward processing cycle, the smell or sight of food triggers an appetitive response that drives approach behaviour. This anticipatory reward response is associated with a craving and the https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=928788,20557&pre=&pre=&suf=&suf=&sa=0,0&dbf=0&dbf=0 (Berridge, 1996 (Berridge, , 2009)) https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=928788,20557&pre=&pre=&suf=&suf=&sa=0,0&dbf=0&dbf=0 pleasure or "liking", which reflects the affective response to the sensory properties of food during consumption (Berridge, 1996). Unlike visual food cues, which primarily represent a future eating experience, orthonasal odours may simultaneously function as predictive cues and as sensory experiences in their own right. Consequently, responses to food odours may reflect both the experienced pleasantness of the odour itself and reward processes associated with anticipated consumption.

Despite the multisensory nature of food perception, relatively few studies have directly compared the effectiveness of different stimulus modalities or systematically examined the use of specific stimulus categories. To date, the majority of studies on eating behaviour and food choice employ only visual stimuli as food cues, which are more easily administered compared to olfactory cues. Howeverhttps://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=2018117&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 the dual nature of olfactory perception through orthonasal and retronasal routes, as well as its direct connection to pathways that bypass thalamic processing (Seubert et al., 2013) , may confer unique advantages to investigations of the different reward components. Given these considerations, two key questions arise. First, whether it can be established experimentally that food-associated odours reliably carry higher hedonic value than non-food odours that are less linked to a specific motivational drive. Second, whether food odour stimulation is a more effective tool to evoke hedonic and motivational responses to food than other sensory modalities, such as visual representations of food. These questions were operationalised in the current review through two main comparisons: (1) reward responses to food vs. non-food odours (i.e., differences in object category associations) (2) reward responses to food stimuli presented in the olfactory domain compared to other sensory modalities (i.e., differences in sensory modality).

Across both comparisons, we examined the role of metabolic state (e.g., hunger vs. satiety) as a motivational factor and potential moderator of these effects. To contextualise any observed differences in hedonic value, we further examined the range and frequency of odour stimuli used across studies, allowing us to assess the generalisability of findings within the field. By identifying whether and how food and non-food odours differ in their hedonic value, as well as how they compare to other sensory modalities, this review maps the hedonic landscape of food-related stimuli across modalities. In doing so, it provides a foundation for more informed methodological choices in experimental studies of eating behaviour.

Study selection

Studies were considered for inclusion in this review if they had an experimental design and examined the reward of orthonasal (non-)food odours and other food stimuli (e.g., pictures/symbols/other) in healthy subjects, including those with a healthy control group. We concentrated on studies that used behavioural outcome measures, such as reported ratings of hedonics, intent to consume, or willingness to pay. Studies that were excluded included animal studies, those involving children too young to indicate behavioural preferences (i.e., newborns, infants, or toddlers), neuroimaging studies without behavioural outcomes, cell/in-vitro studies, studies that did not involve food odours, studies related to aromatherapy, and studies on product development. Each title and abstract was independently reviewed for inclusion by two reviewers using Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016) and, in case of disagreement, at least three authors discussed until agreement was reached. Study selection of full-text papers was also conducted by two independent raters, and in case of disagreement, LS and JS discussed until a consensus was reached.

Data extraction

A data extraction tool from the Cochrane Training Website (Li et al., 2019) was adapted for this review (Supplementary Table 2). Data extraction was done independently by LS and GP and then corroborated. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion by LS, GP and PAK.

Studies were categorised by the comparison they investigated (i.e., food odours vs. non-food odours or food odours vs. other food stimuli) and will be presented according to this structure.

Importantly, we extracted the used reward measures (e.g., liking/wanting ratings, auction tasks, food intake), modalities of stimuli, and whether contrasts of interest were the studies' main aim.

Information regarding study populations and potential methods to manipulate hunger and satiety can be seen in Table 1. Synthesis of results was based on the direction and statistical significance of the reported comparisons of interest (food vs. non-food odours; food odours vs. other sensory modalities), together with qualitative summaries of the reported findings. Where applicable, the direction of the reported effect (Figure 4A) and a network representation of odour co-occurrences across studies were additionally visualised. Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored qualitatively by comparing study characteristics, including participant populations, experimental design, stimulus selection, reward measures, and hunger or satiety manipulations. No sensitivity analyses were conducted because no quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis was performed.

Odour Index

To create an overview of stimuli used across included studies, odours were labelled based on their reported perceptual quality. Perceptual descriptors were taken directly from the original publications where available; when chemical compounds were listed without odour qualities, these were inferred from reference databases (e.g., Good Scents Company) and marked with an asterisk (*, see Supplementary Material 3). Where multiple papers referred to the same or highly similar odour using different descriptors (e.g., "shampoo" vs. "classical shampoo fragrance"), these were standardised into a single label to streamline analysis. Proprietary odours without available sensory descriptions were retained under their reported names (e.g., "Lovely ion" by EmoSens). Next, perceptual descriptors were standardised across studies to reflect primary stimulus identity (e.g. "Almond" and "Almond/marzipan" retaining the same label). Where composite labels, such as "sweet rose" or "cleaning product (rosemary)", were reported, descriptors were simplified to the core odour to enable aggregation across studies. All perceptual odour labels were then grouped into classes of perceptual similarity (e.g., rose and lilac receiving the class name "floral"): Chemical/synthetic/medicinal, dairy/fatty, environmental, floral, fruity, herbs/spices, savoury/meaty, sour/pungent, sweet or woody/green plant. Proprietary or scene-based odours lacking identifiable perceptual descriptors were retained but grouped into "Unclassified", reflecting the absence of a definable odour category rather than perceptual similarity.

The complete set of stimuli was then filtered to only include studies that statistically analysed at least one contrast of interest; either comparing food and non-food odours or comparing food odours to stimuli from another modality. The resulting database of odour stimuli was visualised through a network plot created in Gephi (Bastian et al.https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=15257446&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 2009) , version 0.10.1 202301172018. Here, edges represented the co-occurrence of odours within studies, with thicker edges indicating higher co-occurrence across studies. Repeated instances of the same odour label within a study were counted once. Node colour referred to the class of the odour label.

Risk of Bias (Quality) Assessment

To systematically assess the quality of the included studies, we used the standard quality assessment criteria (QualSyst) for evaluating primary research papers (Kmet et al., 2004). This tool (Supplementary Table 3) was developed to rate internal validity and risk of bias for a variety of methodologies. The checklist for quality assessment of individual studies addresses potential issues such as blinding, randomisation, and sample size using a total of 14 items. For each included study, two independent raters (LS and PAK) decided whether the criteria were met ("yes" = 2, "partial" = 1, "no" = 0, "N/A" = not applicable). The raters then compared ratings and discussed any discrepancies for each item until agreement was reached before calculating a final score (ranging from 0 to 1) for each paper. Notably, risk of bias was only calculated for those papers whose main study outcome was one of the contrasts of interest. Studies that assessed liking for odours representing edible and non-edible objects, but did not explicitly compare these two categories statistically, were included in this review but not evaluated using the risk of bias tool.

Certainty (Quality of Evidence) Assessment

To assess the quality of evidence for our research questions, we employed the Grading of https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=508879&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 (Guyatt et al., 2008) https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=508879&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 strength of clinical recommendations across several key domains: (1) Risk of bias assessing the extent to which the design and conduct of a study may have introduced systematic errors.

Here, we evaluated this domain using the QualSyst (see above).

(2) Inconsistency of results across studies is examined by assessing the variability in effect estimates.

(3) Indirectness assesses the applicability of the evidence to the research question, considering whether the populations, interventions, comparators, and outcomes studied are directly relevant.

(4) Imprecision of the results focusing on the uncertainty of effect estimates due to factors such as sample size and the width of confidence intervals.

(5) Publication bias involves investigating whether the studies included in the review are representative of the entire evidence base or if there is selective publication of studies with positive results. This domain was used to assess the risk of bias due to missing results (reporting bias) qualitatively.

The study design determined the initial rating of the evidence quality, with randomised controlled trials typically starting as "high-quality evidence" and observational studies as "lowquality evidence". The quality of evidence can then be downgraded based on issues identified in the domains or upgraded if certain factors, such as a large magnitude of effect, increase confidence in the results. In an effort to adapt GRADE to non-clinical research, we started the assessment with "high-quality evidence", since all included papers presented experimental research. In applying the GRADE system to this review, we then carefully evaluated the body of evidence for each research question. The final quality ratings were then summarised and used to guide the strength of conclusions drawn from the review, ensuring that our evaluation of the evidence is systematic and reliable.

Discussion

Eating behaviour is inherently a multisensory process, where reward-related information from different senses contributes to decisions about whether and what to eat. Consequently, researchers aiming to study food reward in laboratory settings are faced with the challenge of selecting appropriate methods and sensory stimuli to capture these processes. The present review, therefore, aimed to examine two related questions: whether food odours are associated with greater hedonic value than non-food odours, and how food odours compare to food stimuli presented in other sensory modalities. The evidence discussed here has been inconsistent, imprecise, and subject to potential publication bias, and consequently graded to be of very low certainty. Two studies reported stronger https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=1813784,15081269&pre=&pre=&suf=&suf=&sa=0,0&dbf=0&dbf=0 (Jiang et al., 2008 (Jiang et al., , 2013)) https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=1813784,15081269&pre=&pre=&suf=&suf=&sa=0,0&dbf=0&dbf=0 (Chan et al., 2021) https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=1813784,15081269&pre=&pre=&suf=&suf=&sa=0,0&dbf=0&dbf=0 studies further investigated the influence of hunger, although the observed interactions between metabolic state and modality were not consistent. (2021) reported greater desire to eat following food odours both before and after a meal. Similar to the food vs. non-food contrast, heterogeneous implementations of hunger and satiety manipulations are likely to contribute to these inconsistencies. Additional differences in participant populations, rating scales, and study design further limit direct comparisons. One source of uncertainty also lies in the fact that researchers cannot be sure which underlying construct is being investigated by probing participants' experience through self-reported items of "liking" and "wanting". Both visual and orthonasal food cues are in theory associated with the anticipatory, rather than the consummatory, phase of food reward, since they are predictive of a future eating experience (Berridge, 1996 (Berridge, , 2009)). This distinction is very intuitive in the visual domain: when presented with the photograph of a pizza, participants likely evaluate the pizza itself, therefore judging the expected eating experience, rather than how pleasurable the visual composition of the picture is. Food odours, however, may occupy a more ambiguous position: they could function both as predictive cues for food consumption and as hedonic sensory experiences in their own right. Consequently, while visual food cues likely primarily capture expectations regarding the future eating experience, hedonic ratings of food odours may reflect multiple psychological processes simultaneously, including the directly experienced pleasantness of the odour itself, expected liking of the associated food, and the anticipated pleasure of eating it. This ambiguity could partly arise from the unique organisation of the olfactory system: whereas orthonasal olfaction typically precedes consumption and therefore functions as an anticipatory cue, retronasal olfaction is particularly relevant to the consummatory experience itself (Small et al., 2008). Food odours may therefore occupy a position at the interface between anticipatory and consummatory reward that has no direct equivalent in the visual domain, and any direct comparison of the two modalities is likely compromised by this difference in self-reported scales.

Considering the complexity of odour perception, we propose that future studies distinguish more explicitly between these different aspects of reward by phrasing rating questions accordingly.

For example, one may separately assess the pleasantness of the presented odour, the expected pleasantness of the associated food, and the anticipated pleasure of consuming it. In this context, Piacentini et al. (1993) asked their participants to rate the pleasantness of the imagined taste that was associated with the odour stimulus. Such an approach may help standardise which aspect of the sensory experience participants are asked to evaluate, thereby improving comparability across studies. Furthermorehttps://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=6198081&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 combining ratings with alternative measures of subjective value, such as https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=283327&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 (Becker et al., 1964) https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=283327&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 (Knutson et al., 2000) https://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=283327&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 paradigms (Solarz, 1960) , would present a clearer picture of food-cue-induced hedonic and motivational states. Beyond comparing vision and olfaction, future work should also extend modality comparisons to other anticipatory sensory cues, including haptic information, and should systematically investigate both their independent and combined contributions to food reward.

Limitations

Our synthesis of the reviewed literature is limited by inconsistency in categorisation of odours into food and non-food classes across the literature. Some odours, such as coffee, were considered food stimuli in some studies but non-food in others. Likewise, cultural differences may influence whether certain odours are perceived as edible (e.g., rose being strictly floral vs. evoking rose-water associations). Furthermore, studies differed in the level of categorisation, with some contrasting food vs. non-food odours while others distinguished between more specific subclasses such as high-vs. low-energy or high-protein foods, thereby complicating direct comparisons across studies.

Furthermore, across the studies included in this review, 17 investigated exclusively one gender and one study did not report gender at all, substantially limiting the generalisability of the evidence. Some authors justified this decision by citing reported sex differences in olfactory sensitivity (e.g., Albrecht et al., 2009). Howeverhttps://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=8540350&pre=&suf=&sa=0&dbf=0 meta-analytic evidence shows that these differences are likely small (Sorokowski et al., 2019) , and their underlying causes may involve both biological and sociocultural factors. Moreover, differences in olfactory sensitivity do not necessarily imply differences in reward processing, particularly when within-subject designs are used to investigate changes in reward as a function of metabolic state. Unless a specific research question warrants restricting recruitment, future studies should aim to include more representative samples rather than assuming that one group is more appropriate for investigating food-related reward.

In addition to these literature-based limitations, we highlight that this review did not include a meta-analysis and, consequently, publication bias could not be formally assessed using methods such as funnel plots or regression-based approaches. Nevertheless, we included a measure of publication bias using the GRADE framework. Furthermore, many methodological tools developed for systematic reviews, including GRADE and several PRISMA recommendations, were originally designed for clinical intervention studies and meta-analytic approaches. Their application to this review therefore required several adaptations. While these adaptations were justified by the nature of the available literature, they inevitably limit the immediate transferability of our adapted measures to other reviews using the same systems.

Conclusion

The findings of this review suggest that food odours can elicit stronger reward responses than non-food odours, although this effect is not consistently observed and may depend on moderating factors, while comparisons with visual food cues provide no clear evidence of an advantage for either modality. Given the limited certainty of the available evidence, further research is needed to establish whether food odours have distinct rewarding properties and under which conditions these emerge.
