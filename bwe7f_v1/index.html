Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

We found 16 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Purpose: To examine associations between accelerometer-derived rest-activity indicators and sleep disturbance among older adults. Methods: This analysis included 1,373 person-wave observations from 569 National Health and Aging Trends Study participants. Multilevel models separated within-person and between-person components of the least active five-hour period (L5), coefficient of variation, and behavioral fragmentation. Relative amplitude (RA) was included at the between-person level in the primary model and examined separately in sensitivity analyses. Results: In primary analyses, no rest-activity indicator was associated with sleep disturbance, including when sleep medication use was excluded; within-person estimates were small (95% CI bounds within ±0.031). Weak between-person associations for L5 and RA emerged only in exploratory, model-dependent analyses. Pain, depressive symptoms, chronic disease burden, and educational attainment were associated with sleep disturbance. Conclusion: Rest-activity indicators showed no clear association with self-reported sleep disturbance, and small between-person estimates were model-dependent. Health-related and socioeconomic factors warrant attention in population-based sleep research.

Introduction

Sleep disturbance is common among community-dwelling older adults, with approximately one-third to one-half of adults aged 65 years and older reporting persistent difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep (Foley et al., 2004). Sleep disruption is associated with adverse health outcomes (Medic et al., 2017), and insomnia has been linked to a higher risk of subsequent depression (Li et al., 2016). Identifying factors associated with sleep disturbance remains an important priority in research on aging and health. Age-related changes in circadian regulation and daily rest-activity organization may contribute to sleep difficulties in later life (Vitiello et al., 2004; Zitting & Yuan, 2025).

Daily rest-activity patterns reflect the temporal organization of behavior across periods of activity and rest. The circadian system regulates the timing of sleep and wakefulness, providing a theoretical basis for investigating how daily behavioral rhythms relate to sleep disturbance (Roenneberg & Merrow, 2016). Wrist-worn accelerometers make it possible to characterize these patterns objectively in everyday settings, whereas self-reported activity measures may not adequately capture habitual behavior (VandeBunte et al., 2022).

Nonparametric measures of rest-activity rhythms include the least active five-hour period (L5), which reflects activity during the rest phase, and relative amplitude (RA), which represents the contrast between the most and least active periods (Van Someren et al., 1999). Behavioral fragmentation, measured using the active-to-sedentary transition probability (ASTP), captures the discontinuity of waking activity (Schrack et al., 2019). Day-to-day variability in activity volume provides an additional dimension of behavioral organization. These indicators characterize complementary aspects of daily activity patterns that may have different relationships with sleep disturbance.

Previous research has associated accelerometer-derived rest-activity indicators with physical function, cognition, and neuropsychiatric outcomes in older adults, including participants in the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS; Shim & Onnela, 2026).

Greater fragmentation of activity rhythms has also been linked to depression and anxiety in a population-based cohort (Luik et al., 2015). Evidence directly concerning sleep disturbance remains limited. A population-based cross-sectional study found greater rest-activity rhythm fragmentation among adults reporting poor subjective sleep quality (Oliveira et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, longitudinal research examining the correspondence between rest-activity indicators and self-reported sleep difficulties in community-dwelling older adults remains scarce.

As wrist accelerometry is increasingly incorporated into population-based studies of older adults, clarifying its correspondence with self-reported sleep complaints may help researchers determine how rest-activity indicators should be interpreted alongside symptom reports when studying sleep-related health in later life.

Longitudinal observations provide an opportunity to distinguish between-person differences in rest-activity patterns from within-person fluctuations over time (Curran & Bauer, 2011; Rohrer & Murayama, 2023). Associations observed between individuals with different habitual activity patterns may not necessarily reflect associations between changes in activity patterns and sleep complaints within the same individual (Rohrer & Murayama, 2023).

Separating these associations is important because estimates that combine within-person and between-person variation may obscure their respective contributions (Bell et al., 2019; Curran & Bauer, 2011). An additional analytic consideration is that rest-activity indicators may be correlated with one another. Associations involving rhythm stability and sleep indicators with depression and anxiety in a population-based study were sensitive to adjustment for other rhythm and sleep measures (Luik et al., 2015), suggesting that estimates may depend on how correlated indicators are modeled.

The present study addresses these gaps using three waves of NHATS data, drawn from a nationally representative cohort of U.S. adults aged 65 years and older (Freedman & Kasper, 2019). The study aimed to examine associations between accelerometer-derived rest-activity indicators and self-reported sleep disturbance and to distinguish between-person differences from within-person changes. At the between-person level, we hypothesized that higher relative amplitude and lower behavioral fragmentation would be associated with fewer reported sleep problems. Within-person associations and associations involving L5 and the coefficient of variation were examined without directional hypotheses.

Methods

probability sampling design anchored to the Medicare enrollment file, which covers approximately 96% of U.S. adults aged 65 years and older (Freedman & Kasper, 2019). The study protocol was approved by the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 00002083; NHATS, n.d.), and all participants provided informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2013). Beginning in Round 11, participants in an accelerometer substudy wore an ActiGraph CentrePoint Insight Watch on the wrist continuously for approximately 7 days per wave (Schrack et al., 2022). Waves were linked by unique participant identifiers.

The accelerometry sample was selected in Round 11 and followed through Round 14 (Van de Kerckhove et al., 2026). Participants were eligible if they had valid accelerometer data (at least 4 valid wear days with at least 90% wear time per day) and complete data on sleep disturbance and all covariates. Of 1,377 person-wave observations from 570 participants who met the accelerometer criteria, 4 observations with missing study variables were excluded. The analytic sample comprised 1,373 observations from 569 participants (Round 12, n = 517; Round 13, n = 450; Round 14, n = 406). Of these participants, 333 contributed three waves, 138 contributed two waves, and 98 contributed one wave. Although NHATS includes adults aged 65 years and older, no participants aged 65-69 years were in the analytic sample, and consequently the youngest age category in the models was 70-74 years. The analytic sample was defined by accelerometer participation and data completeness, and NHATS survey weights were not applied, so estimates describe the analytic sample rather than the population.

Measures

Rest-Activity Indicators. Raw accelerometer signals were recorded at 64 Hz and processed into minute-level activity counts using standardized NHATS procedures implemented in the R package arctools (Karas et al., 2021). Nonwear was defined as at least 90 consecutive minutes with zero activity counts, and a valid wear day required at least 90% wear time (1,296 minutes), consistent with NHATS accelerometry processing guidelines (Schrack et al., 2022 (Schrack et al., , 2025)). Analyses used the revised accelerometry files that corrected time-zone misclassification affecting a subset of participants (Etzkorn et al., 2026). Extreme values of the accelerometerderived indicators were winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

Four indicators were derived from daily activity summaries. L5 was the mean activity count during the least active five-hour interval across valid days. RA was calculated as (M10 -L5)/(M10 + L5), following standard nonparametric analysis of rest-activity rhythms (Van Someren et al., 1999), where M10 is the mean activity during the most active 10-hour interval, so that higher values indicate greater contrast between the most and least active periods. The coefficient of variation (CV) of daily total activity characterizes day-to-day variability in activity volume. L5 and CV were examined as complementary indicators of rest-phase activity and dayto-day variability. Behavioral fragmentation was the ASTP, calculated as the number of transitions from an active minute (≥1,853 counts per minute; Koster et al., 2016) to a sedentary minute divided by the total number of active minutes in the day, averaged across valid days, following the calculation approach used in prior NHATS accelerometry research (Schrack et al., 2019). Higher ASTP values indicate more fragmented waking activity.

Sleep Disturbance.

Sleep disturbance was assessed with three NHATS items: difficulty falling asleep for 30 or more minutes (HC20), waking during the night with difficulty returning to sleep (HC21), and frequent use of sleep medication (HC22). Each item was coded 0 (absent) or 1 (present). The items were summed to a composite score from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating more reported sleep-related problems (Foley et al., 2004). Because sleep medication use reflects treatment as well as symptoms, the composite indexes reported sleep-related problems rather than insomnia symptoms alone; a symptom-only score omitting the medication item was therefore also examined (S8). The three items were also analyzed separately. Because the recall period of the sleep items did not coincide with the 7-day accelerometer wear period, the analyses address correspondence between habitual rest-activity patterns and reported sleep problems rather than night-to-night associations.

Covariates. All models adjusted for gender (male or female), race (White or non-White), educational attainment (high school or GED [reference], less than high school, some college, bachelor's degree, graduate degree), and age category (70-74 [reference], 75-79, 80-84, 85-89, 90 years and older), based on their established associations with sleep in older adults (Foley et al., 2004; Li et al., 2016; Mezick et al., 2008). Health covariates were chronic disease burden (count of self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions), pain (any pain in the preceding month), and depressive symptoms measured with the two-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2; Kroenke et al., 2003). Survey wave was included as a standardized continuous fixed effect.

Chronic disease count, PHQ-2, and survey wave were standardized directly (z scores), whereas the rest-activity indicators were standardized after decomposition. The within-person and between-person decomposition was applied to the rest-activity indicators, which were the primary exposures. The time-varying health covariates were entered without decomposition as adjustment variables, so their coefficients are not distinct within-person or between-person associations.

Analytic Strategy

Within-Person and Between-Person Components.

Rest-activity indicators were decomposed into within-person and between-person components using person-mean centering (Bell et al., 2019; Curran & Bauer, 2011; Rohrer & Murayama, 2023): the between-person component was each participant's mean across observed waves, and the within-person component was the deviation from that mean at each wave. Each component was standardized after decomposition, so coefficients refer to a one-standard-deviation difference. Because L5 and RA were strongly correlated (r = -0.80), the within-person component of RA was omitted from the primary model, since adding it raised the VIF of within-person L5 from 1.01 to 4.99 (S5). Both between-person components were retained to evaluate the hypothesized RA association, although they were also correlated (r = -0.78; VIF = 5.66 for L5 and 6.78 for RA), and models excluding each indicator (S6 and S7) were examined to address this overlap. The primary model therefore included both components of L5, CV, and behavioral fragmentation and the betweenperson component of RA. For each indicator, the ICC was obtained from an unconditional random-intercept model, and the within-person SD was the SD of deviations from the person mean across all observations, including the zero deviations of participants observed once.

Linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts were estimated by maximum likelihood in the R package lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) with the BOBYQA optimizer, and fixedeffect tests used the Satterthwaite approximation in the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). The null model provided the ICC of the outcome, marginal and conditional R² followed Nakagawa and Schielzeth (2013), and a likelihood ratio test compared the adjusted and null models. Coefficients represent expected differences in the 0-3 sleep disturbance score per onestandard-deviation difference in the predictor.

Eight sensitivity analyses were conducted: (S1) excluding chronic disease burden, (S2) excluding PHQ-2, (S3) restricting to participants observed in all three waves, (S4) using a frequency-weighted sleep disturbance score (range 3-15), (S5) including both within-and between-person RA, (S6) excluding RA, (S7) excluding L5, and (S8) using a symptom-only score (HC20 plus HC21, range 0-2) that omits the sleep medication item. S6-S8 and the itemlevel models were added after the primary results were examined and are exploratory. The three sleep items were analyzed with population-averaged logistic models estimated by generalized estimating equations (GEE; Liang & Zeger, 1986) with an exchangeable working correlation and robust standard errors (geepack package; Højsgaard et al., 2006), since random-intercept logistic models were unstable, and the composite was also analyzed with a cumulative-logit mixed model (ordinal package; Christensen, 2023). Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Selective attrition was assessed by comparing baseline (Round 12) characteristics of participants who also contributed Round 14 data (completers) with those who did not (noncompleters). Independent-samples t tests were used for continuous variables (rest-activity indicators and sleep disturbance), and chi-square tests were used for categorical variables (gender and race). Tests were two-sided (α = .05) and not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Model assumptions were checked with residual diagnostic plots. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Core Team, 2024).

Results

Descriptive Characteristics of the Sample

Table 1 presents descriptive characteristics of the analytic sample (N = 1,373 personwave observations from 569 participants). Women made up 53.8% of observations (739/1,373), and 85.5% (1,174/1,373) were White. The most common age category was 75-79 years (45.2%), and 21.2% of observations were from participants aged 85 years or older. Educational attainment was high, with 37.8% of observations from participants with a graduate degree and 4.5% from those with less than a high school education. Pain was reported in 58.4% of observations. The mean PHQ-2 score was 0.63 (SD = 0.94) and the mean number of chronic conditions was 2.83 (SD = 1.40).

The mean sleep disturbance score was 0.52 (SD = 0.76). Scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3 occurred in 62.6%, 24.6%, 10.9%, and 1.9% of observations. Difficulty initiating sleep, nocturnal awakening, and frequent sleep medication use were reported in 20.0%, 13.0%, and 19.0% of observations. Mean values of the rest-activity indicators were 124.25 (SD = 75.87) for L5, 0.86 (SD = 0.08) for RA, 0.33 (SD = 0.08) for CV, and 0.30 (SD = 0.08) for behavioral fragmentation.

Variance Structure and Model Fit

In the null model, the ICC was 0.593, indicating that 59.3% of the model-estimated variance in sleep disturbance was between persons, with the remaining 40.7% representing within-person variation. The adjusted model fit better than the null model, χ²(21) = 87.98, p < .001. The marginal R² was .088, the conditional R² was .586, and the adjusted ICC was .546. The rest-activity indicators were stable across waves for L5, RA, and behavioral fragmentation (ICC = .72, .73, and .83) but not for CV (ICC = .18). One within-person standard deviation of the observed deviations corresponded to 31.3 counts for L5 (41% of its total SD), 0.034 for RA, 0.056 for CV, and 0.027 for behavioral fragmentation. Model-based within-person SDs were larger, at 41.1 counts for L5, 0.044 for RA, 0.074 for CV, and 0.036 for behavioral fragmentation.

Rest-Activity Indicators and Sleep Disturbance

None of the rest-activity indicators was associated with sleep disturbance in the primary model, at either the between-person or the within-person level (Table 2; all p ≥ .271). The within-person estimates were small, with 95% CI bounds within approximately ±0.031 points on the 0-3 scale per standard-deviation change in L5, CV, or behavioral fragmentation. The between-person estimates were less precise. For example, each 1-SD higher between-person L5 corresponded to a 0.026-point higher sleep disturbance score (B = 0.026, 95% CI = [-0.096, 0.149]), and each 1-SD higher between-person RA to a 0.034-point lower score (B = -0.034, 95% CI = [-0.167, 0.100]), so bounds extended to approximately 0.17 points per standard deviation.

Sociodemographic and Health Correlates

Pain (B = 0.114, p = .004), depressive symptoms (PHQ-2; B = 0.087, p < .001), and chronic disease burden (B = 0.070, p = .010) were associated with higher sleep disturbance.

Compared with high school/GED, those without a high school diploma reported higher sleep disturbance (B = 0.355, p = .011), and those with a graduate degree reported lower sleep disturbance (B = -0.148, p = .030); other education categories did not differ significantly.

Gender, race, age category, and survey wave were not significantly associated with sleep disturbance, and the estimate for ages 80-84 relative to 70-74 was borderline (B = -0.156, p = .052).

Sensitivity Analyses

Table 3 summarizes S1-S5, which were consistent with the primary model. Excluding chronic disease burden (S1) or PHQ-2 (S2) did not change the pattern, and between-person RA remained nonsignificant in S2 (p = .555). Restricting to three-wave participants (S3) and using the frequency-weighted score (S4) also produced no significant associations. In S5, the withinperson RA estimate was negative but not significant (B = -0.059, SE = 0.032, p = .064).

Including both RA components raised the variance inflation factor (VIF) of within-person L5 from 1.01 to 4.99, and the VIF of within-person RA was 5.62. Table 4 presents exploratory models in which weak between-person associations were observed when L5 and RA were examined separately. Without RA (S6), between-person L5 was weakly associated with more sleep disturbance (B = 0.054, SE = 0.027, 95% CI = [0.001, 0.107], p = .047). Without L5 (S7), between-person RA was weakly associated with less sleep disturbance (B = -0.059, SE = 0.030, 95% CI = [-0.117, -0.001], p = .045). These estimates correspond to about 7% to 8% of the outcome standard deviation per standard deviation of the indicator, and they were not independent since L5 and RA were strongly correlated at the between-person level (r = -0.78). Within-person estimates remained near zero in both models (L5: B = 0.003, p = .822; RA: B = -0.015, p = .303), and the within-person RA estimate of S5 was not reproduced in S7. With the symptom-only score (S8), no rest-activity indicator was associated with sleep disturbance (all p ≥ .382). In S8, PHQ-2 remained associated with the score (B = 0.059, SE = 0.017, p < .001), whereas pain (B = 0.065, SE = 0.034, p = .052) and chronic disease burden (B = 0.039, SE = 0.022, p = .070) were attenuated.

Individual Sleep Items and Ordinal Model

Random-intercept logistic models for the three items gave very unstable between-person estimates, with random-intercept standard deviations of 3.29 (HC20), 6.71 (HC21), and 13.73 (HC22) on the logit scale, as item responses changed little within persons. Population-averaged GEE estimates are therefore reported (Table 5). No rest-activity indicator was associated with difficulty initiating sleep, nocturnal awakening, or frequent sleep medication use (all p ≥ .163).

Within-person ORs ranged from 0.94 to 1.08 and between-person ORs from 0.86 to 1.20, although the 95% CIs of the between-person ORs were wide (0.57 to 1.85). In the cumulativelogit model for the composite score, ORs ranged from 0.81 to 1.17 (all p ≥ .249).

Attrition

Of 517 Round 12 participants, 364 (70.4%) also contributed Round 14 data. Completers and noncompleters did not differ in baseline sleep disturbance (p = .486), L5 (p = .738), RA (p = .099), gender (p = .401), or race (p = .366). Noncompleters had lower CV (0.317 vs. 0.335, p = .027) and higher behavioral fragmentation (0.320 vs. 0.290, p = .001).

Discussion

This study examined whether accelerometer-derived rest-activity indicators were associated with self-reported sleep disturbance among older adults participating in the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS). Three findings are highlighted. First, none of the indicators was associated with sleep disturbance in the primary analyses, at either the withinperson or the between-person level, and this pattern held when sleep medication use was excluded from the outcome; thus, the hypothesized associations for relative amplitude and behavioral fragmentation were not supported. The within-person estimates were also small and precisely estimated (95% CI bounds within ±0.031 on the 0-3 scale per standard-deviation change), suggesting that large within-person associations are unlikely on the scale examined.

Second, small between-person associations for the least active five-hour period (L5) and relative amplitude (RA) appeared only in exploratory models that separated the two indicators, and they were model-dependent. Third, pain, depressive symptoms, chronic disease burden, and educational attainment were more consistently associated with sleep disturbance. Because the study was observational, these findings describe associations rather than causal effects.

The lack of consistent associations in the primary analyses, together with the precision of the within-person estimates, is notable given the growing evidence linking rest-activity organization to health outcomes in later life. Previous studies have associated disrupted activity rhythms with depression and anxiety (Luik et al., 2015) and have identified relationships between accelerometer-derived rhythm characteristics and physical function, cognition, and neuropsychiatric outcomes in NHATS (Shim & Onnela, 2026). These findings, however, concern different outcomes and study populations and do not necessarily imply that rest-activity indicators will be associated with self-reported sleep disturbance in a community-based sample.

One possible explanation for the present results lies in the distinction between the behavioral patterns captured by accelerometry and the subjective experience of sleep difficulties.

Although circadian processes contribute to the temporal regulation of sleep and wakefulness (Roenneberg & Merrow, 2016), measures derived from wrist movement primarily describe how activity and rest are distributed across daily life. They do not directly assess endogenous circadian timing or the perceived quality of sleep. Previous research has shown that self-reported and measured sleep duration are only modestly related (Lauderdale et al., 2008), with further variation in the correspondence between subjective and objective sleep quality among older adults (Vitiello et al., 2004). Thus, the lack of consistent associations in this study may reflect differences in the aspects of sleep health captured by behavioral activity patterns and selfreported symptoms, rather than an absence of any relationship between circadian regulation and sleep.

Features of the outcome measure may also have limited the detection of associations.

Sleep disturbance was indexed by three binary items, yielding a score with only four possible values (0-3) on which most observations (62.6%) were 0, so modest differences in sleep may not have been registered. Reported sleep problems were also relatively stable, with 59.3% of outcome variance lying between persons and item responses changing little across waves, leaving limited within-person variation for changes in rest-activity patterns to explain. In addition, the recall period of the sleep items did not coincide with the 7-day accelerometer wear period. Each of these features would be expected to attenuate associations, and sleep measures that are more finely graded and aligned in time with accelerometer wear would provide a stronger test.

The longitudinal findings also indicate that the level at which rest-activity patterns are examined matters for interpreting their relationship with sleep disturbance (Bell et al., 2019; Curran & Bauer, 2011). Within-person deviations in L5, day-to-day activity variability, and behavioral fragmentation were not accompanied by corresponding differences in reported sleep disturbance across the three annual assessments. At the between-person level, associations involving L5 and RA emerged only in exploratory models in which the indicators were examined separately. The direction of the RA association was consistent with the hypothesis, and the L5 association, for which no direction was predicted, was in the corresponding direction, with greater activity during the rest period and a weaker contrast between active and rest periods accompanying more sleep disturbance. Their dependence on model specification, however, limits the evidence for distinct associations of either indicator with sleep complaints. Because RA is calculated partly from L5 activity, the two measures share information about rest-activity organization, and their strong correlation in the present sample may have reduced the stability of their individual estimates when modeled simultaneously. Prior population-based research has likewise shown that associations involving activity rhythms and sleep-related measures can be sensitive to adjustment for related indicators (Luik et al., 2015). The present findings therefore suggest that apparent associations between individual rhythm measures and sleep disturbance should be interpreted in relation to both their shared information and the source of variation being analyzed. In particular, weak differences observed between individuals should not be assumed to characterize changes occurring within the same individual over time.

In contrast to the rest-activity indicators, pain, depressive symptoms, chronic disease burden, and educational attainment were associated with self-reported sleep disturbance. Greater pain, depressive symptoms, and chronic disease burden were related to more sleep disturbance, whereas higher educational attainment was related to less. Because observations from participants with less than a high school education represented only 4.5% of the analytic sample, education contrasts involving this group should be interpreted cautiously. These associations are consistent with previous evidence linking sleep problems in later life to physical illness, psychological symptoms, and socioeconomic circumstances (Foley et al., 2004; Li et al., 2016; Mezick et al., 2008). These findings indicate that health-related and socioeconomic characteristics remain relevant correlates of reported sleep disturbance even when multiple restactivity indicators are considered. Nevertheless, the associations of pain and chronic disease burden were weaker when sleep medication use was excluded from the outcome. This difference suggests that the observed relationships partly depend on the composition of the sleep disturbance measure. Because medication use may reflect both the presence of sleep problems and their clinical management, combining medication use with symptom reports may capture a broader construct than sleep disturbance alone.

Strengths include repeated accelerometer assessments across three annual waves, explicit within-between decomposition (Bell et al., 2019; Curran & Bauer, 2011), multiple rest-activity indicators including behavioral fragmentation operationalized as the active-to-sedentary transition probability (Schrack et al., 2019), and sensitivity analyses that assessed the influence of correlated indicators and alternative definitions of sleep disturbance.

Attrition analyses indicated that participants who did not complete follow-up had lower CV and higher behavioral fragmentation at baseline. These differences suggest possible selective attrition, although whether and in what direction attrition may have biased the observed associations remains uncertain.

This study has several limitations, organized into three areas. First, regarding measurement: the sleep disturbance composite consisted of three coarse binary items whose recall period did not coincide with the accelerometer wear window, and the composite included sleep medication use, which may reflect treatment response rather than symptoms; more finely graded items temporally aligned with device wear would provide a stronger test. Second, regarding design and sample: the rest-activity indicators capture observable behavior rather than endogenous circadian phase (which requires direct physiological assessment); the analytic sample was predominantly White and highly educated and analyses did not apply NHATS accelerometry weights, so findings reflect associations within the analytic sample rather than population estimates; additionally, only 4.5% of observations came from participants with less than a high school education, so comparisons involving that group should be interpreted with caution; participants who did not complete follow-up differed from completers in baseline activity variability and fragmentation, indicating possible selective attrition; and three annual observations may not capture shorter-term relationships between daily activity and sleep. Third, regarding analytic considerations: limited within-person variation in individual sleep items constrained GEE analyses; the strong correlation between L5 and RA made their between-person effects sensitive to model specification; the relatively low stability of CV across waves raises questions about how well the person-level mean represents a stable individual characteristic; and exploratory analyses were conducted without adjustment for multiple comparisons in an observational design that precludes causal inference.

Future research should examine rest-activity organization alongside subjective sleep complaints, objective sleep characteristics, and direct measures of circadian phase. Such assessments could help determine whether the weak associations observed here reflect differences between behavioral and subjective measures or variation in the physiological processes underlying sleep. More frequent repeated measurements would also allow investigation of whether changes in rest-activity patterns are related to sleep difficulties over shorter intervals than those captured by annual assessments. Given the sensitivity of the exploratory findings to model specification, careful consideration of correlations among rhythm indicators and separation of within-person and between-person associations will remain important in longitudinal research.

For population-based research on aging, these findings suggest that wrist accelerometryderived rest-activity summaries should not be treated as substitutes for directly reported sleep complaints. They may provide complementary information about daily behavioral organization, but their limited correspondence with the sleep items examined here underscores the importance of retaining symptom-based assessments. Pain, depressive symptoms, chronic disease burden, and educational attainment also warrant attention when investigating sleep-related health in later life.
