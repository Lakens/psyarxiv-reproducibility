Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Autistic masking has typically been understood as the suppression of natural Autistic traits within neuronormative social contexts. However, social interactions are inherently embedded within broader sensory environments, highlighting the need to consider how sensory experiences shape masking processes. Although sensory experiences have been recognized within existing masking research, the sensory dimensions of masking remain comparatively underexplored. In this perspective, we examine sensory masking as an interrelated dimension of broader Autistic masking processes, defined as the conscious or subconscious suppression, inhibition, or override of sensory responses to meet environmental or social expectations. Drawing on recent research and our own lived experience, we describe how these sensory dimensions may emerge developmentally, often as a learned adaptation to unsafe or invalidating experiences. We then consider the cumulative effects of sustained sensory masking. While sensory masking may serve a functional purpose, its chronic and persistent engagement may contribute to burnout and negatively impact well-being and mental health, particularly in the context of prolonged exposure to sensory aversive environments. We also distinguish these sensory dimensions from related processes of sensory self-regulation and sensory unmasking, which involve supporting regulation and reclaiming autonomy through safe sensory self-expression. We conclude by outlining constructive approaches that support sensory safety, self-regulation, and well-being, and propose future research directions to explore the mechanisms, impacts, and potential supports associated with the sensory dimensions of masking.

Introduction

A utistic masking has most commonly been discussed within broader frameworks of camouflaging, which describe the conscious or subconscious use of strategies to minimize the visibility of autistic traits in social contexts. 1, 2 Within these frameworks, masking is often conceptualized alongside compensatory and assimilative strategies used to navigate neuronormative social expectations. Related concepts, such as adaptive morphing, 3 have also highlighted the fluid and context-dependent ways Autistic people may adapt their presentation across environments. Consequently, masking has most often been examined in relation to externally observable social and communication behaviors, such as modifying language, suppressing Autistic mannerisms, or forcing eye contact, 4, 5 and is most often examined in social and communication contexts. 2, [6] [7] [8] While camouflaging is often used in academic literature as an umbrella term, we use the term masking throughout this paper as it more closely reflects common community language and lived-experience discussions within Autistic spaces.

Nevertheless, social and communication experiencesand indeed, our very existence-are inherently sensory rich, shaped not only by social cues (e.g., gestures, voices, facial expressions) but also by the surrounding sensory environment (e.g., background noise, lighting) and our internal senses (e.g., interoceptive experiences such as body cues or feelings of overwhelm). Given that sensory processing differences are a hallmark of Autistic neurocognition, 9 it is important to consider the totality of sensory experience and subsequent processing of information in the context of Autistic masking. In this perspective, we explore the sensory dimensions of masking in autism, focusing on how sensory processing, regulation, and adaptation shape how masking is experienced. While this perspective focuses on Autistic experience, we acknowledge that similar forms of sensory masking may also be present across other neurodivergent populations. 6 Drawing on research and the lived experiences of Autistic authors, we outline key features of these sensory processes and highlight directions for future research. By explicitly considering the sensory dimensions of masking, we aim to broaden current conceptualizations of masking, highlight an underexplored aspect of Autistic experience, and identify priorities for future empirical research.

Masking as a Sensory and Social Experience

Social masking has received increasing attention in autism research over the past decade, describing how Autistic people often monitor and modify their behavior, language, and nonverbal cues to align with neuronormative expectations in everyday interactions. 2, 10, 11 However, ongoing debate remains regarding its conceptual boundaries, theoretical foundations, measurement, and relationship to related constructs such as impression management. [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] Recent integrative frameworks have further highlighted the multidimensional nature of masking and camouflaging, including not only behavioral strategies but differences in motivations, effort, ability, awareness, and the extent to which masking may become habitual or automatic. 17, 18 Despite this broader conceptualization, the sensory dimensions of masking remain comparatively underexplored. Across these varied forms, masking can involve continuous self-regulation, imposing a heightened cognitive load on Autistic people. [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] Masking is typically examined in social contexts; however, social interaction is necessarily a sensory experience and shaped by the broader sensory environments in which it occurs. 21, 24, 25 These dimensions intersect in social contexts, where the sensory environment can be challenging and shape how interactions are experienced.

Masking may, therefore, involve the ongoing inhibition of sensory responses both within and beyond social contexts, alongside the regulation of behavior in social circumstances. However, masking research has traditionally focused on social and communication characteristics, and has largely overlooked sensory experiences, which are also a core dimension of Autistic experience that may be masked. Examples of this can be found within the existing masking literature. Autistic people have described masking "sensory differences such as pain due to sounds," including one participant who described experiencing a weekly meltdown in response to the "extreme" noise and activity of a shopping center that was "carefully hidden as much as possible" 18 (p. 2384). Separately, sensory environments may increase the cognitive and regulatory demands of masking, amplifying the effort required to navigate social situations. Furthermore, sensory demands may interact with social demands, making masking more difficult to sustain in particular environments. For example, in Miller et al., 4 a participant noted that "sensory processing difficulties, particularly auditory, make masking tougher" (p. 334). Social interactions are almost never experienced in isolation from the sensory context: noise, lighting, or crowding, and the combined demands of social engagement and sensory masking can intensify cognitive fatigue and make participation far more effortful. [26] [27] [28] Like social masking, the persistent suppression or override of sensory responses can place substantial cognitive and emotional demands on Autistic people, with potential impacts on mental well-being, exhaustion, distress, and quality of life. 3, 29, 30

Sensory dimensions of masking

In this perspective, we focus on the sensory dimensions of masking, defined here as the conscious or subconscious suppression, inhibition, or override of sensory responses within everyday contexts. For instance, to meet neuronormative expectations, Autistic people may suppress reflexive responses to sensory input, such as covering their ears, moving away from a distressing stimulus, or expressing discomfort, particularly when stimuli are experienced as overwhelming or unsafe (e.g., sudden noise or flickering lights). In addition, this may include suppressing sensory responses that provide a selfregulatory function such as tapping, rocking, vocalizations, or reactions to stimuli that are perceived as aversive-especially in public or socially restrictive contexts. 5, 11, 31 We further propose that sensory masking may emerge, in part, through the repeated invalidation of Autistic sensory experiences from an early age. This may occur through external feedback or through comparison with others, where one's own sensory responses are perceived as different or less valid. 32 Sensory masking not only involves the modulation of responses to external stimulus (e.g., sound, light, touch), but also the attenuation or reinterpretation of internal bodily signals (i.e., interoceptive signals), including states such as discomfort, overwhelm, or affective responses to sensory input. 30, 33, 34 Consequently, these responses may be enacted consciously or become increasingly automatic over time, evolving across the lifespan. 10, 35 Over time, this could involve reduced access to or trust in one's own interoceptive signals, as individuals learn to override internal cues in favor of externally expected responses. 33 The developmental shaping of sensory masking and adaptation. Sensory masking is unlikely to emerge in early infancy or early toddlerhood, but rather appears to develop as a learned and conditioned response. 35 Young Autistic children are more likely to respond organically to overwhelming or aversive sensory stimuli. Indeed, studies have consistently demonstrated a higher rate of observable sensory responses and distress, including meltdowns and shutdowns, in younger Autistic children compared with Autistic adults. [36] [37] [38] Drawing on the cascading developmental model, 35 we propose that, over time, Autistic young people may learn to suppress or mask their natural sensory responses due to repeated exposure to unpredictable sensory environments, direct or perceived invalidation of their instinctive responses, or emotional distress. For example, a child who consistently experiences distress in response to loud auditory input may, over time, learn to inhibit visible reactions such as running away, ear covering, or vocalizing distress, often perceived as stigmatizing. 11 Similarly, children who seek or respond strongly to tactile (e.g., itchy uniform or clothing tag) or movement (e.g., chaotic classroom environment) input may gradually learn to hide or redirect these behaviors (even unknowingly) due to ongoing feedback from others, or from self-awareness that their response differs from others. [39] [40] [41] These patterns may initially be context-specific but can later become generalized across environments and modalities, particularly with repeated exposure. 42 Over time, as Autistic children navigate social expectations, environmental demands, or discouragement of their natural sensory responses, a gradual pattern of developmental suppression may begin to take root. 35, 43, 44 These adaptations can become internalized, resulting in automatic or subconscious suppression that can persist into adolescence and adulthood. 45 This perspective also aligns with concept of adaptive morphing, 3 which frames masking-related adaptations not as deliberate deception, but as biologically shaped and safety-driven responses that may emerge through lifelong conditioning. For some Autistic people, sensory masking may develop as an adaptive response to previous experiences of punishment, bullying, invalidation, or exclusion following sensory expression. For instance, an Autistic person may automatically ignore the discomfort of their work uniform as they have been repeatedly told that the rejection of school uniform is unacceptable. 39 Similarly, a loud, crowded room may not only overwhelm an Autistic person's sensory processing but also necessitate additional masking to navigate social interactions within that environment without drawing attention to their sensory distress.

TOWARD SENSORY ACCOUNTS OF AUTISTIC MASKING: A PERSPECTIVE

This dual burden can exacerbate stress and lead to heightened fatigue and anxiety. 46, 47 Managing contrasting or unpredictable sensory stimuli may also impact the effectiveness of masking strategies. 23 However, when sustained over time, the continued suppression of sensory responses may itself contribute to distress, trauma-related experiences, and reduced opportunities for sensory regulation and recovery. 17, 18 Nevertheless, it is important to note that Autistic people who may be affected by more severe sensory issues across modalities "may not have the option to mask their autism" 31 (pp. 48-51). Many Autistic people can express self-regulatory and sensory responses to sensory stimuli, such as body weaving, rocking, spinning and vocalizations, which may reflect efforts to manage or respond to fragmented, fluctuating, attenuated or confusing sensory experience, [48] [49] [50] and these reactions are often highly stigmatized. 41, 51, 52 For these Autistic people, managing sensory distress in accordance with social expectations may require immense effort to suppress, override, or regulate natural sensory responses, potentially contributing to exhaustion, anxiety, distress, emotion dysregulation, and social withdrawal. 50, 53 For some, the intensity of sensory load may make such suppression difficult or impossible, even when social expectations are understood. Visible sensory responses should therefore not necessarily be interpreted as reflecting differences in social motivation or understanding, but may instead reflect the demands of navigating overwhelming sensory environments. 41, 54 As with social masking-which is often understood as a strategy for social survival and safety 2-5,10,55,56 -we suggest that sensory masking may similarly function as a survival mechanism. It involves knowingly or unknowingly dismissing or suppressing instinctive sensory responses and replacing them with learned behaviors to maintain a sense of safety in one's environment. These behaviors can reflect contextual demands, like wearing tactile-aversive clothing, 57 suppressing sensory overwhelm from noise or lighting to focus, 58 or navigating sensory challenges embedded within social interactions. 26 Just as social masking has been framed as a response to social risk, 4, 11 we argue that sensory masking similarly could reflect in-the-moment efforts to manage environmental threat and discomfort.

As people navigate through different life stages and face evolving environmental demands, the adaptive strategies employed to manage sensory experiences, including sensory masking, may undergo changes. 42, [59] [60] [61] This dynamic process involves adjustments in cognitive loading, as people develop new coping mechanisms or experience shifts in the effectiveness of existing strategies. Over time, the chronic experience of sustained sensory masking could contribute to Autistic burnout. 19, 22, 36 Cognitive effects of masking sensory responses. For some Autistic people, challenging sensory environments can contribute to overwhelm, distress, fatigue, and reduced capacity to engage, making the sensory context particularly relevant to understanding the demands of masking. Like social masking, we acknowledge that sensory masking may often be necessary for Autistic people to feel safe and able to participate in community and social activities. However, it can come at a cost. When sensory input becomes overwhelming, sustained exposure may contribute to sensory overload, while the additional cognitive effort required to suppress or override sensory responses may further increase the demands placed on the individual. 23, 62 Continued exposure to adverse sensory conditions, in conjunction with the sustained effort of sensory masking or persistent override of natural sensory responses, can leave Autistic people increasingly vulnerable to distress and exhaustion. In our current work, one participant described their process with loud environmental noise, "the sound is just so overwhelming all of me is focusing on just getting through that, leaving no energy left."

Another aspect worth exploring is the role of hypervigilance in masking from a sensory perspective. Hypervigilance is a multifaceted process involving threat detection, sensory processing, cognitive appraisal, and physiological responses. Understanding how these interacting processes influence sensory experiences may help explain why sustained sensory masking can be cognitively and physically demanding. Hypervigilance involves neuroception, or the constant detection of threat via subconscious processes. 63 From a sensory perspective, it involves a state of heightened sensory alertness, often associated with a fight-or-flight response, in anticipation of potential threats or dangers-particularly in hostile or inhospitable sensory environments. 64 Additional processing of neuroceptive information involves the integration of interoceptive signals from within the body and external sensory signals, alongside affective (emotional) and cognitive processes in response to the physical and social worlds. 63 This may include acute awareness of fluorescent lighting, background noise, or nearby movement. As such, hypervigilance engages with both subconscious and conscious cognitive functions.

The complex interplay between hypervigilance and the suppression of instinctive sensory responses adds further cognitive strain, 11, 65 intensifying the demands Autistic people face when navigating sensory environments. 26, 27 For example, an Autistic person in unpredictable and sensorily busy environments, such as a busy shopping mall or classroom, may be hyperaware of every sound, vibration, and movement in the space, while simultaneously suppressing their instinct to cover their ears or stim in response to the overwhelm, to avoid drawing attention or being judged. Autistic people's responses to such environments may include autonomic responses, or involuntary physiological responses to perceived threat, which can involve dysregulation and difficulty returning to baseline. This autonomic activity may be reflected in measures such as heart rate variability (HRV), the variation in time between successive heartbeats, with lower HRV associated with greater anxiety and reduced autonomic flexibility. 66, 67 Although these relationships have not yet been directly examined in sensory masking, physiological indicators of autonomic regulation may provide one potential avenue for investigating the mechanisms and longer-term consequences of sustained hypervigilance.

Over time, sustained sensory masking may have cumulative effects. Continually suppressing, overriding, or dismissing sensory responses may contribute to disruptions in interoceptive processing, including difficulty identifying or responding to internal states such as fatigue, distress, or sensory discomfort. 68, 69 Reduced access to or trust in internal bodily signals may further contribute to exhaustion, dysregulation, distress, and difficulty recognizing needs for rest, recovery, or sensory regulation. 35 Autistic burnout can further reduce one's ability to prioritize and ignore sensory inputs, adapt to complex sensory environments, or tolerate hostile sensory stimuli. 19, 22 These cumulative effects may negatively impact well-being, contributing to depression, anxiety, trauma-related experiences, Autistic burnout, and even suicidal thoughts. 11, [19] [20] [21] These effects can, in turn, limit everyday participation and overall quality of life. 23, 70 This reduction of access to and trust in interoceptive input may also lead to depersonalization, in further detachment from one's own body, mind, or identity. In the long term, without opportunities for self-regulation or reprieve, the cumulative effects of sustained masking, sensory overload, burnout and hypervigilance may contribute to trauma-related responses, sometimes resembling dissociation. 21, 71 Trauma can include big "T" trauma such as bullying or violence due to sensory expressions, or small "t" trauma, for example, feeling overwhelmed daily at school. 3, 20, 72 Autistic people may feel detached from their bodies or emotions or experience the world as distant or unreal-experiences that may arise in the context of prolonged sensory masking. 33, 68 When dissociation occurs, sensory experiences may become numbed, distorted, or intensified, reflecting a disconnection from one's environment. 71 While dissociation can serve as a short-term coping strategy, 73 its long-term effects are often detrimental, contributing to mental health issues, difficulties with concentration and memory, and physical symptoms such as numbness and reduced pain awareness. 74 Recovery from Unwanted or Sustained Sensory Masking. Given the cumulative toll of sustained masking or persistent override of a sensory response, it is essential to explore approaches that may be helpful in supporting Autistic people to engage with sensory environments while minimizing the need to suppress their actual responses. Considering sensory masking as another dimension of masking may extend our understanding of Autistic sensory experiences beyond traditional behavioral descriptors of hyper-and hyporesponsivity by recognizing that sensory responses may also be shaped by masking processes. This does not imply that all sensory responses involve autonomous choice; rather, sensory responses may reflect multiple interacting processes, including sensory responsivity, context, lived experience, selfregulation, and, in some instances, sensory masking as an autonomous choice. 4, 10, 20, 33, 54 Autistic people often engage in self-regulatory behaviors to manage sensory overwhelm. 75, 76 One way this can occur is through the use of individualized, self-selected sensory input or strategies to support regulation and predictability within the environment. 76, 77 Examples include stimming, fidgeting with objects, seeking specific textures, or listening to familiar sounds or music. In our current empirical work, one Autistic participant described their experience with background noise as increasing their ability to focus, "This is just normal background noise, it's kind of reassuring to have this kind of noise going on, as it helps me know where I am in space and what's going on around me." These strategies can serve as an "inthe-moment" tool 3 to support self-regulation and help restore a sense of stability or return to "baseline" in a versatile or overwhelming sensory environment. These strategies are consistent with concepts developed by the Autistic community such as sensory toolkits or "sensory diets." 75, 78 Importantly, the use of sensory toolkits or other sensory regulation strategies should not necessarily be interpreted as sensory masking. The same sensory behavior may function as regulation or masking depending on its context and purpose; sensory masking may occur when a preferred or instinctive sensory response is suppressed, inhibited, or modified in response to environmental or social expectations or anticipated repercussions.

Despite their functional and regulatory value, these selfregulatory behaviors-particularly stimming-are deeply misunderstood and pathologized. 41, 79 Many therapeutic approaches continue to focus on reducing or eliminating these behaviors. 80, 81 As a result, Autistic people often report feeling unsafe or discouraged from utilizing sensory selfregulatory strategies in public places like schools, workplaces and community settings. 41, 54 Furthermore, an existing body of Autistic-led research has emphasized that sensory self-regulation is not merely a coping strategy but a core aspect of Autistic identity and well-being. 3, 41 Restricting access to these self-regulatory strategies can leave Autistic people dysregulated and distressed, ultimately limiting their ability to participate fully in everyday life. 54 Supporting sensory unmasking. More broadly, unmasking refers to the process of reducing or no longer consciously or unconsciously suppressing Autistic responses when it feels safe to do so. From a sensory perspective, this may involve developing awareness of and responding to sensory experiences and needs that may previously have been suppressed or overridden, and, where appropriate, allowing sensory preferences and responses to be experienced or expressed. Sensory masking in autism is a complex adaptation to navigate a world that often does not cater to Autistic sensibilities. It is deeply intertwined with feelings of fear of negative evaluation 7, 8 and the deep need for safety. Unmasking may require courage and vulnerability-facilitated by trust-to navigate the uncertainty and risks associated with expressing oneself in ways that may differ significantly from neuronormative expectations. 2, 10, 31, 82 Supporting sensory unmasking may involve reconnecting with interoceptive signals, enabling and empowering individuals to identify and respond to internal cues in ways that support regulation and well-being. 33, 68, 83 Since sensory masking may be adaptive in some contexts, the goal is not to discourage all sensory masking. Rather, efforts should focus on reducing the need for chronic or involuntary sensory masking by expanding Autistic people's autonomy, access to accommodations, and opportunities to regulate sensory experiences in ways that are flexible, supported, and self-determined.

Autistic people are, however, disproportionately at risk of vulnerability experiences and victimization, 84, 85 which may further complicate decisions around reducing masking as a strategy. These experiences can make the prospect of unmasking daunting for three reasons. First, the fear of negative evaluation and rejection is heightened, potentially exacerbated by legitimate fears for physical and emotional safety in Autistic people who might already have experienced negative interactions when their authentic behaviors were visible. 7, 8, 10, 86 Second, unmasking would require Autistic people to understand their own sensory masking tendencies-particularly those subconscious, conditioned responses-which can be mentally and emotionally demanding to gain insights into. 62, 87 Third, societal stigma and misconceptions about autism can lead to misinterpretation of natural sensory responses or selfregulatory behaviors, further discouraging Autistic people from "dropping their guard." 5, 11, 82 These factors create a complex web of challenges that Autistic people must navigate when considering whether to unmask.

Establishing sensory safety.

Central to supporting reductions in sensory masking (should an individual wish to) and the authentic expression of sensory responses or regulatory strategies is establishing a sense of safety through building trust, whether in people, environments, or within policies and systems. 88, 89 A sense of safety is pivotal for Autistic people to feel less fearful of or worried about negative evaluation, allowing them to unmask and/or engage in sensory self-regulatory behaviors. [90] [91] [92] Sensorially safe environments are those that are sensory-friendly, nonjudgmental, and inclusive, offering a sense of belonging, welcome and understanding. 26 Practically, this could mean allowing Autistic people to familiarize themselves with their sensory environments as familiarity could alleviate some of the required cognitive load that sensory masking would usually require. 43, 44 Other people, particularly those who are non-autistic, play a crucial role in facilitating safe expression of sensory needs, responses and self-regulatory strategies. Trustworthiness can be demonstrated by accepting, empathizing and respecting Autistic person's unique sensory experiences and needs. People can support Autistic people by creating environments that are sensory-friendly, such as reducing background noise, offering quiet spaces or being mindful of lighting and odors. [93] [94] [95] Encouraging and respecting the use of sensory self-regulatory techniques and personalized sensory "tools," whether it be sound or lighting choices, self-regulatory sensory expressions or tools like fidgets or weighted items, also demonstrates support. Furthermore, fostering an atmosphere of acceptance and understanding-where Autistic people feel safe to express their sensory needs without fear of judgment and stigma is essential. 96 This support not only benefits Autistic people but also promotes a more inclusive and empathetic society, where diversity in sensory processing is recognized and accommodated. In essence, supporting sensory unmasking is about creating spaces where everyone can thrive in their own unique way.

Allowing sensory reset.

Having time and space for sensory reset can bring positive outcomes for Autistic people. 38, 77 When Autistic people feel safe to unmask, take time to regulate, and embrace and explore sensory joys, they may experience reductions in stress and anxiety that often come with sensory overload and suppression of natural sensory responses, and shift into a balance where sensory masking can be used as a tool when needed, but is not a persistent way of being. 20, 90 In addition, allowing for time to unmask in sensorially safe environments could allow Autistic people to recharge and reset, to participate more fully in social and professional settings as desired, improve the ability to engage, and overall, potentially enhancing a person's wellbeing and quality of life. 97 Engaging in monotropic states could also facilitate sensory reset. Monotropism refers to a cognitive style characterized by intense focus on or deep immersion in specific interests or stimuli, which has been identified as a trait that is core to the Autistic experience in Autistic-led literature. 64, 98, 99 In sensory terms, the intense focus or deep immersion can often increase the ability to block out and ignore other sensory environmental stimuli. 64, 100 When Autistic people are able to engage deeply in their passions, the need for constant sensory masking may be reduced, as monotropic focus tends to filter out competing sensory input, 28, 32 thus facilitating sensory reset.

Ultimately, when masking of sensory responses is shaped by experiences of threat or chronic stress, it may become an automatic and undesirable response to overwhelming or unsafe environments. 71 In these cases, it can create a dissonance between a person and their sensory experiences, leading to long-term dysregulation, exhaustion, and difficulty recognizing or responding to sensory needs. Nevertheless, if we can create safe spaces, normalize sensory self-regulation and expression, support sensory unmasking, and facilitate sensory reset, sensory masking can shift from being an involuntary survival mechanism to a tool that can be utilized with autonomy. By combining these approaches, Autistic people can have more agency over when and how they engage with the world in a sensory-safe and enjoyable way, rather than being trapped in a constant state of hypervigilance, survival and suppression. A sense of safety and trust, both in people and in the predictability of sensory environments, plays a crucial role in making this possible, ensuring that masking is not an imposed necessity but one tool among many for navigating the world.

Future Research Directions

First, while the extant literature has primarily focused on social masking, expanding research into the role of sensory masking could help gain a deeper understanding of its neurobiological mechanisms and impacts, including the developmental trajectory of these phenomena. 56, 91, 101, 102 Longitudinal studies could help track how sensory masking or the conscious or unconscious ongoing inhibition of sensory responses changes over time and contexts, its connection to certain strategies like sensory self-regulation and sensory expression, and its cumulative effects-such as burnout, hypervigilance, and dissociation. It would also be useful to explore whether some environments make sensory masking more necessary, while others help reduce its impact. Future research should also examine the relationship between sensory masking and broader processes such as impression management, including whether and how socially motivated suppression of sensory responses may differ between Autistic and non-autistic people in its underlying sensory experience, function, and consequences.

Second, developing approaches to better capture or measure sensory aspects of masking, similar to existing masking and camouflaging frameworks such as the Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire, 42, 103 could provide a structured and rigorous way to assess its prevalence and impact. Future work should also consider how different methodological approaches may provide complementary insights into sensory masking: Qualitative interviews may explore lived experiences and how sensory masking develops and is understood; self-report measures may capture its frequency and perceived impacts; ecological momentary assessment may examine when and in which contexts sensory masking occurs in daily life; observational methods may capture behavioral expressions of sensory masking; and psychophysiological measures may help investigate associated physiological processes. Future research is also needed to distinguish sensory masking from related sensory processes, including interoceptive differences, sensory hyporesponsivity, and sensory regulation, and to better understand how these processes overlap, diverge, and interact over time. A greater understanding of sensory masking will not only contribute to autism research but also inform policy changes for workplaces, schools, and public spaces, supporting the development of environments that are more accessible and affirming for Autistic people. 70 Third, further research is needed to better understand the developmental trajectory of sensory masking across the lifespan, including how sensory suppression, self-regulation, and sensory expression may change over time and contexts. 35 Longitudinal research could examine how sensory masking develops and changes across life stages and contexts, including transitions from playgrounds and classrooms to workplaces, where sensory and social demands and the feasibility of sensory (un)masking may shift. Retrospective qualitative approaches may also provide insight into how sensory masking begins, persists, and changes across the lifespan. 104 Research could explore how sensory masking emerges developmentally through repeated exposure to sensory overwhelm, social expectations, environmental demands, or the invalidation of sensory experiences. It may also be important to investigate how prolonged sensory masking influences longterm outcomes such as burnout, hypervigilance, dissociation, interoceptive processing, and difficulties recognizing or responding to internal sensory and emotional states.

Finally, a key research question is how to support Autistic people in navigating sensory masking in ways that are safe, flexible and self-determined across contexts. Research could explore whether sensory-friendly environments, personalized regulation tools, and broader community accommodations reduce the need for persistent or unwanted masking. Research should also consider the intersection between sensory masking and other identity-based experiences, such as gender and cultural background, to understand how different factors influence sensory masking, sensory self-regulation and sensory self-expression. 105, 106 Research should also include investigating the sensory dimensions of masking with regard to stigma, and how stigma can be addressed and mitigated.

Conclusion

Masking may involve the suppression of responses, including sensory, as Autistic people navigate environments that can feel overwhelming, unsafe, or socially demanding. It reflects the tension between authentic sensory experiences and societal expectations of "normal" sensory reactions, shaped by past experiences of sensory overload or experiential rejection. Rather than representing a distinct or separate construct, the sensory dimensions of masking highlight how sensory experiences are embedded within broader masking processes. While sensory masking as a strategy can help Autistic people function in unsupportive contexts, it also carries significant emotional and cognitive costs.

To reduce the need for masking, greater awareness and meaningful changes to sensory environments are essentialnot only to accommodate sensory processing differences, but to support Autistic people's autonomy to engage in sensory self-regulation or unmasking, ultimately creating sensory safety and time and space for reset. Recognizing these sensory dimensions of masking as both adaptive and potentially burdensome invites more inclusive design, enabling Autistic people to participate more fully and authentically in everyday life.
