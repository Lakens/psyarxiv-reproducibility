Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

The role of financial resources in subjective well-being is extensively studied, yet with many unresolved debates on the precise association, inflection points, or applications. Using multidimensional data from 91 countries (N=53,589), we find near-universal support for a positive association, which is strongest in wealthier countries and, globally, highly elastic as household incomes surpass local costs of living. Uniquely, we observe a "psychological inheritance", where the strongest associations are determined by economic class in childhood, even after significant upward mobility by adulthood. We also find inelasticity in well-being at the top and bottom of income distributions, which largely indicates satiation and decoupling at wealth extremes. Across countries, well-being surpasses average at household incomes around 4.5 times the cost of living. Downward mobility is linked with substantial declines but likewise better outcomes than those of equivalent adult incomes that started lower economically, suggesting a psychological endowment where starting point and relative stability are as vital as growth, though the association is strongest in wealthier countries.

The link between income and well-being is therefore strongly supported while personal financial history and broader economic environment have substantial impacts on how money relates to happiness, and thus, to individual and population well-being.

Introduction

Enabling, protecting, promoting, and advancing the well-being of individuals and communities is the ultimate priority for national prosperity. Such outcomes may directly impact economic circumstances for individuals and populations (Occhipinti et al., 2023 (Occhipinti et al., , 2023;; WHO, 2025). At the same time, financial status has significant and direct impacts on individual well-being and mental health (Elgar et al., 2021; Iannello et al., 2021; Owens & Hadley, 2024; Topp et al., 2015), establishing a bidirectional relationship (Dote Pardo & Parra-Domínguez, 2025; Zhang et al., 2023).

Whether money can "buy" happiness (Giannetti et al., 2015; Killingsworth, 2021; Matz et al., 2016; Thomson et al., 2022) or merely suppress the common threats to various aspects and dimensions of well-being (J. Chen et al., 2025; Killingsworth et al., 2023; Li & Managi, 2023; Thomson et al., 2022) is a going debate. Yet, selecting optimal analyses and interpreting conclusions about relevant findings are largely subjective, a universal interpretation is unlikely to ever reach consensus. It is, however, well established that the significant absence of financial resources (i.e., poverty) -at the individual, community, or national level -is strongly associated with poor well-being and mental health (Jantsch et al., 2024). Subsequently, increased income is associated with greater positive emotion, particularly for those close to the poverty line (Bufe et al., 2022; Cheng et al., 2021; Lai et al., 2023).

Diminished effects of greater levels of income have also been observed. For individuals, Kahneman and Deaton (2010) canonically found a steady rise in life evaluation and emotional well-being concurrent with increases to income, though the effect diminished between $60,000 and $90,000, with a $75,000 mid-point that was 3.4 to 6.9 times the household poverty line at the time. Subsequent evidence was argued to show an unmistakably linear relationship (with log of family income), concluding that if diminishing effects exist, those estimates were not in proximity to where they had been reported (Killingsworth et al., 2023; Stevenson & Wolfers, 2008 , 2013).

At a population level, the Easterlin Paradox illustrates that, while higher-income countries tend to have greater well-being than low-income countries, increased national wealth does not necessarily lead to a corresponding improvement in national well-being (Easterlin, 1974; Easterlin & Sawangfa, 2010). However, longitudinal studies have challenged this notion, concluding upward movement cooccurs (Dwyer & Dunn, 2022; D. W. Sacks et al., 2012). Factors such as social comparisons, the buffering of negative experiences in poorer countries, and time scarcity in wealthier countries (Connolly & Gärling, 2023) may instead explain why overall societal well-being does not keep pace with national increases in income over time. Improvements in population well-being may also be slowed given the pace of wealth distribution has increased at the top 10% while wealth in the middle and lower halves has remained stagnant (Karamcheva et al., 2024).

Multiple studies have highlighted the differences in the impact of income and wealth on well-being between rural and urban areas (Ding et al., 2021) and different cultural contexts (Dominko & Verbič, 2021), as well as across different age groups and life stages (Gao et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2024).

Income inequality also plays a significant role across regions, and can inhibit improved well-being even when individuals move upward economically (Du et al., 2024; Humair & Salahuddin Ayyubi, 2024; Tibber et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024), even if its national effects are less clear (Sommet et al., 2026). For individuals and communities, movements around relative wealth or poverty can take on different meanings depending on these factors, particularly when the benefits of wealth toward wellbeing have been satiated (Jebb et al., 2018; D. W. Sacks et al., 2012) or where community-wide absence of economic opportunity means well-being is derived from entirely different factors (Galbraith et al., 2024). Yet, few studies account for household size and relative income in the context of well-being.

At both the community and individual levels, analyses of upward economic mobility have shown mixed findings regarding association with well-being and health (Kwon et al., 2022), whereas others show a positive correlation with well-being (Machado et al., 2021) and health outcomes (Dolan & Lordan, 2021). In contrast, downward mobility has also been shown to exert a greater effect on wellbeing (Bulczak & Gugushvili, 2022; Hiyoshi et al., 2023) while national economic stagnation (as opposed to stability, which is typically positive) has shown negative impacts (E. Chen et al., 2022).

The interaction between economic mobility and well-being has also been studied in a temporal frame: past well-being is linked to an improvement in future income (Kind & Wittekopf, 2025), and past poverty is associated with poorer health outcomes despite subsequent upward mobility (Hiyoshi et al., 2023).

Conclusions and precise inflection points vary globally, such that how linear the relationship is between income and well-being is not stable even within a population (Killingsworth et al., 2023). At the same time, the most informative studies on the topic have largely relied on single-item measures of happiness or life satisfaction while also having to adjust measures of income for patterns to emerge. This is inherently critical and limiting: while governments have long looked at GDP as a proxy for population well-being, sufficient evidence indicates that GDP (i.e., national income) alone fails to fully capture it (Giannetti et al., 2015) and that single-item measures lack depth as well as actionability (Ruggeri et al., 2020).

Thus, a comprehensive alignment of where effects and lack of associations between income and wellbeing exist is of utmost importance. If specific inflection points exist, precision around those plateaus can be critical to identify policy levers and needs. However, simply incorporating a broader, contextualized understanding of both constructs will better provide a necessary set of insights to leverage that genuinely, as opposed to vaguely, could impact well-being. Ideally, that broadness includes diversity in both measurements and in analytical approaches to paint a complete picture rather than rely on or base adamant conclusions on a single finding.

To address those questions, we analyzed data from a global survey on mental, financial, and social well-being (research questions are articulated in full in Supplement 2.0.0 and 3.0.0). Our aim was not to retest existing questions, but to address both the contextual aspect of income and its association with well-being as well as the relative aspect compared to local living standards all within a standardized set of robust measures (beyond the limited single-item measures used typically). The primary objective was to provide a clearer pattern for the link between well-being and financial status (static and mobility) by looking at raw and relative incomes across countries while also factoring in the role of prior economic circumstances. We took this approach to provide robust input on the link between financial circumstances and well-being, testing comprehensive measures from multiple approaches. We also factored in concerning omissions about income thresholds in existing work.

Though our aim was understanding the role of relative income and economic history, we also provide consistent evidence that appears to validate and reconcile competing views on the income-well-being relationship, and a driving paradox in economic theory.

Methods

We use the data obtained by a previous study (Ruggeri et al., 2026), which provides estimates on selfreported sociodemographics, financial circumstances, and well-being across 92 countries. Participants were recruited through a dynamic, multi-layered engagement approach. They completed an online survey with questions about mental health, personal finances, and demographics. All measures were translated and adapted to local contexts, including aligning all incomes to pre-tax, incorporating national indicators of GDP, and adding a relative cost of living value for each individual (described later; extensive details throughout Supplement 3).

An extensive data cleaning process removed responses that had either a significant number of flags or gave free-text responses that were simply implausible. Exclusions were applied prior to or (naive to) analyses to avoid biasing the standard toward any particular result.

For the purposes of this study, we analyzed data in several ways. To present general patterns, we present descriptive models that combine income (individual and national), multiple validated wellbeing measures, and various local standards to establish relative financial circumstances. We used the 10-item multidimensional psychological well-being (MPWB) scale for the comprehensive well-being measure. The scale can be used as individual measures, a sum score (ranging from 10 to 70), and as factor scores. The factor scores can be estimated using either a global scale or as within-country scores that account for heterogeneity in responses between countries. For robustness, all approaches were tested, though within-country factor scores are typically considered the most appropriate given income is largely a within-country construct.

We then ran a series of robust, inferential models to test the direct link between childhood SES (chSES), (relative) financial circumstances, and a composite, locally-standardized measure of psychological well-being. Data involved approximately 52,000 participants for most analyses and 38,000 for those using chSES, which was an optional question with five categories (from "Poor" to "Wealthy"). Deviations for specific questions where values or participants were excluded or missing are detailed extensively in the supplementary materials.

Analyses proceeded through a progressive, structured model-building approach that iteratively relaxed the parametric constraints imposed on the data as warranted by formal model comparison and diagnostic evaluation. For each research question, the variables were operationalized and transformed as appropriate for the specific modelling objective. For example, a continuous measure of income was log-transformed to address skewness, and predictors were mean-centered in multilevel specifications to separate out between-and within-country effects. Models were estimated sequentially, with increasing structural complexity. Initial linear mixed models were extended, for example, to incorporate nonlinear smooths, random smooths, and factor-smooth country deviations where suitable. These extensions allowed for the evaluation of the heterogeneity in the form and magnitude of the income-well-being associations under analysis.

Model selection relied on information criteria (e.g., AIC or BIC), variance explained (e.g., R²), and error metrics (e.g., RMSE), retaining models only for which formal comparisons indicated a significantly improved fit. Where applicable, models accounted for the hierarchical country-level structure of the data, and observed nonlinearities were explicitly captured with multilevel generalized additive models. This approach enabled the simultaneous estimation of both the global trends and country-specific heterogeneity in the functional form and magnitude of the effects.

Because data were collected to address myriad topics regarding mental, financial, and social wellbeing, analyses were carried out in parallel to other work (Ruggeri et al., 2026). To ensure this would not bias analyses, the lead statistician on this study worked separately from all other team members, particularly the lead investigator and individuals responsible for data management (see Supplement 2.0.0 and 3.0.0). In this way, decisions on data cleaning and hypothesis-building were conducted independently from the lead analyst, who was also blinded to all discussions and processes. Once data were prepared, the analyst was provided a context-free set of research questions, blinded to hypotheses, with the full set of questions and hypotheses uploaded to a private pre-registration page (BLINDED). The seven primary questions asked about the best empirical description of the incomewell-being relationship, adding layers for national wealth, childhood wealth, income satiation, decoupling, and economic mobility.

All questions were tested first using income alone then re-tested with cost-of-living proxy factored into income to account for heterogeneity in wealth relative to location, living standards, and household size. These data were purchased from an international organization that produces national guidance for 186 countries on necessary wages to maintain basic living standards, see Supplement 1.1.1 and 1.2.3. The purpose in testing these was to identify whether unique distinctions in well-being exist explicitly around base costs of living (though living wages are distinct from costs of living (Guzi et al., 2026), we treat them as an appropriate and highly correlated proxy). This overcomes the limitation of how identical incomes may have very different meanings for different households within the same country. Well-being measures were based on data collected in 2025; income and living wage were primarily for 2024 (as the survey asked for the most recent annual income or monthly average).

Results

To provide a comprehensive, unifying analysis of the link between wealth and well-being, we tested seven critical questions (see Methods) that accounted for income, chSES, national wealth (using both GDP and World Bank income categories), as well as other social and financial indicators. We present these systematically as means of providing an ordered, empirical narrative of the association between financial circumstances and multidimensional well-being.

Except where stated otherwise, analyses reflect 53,589 participants from 91 countries. National and global averages are already reported elsewhere (Ruggeri et al., 2026), but typically indicate languishing to moderate mental health (generally around 50 to 55 on a scale from 10 to 70, where scores above 45 indicate positive overall well-being). To align with country-specific incomes and currency norms, well-being values from the MPWB scale primarily made use of within-country factor scores in this study. That accounts for variability in how countries, languages, and cultures differ in how they respond, and standardizes all national means to zero.

Incomes were initially reported in deciles. A continuous measure of gross income was extrapolated from a combination of these deciles and cleaned, free-text income entries from the original survey in which tax deductions back-calculated, inflation rates applied, and conversion (where useful) to a USD equivalent to give a unifying estimate of household income (see Supplement A0.3. for relevant income data cleaning, and Supplement 2.02 -2.03 (and Supplement 3.02-3.03) for tax deduction back calculations).

Income and well-being

Across over 50,000 people from 91 countries, we find an unambiguous, positive association between income and well-being, with an overall modest effect (see Figure 1). While different analytical approaches to this may produce idiosyncratic nuance (see Supplement 2 and 3), particularly in how specific deciles may be more or less similar, there were no substantive exceptions to the general trend.

The best-fitting global model using the income deciles was a linear model that treated the deciles as a numeric ladder with a separate dummy for "no income". This indicated that within-country MPWB factor score increased significantly across income levels (β = 0.047, SE = 0.002, t = 29.12, p < 0.001), while no-income was associated with a negative "penalty" on well-being (β = -0.132, SE = 0.023, t = -5.65, p < 0.001). While robust, the global effect size is relatively small; F(2, 52417) = 553.6, p < .001, R² adj. = 0.021. We repeated the analysis using a log-transformed estimate of income on a continuous scale. The iterative model comparison indicated that a multi-level generalized additive model (GAM) that estimated a global smooth on log income (mean-centered, inclusive of no-income individuals) and a country-level random intercept best described the association. The GAM indicated a significant nonlinear but predominantly positive link between income and within-country MPWB (edf = 9.43, F = 86.31, p < 0.001) with between-country heterogeneity (edf = 65.46, F = 3.46, p < 0.001), variance explained was again modest (R² adj. = 0.065). Further details on the model and its competitors are provided in Supplement 2.1b.

The general relationship between income and well-being largely repeats when applying either the income deciles or using the computed value for income relative to living wage. However, using relative income provides distinct features that make for more interpretable, applied insights. Primarily, we directly observe that income equal to living wage is associated with below-average well-being, and that average well-being is only observed when relative income approaches log =1 (that is, approximately 2.7 times living wage) when all groups are combined. We also observe an extreme drop for those reporting no income, (β = -0.39, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001), which is observable here as zero income is numerically distinct from the minimum relative income calculations -unlike with the income variable prior.

National wealth, income, and well-being

Studies on money and happiness largely model high-income countries of similar social and economic systems, and even among those, disproportionately center on US data. To account for this, we tested whether the income-well-being gradient differed by World Bank country-income group by regressing within-country MPWB factor scores on income decile and its interaction with World Bank countryincome group.

A random-slope multi-level model showed a positive within-country association between income decile and MPWB factor scores (within-country) in low-income World Bank classified countries (β = 0.039, t = 3.63), with variability in both intercepts and slopes. The income gradient was significantly steeper in high-income countries (interaction β = 0.033, t = 2.97), implying a slope estimate of 0.072 per income decile. Lower-and upper-middle -income countries did not differ significantly from lowincome countries. The model also included an explicit no-income penalty (β = -0.132, t = -5.44). A version of this model using predicted means is presented in Figure 2, which shows that the relationship between income and well-being is significantly steeper in higher-income countries.

Using log income, the results were extended by indicating that nonlinear income effects by national income group significantly improved model fit, with all group-specific smooths significant( low income: p = 0.001, all other groups p < 0.001 and varying in complexity from low-income (edf = 1.02) to high-income countries (edf = 5.17). The income-wellbeing association was strongest and most nonlinear in the high-income group (F = 153.44). Overall fit was modest (R² adj. = 0.022).

When repeating the model using a ratio of income to living wage, we find a generally similar pattern, with stronger income-well-being associations in higher income countries. There was, however, a compelling distinction: across national income groups, incomes at or below living wage are associated with at or below average well-being, which -universally -only reliably exceeds average at approximately log = 1.5 (see Figure 2) and translates to approximately 4.5 times the living wage. In real terms, this means for a location where a basic living standard requires an income of $30,000, mean well-being only significantly exceeds national average at an annual gross income of approximately $135,000. This pattern aligns with the earlier finding of average well-being only by log=1, though we emphasize the latter log=1.5 is more useful given national wealth is factored in.

A common criticism of this topic is that many studies on this topic rely on US data. To provide insights on this, we also tested whether the relationship was unique for the US compared to other high-income countries. Using multiple modeling approaches, there is evidence of a steeper-thanaverage association in the US compared to other high-income countries, though it is not an outlier (particularly when using the living wage ratios) nor does it have a distinct nonlinear shape relative to peer countries. Furthermore, when accounting for unique heterogeneity within each country, this effect becomes very small and the US pattern falls well within the expected range of high-income variability. Also, as will be noted later, the US does not have the strongest overall association, thus while it may be distinct from other high-income countries in the aggregate, it is not an outlier when compared to all countries individually.

Economic mobility, income, and well-being

Prior to any argument around income satiation -that is, the point at which money no longer measurably advances well-being identified by Kahneman and Deaton, among others -models must factor in not only an individual's current circumstance, but their previous economic standing and trajectory. Though two adults may arrive at the same income, they may not have reached it in the same timeline or through the same means. Likewise they are not guaranteed to relate to, spend, save, or otherwise leverage it in any homogenous way. Put simply: the same $10 (or $100 or $1,000) can affect two lives in very different ways. To account for this, we used a proxy of economic history by incorporating a measure of self-reported childhood socioeconomic status (chSES) into the models (see Figure 3, all panels).

A multilevel model was used to estimate the within-country association between income decile and MPWB as a function of chSES, allowing both random intercepts and random income slopes across countries. Income was positively associated with well-being overall (b = 0.076, SE = 0.006, t = 13.21), and this association varied modestly by chSES: slopes were strongest among those reporting "Poor" (b = 0.079) and somewhat weaker for higher chSES (e.g., "Above average but not wealthy," b = 0.049), with small negative interaction terms indicating attenuation of the income gradient at higher chSES (largest for 'Above average but not wealthy': interaction b = -0.029, t = -4.34).

Looking to the continuous income variable a multilevel GAMM including chSES as a categorical fixed effect, separate smooth functions of log income for each group, and a country-level random intercept showed strong main effects of chSES (all higher categories significantly above "Poor," p < .001) and significant nonlinear income-well-being associations within every SES group (all smooths p < .001), with differing degrees of curvature (edf ≈ 1.03-3.79), indicating heterogeneity in both baseline levels and functional form (R² adj = 0.034). Effects were largely replicated in both direction and magnitude when income was replaced by living wage ratio estimates.

Across multiple approaches, we find an unambiguous interaction for chSES: at all adult income deciles and incomes relative to costs, well-being is higher for individuals born into greater wealth.

Adults that have moved up significantly from childhood wealth consistently have lower well-being than those with wealthier chSES. This is often true even if they out-earn those individuals as adults (e.g., an adult in the 9th decile from below-average chSES has lower well-being on average than someone from wealthy chSES in the 5th decile as an adult). Concurrently, the income level for adults from low chSES to reach average well-being is significantly greater than those from high chSES. In other words, to reach average national well-being, someone from a poor family may need to out-earn someone from a wealthy family by 5-10 times in adulthood. Such outcomes are rare (see Supplement 2.7b). We refer to this pattern, which holds strong relevance in subsequent findings, as "psychological inheritance."

Childhood SES and national wealth

We also tested whether psychological inheritance effects were consistent across national income groups (see Figure 4). A model estimating the three-way interaction between income-decile, chSES, and national income group was a significantly better fit than a model that estimated only the two-way interaction between income-decile and chSES (Δχ²(12) = 31.6, p = .001). This result held even after removing the sparsely populated cell of low-income country individuals born to wealth (an extreme tail) (Δχ²(11) = 30.23, p = .001). Regarding the living wage ratio estimates, the addition of the threeway interaction did not significantly improve the fit of the model -income gradients differ by chSES and by national income context, but there is no additional cross-level moderation of SES differences by country income group in the living wage analyses.

Income satiation and well-being

We did not seek to validate or refute whether there is a satiation point where increased wealth is not associated with a substantive change in well-being, nor did we intend to align with any particular view supporting or opposing it. Instead, we sought to model the role of income along with a multidimensional measure of well-being that factored in delineations for economic background and context, which is a problematic omission given our findings on chSES impacts. Whether the climb from $50,000 to $60,000 is more significant on well-being than from $500,000 to $510,000 (equivalent increase) or even to $600,000 (proportional increase) may seem relevant to few, but as previous work inspired corporate initiatives to target satiation points (e.g., $75,000 base salaries), it is important that these are accurately reported.

Here, satiation is defined as the point/region on the income distribution at which further increases in income no longer yield substantively meaningful gains in psychological well-being. This distinguishes between (a) consistently positive slopes (no satiation), (b) decreasing but clearly positive slopes (diminishing marginal returns), and (c) slopes that are both negligibly small and statistically indistinguishable from zero (evidence of satiation). We tested income and living-wage (LW) satiation using parallel multilevel GAM pipelines predicting within-country MPWB from penalized smooths of log income or log LW ratio with country random intercepts (see Figure 5).

For log income, the pooled smooth was significantly nonlinear (edf = 4.74, F = 175.68, p < .001; adj. R² = 0.0178), but the derivative declined gradually and remained positive across the central 1st-99th percentile range, indicating no global satiation region. Allowing curvature to vary by World Bank income group (edf_low = 1.004; edf_LMI = 1.66; edf_UMI = 2.7; edf_HI =3.29; all p < .001; adj. R² = 0.022) revealed stronger nonlinearity in upper-middle and high-income countries (HI = 235.35), yet the derivative-based satiation criterion was not satisfied in any group. A factor-smooth specification permitting country-specific income curves substantially improved model fit relative to the randomintercept model (ΔDeviance = 460.08, ΔAIC ≈ -392.2, p < .001; country-specific edf = 137.06, F = 2.28, p < .001, adj. R² = 0.027), confirming cross-national heterogeneity in functional form. However, country-level derivative analyses identified only scattered near-zero intervals that did not cluster systematically by income group, indicating diminishing marginal returns without a universal or macro-structured satiation point.

For log LW ratio, the pooled smooth was significantly nonlinear(edf = 4.84, F = 190.39, p < 0.001; adj. R² = .019) and identified an upper near-zero interval between approximately 20.6 times and 63.7 times the living wage (log(LW ratio) = 3.03 -4.15). At the midpoint, the marginal effect was 0.0048 (95% CI [-0.021, 0.031]). For context, this would indicate that a location where a living wage is approximately $30,000, the point at which income no longer covaries with well-being is approximately $617k-$1.91M (midpoint ~ $1.26M). As with income, allowing country-specific smooth deviations significantly improved fit and detected upper satiation regions in 16 of 90 countries (17.77%), with a median midpoint of 31.74 times LW (IQR 28.4; Q25 21.76, Q75 50.22). In the remaining countries, slopes declined in the vast majority or remained the same, though marginal effects remained positive or statistically distinguishable from zero. Thus, at significant wealth, elasticity in the relationship lowers considerably and approaches zero. Decoupling wealth from well-being

At the inverse end of satiation, it is important also to ask if there are points at which no association between income and well-being exist. This is particularly important to ask at a national level of countries with a significant absence of economic opportunity: if income is universally associated with well-being, then economic development is a requirement for any significant improvement. Inversely, learning how individuals advance their own well-being in the absence of economic opportunity may offer substantial insights for effective policy, irrespective of national or individual wealth (our data were limited to test this aspect, but we have included a path for future research in Supplement 2.6b).

However, this may also be a heavily westernized framing, and critical dimensions of well-being, such as positive emotion, relationships, and self-esteem, are derived in entirely different ways.

Based on analyses of GDP, we find clearly diminishing associations between income and well-being, but stop short of absolute, national-wealth-driven decoupling (see Figure 6). To test whether the income-well-being association weakens or collapses as a function of national wealth, we first estimated country-specific income-well-being slopes and plotted them against log GDP per capita.

Slopes were generally positive across the full range of GDP. For countries under roughly US$10,000 GDP per capita, slopes were almost universally lower than 0.2. Weaker slopes were also observed in countries with GDPs above $10,000 and even $50,000 per capita, leading us to conclude that while diminishing effects may be more common among lowerincome countries, such decoupling is not purely the result of limited national wealth. Additionally, outliers such as Bahrain and UAE may be more reflective of distinct income distributions for disparate communities (e.g., professional class and migrant labor class) where the "richest poor person" may have greater well-being due to relative position and the "poorest rich person" may likewise be the inverse. Both points would also explain why some patterns of stagnant well-being appear in economically advancing countries.

Economic mobility and well-being

Rightly, most research on the role of economic mobility in well-being asks about the value of upward movement. However, while often dismissed as non-critical (for understandable reasons), downward mobility may also reveal critical patterns for understanding and addressing national well-being.

We modelled adult income and chSES as parallel five-rung ladders; both were independently associated with within-country MPWB in an additive multi-level model (income: β = 0.1335, SE = 0.0047, t = 28.03; chSES: β = 0.0991, SE = 0.0049, t = 19.88). Allowing for the interaction significantly improved model fit (Δχ²(1) = 24.99, p < 0.001). The interaction term was negative, indicating diminishing returns across advantage (β = -0.0216, SE = 0.0043, t = -5.01).

As indicated in Figure 7, an unbroken trend of extreme importance accounts for all directions of economic mobility. First, higher chSES is universally associated with greater well-being within adult income groups (e.g., adults with above-average incomes born with above-average wealth have higher average well-being than adults with the same income born into average, low-income, and poor homes). Second, any downward movement is associated with lower well-being compared to individuals who stayed in the higher income group as an adult. Third, adults who move downward still have greater average well-being than individuals who started in lower chSES in the same group.

Thus, no amount of upward economic mobility matches the well-being of those who were born at the same level; any downward movement is associated with lower well-being than those who remained at the same level, but above those who started lower.

To complement those effects, we plot two additional mobility patterns in Figure 7. The first approach shows how all upward mobility is associated with improvements in well-being compared to people who remained in the same income class as adults -effects are largest for those who increased most significantly. The inverse is also true: the greatest declines are found in individuals with the furthest downward movement. Collectively, the model produces a Simpson paradox: although higher income is clearly associated with greater well-being, it also creates larger opportunities for significant declines and fewer opportunities for upward movement (compared to low incomes) (see also Supplement 2.7c).

The second plot shows the average difference in well-being at each movement amount compared to individuals who remained in the chSES category on the LW dimension. This shows a uniform trend of upward movement associated with better well-being and downward movement associated with worse.

Repeating analyses for relative income yielded nearly identical effects, with slightly more stable patterns as indicated in Figure 7.

In line with psychological inheritance, we conclude the collection of these patterns also indicates a "psychological endowment" effect in which economic decline means larger margins to fall in wellbeing. However, the endowment also means the effect is still less than the difference in what was inherited initially. In other words, even if the decline is larger, those who started off wealthy still end up with greater well-being than those with similar adult incomes. with worse well-being than those that remained. The largest inclines and declines are observed at the ends of the income distribution but all economic mobility from and to any group associated with at least small differences.

Discussion

Research on the link between income and well-being has primarily focused on wealthier populations, few or limited measures (e.g., happiness, life satisfaction), and minimal consideration of the individual economic experience (past, present, anticipated). To comprehensively test salient questions about that relationship using a broader set of measures that accounts for relative effects, we modelled the association between income and well-being using over 20 indicators from 53,589 people in 91countries. Within those steps, we also included questions that would fill in gaps and account for debates about prevailing conclusions, then re-ran analyses to control for individual circumstances, such as local cost of living and economic history.

Through these analyses, we find an unambiguous, global (but not universal) positive association between income and well-being, where incomes around national averages and cost of living are highly elastic yet where the tails (absence or extreme) yield informative exceptions. The nearly-uniform effects of economic mobility -in both directions -serve as further corroboration that income has an impact on well-being, which accelerates as it increases (or decreases). With only minor exceptions, which were primarily nuances rather than deviations, findings aligned with primary hypotheses, which allow us to provide a unified narrative on the direct association with well-being.

Though consistent, we note the generally modest effect sizes in most analyses, indicating that while income is a strong predictor of well-being, it is not the only factor, and likely not among the most critical. These would surely relate to matters such as physical health and safety, satisfaction of basic needs and security, as well as fundamental freedoms. Yet, in using standardized scores of well-being, we can see clearly that income is far from irrelevant: modest changes income around local averages can shift an individual from having an overall negative to overall positive score, and the total ranges explained in some of our analyses cover as much as 20% of the total range in well-being. This elasticity should reinforce the importance of personal finances in policymaking.

It was not our intention to identify specific income thresholds for happiness, but rather to focus on general trends. However, we were generally compelled that across the World Bank income groups, a household income equivalent to approximately 4.5 times the living wage was the level at which wellbeing was reliably above average (see Supplement 3.2b). This is potentially of extreme relevance to policymakers, employers, and individuals: below that value, individuals primarily exhibit belowaverage well-being at a time when global mental health is languishing (i.e., the average itself is generally low).

There are naturally exceptions to and deviations from the positive association between income and well-being, which are extremely informative. First, it is clear that the relationship is strongest in higher-income locations: being low-income in a high-income area has a more negative relative association than having lower wealth in a poorer country. Thus, upward movement in such countries shows a clear, positive effect on well-being, indicating a highly elastic relationship. Of note, we do not find this to be uniquely or especially true in the US compared to other high-income countries. Discouragingly, on average, no amount of upward movement can equate to always having wealth (Wang & Hanson, 2024). It is clear that the broad, protective effects of significant wealth in childhood require at least two generations to materialize for the upwardly mobile. This makes sense when considering the supportive, resourceful networks experienced as individuals move up in wealth (Chetty et al., 2022); advancing in income alone does not solely account for the disadvantages of lower childhood wealth or the burdens that may carry into adulthood (Graham, 2009 (Graham, , 2010;; D. Sacks & Wolfers, 2010). Instead, the association is impacted by childhood well-being, which varies by wealth (Killingsworth et al., 2023). This trend, which we refer to as psychological inheritance, was observed in countries of all wealth levels, though the effect is more stable and decisively stronger in higher-income countries (van Hoorn & Sent, 2016). That understanding is vital to more appropriately understanding that income is not uniformly capable of ensuring well-being, unlike existing theory has implied, and therefore policies aimed at national prosperity cannot solely target incomes.

Elasticities in well-being, however, can vary heavily in relation to costs of living. At the top and bottom of income distributions for individuals and countries, there are clearly cases to be made for the existence of satiation (among the wealthiest individuals) and decoupling, which is visible in some locations but not limited to the lowest-income regions (Graham, 2009). This is noteworthy in that it confirms that after reaching a certain level surpassing costs of living, the relationship is strongest. Yet, above some levels of wealth, greater income may fail to offer increasing value toward well-being.

However, the level at which satiation appears to occur, we find, is substantially greater -and thus much harder to attain, particularly for those from lower initial wealth (Isaacs, 2007) or locations with fewer economic opportunities -than in previous findings.

Our goal in this study was not to redefine, resolve, or update satiation points; yet, confirming the existence of one along with some precision in real terms is immensely valuable for tangible policy recommendations that far exceed the standard but vacuous "good income" framing. Across GDP groups, we find even average well-being is not typically reached until household incomes approach around 4.5 times the relative cost of living. Not coincidentally, this point is near the middle of the most elastic points on the curve. Though we urge discernment in how the exact number is treated, it was compelling in how it was so consistently observed across countries despite substantially heterogeneity in other analyses.

In unrelated work, using data from the Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), we had identified individuals at around the 67th percentile as being at the threshold for where optimal financial behaviors (e.g., maximized retirement savings) are observed (Federal Reserve, 2023). A problem with pegging economic policy recommendations to percentiles is that, if incomes shift, percentiles change, and population mobility is zero-sum. In further exploring our finding of 4.5 times living wage, we found that the two values were nearly identical when applied in real terms. That is, where living wage was around 30,000, a household income of around 135,000 was typically in the 65th to 70th percentile. Unlike percentiles, though, a multiplicative of a moving value provides a sustainable policy target for incomes that match the purpose of measuring GDP in the first place (i.e., estimating and inferring population well-being). This overlap has motivated a new line of work to assess the validity of these values, as our data limit the extent to which we can do so.

Where weaker ties between income and well-being were observed, it seems evident that some individuals are able to generate at least some positive affect from their environment in the absence of sufficient financial resource and economic opportunity (Galbraith et al., 2024). Such patterns would also explain why a recent meta-analysis showed that within-country economic inequality is not a universally strong predictor of national well-being (Sommet et al., 2026). While our exploratory analyses were limited by the data collected at providing any clear factors that could drive well-being in such contexts (see Supplement 2.6b), we still consider this a meaningful path for future research.

Given the limited number of individuals that move from lower chSES to significant wealth, satiation effects largely relate to those who have considerable wealth in childhood and are typically in a wealthier economic environment. For lower chSES individuals, it is unsurprising that upward movement seems more likely to accelerate well-being. This aligns with evidence that sudden financial windfalls produce greater positive affect in those worse-off (Dwyer & Dunn, 2022). The loss of added (subjective) value from increased wealth is a privilege of those that have only known wealth, not of the positive deviant.

Such patterns make sense but also serve as a warning: while someone that has always and exclusively known wealth, added income offers little marginal value (or is at least highly inelastic). Someone that has moved up significantly likely still has burdens not experienced by the former (e.g., student loans, financial care for older family members, lack of support networks). Put bluntly: rich adults can still have poor parents. Those effects do not disappear with a good paycheck and may even be impeded by unequal treatment despite being upwardly mobile (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KTkcdJ. Sacks & Wolfers, 2010). Policymakers need better frames to consider this. Concurrently, the unambiguous effects for downward economic movement are a warning sign for regions of atrophying work opportunities. While a significant psychological endowment may offer protective effects, not all downward movement starts from substantial wealth. Instead, consider that the same effects are observed by those at all chSES: adults from middle and lower-middle class that do not secure similar economic opportunities as their parents are at risk of lower well-being across a significant number of years. If young adults are unable to reach that income level and do not feel the prospects will change in the foreseeable future, our findings would indicate a significant decline across a large population is inevitable.

Nationally, our patterns provide strong evidence that country-level mobility in all directions can accelerate or impede population well-being. Where there is greater wealth, it is unsurprising that wellbeing is higher. But where economic opportunity is only recently expanding, it equally makes sense that upward national well-being may rise even faster in the short run. Thus, rather than paradoxical, it is essentially systematic that when locations of great wealth experience cyclical or even seismic strains of economic opportunity at the individual level, there are simply more people capable of experiencing downward mobility. If some continue to move up but movement does not significantly outpace any downward movement, national gains in well-being will not be experienced.

Limitations

Our purpose in this manuscript was to present a robust argument about the limitations of income alone as an indicator of well-being when factoring in economic history. We do not claim it is a perfectly robust dataset nor body of analyses. Instead, we hope it presents a strong basis for further work, particularly studies that combine longitudinal and objective data as opposed to self-reported measures from a single collection. An extensive discussion of data limitations is already published in a separate manuscript (Ruggeri et al., 2026) with associated appendices and an interactive, online database.

Most relevant for this study, however, is that the analyses we present focus on large-group means and averages, thus they mainly offer a snapshot general trends at a moment in time. As in all such approaches, they do not speak to individual experiences, outliers, exceptions, or "incomplete stories" (that is, their income, well-being, and other measures are a moment in time, not necessarily where they were yesterday or tomorrow or in ten years).

No one should therefore interpret these as absolute: of course there will be individuals that reach the middle class from poverty and find themselves far happier than those that have always been wealthy.

There will, tragically, always be people that have had objectively ideal circumstances that suffer from poor mental health, attempt suicide, or otherwise not experience the emotional privilege enjoyed by most in their economic class peers. We therefore stress that our focus is general means; many deviations and exceptions absolutely exist and those neither verify nor invalidate what we conclude.

Additionally, it became clear that many existing disagreements on the topics in this article derive from differentiated approaches to data and analyses. Decisions on whether to use log income, raw income, disposable income, and so on have direct and nonzero impacts on conclusions, to say the very least. In fact, some of the strongest debates on this topic bluntly claim the alternate view is based on statistical shortcomings or artefacts (Dwyer & Dunn, 2022; Graham, 2010; D. W. Sacks et al., 2012; D. Sacks & Wolfers, 2010). Though re-testing those debates was not our focus we aimed to avoid those issues by using a broad set of approaches to data and modelling but that also meant we avoided a straight-line approach of methods. We also do not believe in a one-conclusion-fits-all interpretation of the link between income and well-being; there is clear heterogeneity within and between populations. It may be that such ambivalence adds comprehensiveness at the expense of depth. To that end, these data could be revisited with a predefined, narrow set of analytical guidelines, which may impact results.

Alternatively, the same approaches we use but applied to a different dataset may also impact conclusions. We enthusiastically support all such (and other) attempts to test these effects using our arguments as a baseline.

Conclusion

We present an unambiguous link between income and well-being through comprehensive analyses incorporating multiple contextual measures of individual economic circumstances. We conclude that the association is generally linear, though when adjusting income to account for costs of living, the pattern shifts to a log-linear relationship where elasticity is greatest as incomes pass living wage, but flatter at extremes. Those findings appear to reconcile existing debates on the relationship, while noting the satiation point here, around thirty times the living wage, is significantly higher than others have reported and associated with extreme wealth.

Most critically and central to our primary interest, however, is that we find childhood wealth is a strong predictor of well-being in adults, particularly in countries with greater national wealth. The greater the wealth someone is born into, the greater their well-being is likely to be as an adult, even if not achieving the same economic position. Concurrently, the diminishing value of higher income is primarily a privilege of only the wealthiest; upward economic mobility will continue having value for the majority even if it will rarely be enough to flourish.

Policymakers concerned about national prosperity must heed these insights with urgency. Similarly, employers now have a minimum target income level -around 4.5 times the local cost of living -if they generally wish for employees and their communities to flourish. Ultimately, individuals have no choice over the families or environments they are born into, so investments must be made toward those factors that, at all levels, ages, and backgrounds, promote the well-being of the populations they serve.
