Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

Experiment-based calibration is a novel method for measurement validation, which -unlike classical validity metrics -does not require stable between-person variance. In this approach, the latent variable to be measured is manipulated by an experiment, and its predicted scores -termed standard scores -are compared against the measured scores. Previous work has shown that under plausible boundary conditions, the correlation between standard and measured scorestermed retrodictive validity -is informative about measurement accuracy, i.e. combined trueness and precision. Here, I expand these findings in several directions. First, I formalise the approach in a probability-theoretic framework with the concept of a standardised calibration space. Second, I relate this framework to classical validity theory and show that the boundary conditions in fact apply to any form of criterion validity, including classical convergent validity. Thus, I state precise and empirically quantifiable boundary conditions under which criterion validity metrics are informative on validity. Third, I relate these boundary conditions to confounding variables, i.e. correlated latent variables. I show that in the limit, calibration will converge on the latent variable that is most closely related to the standard. Finally, I provide a framework for modelling the data-generating process with Markov kernels, and identify sufficient conditions under which the data generation model results in a calibration space. In sum, this article provides a formal probability-theoretic framework for experiment-based calibration and facilitates modelling and empirical assessment of the data generating processes.

1. Introduction

Experiment-based calibration is a technique for validation of measurement instruments. The conceptual idea is to evaluate measurement accuracy by comparing the outcome of a measurement method or instrument against a standard, defined as the predicted outcome of a well-defined experiment. This is a canonical technique in the physical sciences [14]. For example, the measurement of physical length can be evaluated against a standard based on the speed of light generated by a helium-neon laser.

Recently, a similar approach has been introduced to validate the the measurement of latent variables in experimental psychology, such as memory, attention, or perception [4, 3, 5]. For example, the measurement of perceived lightness can be evaluated against standard values derived from physical luminance together with a formal theory of lightness perception (e.g., the CIECAM02 model).

Experiment-based calibration was introduced to physical quantities in the mid-20th century, long after the laws of physics were already well understood.

In consequence, there is little doubt in the physical sciences whether, and how, a calibration experiment generates the quantity that one would like to measure (i.e., the measurand ). For example, there is no uncertainty that the helium-neon laser generates light that travels with an invariable speed, and the predicted outcome of an experiment can be very accurately computed. In contrast, uncertainty about the effect of an experimental manipulation is likely higher in psychology and social sciences. Sometimes, we can be quite sure that an experiment impacts on the measurand. Physical luminance of an object influences perceived lightness, and the CIECAM02 model is a very good quantitative model of this impact-but even this model does not numerically capture all aspects, such as the Helmholtz-Kohlrausch effect [9]. In other instances, the relation might be even quite loose or speculative. For example, when manipulating the number of practice trials in a learning task, this will on average affect resulting memory, but the numerical relation of trial number to memory cannot be precisely specified. This raises two key questions. (1) If the relation between experimental manipulation and the latent variable is quite loose, can calibration still yield information on measurement accuracy? (2) If yes, and if several latent variables are loosely impacted by the experimental manipulation, then how can we be sure which one we are actually measuring? For example, changing the luminance of an object changes its perceived lightness but might also affect its aesthetic perception, which we can think of as an "experimental confound". How can we ensure, in a calibration experiment, that we are evaluating the measurement of lightness perception and not of aesthetic value?

To formalise such questions, we briefly recapitulate the calibration approach for psychological latent variables (figure 1) [3]. The standard value of a measurand is generated as intended or predicted value of a certain experimental manipulation; denote it with S. Denote the (unknown) true value of the measurand with T , and the measured value of the measurand with Y . Because psychological variables have no natural scale, and actual scale might differ between samples and situations, comparing one measured value against one standard value bears little information. Instead, we will realise several (two or more) values of S, and compare them with the ensuing values of Y . Hence, S, T , and Y are defined as random variables, with the value of S randomly determined at every realisation of an experimental treatment. This experiment will be done multiple times and with multiple persons (in a within-or between-subjects design), such that we can evaluate the correlation between S and Y, ρ SY , which we term retrodictive validity [2].

As will be shown in detail below, ρ SY depends, among other things, on the measurement error ε := Y -T . Measurement error consists of two components.

The first is a random component, whereby Y takes different values on repeated measurements even when T is constant. This corresponds to a lack of precision of the measurement [6]. The second component is systematic, whereby the average of Y , across repeated measurements of constant T , deviates from T . This corresponds to a lack of trueness of the measurement (also termed bias) [6]. Naturally, imprecision contributes to the variance of ε. Perhaps counterintuitively, bias can also contribute to the variance of ε, when the bias is different for different values of T . In contrast, a "global bias" (the average of Y deviates from the average of T ) is of no interest here because the anchor point of a psychological variable is usually unknown anyway. Retrodictive validity, ρ SY , depends on error variance, so we can now compare two measurement methods by their retrodictive validity: the method with higher retrodictive validity will have lower error variance, which can mean higher trueness, or higher precision, or both. Thus, retrodictive validity can be taken as a criterion to compare measurement methods. This is conceptually similar to other forms of criterion validity [12], where the criterion is defined as the intended effects of the experimental manipulation.

To address the aforementioned key questions about uncertainty in the experimental manipulation, previous work has introduced the concept of experimental aberration ω := T -S, i.e., the difference between predicted and actually realised values of the latent variable [3]. As shown in the next section, retrodictive validity depends not only on measurement error but also on experimental aberration. In psychological experiments -in contrast to calibration in physicsexperimental aberration will presumably depend on the specific sample and experimental minutiae in ways that are often not understood in detail. Thus, it might differ between two similar calibration experiments. Hence, previous work has proposed to pitch two measurement methods against each other within the same experiment [3]. In this case, experimental aberration is constant for all tested measurement methods. This is particular simple when different measurement methods consist of different data transformations, applied to the same observables (e.g., reaction times). However, there are also many applications in which different observables can be acquired simultaneously for comparison.

Examples are key presses for which response time and response force can be observed simultaneously [17], or the simultaneous recording of skin conductance and electrocardiogram [11].

While aberration is constant for all measurement methods calibrated simultaneously, retrodictive validity depends also on the covariance of aberration and measurement error, which is different between methods. This is why previous work has analysed the covariance conditions under which retrodictive validity is informative on measurement error [3]. In result, if measurement error is uncorrelated with experimental aberration, then a method with higher retrodictive validity is guaranteed to have smaller measurement error variance. If this condition is not fulfilled, then there are boundary conditions under which ρ SY can be informative on measurement error. These will be recapitulated below.

With the current article, I seek to fill, in probability-theoretic terms, four gaps left by previous work. First, the previous model assumed a discrete distribution of S [3]. Here, I simplify and generalise the previous calibration model to arbitrary including continuous distributions of S and show that the previously derived boundary conditions remain applicable. Secondly, I demonstrate that there is a dual problem in classical validity theory, where "construct-irrelevant variance" or "methods variance" is formally analogous to covariance between experimental aberration and measurement error in calibration. I will show that the same boundary conditions apply to convergent validity. Third, I address the problem of experimental confounds in calibration. Finally, in order to enable simulations and theoretical work on the formal relationship between variables, I present a framework to model the data-generating relation between S, T , and Y.

3. A dual problem in classical convergent validity

Assume we seek to measure a real-valued latent variable T with two different measurement procedures which result in measured scores Y 1 and Y 2 . Then ρ Y1,Y2 := Cor (Y 1 , Y 2 ) is a convergent validity score. Convergent validity assessment is performed to demonstrate that both methods measure the same latent variable and not two different latent variables that are only loosely correlated.

However, there is a possibility that the correlation between the two methods is high not because the methods converge on the same latent variable but simply because the measurement errors of the two methods are correlated [12]. A typical reason for such correlation is the presence of "methods effects", i.e. variance components that depend on the measurement methods, rather than on the true scores. For a classical example from educational research, language abilities will affect any mathematics test that requires verbal reasoning. Two mathematics tests that use verbal reasoning will thus be correlated, regardless of how well they measure mathematics abilities. Such methods effects can be detected by assessing discriminant validity -i.e. the correlation between measured scores of two clearly different latent variables, established with the same method. In our example, mathematics scores might correlate with scores on a history test which assesses a different latent variable, because the measurement errors are correlated, i.e. discriminant validity is low here. Construct-irrelevant variance is perceived as a "threat to validity" [13], but how threatening is it really? In an application scenario, discriminant validity will often be non-zero because measurement instruments for the same latent variable typically target the same response channels (e.g., both requiring language). 4. The orthogonality assumption

In this section, we dissect the reasons why a calibration space might not be orthogonal, i.e. Cor (ω, ε) ̸ = 0. At first glance, orthogonality appears very reasonable: experimental manipulation and measurement instrument are procedurally unrelated, unlike two different measurement methods in classical convergent validity assessment. Hence, the measurement instrument does not "know" that on a particular experiment trial, the realised true score was higher than expected, and so cannot "counter" this with a particular value of measurement error. The problematic case that Cor (ω, ε) < 0 (aberration and error are anticorrelated) would imply that (some of) the aberration, induced by the experiment, is subtracted from the true value during the measurement. Unless the measurement system is "informed" about the standard values or aberration, how could this possibly happen?

One explanation can be found in the notion of confounds. Assume that many psychological latent variables are weakly correlated. When we seek to manipulate or measure a psychological variable, then we might term any other latent variable that is correlated as confound. Now assume that we seek to calibrate the measurement of a particular psychological variable T 1 , but both our experimental manipulation and the measurement system actually tap into a confounder variable T 2 . To illustrate this with an extreme example, imagine that we seek to calibrate the measurement of aesthetic value. However, our naive calibration experiment manipulates the luminance of of a physical stimulus, and our naive measurement instrument measures the perception of lightness. Assuming that aesthetic value is weakly correlated with lightness, then the assumptions of a calibration space in definition 1 are fulfilled. However, for each realisation of S, the true score for aesthetic value T will quite dramatically deviate from S.

In turn, because we are measuring lightness rather than aesthetic value, the measured value Y will again dramatically deviate from T , but in the opposite direction. Thus, ω and ε are "very" anti-correlated. From theorem 6, it follows that we can potentially increase retrodictive validity by increasing measurement error. In doing so, Y will become an even worse measure of aesthetic value and, at the same time, become an even better measure of lightness, which however we are not interested in. This demonstrates that non-orthogonality results from mis-specification of the latent variable T . However, we can always find a more appropriate latent variable T ′ for which the calibration space is orthogonal (see lemma 4).

Non-orthogonality implies that for a given latent variable, both the calibration experiment and the measurement instrument are mis-specified in the same way. We might sometimes have more subtle doubts about the appropriateness of the calibration procedure, and we might be wondering which latent variable we are actually measuring. The crucial role of the calibration standard when arbitrating between confounds is discussed in the next section.

6. Constructing a generative calibration model

The previous sections defined a calibration space as a tupel of a probability space and a set of real-valued random variables with minimal constraints, and analysed their interrelation. Researchers will often be interested in modelling or simulating how these variables come about [1]. Throughout, we assume orthogonality, noting that we can always construct a non-orthogonal calibration space from an orthogonal one and vice versa.

The first is to cast the data-generating relation as a three-stage probabilistic process with a starting distribution for S, and transition (Markov) kernels that specify T and Y . 7. Discussion

Experiment-based calibration is a relatively novel approach to psychometrics in experimental fields [3], with several examples of practical application [2, 5] and simulations [1].

The current work generalises and expands the previous work into several directions and thus contributes to laying a rigorous groundwork for this research program. First, I show that previously derived boundaries under which retrodictive validity can be informative on measurement accuracy hold for any distribution of the experimental standard values. Second, I show that the same boundaries apply in the assessment of convergent validity in classical psychometrics. Third, I dissect the role of confounding variables, i.e.

variables that covary with the latent variable of interest. When a single method is analysed, if this method relates more closely to latent variable T 1 than to latent variable T 2 , then T 1 will have a less close relationship to the standard values. When multiple methods are compared under ideal conditions, then calibration will converge on the latent variable that has the closer relationship to the standard values. This underlines the importance of the experimental standard. Finally, I lay out a generative calibration model and derive identities that can be used for analytical and numerical modelling and simulations [1].

Experiment-based calibration requires that substantive theory allows predicting standard values in an experiment, and is thus suitable for consolidated research areas. This reflects the approach of theory-based measurement, whereby any measurement of latent variables should be motivated and defined via substantive theory rather than by the measurement instrument (including its latent variable model) [7]. Interestingly, calibration can also help solving a problem well-known in measurement theory: that there is an (unknown) non-linear mapping from the latent variable to the measured score. Such non-linearity renders, for example, interpretation of non-crossover interaction effects in linear models ambiguous: what appears as an interaction at the level of the measured score might have been caused by a non-linear mapping from latent to measured score, when in fact there is no interaction at the level of the latent variable Loftus [10], Wagenmakers et al. [18]. While statistical techniques have been developed to identify such interactions Dunn and Anderson [8], calibration could aid solving the problem in a more fundamental manner. Non-linearities in the mapping from latent to measured score reduce trueness of the measurement -and thereby, retrodictive validity. If this non-linearity were known, it could be embedded in the measurement model, thus rendering the mapping from latent to the resulting measured score linear. This would, in turn, increase retrodictive validity. In consequence, including various candidate non-linearities into the measurement model and selecting the one with highest retrodictive validity will, in the limit, minimise non-linearities. As one result, this will improve the interpretation of interactions in substantive experiment using this measurement method.

As any tool, experiment-based calibration comes with limitations. Most notable is the orthogonality assumption, i.e. that aberration and error are uncorrelated [3]. In the present work, I show that violations of this assumptions correspond to a mis-specified substantive theory. As another limitation, calibration does not cleanly separate trueness and precision. Previous work has identified surrogate metrics that can be used for this purpose [3], and alternatively, calibration can be complemented with test-retest reliability assessment, which directly assesses precision [12]. Ultimately, it might be possible to derive clear metrics of uncertainty on psychological measurement [16, 15]. With the present work, I hope to aid the modelling work that will put this approach into practice.
